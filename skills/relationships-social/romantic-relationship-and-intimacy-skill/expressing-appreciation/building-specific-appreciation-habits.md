# Building specific appreciation habits

**Goal:** make your partner regularly feel noticed and valued — in ways that land for
*them* — so the relationship doesn't slide into running a household together while
quietly feeling unappreciated.

## 1. Specific beats general
"Thanks for everything" is pleasant but forgettable. Specific appreciation names
**what they did** and **what it meant to you**:
> "Thank you for sorting out the car insurance — I'd been dreading it and it's such
> a relief to not have it hanging over me."

Formula: *I noticed* [specific action or quality] *and it* [effect on you / what it
says about them].
Also appreciate **qualities**, not only tasks: "I love how patient you were with your
mum on the phone today."

## 2. Make it a habit, not an occasion
- **Daily:** one specific thank-you or appreciation, said out loud or texted. It
  takes ten seconds.
- **Notice the routine work.** The things that get taken for granted are usually the
  repetitive ones: cooking, school runs, remembering birthdays, earning, managing the
  calendar. Name them.
- **Weekly:** at a quiet moment, tell them one thing you appreciated about them that week.
- **In front of others** (kindly, not performatively): crediting your partner to
  friends or family is a strong signal.
A useful mental habit from couples research: **scan for what your partner is doing
right**, not only what's missing. Contempt grows when you only notice the faults
(see relationships-social.romantic-relationship-and-intimacy-skill.couple-communication.soft-start-ups-and-turning-toward-bids).

## 3. Love languages — useful shorthand, not science
Gary Chapman's popular framework proposes five ways people express and receive love:
**words of affirmation, acts of service, receiving gifts, quality time, physical touch**.
- **Useful idea:** people often give love in the form *they* like to receive it, and
  miss that their partner wants something else. One person mows the lawn (acts of
  service) while the other longs to hear "I love you" (words).
- **Caveat:** research does not strongly support the idea that each person has one
  fixed "language", or that matching languages predicts relationship success. Most
  people value several. Treat it as a conversation starter, not a diagnosis.
- **Better than a quiz:** ask directly.
  > "When have you felt most loved by me recently? What's something small I could do
  > more of?"
  Also listen to complaints — "we never just sit together" is often a request in disguise.

## 4. Countering taken-for-grantedness
If you feel unappreciated:
- Say it as a need, not an accusation: "I've been feeling a bit invisible with all
  the school stuff. It would mean a lot to hear you notice it sometimes."
- Be concrete about what would help.
- Model it: appreciation tends to be reciprocated.

If your partner says they feel taken for granted:
- Don't defend with a list of what *you* do. Acknowledge it first: "I'm sorry — you're
  right, I haven't said it. I do notice."
- Then change the behaviour; one heartfelt statement fades without follow-through.

## Gotchas
- **Appreciation isn't a payment for compliance** or a softener before a complaint
  ("compliment sandwich") — partners learn to brace for the "but".
- **Sarcastic praise** ("oh, *thanks* for finally doing the dishes") lands as contempt.
- **Gifts aren't a substitute** for attention or for repairing a hurt.
- Cultural and family backgrounds shape how comfortable people are with verbal
  affection; some people show love more through action. Learn your partner's dialect.

## Verify
- [ ] In the last day you've given at least one specific appreciation (action + effect).
- [ ] You can name two or three ways your partner most likes to be shown love — because you asked.
- [ ] You've appreciated a quality, not only a task, this week.
- [ ] You can list the routine work your partner does that rarely gets thanked.
