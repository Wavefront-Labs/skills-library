# Soft start-ups and turning toward bids

**Goal:** make everyday communication with a partner easier and warmer — raise
complaints without starting a fight, and respond to small moments of connection so
goodwill builds up over time.

Several of the concepts here come from John Gottman's research on couples (soft
start-up, bids, the "four horsemen"). The specific statistics often quoted from that
work are not reproduced here; the habits are widely taught in couples counselling.

## 1. Bids for connection — and turning toward them
A **bid** is any small attempt to get the other person's attention, interest or
support: "Look at that bird", a sigh, a shared link, "how was your day?", a hand on
the shoulder.
There are three ways to respond:
- **Turning toward:** acknowledging it, even briefly ("Oh wow, what is it?").
- **Turning away:** not noticing or ignoring it (eyes stay on the phone).
- **Turning against:** an irritable or dismissive response ("Can't you see I'm busy?").
Small turn-toward responses, many times a day, are a large part of how couples
maintain closeness. **Practice:** for a week, notice bids — yours and theirs. You
don't have to drop everything; "Hang on, two minutes and I'm all yours" still counts
as turning toward.

## 2. Soft start-up: how to raise a problem
The first minutes of a difficult conversation strongly shape how it goes. A harsh
start-up (blame, sarcasm, "you always") tends to get defensiveness back.
A soft start-up has four parts:
1. **Start with "I", describe the situation, not their character.**
2. **Say what you feel.**
3. **Say what you need, positively** (what you'd like, not what they did wrong).
4. **Keep it calm and short.**

| Harsh | Soft |
|---|---|
| "You never help with the kids' bedtime." | "I'm really tired by 8pm. Could you take bedtime on Tuesdays and Thursdays?" |
| "You're so selfish with money." | "I got anxious seeing the card bill. Can we sit down Sunday and look at the budget together?" |

Also: choose the moment (not as they walk in the door, not in front of others).

## 3. Watch for the "four horsemen"
Patterns that predict conflicts escalating:
- **Criticism** — attacking character ("you're lazy") instead of the behaviour. Antidote: soft start-up.
- **Contempt** — eye-rolling, mockery, sneering superiority. The most corrosive.
  Antidote: build a habit of appreciation (see
  relationships-social.romantic-relationship-and-intimacy-skill.expressing-appreciation.building-specific-appreciation-habits).
- **Defensiveness** — counter-attacking or playing the victim. Antidote: take
  responsibility for even a small part ("You're right, I did forget").
- **Stonewalling** — shutting down, going silent. Usually a sign of being overwhelmed.
  Antidote: a structured break (below).

## 4. Flooding and time-outs
When heart rate and stress spike ("flooding"), listening and problem-solving get much
harder. If either of you is flooded:
- Say so and **name a return time**: "I'm getting too worked up to listen well. Can
  we take 30 minutes and come back to this at 8?"
- During the break, do something calming — not rehearsing your argument.
- **Come back at the agreed time.** A time-out with no return is just stonewalling.

## 5. The stress-reducing conversation (daily debrief)
A 15–20 minute daily check-in about stress *outside* the relationship (work, family):
- Take turns; the listener's job is to be on the speaker's side, not to fix things
  or play devil's advocate.
- Ask questions, show understanding ("that sounds infuriating"), and offer help only
  if asked.

## Gotchas
- **These habits don't fix abuse.** If one partner is afraid, controlled or hurt,
  communication tips are not the answer — contact a domestic-violence service.
  Couples therapy is generally not recommended where there is ongoing abuse.
- Soft start-ups don't mean never raising issues; bottling up is its own problem.
- Repair attempts (a joke, "I'm sorry, let me try that again") mid-argument help a
  lot — accept your partner's when they offer them.
- Persistent gridlock is a reason to see a couples therapist, not a failure.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name three bids from the last day and how you responded.
- [ ] Your last complaint began with "I feel... I need...", not "you always".
- [ ] You can recognise criticism vs. complaint, and contempt in yourself.
- [ ] You have an agreed time-out phrase and return time.
- [ ] You've tried a stress-reducing debrief at least a few times.
