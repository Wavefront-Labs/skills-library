# Reciprocity in friendship — keeping the give-and-take balanced

**Goal:** friendships where both people feel the effort and support roughly even out
over time — and a calm way to fix it when they don't. The key phrase is **over time**:
friendships aren't ledgers, and imbalance in a given month is normal.

## 1. What gets exchanged
Effort in a friendship comes in several currencies:
- **Initiation** — making plans, reaching out first.
- **Logistics** — travelling, hosting, booking.
- **Emotional support** — listening, being there in hard times.
- **Practical help** — favours, lifts, moving house, childcare.
- **Interest** — asking about their life, remembering details.
Imbalance in one currency is often balanced by another: a friend who never initiates
might always host, or be the one who shows up in a crisis. Before deciding a
friendship is one-sided, check all the currencies.

## 2. Noticing real imbalance
Warning signs over **several months**, not one busy patch:
- You initiate nearly every contact; if you stop, contact stops.
- Conversations are mostly about them; they rarely ask about you.
- They're present for good times but absent in your hard times.
- You feel drained or resentful after seeing them more than you feel good.
Also check the reverse: are *you* the one taking? Friends going through illness,
new babies, bereavement or depression may genuinely have less to give for a while —
that's what friendship is for.

## 3. Asking for help (the half people skip)
Reciprocity breaks when one person never lets the other give.
- **Ask specifically:** "Could you help me carry a couch on Saturday morning? About
  an hour." Specific, bounded asks are easier to say yes to.
- **Accept offers.** "Thanks — actually, yes, that would really help."
- **Being asked can deepen a bond.** The "Benjamin Franklin effect" — people tend to
  like those they've done favours for — is a widely cited idea, though the research
  base is modest; asking a small favour is low risk either way.

## 4. Raising one-sidedness
If it bothers you, say so before resentment builds — kindly, specifically, with no
verdict:
- "I've noticed I'm usually the one who sets things up, and sometimes I wonder if
  you're keen. I'd love it if you reached out sometimes too."
- "I've been leaning on you a lot lately — I want to make sure I'm there for you too.
  How are *you* doing?"

don't realise; they assumed you liked organising.

## 5. Adjusting your effort
If raising it changes nothing:
- **Match their level** for a while — reach out less and see what happens. Some
  friendships settle at a lighter level, which is fine.
- **Reframe the friendship** — they might be a great "once a year catch-up" friend
  rather than a weekly one.
- If it's consistently draining or exploitative, see ending-friendships.

## Gotchas
- Keeping a literal tally (who paid, who texted last) poisons the friendship faster than the imbalance does.
- Giving to create obligation, then resenting it when it isn't repaid.
- Never asking for anything, then feeling unsupported — they may not know you need anything.
- Mistaking different styles (a poor texter who's brilliant in person) for low effort.

## Verify
- [ ] You checked effort across all currencies, over months, before concluding imbalance.
- [ ] You've asked a friend for a specific, bounded favour recently or accepted an offer.
- [ ] Any imbalance you raised was framed as a wish, not an accusation.
- [ ] You've adjusted your effort level rather than silently stewing.
