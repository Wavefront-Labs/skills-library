# Maintaining friendships — staying in touch over time and distance

**Goal:** keep the friends you already have, through moves, new jobs, partners and
children. Most friendships don't end in a fight; they end in drift. Maintenance is
cheap compared with rebuilding (see rebuilding-lapsed-friendships).

## 1. Standing rituals beat ad-hoc plans
Every plan you have to arrange from scratch is a chance for it not to happen. Set
recurring defaults:
- **"First Thursday of the month, same pub."**
- A weekly or fortnightly walk or call at a fixed time.
- **An annual trip or weekend** with a long-distance friend or group, booked a year ahead.
- A shared tradition: a birthday dinner, a film you see every December, a fantasy
  league, a book swap.
Rituals survive busy seasons because they don't need a decision each time.

## 2. A light contact rhythm
Rough tiers (adjust to taste):
| Friend | Contact | Meet |
|---|---|---|
| Closest few | Weekly-ish messages | Monthly or as often as distance allows |
| Good friends | Every few weeks | Every few months |
| Wider circle | A few times a year | Once or twice a year |

These aren't rules — some friendships happily go months without contact and pick up
instantly. The point is to choose, not drift.

## 3. No-occasion check-ins
The most effective maintenance is small and unprompted:
- "Saw this and thought of you" + a photo, link or meme.
- "How did the [thing you mentioned] go?" — remember and follow up.
- A voice note on your walk home.
- Responding to their news within a day, even briefly.
Research on "reaching out" (e.g. a 2022 study by Liu and colleagues on surprise
check-ins; specifics unconfirmed here) suggests people appreciate unexpected
contact more than senders predict.

## 4. Long distance
- **Mix media:** text for small stuff, voice notes for warmth, a scheduled video call
  for depth, an occasional physical letter or parcel.
- **Do something together remotely:** watch the same show and message during it,
  play an online game, cook the same recipe on a call.
- **Plan visits with dates.** "You must come visit!" means nothing until a weekend
  is named.
- **Time zones:** agree on a reliable call window rather than spontaneous attempts.

## 5. Through life transitions
- **New parents:** go to them; bring food; expect short, interrupted visits; keep
  inviting even when they say no for a year.
- **New partner, new job, a move:** the friend's bandwidth drops. Lower the ask
  (a 20-minute coffee) rather than stop asking.
- **Hard times:** this is when maintenance matters most (see grief-and-hardship-peer-support).

## 6. A simple system
Memory fails; a system doesn't.
- **Birthdays in your calendar** with a reminder a few days ahead.
- A **short list** of friends you want to keep close, reviewed monthly: "Who haven't
  I contacted in a while?" A note in your phone works; so does a spreadsheet or a
  "personal CRM" app.
- **Notes of their news** (a new job, a sick parent) so you can follow up.

## Gotchas
- Waiting for the "right time" to reach out — a short message now beats a perfect one never.
- Keeping score of who initiated last; occasional imbalance is normal (see reciprocity-in-friendship).
- Only contacting to ask for something.
- Social-media "likes" mistaken for contact.

## Verify
- [ ] Each close friendship has at least one standing ritual or recurring call.
- [ ] Friends' birthdays are in your calendar with reminders.
- [ ] You've sent at least one no-occasion check-in this week.
- [ ] Long-distance friendships have a dated next visit or call.
