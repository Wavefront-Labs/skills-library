# Ending and downgrading friendships — stepping back with minimal harm

**Goal:** when a friendship has become draining, harmful or simply no longer fits, end
or reduce it in a way that is honest, proportionate and leaves as little damage as
possible — for them, for you and for shared friends.

## 1. First, decide what you actually want
- **Downgrade** — keep it friendly but less close: fewer meetups, lighter topics, group
  settings instead of one-to-one. Most "I'm done with this friend" feelings are actually
  this.
- **End** — no ongoing friendship, though you might be civil when you meet.
- **Cut off** — no contact at all. Appropriate when there's abuse, manipulation,
  harassment or danger. Your safety comes before politeness.
Also ask: is this a fixable problem you haven't raised? If so, try the conversation in
reciprocity-in-friendship or conversation-and-active-listening.difficult-conversation-entry first.

## 2. Option A — the gradual fade
Suitable when the friendship is casual, the other person isn't heavily invested, and
there's no issue that needs naming.
- Initiate less; respond warmly but briefly; decline one-to-one plans while still
  saying yes to group events sometimes.
- **Be kind, not deceptive** — avoid inventing elaborate excuses you'll have to keep up.
  "I've got a lot on at the moment" is true enough for most fades.
- **Risk:** the other person may be confused or hurt, keep asking, or eventually ask
  what's wrong. If they ask directly, answer honestly (Option B).

## 3. Option B — the direct conversation
Better when the friendship was close, when they've asked, or when a fade would be cruel.
- **In person or a call** for close friends; a thoughtful message is acceptable if
  meeting would be unsafe or impractical.
- **Speak about yourself and the fit, not their character:**
  "I've realised I need to step back from our friendship. I've felt for a while that
  we want different things from it, and I don't think I can be the friend you need
  right now. I'm grateful for [specific good thing]."
- **Be clear about what changes.** "I won't be coming to Thursday dinners" leaves less
  confusion than vague distance.
- **Don't relitigate every grievance.** If they ask why, give one or two honest reasons,
  briefly. You don't need their agreement to make the decision.
- **Expect hurt or anger;** acknowledge it ("I understand this is painful") without
  reversing your decision on the spot.

## 4. Ghosting
Disappearing without explanation is generally the most hurtful option for a close
friend, because it leaves them guessing. It is reasonable when contact is unsafe or
the other person has ignored a clear boundary.

## 5. Mutual friends and shared spaces
- **Don't recruit sides.** Tell mutual friends only what they need ("We're not close
  any more, but I'm happy to be at the same events").
- **Be civil at shared events:** a nod and a hello; no cold-shouldering that makes
  others uncomfortable.
- **Social media:** muting is quiet; unfollowing or blocking sends a message — choose
  deliberately. Block if there's harassment.

## 6. Afterwards
- Expect grief, even when you chose it. Friendship endings are real losses.
- **Hold the boundary** if they reach out repeatedly; a brief, consistent reply
  ("I meant what I said; I wish you well") is enough. You can stop replying after that.
- If they reach out later in a genuinely different way, you're allowed to reconsider.

## Gotchas
- Fading on a close friend who then has to discover it — usually crueller than a kind, clear talk.
- Making the conversation a list of their faults.
- Ending things by post or group chat.
- Staying in a friendship out of guilt that is damaging your wellbeing.

## Verify
- [ ] You decided between downgrade, end or cut-off before acting.
- [ ] You chose fade vs. conversation based on how close and invested they are.
- [ ] Any conversation focused on fit and on what changes, not their character.
- [ ] Mutual friends weren't asked to take sides; you're civil in shared spaces.
- [ ] You're holding the boundary consistently.
