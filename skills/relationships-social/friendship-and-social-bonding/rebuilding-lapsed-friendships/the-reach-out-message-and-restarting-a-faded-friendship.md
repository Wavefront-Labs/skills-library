# Rebuilding lapsed friendships — the reach-out and restart

**Goal:** get back in touch with someone you've lost contact with — whether the
friendship faded quietly or ended badly — and give it a real chance to restart. The
good news: dormant friendships often restart faster than new ones form, because the
shared history is still there.

## 1. Diagnose: fade or rupture?
- **Fade** — nobody did anything wrong; life (moves, kids, jobs) got in the way. Most
  lapsed friendships are this. The reach-out can be light.
- **Rupture** — there was a hurt, a fight, a betrayal, or one of you went silent in
  a moment the other needed you. This needs acknowledgement before the friendship
  can restart (section 4).

## 2. The fade reach-out — keep it light and easy to answer
A good message has four parts:
1. **A warm reason:** "I was just thinking about you" / "Saw a photo from our trip to the coast."
2. **Light acknowledgement of the gap** — one line, no grovelling: "I know it's been ages."
3. **Genuine interest:** "How are you? How's the [job / city / kids]?"
4. **No pressure:** "No need to write an essay back — would just love to hear how you are."

Example:
"Hey Ana! A song came on today that I haven't heard since that road trip, and it made
me think of you. I know it's been far too long — how are you doing? Still in Bristol?
No pressure to reply at length, I'd just love to catch up sometime."

Text, email or a social-media message all work for a fade; pick the channel they
actually use.

## 3. If they reply — make a plan, then a rhythm
- Respond warmly and quickly; ask a follow-up.
- Within a few messages, **propose something concrete**: a call, a coffee, a visit when
  you're in town.
- After the first catch-up, **suggest a next one** before parting, or set up a light ritual
  (see maintaining-friendships). Without rhythm, the lapse repeats.

## 4. After a rupture — acknowledge, own your part, don't demand
- **Lead with acknowledgement:** "I've thought a lot about how things ended between us,
  and I'm sorry for my part in it — especially [specific thing]."
- **Own only your part,** specifically. Don't list theirs.
- **No justifications in the apology.** "I was going through a lot" can come later if asked.
- **Leave the door open, not pushed:** "If you're open to it, I'd really like to talk.
  If not, I understand."
- **Expect slowness.** They may need time or may decline. Respect it.
For the apology itself in depth, see the conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation field.

## 5. If they don't reply
- One gentle follow-up after a few weeks is fine ("Just floating this back up in case
  it got buried").
- After that, leave it. You've done your part; the door is open if they want it.
- Don't read silence as hostility — people are overwhelmed, embarrassed, or bad at messages.

## Gotchas
- **Over-apologising** for a fade makes it heavier than it was; both of you let it drift.
- **Opening with a request** (a favour, a business pitch, a wedding invite) after years
  of silence feels transactional. Reconnect first.
- **Reliving the past only** — catch up on who they are now, not just nostalgia.
- **Expecting the old closeness instantly**; rebuild in steps.
- Reconnecting with someone who was harmful to you is optional, not a moral duty.

## Verify
- [ ] You decided whether this was a fade or a rupture before writing.
- [ ] Your message had a warm reason, a one-line acknowledgement, a question and no pressure.
- [ ] For a rupture, you owned your part specifically and left the door open without pushing.
- [ ] If they replied, a concrete next step was set within a few messages.
- [ ] If they didn't, you sent at most one follow-up.
