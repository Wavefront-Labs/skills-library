# Making friends — turning acquaintances into friends as an adult

**Goal:** go from "people I see sometimes" to "people I choose to spend time with".
Adult friendships rarely happen on their own the way school ones did; someone has to
initiate, and it can be you.

## 1. Why it's harder now — and what that tells you
School and university supply three ingredients for free: **proximity**, **repeated
unplanned contact**, and **a setting where people can let their guard down**.
Adult life removes most of them, so you have to rebuild them on purpose.

Communication researcher Jeffrey Hall's 2018 study estimated it takes very roughly
**~50 hours** together to move from acquaintance to casual friend, **~90** to friend,
and **200+** to close friend (figures approximate; the study was based on
self-reports). The practical lesson: friendship is mostly accumulated time, so plan
for repeated contact, not one great conversation.

## 2. Get repeated contact
Choose one or two **recurring** settings where the same people show up weekly:
- A class, team sport, running club, choir, climbing gym, volunteering shift,
  board-game night, faith community, community garden.
- Recurring beats one-off: a weekly 7pm pottery class beats a big networking event.
- Show up consistently for at least **two to three months** before judging it.

## 3. Make the first move — specific and low-stakes
Acquaintances often like each other and both wait. Break the stalemate:
- **Extend the existing context first:** "A few of us are grabbing a drink after
  class — want to come?"
- **Then a specific one-to-one plan:** "I'm going to that market on Saturday morning —
  want to come along?" A day, a place, an activity. "We should hang out sometime" rarely
  happens.
- **Activity-based** plans (walk, coffee, a gig, a hike) are easier than open-ended "hanging out".
- **Get contact details** as part of the invitation: "Give me your number and I'll send
  you the details."

## 4. Move beyond the shared context
A "work friend" or "gym friend" becomes a friend when you see each other **outside**
the setting that introduced you. The first out-of-context plan is the key step.
After that, mix one-to-one time with small-group time.

## 5. Keep initiating — longer than feels comfortable
- Expect to initiate the first few times. Many people are delighted to be asked but
  rarely ask first; that isn't necessarily disinterest.
- **Rule of thumb:** after two or three invitations that are declined *without a
  counter-offer* ("Can't Saturday, but how about Sunday?"), step back and let them
  initiate. A decline *with* a counter-offer is a yes.
- **Follow up** on what they told you: "How did the interview go?" shows you remember.
- Self-disclose gradually as you spend time together (see deepening-friendships).

## Gotchas
- Waiting to be invited. Research on the "liking gap" (people underestimate how much
  others enjoyed talking with them) suggests your reach-out is likely more welcome than
  you fear — finding cited by several studies, magnitude unconfirmed here.
- Spreading yourself too thin: ten new acquaintances you see once each won't become
  friends; three you see weekly might.
- Treating rejection of a plan as rejection of you. People are busy.
- Heavy disclosure too early can overwhelm; let depth follow time.
- Only ever meeting online. Video helps, but in-person contact builds friendship faster.

## Verify
- [ ] You attend at least one recurring activity with the same people.
- [ ] You've made a specific invitation (day, place, activity) to someone in the last month.
- [ ] You've met at least one acquaintance outside the setting where you met.
- [ ] You can tell a "no with counter-offer" from a "no" and respond accordingly.
