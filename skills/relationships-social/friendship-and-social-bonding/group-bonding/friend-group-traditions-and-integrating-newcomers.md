# Group bonding — traditions, newcomers and keeping the group together

**Goal:** a friend group that keeps meeting, feels like "us", and lets new people in.
Groups don't stay together by accident; nearly every long-running friend group has
someone (or a rotation) doing the convening, and a few traditions that do the rest.

## 1. Traditions are the glue
A tradition removes the "should we do something?" problem.
- **Recurring:** a monthly dinner, a Sunday run, a quiz-night team, a game night.
- **Annual:** a summer trip, a Friendsgiving-style dinner, a birthday weekend, a
  New Year's ritual.
- **Small rituals within meetings:** a toast, a round of "highs and lows", the same
  takeaway after the pub.
Start one with a light "Shall we make this a thing?" after an evening that went well.
Book the next date before people leave.

## 2. Share the convening load
- **One organiser** is normal, but if they burn out, the group stalls. Rotate hosting
  or picking the activity.
- **Make it easy to say yes:** propose a date and plan, not an open question. "Dinner at
  mine on the 14th, 7pm — who's in?" beats "When's everyone free?"
- **Use a scheduling poll** for groups of 5+ (any polling feature in your chat app or a
  scheduling site); pick a date when most can make it, not all.
- **Default to going ahead** with whoever can come. Waiting for everyone means never meeting.

## 3. The group chat
- Good for logistics, photos, light banter and keeping in loose contact between meetups.
- Bad for conflict, serious news about one member (ask them first), and decisions
  that need discussion.
- **Watch for quiet members** — someone who stops posting may be drifting; a direct
  message ("Haven't heard from you in a bit — all good?") helps.

## 4. Integrating newcomers
New partners, colleagues or friends-of-friends:
- **Brief them** beforehand: who's who, a little context.
- **Translate inside jokes** or drop them for the evening. A wall of references nobody
  explains tells a newcomer they're outside.
- **Assign a buddy** — someone who introduces them and checks in during the night.
- **Ask them questions** and pull them into the conversation (see
  conversation-and-active-listening.turn-taking-and-flow).
- **Invite them again.** One event rarely integrates anyone; the second and third do.
- Include them in the group chat if they're becoming regular — ask the group first if
  that's the norm.

## 5. Preventing splits
- **Sub-groups are natural** (people closer to some than others). Trouble starts when
  sub-group events are visibly flaunted to excluded members. Be discreet with photos
  of smaller gatherings.
- **Don't make members choose sides** in conflicts between two people; let them sort it
  directly (see conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation).
- **Life stages diverge:** members with kids, night-shift jobs or who move away. Vary
  formats — a daytime picnic kids can come to, a video call for the one abroad.

## Gotchas
- Waiting for everyone's availability before setting a date.
- Gossiping about absent members — everyone learns the group talks about the absent.
- The organiser never being thanked or relieved.
- Newcomers being interviewed rather than welcomed.

## Verify
- [ ] The group has at least one recurring or annual tradition with a next date set.
- [ ] Organising rotates or the organiser has been explicitly thanked and helped.
- [ ] The last newcomer was briefed, buddied, included in conversation and invited again.
- [ ] Smaller gatherings aren't flaunted in front of those not invited.
