# Showing up, keeping weak ties warm and pulling your weight

**Goal:** remain a valued member of a community — a neighbourhood, congregation, club,
school community, cultural group — over the long run, not just in the first flush of
joining. Long-term belonging rests on three habits: **turning up for others**, **small
regular contact**, and **doing your share**.

## 1. Show up for others' occasions
Communities remember who came.
- **Funerals and memorials** rank highest: going matters even if you didn't know the
  deceased well; you're there for the living. A short card if you can't attend.
- **Celebrations:** weddings, christenings, milestone birthdays, a member's exhibition or
  match, a retirement.
- **Hard times:** hospital visits (check first), a meal after a new baby or a death.
- **Community events:** the fete, the clean-up day, the AGM nobody wants to attend.
Attendance is often the contribution; you don't need to stay long or know what to say.
Keep a calendar of dates you'd want to mark.

## 2. Keep weak ties warm
Sociologist Mark Granovetter's "strength of weak ties" work showed acquaintances are a
major source of new information and opportunities; other research links everyday
interactions with acquaintances to wellbeing. Weak ties fade without small maintenance.
- **Greet and chat** when you cross paths: name + one remembered detail.
- **Low-effort touches:** reacting to news, a quick "saw this and thought of you",
  congratulations on a milestone.
- **Rotate coffee or walks** with people on the edge of your circle — one or two a month
  keeps a wide network alive.
- **Note-keeping:** some people keep simple notes (names, kids' names, what's going on)
  to remember details. Treat it as care, not a CRM.

## 3. Pull your weight
Communities run on unpaid work that a small core usually does (the "80/20" pattern of
volunteering is widely observed, though the exact ratio varies).
- **Take a regular, bounded job:** the rota, the newsletter, the treasurer for a year,
  bringing food once a month. Bounded commitments are more sustainable than open-ended ones.
- **Volunteer for the unglamorous tasks** sometimes: setting up, clearing up, washing up.
- **Hand over well** when you step back: notes, a successor, a transition period.
- **Say no clearly** rather than say yes and drop it. Reliability counts more than volume.

## 4. Through life changes
- **New baby, illness, caring duties:** tell people you're stepping back, and stay
  minimally in touch (a message, the occasional event). Communities forgive absence; they
  forget silence.
- **Moving away:** keep a few key ties; return for big occasions if you can.
- **Conflicts:** address them directly rather than drifting away; communities are small
  and grudges outlive the issue.

## Gotchas
- **Only showing up when you need something** — people notice.
- **Burnout in the core:** if you're doing everything, ask for help and share roles before
  you quit entirely.
- **Clique drift:** long-time members can become a closed circle; keep welcoming newcomers
  (see relationships-social.community-belonging-and-social-integration.welcoming-newcomers).
- **Performative contribution** (taking credit, gatekeeping roles) erodes goodwill.

## Verify
- [ ] In the last year you attended at least a few others' occasions, including a hard one.
- [ ] You have a calendar of dates important to people in your community.
- [ ] You had a light-touch contact with a dozen or more acquaintances this month.
- [ ] You hold one bounded, regular contribution and are reliable at it.
- [ ] When you stepped back from something, you handed it over properly.
