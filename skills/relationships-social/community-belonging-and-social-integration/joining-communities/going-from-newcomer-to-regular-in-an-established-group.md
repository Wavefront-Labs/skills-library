# Going from newcomer to regular in an established group

**Goal:** become part of an existing community — a club, team, congregation, choir,
volunteer crew, hobby group — rather than a perpetual visitor. Belonging comes mostly
from **repeated presence over time**, not from a brilliant first impression.

## 1. Choose groups that make repetition easy
- **Recurring and frequent:** weekly beats monthly. One estimate often cited (Jeffrey
  Hall, 2018) is that casual acquaintances become friends after roughly 50 hours together
  and close friends after roughly 200; treat the numbers as indicative.
- **Shared activity, not just mingling:** doing something together (singing, building,
  running, cooking) gives you something to talk about and lowers pressure.
- **The same core people** each time. Large drop-in events with a different crowd every
  week make it hard to become known.
- **Fit:** go to two or three first sessions of different groups before committing to one or two.

## 2. Show up consistently (the main skill)
- **Commit to 6–8 sessions** before judging. The first three often feel awkward;
  people start remembering you around the fourth or fifth.
- **Arrive a little early and stay a little after.** The real social life of groups
  happens before and after the official part.
- **Same time, same place.** Familiarity breeds liking (the "mere-exposure" effect).

## 3. Learn the unwritten norms
Every group has them: where newcomers sit, how people dress, whether you bring food,
how long people chat, how decisions are made, who the informal leaders are.
- **Watch first** — for the first few sessions, notice more than you contribute.
- **Ask a friendly regular:** "I'm new — anything I should know about how things work here?"
  People like being asked for advice.
- **Don't propose changes early.** "At my old club we did it like this" is a classic
  newcomer misstep. Earn standing first; suggest after months, not weeks.

## 4. Make yourself known
- **Introduce yourself with a hook:** "I'm Sam, just moved here, trying out the choir."
- **Learn names** and use them next time ("Hi Priya, how did your race go?").
  Remembering a detail from last week is the fastest way to become familiar.
- **Talk to other newcomers too** — they're often the most open.

## 5. Take a small job
Volunteer for something low-stakes and visible: setting up chairs, making tea, keeping
the sign-up sheet, bringing biscuits. Contribution moves you from consumer to member, and
gives you reasons to talk to people.

## 6. Extend beyond the group
After a few weeks, invite one or two people to something adjacent: "A few of us are
grabbing a drink after — want to come?" or "Fancy carpooling next week?"

## Gotchas
- **Expecting instant warmth.** Established groups can seem cliquey at first; it's
  usually familiarity, not rejection.
- **Overcommitting early** (joining the committee in month one) can backfire.
- **Disappearing after a bad session.** One awkward evening is normal.
- **Group culture mismatch:** after 8 sessions, if it still feels wrong, try another group.
- Introversion or social anxiety: set a tiny goal per session (talk to one person) and
  count showing up as the win.
