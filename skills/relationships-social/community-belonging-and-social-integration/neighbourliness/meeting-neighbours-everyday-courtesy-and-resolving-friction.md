# Meeting neighbours, everyday courtesy and resolving friction

**Goal:** neighbours who know your name, would take in a parcel or call if something
looked wrong — and, when friction comes up, can sort it out on the doorstep instead of
through letters, councils or courts.

## 1. Introduce yourself early
The first weeks after moving in (theirs or yours) are the easiest window; after months
of nodding, a first introduction feels odd.
- Knock or catch them outside: "Hi, I'm Alex, we've just moved into number 12. Thought
  I'd say hello." Keep it to two minutes.
- A small gesture helps but is optional: a card with your name and number, biscuits.
- **Share contact details** — phone or a street group chat — so problems can come to you
  by message rather than by complaint.
- If new neighbours arrive, go to them. It sets the tone of the street.

## 2. Everyday courtesy
- Greet by name; a wave counts.
- Keep shared areas clear: bins in on time, no parking across driveways, tidy front.
- Mind sound transmission, especially in flats: heavy footsteps, late washing machines,
  bass through walls. Check what carries.
- Offer small favours (parcels, watering, keeping an eye out) and accept theirs — small
  reciprocal favours build the relationship. See
  relationships-social.community-belonging-and-social-integration.mutual-support-networks.

## 3. The heads-up habit
Tell neighbours **before** you do something that affects them:
- "We're having a birthday party Saturday, should be done by 11. Here's my number if it
  gets too loud." (Inviting them is a nice touch.)
- Building work, tree removal, a skip on the street, a new dog.
A heads-up turns a potential complaint into a non-event; most neighbour disputes start
with a surprise.

## 4. When something bothers you
1. **Check it's worth raising** and whether it's a one-off (a single party) or a pattern.
2. **Raise it early, in person, privately and calmly.** Not a note on the windscreen,
   not a post in the community Facebook group.
3. **Assume they don't know.** People are often unaware of how their noise or parking affects others.
   "Hi — not sure if you realise, but the dog barks quite a lot during the day when
   you're out. It's getting to us a bit. Any ideas?"
4. **Be specific and offer a practical ask:** times, frequency, a concrete request.
5. **Listen** — there may be a reason, or something you're doing that bothers them.
6. **Follow up with thanks** when it improves.

## 5. When someone complains to you
Don't get defensive on the doorstep. "Thanks for telling me, I didn't realise. Let me
see what I can do." Then actually change something and let them know.

## 6. If it doesn't resolve
Escalate in steps, keeping a calm record (dates, times, what was said):
1. A second conversation or a polite written note.
2. Building management, landlord, strata/HOA, or residents' association.
3. **Community mediation** — many areas have free or low-cost services; see
   human-services.non-clinical-counselling.conflict-and-mediation-support.informal-mediation-of-relationship-and-neighbour-disputes.
4. Council or authorities (noise nuisance, safety) — rules vary by area.
5. Legal routes as a last resort (boundaries, trees). Get local advice; lawsuits rarely
   end neighbourliness well.

## Gotchas
- **Passive-aggressive notes** escalate faster than any conversation.
- **Recruiting other neighbours** against someone before talking to them.
- **Raising it when you're furious** — wait until you can be calm.
- **Cultural differences** in noise, cooking smells, children's play and privacy.
- Safety first: if a neighbour is threatening or violent, skip straight to authorities.

## Verify
- [ ] You know your immediate neighbours' names and they have your contact details.
- [ ] You gave a heads-up before your last disruptive event.
- [ ] Any friction was raised in person, early, with a specific request.
- [ ] Complaints to you were met with thanks and a concrete change.
- [ ] You know your local mediation option before you need it.
