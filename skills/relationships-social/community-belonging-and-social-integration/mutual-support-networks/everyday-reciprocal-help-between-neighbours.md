# Everyday reciprocal help between neighbours

**Goal:** a small web of people nearby who help each other with ordinary things —
borrowing a ladder, covering a school pickup, checking on an older neighbour, bringing
meals after a birth or a death. Informal, not an organisation (for organised mutual aid
see human-services.community-services-and-development.mutual-aid-coordination.request-offer-matching-and-distribution-logistics).

## 1. Start small and early
Mutual help grows from tiny exchanges: taking in a parcel, lending sugar, putting out a
neighbour's bins when they're away. Each small favour signals "you can ask me".
Offer specifically: "I'm home most days — happy to take parcels for you."

## 2. Ask, too
This is the counter-intuitive part. Many people offer freely but never ask, which keeps
relationships one-way and makes others reluctant to ask *you*. Asking for a small favour
can increase the other person's warmth towards you (often called the "Ben Franklin
effect"; evidence is modest). Borrow the drill. Ask for the plumber's number.

## 3. Simple structures that work
- **Street or building group chat** (WhatsApp, Signal, a local app): lost cats, "anyone
  got a…", parcel notices, alerts. Keep a light moderation rule: no selling, no politics.
- **Tool and item lending:** a shared list of who owns a ladder, pressure washer, cot,
  trailer. Some areas run a formal "library of things"; "Buy Nothing" groups do gifting.
- **Childcare swaps:** two or three families rotate after-school care or an evening out.
  Agree hours, ages, and rules (food, screens, pickup) up front.
- **School-run rota:** a shared calendar; confirm car-seat and insurance arrangements.
- **Meal trains:** after a birth, illness or death, one person sets up a schedule
  (online meal-train sites or a simple shared doc), noting dietary needs and drop-off times.
- **Looking out:** for older or isolated neighbours, an agreed check (curtains open,
  daily wave, a call) and a key-holder for emergencies — with their consent.

## 4. Keep it fair without keeping score
- **Rough balance over time**, not tit-for-tat. Anthropologists distinguish this
  "generalised reciprocity" (I help; it comes back somehow) from strict exchange; strict
  ledgers kill neighbourliness.
- **Return borrowed things promptly, cleaned, refuelled, with a thank-you.** Damage it?
  Replace or repair without being asked.
- **Notice imbalance:** if you're always the taker, find a way to give back
  (baking, a skill, your time). If you're always the giver and resentful, say no
  occasionally or ask for something.
- **Make it easy to say no:** "No worries at all if not."

## 5. When something goes wrong
- A broken item, a missed pickup, a child hurt on someone else's watch: acknowledge,
  apologise, make it right quickly. Unaddressed, small incidents end swaps.
- Agree in advance what happens in an emergency during a childcare swap (contacts,
  medical consent, allergies written down).

## Gotchas
- **Over-reliance on one generous neighbour.**
- **Exclusion:** group chats can become cliques; invite new arrivals and people who
  aren't online (a note through the door).
- **Privacy and safety:** don't post when people are away; be careful with keys and access.
- **Liability and insurance** for lending vehicles or expensive tools, and safeguarding
  for childcare — check your own situation; informal arrangements vary in legal standing.

## Verify
- [ ] You've both offered and asked for a favour from a neighbour this month.
- [ ] There's a channel (chat or list) your street or building uses to ask for help.
- [ ] Any childcare or school-run swap has written contacts, allergies and rules.
- [ ] Everything you've borrowed has been returned in good condition.
- [ ] You know which neighbours might need someone looking out for them, and they've agreed to it.
