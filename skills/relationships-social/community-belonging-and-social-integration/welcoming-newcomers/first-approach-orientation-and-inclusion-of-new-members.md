# First approach, orientation and inclusion of new members

**Goal:** a newcomer leaves their first session feeling noticed, knowing how things work,
and with a reason to come back. Groups lose most potential members in the first few
visits; the welcome is where retention is won or lost. This is the community-side
counterpart of
relationships-social.community-belonging-and-social-integration.joining-communities.

## 1. The first five minutes
- **Notice them.** Unknown face, hovering at the edge, looking at the noticeboard or phone.
- **Approach — don't wait for them to.** Newcomers rarely initiate; established members
  rarely notice they're standing in a closed circle.
- **Simple opener:** "Hi, I don't think we've met — I'm Jo. First time here?"
- **Ask an easy question:** how they heard about it, what brought them.
- **Don't overwhelm** with a wall of information or an immediate pitch for the committee.

## 2. Orientation: explain the unwritten rules
Every group has norms that regulars no longer see. Tell them, briefly:
- What happens when (the break, the end, the drinks after).
- Practical stuff: where to sit, what to bring, how to pay, where the toilets are.
- The quirks: "We always clap the newest person after the first run," "people bring
  a plate on the last Friday."
- Who's who: the organiser, the person to ask about X.
A one-page "new here?" sheet or a pinned post saves repeating it, but doesn't replace a person.

## 3. Connect them to two people
Introduce them to at least two members with a hook: "This is Sam — they've just moved
from Leeds and they're into hill running, like you." A newcomer who knows two names is
far more likely to return than one who only knows the welcomer.

## 4. Open the circle
- **Pac-Man rule** (credited to Eric Holscher of the Write the Docs community; popular at tech meetups):
  when standing in a group, leave a gap for one more person to join. Physically
  open circles invite people in.
- **Switch insider talk** to things the newcomer can join; explain in-jokes or skip them.
- **Include them in after-plans:** "A few of us are going for food — come along."

## 5. Follow up
- **Name and remember:** greet them by name next time. This single act signals belonging.
- **Message after the first visit** if you got their details: "Great to meet you — hope
  to see you Thursday."
- **Notice when they don't return** after a few visits; a light "missed you last week"
  can bring people back.

## 6. Give them a way in
After a couple of visits, offer a small, real role: bring the snacks, take photos,
help set up. Being needed is a strong belonging signal. Some groups use a **buddy
system** — a named regular checks in with each newcomer for their first month.

## Making it a group habit
- Appoint **rotating greeters** each session, so welcoming isn't left to chance.
- Review retention every few months: how many new people came, how many stayed?
- Ask recent newcomers what helped and what felt cold.

## Gotchas
- **Only welcoming people like you.** Notice whom you instinctively approach; make a
  point of welcoming people who differ in age, background or ability.
- **Over-welcoming:** intense attention can feel like a sales pitch.
- **Leaving it to the organiser** — the best welcomes come from ordinary members.
- **Accessibility:** check whether the venue, timing or cost excludes people.

## Verify

- [ ] You explained at least the practical routine and one unwritten norm.
- [ ] Each newcomer was introduced to at least two members.
- [ ] You greeted returning newcomers by name and followed up with absentees.
