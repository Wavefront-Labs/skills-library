# Correspondence etiquette — notes, invitations, replies and digital manners

**Goal:** handle the written side of social life — thanking, inviting, replying,
consoling, messaging — so that people feel acknowledged and nobody is left guessing.
For long-form formal or condolence *letters*, see
languages-linguistics.everyday-composition.letter-writing.writing-formal-complaint-request-and-condolence-letters.

## 1. Thank-you notes: specific, prompt, short
Structure (3–5 sentences is plenty):
1. **Greeting** by name.
2. **Name the thing** — the gift, the dinner, the favour. "Thank you for the
   cookbook" beats "thank you for the gift".
3. **Say how you'll use it or what it meant.** "We made the lentil soup on Sunday."
   For money, mention what it'll go towards rather than the amount.
4. **Look forward or back to the relationship.** "It was lovely to see you at the party."
5. **Sign off** warmly.

Timing: aim for within a week or two; a late note is still far better than none.
Wedding thank-yous are commonly expected within about three months (conventions
vary by etiquette authority — unconfirmed as a hard rule). Handwritten is the gold
standard for gifts and hospitality; a thoughtful email or message is acceptable for
small or casual kindnesses and with people who live digitally.

## 2. Invitations: answer every question before it's asked
Include **who** is invited (be explicit: "you and Sam", "kids welcome", "adults only"),
**what** the event is, **when** (date, start time, end time if it matters), **where**
(full address, parking/transport), **dress** level, **food** (a full meal vs. nibbles),
and **RSVP by** a date with how to reply. Send roughly 2–4 weeks ahead for casual
gatherings, 6–8 weeks for formal events, earlier (save-the-date) for weddings and
travel.

## 3. Replying (RSVP) — the guest's half
- Reply by the stated date, yes or no. Silence is not a "no"; it leaves the host
  guessing at numbers.
- Don't add people ("can I bring…?") unless the invitation clearly allows it; ask
  privately if you must, and accept a no gracefully.
- Declining: "Thank you so much for the invitation — sadly we can't make it, but we
  hope it's a wonderful evening." No elaborate excuse needed.
- If plans change after an RSVP, tell the host as soon as you know.

## 4. Condolence notes
Keep them short and personal: acknowledge the loss by the person's name, share one
memory or quality if you knew them, offer something concrete if you mean it
("I'll bring dinner on Thursday"). Avoid explaining the loss ("at least…",
"everything happens for a reason"). Send within a few weeks; a note months later
is still welcome.

## 5. Digital message manners
- **Response time:** for a direct personal message, within a day or so is courteous;
  if you can't answer properly, a quick "seen this, will reply properly tonight" stops
  the other person wondering.
- **Group chats:** keep side conversations to direct messages; don't use a group to
  pressure one person; mute rather than leave noisily.
- **Voice notes:** fine with people who like them; keep them short and don't send
  them to people who've said they prefer text.
- **Bad news and serious topics** — break-ups, bereavements, apologies for something
  big — merit a call or meeting, not a text, where possible.
- **Tone:** text strips tone. Reread before sending anything that could be read as
  curt. Full stops after one-word replies can read as cold to some people
  (generational and regional; not universal).
- **Forwarding and screenshots:** don't share someone's private message without asking.

## Gotchas
- Generic thank-yous ("thanks for everything!") feel like a form letter.
- Invitations that leave plus-ones or children ambiguous cause the most awkwardness.
- A reply-all to a large group for a personal answer.
- Sending a thank-you only via social-media comment for a significant gift.

## Verify
- [ ] Every thank-you names the specific gift or act and says what it meant.
- [ ] Your invitation answers who, what, when, where, dress, food, RSVP-by.
- [ ] You've replied to all pending invitations by their dates.
- [ ] Serious news went by voice or in person, not a text.
