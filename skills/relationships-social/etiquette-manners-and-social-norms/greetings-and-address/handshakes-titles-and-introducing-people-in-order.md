# Greetings and address — handshakes, titles and introductions

**Goal:** start every encounter cleanly: the right physical greeting, the right name
or title, and introductions that leave both people with something to talk about.
For remembering the name afterwards, see
mind-self.mnemonics-and-memory-technique.names-and-faces.remembering-names-at-introductions.

## 1. The physical greeting
- **Default in most Anglo/European professional settings: the handshake.** Stand up,
  make eye contact, web of your hand meets theirs, firm but not crushing, two or three
  pumps, then release. Say your name as you shake.
- **Read the other person's lead.** Watch their hands and body as you approach. If they
  bow, nod, place a hand on the heart, or keep hands at their sides, mirror that rather
  than pushing a handshake. Some people do not shake hands with the opposite sex for
  religious reasons; a hand on your own chest with a nod is a gracious alternative.
- **Hugs and cheek kisses** are for people who already greet you that way, or where the
  local norm is clear. Cheek-kiss counts and sides vary by country and even region
  (one, two, three; starting left or right) — follow the local person's movement.
- **Health or preference:** a nod, wave or brief elbow/fist bump is fine; say warmly
  "I'll skip the handshake, but it's great to meet you."

## 2. Names and titles
- **Use the title and surname until invited otherwise** with elders, clients,
  officials and anyone senior in a formal setting: "Dr Okafor", "Ms Lee". In many
  workplaces and in Australia/US casual settings, first names are the norm from the
  start — copy how they introduce themselves.
- **Ms** is the safe default for a woman when marital status or preference is unknown.
  **Mx** is used by some non-binary people; use whatever a person has told you.
- **Doctor/Professor/military ranks/clergy:** use the title in formal contexts even if
  socially they may wave it off.
- **Pronunciation:** if you're unsure, ask — "Can you say your name for me so I get it
  right?" — then repeat it back. Getting it right later matters more than the first try.
- **Honorific systems** (e.g. Japanese -san, Korean titles by role, Spanish
  usted/tú, French vous/tu, German Sie/du) carry real weight; default to the formal
  form until the other person switches.

## 3. Introducing two people — order of precedence
The traditional rule: **say the name of the more senior/honoured person first**, and
present the other person *to* them.
- Younger to older: "Grandma, this is my friend Jo. Jo, this is my grandmother, Ruth."
- Junior to senior: "Ms Patel, I'd like to introduce our new analyst, Tom Reyes."
- Peer to client/guest in business: the client is the honoured person.
- A single person to a group: name the person, then go round the group.

In practice, getting both names out clearly matters more than strict order.

**Add a hook** so they can start talking: "Tom's just moved from Leeds — Ms Patel,
you lived there for years, didn't you?"

## 4. Introducing yourself
First and last name, plus one line of context: "Hi, I'm Alex Moreno — I'm Priya's
neighbour." Then a question back. At events where you've been forgotten, introduce
yourself before the other person struggles: "Alex — we met at Priya's barbecue."

## Gotchas
- Staying seated while greeting someone who is standing (stand if you can).
- The "dead fish" or the "bone crusher" handshake; match the other person's grip.
- Moving to a first name or nickname ("Liz" for Elizabeth) without being invited.
- Forgetting to introduce a companion: if you're with someone and meet a third
  person, introduce them within the first few seconds.
- Assuming pronouns or titles from appearance.

## Verify
- [ ] You watched for the other person's greeting cue before extending a hand or a hug.
- [ ] You used a title and surname until invited to use a first name.
- [ ] You asked for and repeated any name you weren't sure how to pronounce.
- [ ] Your introductions named the honoured person first and included a talking hook.
- [ ] Nobody you were with was left unintroduced.
