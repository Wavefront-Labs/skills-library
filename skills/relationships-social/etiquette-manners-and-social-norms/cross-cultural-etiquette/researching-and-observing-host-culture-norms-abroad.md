# Cross-cultural etiquette — learning a host culture's rules fast

**Goal:** arrive in an unfamiliar country, community or business culture and behave
in a way locals read as respectful — even before you know the details. Specific rules
below are widely reported examples, not a complete guide; norms vary by region,
generation and setting, so always confirm with a local source.

## 1. Before you go: the ten-question research list
Look up (a recent country guide, an embassy/business-council briefing, and ideally a
person from the culture) the answers to:
1. **Greeting:** handshake, bow, cheek kiss, or none? Between which genders?
2. **Forms of address:** titles, surnames, family-name-first order?
3. **Punctuality:** is "on time" literal, or is 15–30 minutes late normal socially?
4. **Shoes:** removed in homes, temples, some offices?
5. **Hands and gestures:** is the left hand considered unclean for eating or passing
   things (parts of South Asia, the Middle East, Africa)? Are thumbs-up, OK sign,
   pointing or showing the soles of the feet offensive?
6. **Dress:** covered shoulders/knees for religious sites; head covering?
7. **Food and drink:** alcohol norms, refusing food, finishing your plate (praise in
   some places, a signal you're still hungry in others).
8. **Gifts:** expected? Opened in front of the giver or later? Taboo items or numbers
   (e.g. clocks and the number four in some Chinese contexts; chrysanthemums tied to
   funerals in parts of Europe and Asia; even-numbered bouquets in Russia).
9. **Tipping:** expected, included, or insulting?
10. **Topics to avoid:** politics, religion, the royal family, money, a historical conflict.

## 2. On arrival: watch, wait, copy
- **Be the second to act.** Let your host greet first, sit first, start eating first.
- **Copy the most senior local** in the room, not the most relaxed one.
- **Turn the volume down** — voice, gestures, displays of familiarity — until you've
  calibrated. Quieter is rarely the wrong direction for a guest.
- **Ask, don't guess:** "Should I take my shoes off?" is always welcome.

## 3. Business-meeting conventions worth knowing
- **Business cards:** in Japan and much of East Asia, present and receive with both
  hands, text facing the recipient; read it, and lay it on the table during the
  meeting rather than pocketing it immediately. Never write on someone's card in
  front of them.
- **Hierarchy:** in high-power-distance cultures the senior person speaks first and
  decisions may not be made in the room.
- **Directness:** in high-context cultures, "that would be difficult" may mean no.
  Don't press for an explicit refusal; it can cost the other person face.
- **Relationship before business:** in many cultures several social meetings or meals
  precede any deal talk. Rushing to the agenda reads as rude.

## 4. When you slip
Apologise briefly and sincerely, ask how it should be done, and do it right next
time: "I'm sorry — I didn't know. How should I have done that?" Most hosts are
generous to a visitor who is visibly trying. Don't over-apologise or make a joke at
the culture's expense.

## Gotchas
- Treating one source as definitive — guidebooks date fast and flatten regional
  differences.
- Over-performing a custom you half-understand (an exaggerated bow) can read as mockery.
- Assuming a diaspora or expat colleague follows home-country norms.
- Photographing people, religious ceremonies or sites without asking.
- Your home "neutral" (first names, direct "no", firm handshake) is itself a cultural norm.

## Verify
- [ ] You have answers to the ten research questions from at least two sources.
- [ ] You know the greeting, shoe, hand and dress rules before your first visit to a home or temple.
- [ ] In the first meeting you let the senior local lead and copied them.
- [ ] You have a ready, simple apology-and-ask line for slips.
