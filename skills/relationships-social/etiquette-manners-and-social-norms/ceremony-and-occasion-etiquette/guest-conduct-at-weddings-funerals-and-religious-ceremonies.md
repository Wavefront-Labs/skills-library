# Ceremony and occasion etiquette — being a guest at the big moments

**Goal:** attend a wedding, funeral or religious ceremony — including one from a
tradition you don't know — and behave so the people at the centre of it never have
to think about you. The principle: **the occasion belongs to them; your job is to
show up, follow the lead, and not add burden.** For delivering a toast or eulogy, see
media-communication.public-speaking-and-rhetoric.occasional-speaking.toasts-acceptances-and-mc-work.

## 1. Weddings
- **RSVP by the date, exactly as invited.** Don't add a plus-one or children the
  invitation doesn't name (see correspondence-etiquette).
- **Arrive 15–30 minutes before the ceremony start.** Entering after the processional
  starts is the one lateness people notice. If late, wait at the back until a pause.
- **Phones:** silent and away during the ceremony, especially if it's announced as
  "unplugged". Never step into the aisle or in front of the photographer.
- **Dress:** follow the stated code; traditionally avoid white or ivory (see dress-codes).
- **Gifts:** use the registry or the couple's stated preference (cash, honeymoon fund);
  many cultures give cash in an envelope as standard. A common rule of thumb is that
  a gift can be sent up to some months after the wedding — conventions vary.
- **At the reception:** sit where the seating plan says; congratulate the couple
  briefly (they have many people to greet); don't give an unscheduled speech or
  monopolise the microphone.

## 2. Funerals and memorials
- **Arrive early, sit behind the family's reserved rows.** Dark, subdued clothing
  unless the family asks otherwise.
- **What to say to the bereaved**: short and sincere. "I'm so sorry about Dad. He
  was always kind to me." Then let them move on. Avoid "I know how you feel", "at least
  she's not suffering", "everything happens for a reason", and telling your own loss story.
- **Receiving lines and viewings:** a sentence each; don't hold up the line.
- **Flowers and donations:** check the notice — many families request "donations in
  lieu of flowers" to a named charity. Some traditions don't use flowers (traditional
  Jewish funerals generally don't, for example).
- **After:** a wake, reception or shiva visit follows customs of the family's
  tradition; bring food if that's the custom, keep the visit short, follow the family's cues.
- **Follow-up:** a note or message weeks later, when the crowds have gone, is often
  valued most (see grief-and-hardship-peer-support).

## 3. Religious services you don't know
- **Ask your host** what to wear, whether to bring anything, and what non-members do
  during rituals (e.g. communion, prayer postures).
- **Covering:** many places of worship expect covered shoulders and knees; some
  require head coverings (often provided at the door) or shoe removal (mosques,
  gurdwaras, many Hindu and Buddhist temples).
- **Participation:** stand and sit with the congregation; you may stay silent during
  prayers and abstain from rituals that imply belief — quietly remaining seated or
  standing respectfully is accepted almost everywhere.
- **Photography:** ask first; often prohibited during the service.

## 4. Toasts and formalities
- Wait for the host or MC to begin toasts; don't drink when you are the one being toasted.
- If asked to speak: under three minutes, about the honoured person, no inside jokes
  or embarrassing stories, no ex-partners.

## Gotchas
- Posting photos of the couple before they've shared any themselves.
- Treating a funeral reception as purely sombre, or purely a party — follow the family's tone.
- Bringing a large boxed gift to the venue when the couple are travelling; send it instead.
- Assuming your tradition's mourning customs apply (e.g. open casket viewing, timing of burial).

## Verify
- [ ] RSVP returned on time and exactly as invited.
- [ ] Seated before the ceremony began, phone silent and away.
- [ ] Words to the bereaved or the couple were short, about them, and free of clichés.
- [ ] For an unfamiliar service, you asked a host about dress, coverings and participation beforehand.
