# Dining etiquette — reading the table and getting through a formal meal

**Goal:** sit down at an unfamiliar place setting and eat a multi-course meal without
anxiety, without making others uncomfortable, and with a handful of rules that cover
almost every situation. This is written from Anglo-European/North American formal
conventions; other dining cultures differ (see cross-cultural-etiquette in this field).

## 1. Decode the place setting before you touch anything
- **Cutlery works outside-in.** The pieces furthest from the plate are for the first
  course; move inward with each course. Forks left, knives and soup spoon right.
- **Dessert cutlery** often sits horizontally above the plate (spoon handle pointing
  right, fork handle pointing left), or arrives with dessert.
- **"BMW" — Bread left, Meal centre, Water/Wine right.** Your bread plate is the one on
  your left; your glasses are on your right. Taking a neighbour's roll is the single
  most common slip at round tables. A quick check: make "b" and "d" with your thumbs and
  index fingers — left hand b (bread), right hand d (drink).
- **Glasses** are usually arranged in the order they'll be used, again roughly
  outside-in: water largest, then red, white, sometimes a flute.
- If unsure, wait a beat and watch the host. Copying the host is always correct.

## 2. The sequence of a meal
1. **Napkin:** unfold it onto your lap once seated (or once the host does). If you leave
   mid-meal, put it on your chair; at the end, loosely to the left of the plate — not
   crumpled on the plate, not refolded.
2. **Wait to start** until everyone at a small table is served, or the host begins or
   says "please start". At a large banquet, starting once your nearby group is served is
   accepted (convention varies; when in doubt, wait).
3. **Bread:** tear a bite-sized piece, butter that piece, eat it. Don't butter the whole
   roll or cut it with a knife.
4. **Soup:** spoon away from you, sip from the side of the spoon; tilt the bowl away to
   get the last of it.
5. **Pass dishes to the right** (counter-clockwise) when serving family-style; salt and
   pepper travel together even if only one was asked for.
6. **Pace with the table.** Don't finish far ahead or far behind; slow down or put
   cutlery down between bites to match.

## 3. Cutlery styles and signals
- **Continental (European):** fork stays in the left hand, tines down; knife in the right
  throughout. **American ("zig-zag"):** cut, then put the knife down and switch the fork
  to the right hand to eat. Both are correct; pick one and be consistent.
- **Resting** (pausing): knife and fork apart on the plate, roughly an inverted V, or
  crossed (continental).
- **Finished:** knife and fork together, parallel, across the plate (the "4 o'clock" or
  "6 o'clock" position, depending on country), knife blade facing in. Staff use this to
  clear. Never put used cutlery back on the tablecloth.

## 4. Conversation and conduct at the table
- Phones off the table. Elbows off while eating (fine between courses in many places).
- Talk to the people on **both sides** of you; at formal dinners the host may "turn the
  table" — switch conversation partner between courses.
- Small bites so you can answer a question within seconds.
- Food you can't or won't eat: decline quietly or leave it; never announce it.
  Allergies and dietary needs go to the host **ahead of time**, not at the table.
- Something you can't swallow (bone, gristle): remove it the way it went in — fork or
  fingers — onto the side of your plate, discreetly.

## 5. Host duties in brief
Tell guests the dress level and start time, seat people to mix them (not couples
together, at formal dinners), start eating to signal others, watch for empty glasses
and plates, and never rush clearing while a guest is still eating.

## Gotchas
- Using the neighbour's bread plate or water glass — check BMW before you sit.
- Waving cutlery while talking; put it down to gesture.
- Rules vary: in parts of Europe hands should stay visible on the table; in the UK and
  US, hands in the lap between bites is normal. Defer to what locals do.
- A slip is never as bad as drawing attention to it. Recover silently and carry on;
  if someone takes your bread plate, just use the edge of your main plate.

## Verify
- [ ] Before sitting, you can identify your bread plate, glasses and first-course cutlery.
- [ ] Napkin is on your lap, on your chair if you step away, left of the plate at the end.
- [ ] You waited for the host's cue to start and kept pace with the table.
- [ ] Your finished signal was clear (cutlery together across the plate).
- [ ] Dietary needs were communicated before the event, not at the table.
