# Everyday public manners — courtesy in shared space

**Goal:** move through queues, trains, shops, waiting rooms and footpaths without
imposing on strangers, and handle other people's breaches without escalating. The
test for any public behaviour: **would it be fine if everyone here did it at once?**

## 1. Queues
- **Join at the back** and keep the line obvious: stand close enough that the gap
  doesn't look like the end, not so close you're touching.
- **Holding a place** for one person who steps away briefly is generally tolerated;
  "saving" for a group who then arrive is not.
- **Multiple tills:** a single serpentine queue is fairest; where there are separate
  lines, don't hop to a faster one past people waiting longer.
- **Be ready when you reach the front** — card out, order decided.
- **If someone cuts in:** assume an honest mistake first. "Sorry, I think the queue
  starts back here" said calmly works most of the time. Queue norms vary by country;
  in some places a looser crowd is normal and insistence reads as odd.

## 2. Phones and audio
- **No speakerphone, video calls or audio without headphones** in shared indoor space
  or transport. Check your headphone leakage at full volume.
- **Keep calls short and quiet** on transport, in shops and waiting rooms; step outside
  for long or personal calls.
- **Don't text while at the front of a queue or while someone is serving you.**
  Pause the conversation and give them your attention.
- **Silent in** cinemas, theatres, places of worship, libraries, medical waiting rooms,
  quiet carriages. A lit screen in a dark theatre is disruptive too.
- **Walking while looking at a phone:** step to the side to read it.

## 3. Transport and moving through space
- **Let people off before you get on.**
- **Offer priority seats** to people who are elderly, pregnant, disabled, injured or
  carrying a small child — and remember many disabilities are invisible. A simple
  "would you like a seat?" lets them decline.
- **Bags off seats** when it's busy; backpacks off your back in a crowded carriage.
- **Escalators:** in many cities, stand on one side and walk on the other (the side
  varies — London, Osaka and much of the US stand right; Tokyo and much of
  Australia stand left — watch the locals). Some transit authorities now ask everyone to stand.
- **Footpaths:** keep to one side as the local traffic does; don't stop dead at the
  top of stairs or in doorways.
- **Doors:** hold for the person close behind you; don't make someone far away jog.

## 4. Noise, space and shared facilities
- Keep voices and music to your own space; earlier and later in the day, quieter still.
- Respect personal space: in most Western settings, roughly an arm's length with
  strangers where possible.
- **Gyms and shared kitchens:** wipe equipment, re-rack weights, clean up after yourself.
- **Say please, thank you and sorry** to staff — every time.

## 5. When someone else is being inconsiderate
- Decide if it's worth it: a brief annoyance usually isn't.
- If it is, be specific, polite and in private-ish tone: "Excuse me, would you mind
  using headphones? It's quite loud." No audience-seeking, no sarcasm.
- If they refuse or escalate, move away or tell staff. Your safety outranks being right.

## Gotchas
- "Saving" seats or places for latecomers in a busy venue.
- Assuming someone doesn't need a priority seat because they look fine.
- Blocking doors and aisles while you check directions.
- Filming strangers' small breaches for social media.

## Verify
- [ ] In queues you joined at the back, kept the line clear and were ready at the front.
- [ ] No audio played aloud in shared space; calls kept brief and quiet.
- [ ] You let people off first and offered priority seats.
- [ ] Any breach you raised was addressed calmly, specifically, once.
