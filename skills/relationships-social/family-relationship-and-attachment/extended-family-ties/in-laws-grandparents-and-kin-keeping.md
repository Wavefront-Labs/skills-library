# In-laws, grandparents and kin-keeping

**Goal:** a wider family that stays in touch and gets on reasonably well, without one
person quietly doing all the work or one couple's marriage taking the strain.

## 1. In-laws: the "own side" rule
The single most useful practice: **each partner handles communication and conflict with
their own family.** You raise issues with your parents; your partner raises issues with
theirs. A request from a son- or daughter-in-law is easily heard as an outsider attacking;
the same request from their own child is heard as family business.
- Agree as a couple first, then the blood relative delivers it: "We've decided we'll do
  Christmas at ours this year."
- Present decisions as "we", never "she wants" or "he won't let us".
- Your partner comes first. Taking your parent's side against your partner in front of
  them is the classic in-law injury.

### Building a relationship with your in-laws
- Learn what they care about; ask about their history and photos.
- Small, regular gestures: remembering birthdays, sending a photo, asking for a recipe.
- Accept that you're joining an existing culture with its own habits; you don't need to
  adopt it, but noticing it avoids accidental offence.
- Lower the bar: cordial and respectful is a success. Not every in-law becomes a friend.

## 2. Grandparents and grandchildren
Grandparent involvement is associated in research with benefits for children, though
studies vary on how large and in which contexts.
- **Agree the ground rules** (food, screens, discipline) with grandparents gently, and be
  flexible on small things — "Grandma's house rules" are a normal part of childhood.
- **Long distance:** scheduled video calls (short, active: reading a story, showing a
  drawing), postcards, a shared photo album app, recorded bedtime stories.
- **Let the bond be theirs.** Step back during visits rather than mediating every interaction.
- If grandparents undermine your parenting repeatedly, use the own-side rule.

## 3. Kin-keeping: the invisible job
Sociologists use "kin-keeping" for the work of maintaining family ties: tracking
birthdays, organising gatherings, passing on news, sending cards. It is often done
mostly by one person (frequently a woman in heterosexual couples, per the research)
and rarely acknowledged.
- **Make it visible:** a shared family calendar with birthdays and anniversaries, a
  household list of who sends what.
- **Split it:** each partner covers their own side's cards and calls, or divide by task.
- **Light-touch tools:** a family group chat, an annual reunion with a rotating host,
  a shared photo stream.
- **Cousins:** shared holidays or a yearly cousins' day build ties that outlast the
  grandparents.

## 4. Holidays and gatherings
- **Rotation plans** (alternate years, or Christmas Eve/Day split) agreed in advance.
- Decide your own family's traditions too; you're allowed to host.
- Have an exit time and an agreed signal with your partner.
- Keep known flashpoints (politics, old feuds) off the table with a pre-agreed redirect.

## Gotchas
- **Venting about in-laws to your partner** forces them to defend or betray their family;
  ask for help with a specific situation instead.
- **Comparing sides** ("your parents see the kids more").
- **Gifts and money** from grandparents can come with expectations; clarify early.
- Estrangements in the wider family: don't force relatives into choosing sides.

## Verify
- [ ] Each partner raises issues with their own family; decisions are presented as "we".
- [ ] A shared calendar holds the family's birthdays and key dates.
- [ ] Kin-keeping tasks are visibly shared, not done by default by one person.
- [ ] Grandchildren have a regular contact rhythm with each grandparent who wants one.
- [ ] Holiday arrangements for next year are agreed before this year's are over.
