# Adult sibling closeness, rivalry repair and shared duties

**Goal:** a sibling relationship that works between the adults you are now — close
enough to rely on, honest enough to survive the hard family moments (illness, ageing
parents, inheritance) that tend to arrive in midlife.

## 1. Update the roles
Families freeze siblings in childhood parts: the responsible one, the baby, the clever
one, the troublemaker. Adult friction often comes from being treated as that role.
- Notice when you're doing it: "Of course *you'd* forget."
- Ask about their current life as you would a friend's — job, partner, what they're
  into now. You may not actually know your sibling as an adult.
- Spend time one-to-one, away from parents. Family gatherings pull everyone back into
  old roles.

## 2. Keep contact low-effort and regular
A sibling group chat, a monthly call on a fixed day, an annual trip without partners or
kids. Closeness tends to follow frequency more than intensity.

## 3. Repairing old rivalry
Common roots: perceived favouritism, uneven help, a past betrayal, competition over
achievement.
1. **Pick one issue**, not the whole childhood.
2. **Private, calm setting**, not at a family event or by text.
3. **Speak from your experience:** "Growing up, I felt like Mum always took your side, and
   I think I still get prickly about it." Not "You were the favourite."
4. **Listen to their version.** Siblings often experienced the *same* family very
   differently — research on "non-shared environment" supports that children in one home
   don't have identical upbringings.
5. **Separate the sibling from the parent.** Much sibling resentment is really about a
   parent's choices; the sibling didn't choose to be favoured.
6. **Agree one small change** going forward. Repair is a process over months, not one talk.

## 4. Shared duties: ageing parents and family logistics
This is where adult sibling relationships most often break.
- **Have the talk early**, before the crisis: who lives near, who has time, who has money,
  who has skills (medical, legal, admin).
- **Split by strengths, not equally by hours.** The sibling 300 km away can manage bills,
  insurance and calls with the GP; the local one does visits. Count both as real contributions.
- **Write it down** in a shared doc or chat pin: who's doing what, how decisions are
  made, how costs are split.
- **Regular sibling check-in** (monthly call) to rebalance before resentment sets in.
- **Thank the hands-on carer often** — the local sibling usually carries the heaviest
  invisible load.
- Wills and inheritance: encourage parents to be clear and to tell all children the same
  thing; vagueness is a common source of sibling disputes. Get legal advice for anything
  formal.

## Gotchas
- **Triangulating:** venting about one sibling to another, or using a parent as messenger.
  Talk to the person directly.
- **Partners** may amplify grievances; keep sibling issues between siblings where possible.
- **Unequal closeness** is normal; you may be closer to one sibling than another.
- **Estrangement** from a sibling may sometimes be the right call (abuse, ongoing harm);
  see relationships-social.family-relationship-and-attachment.attachment-repair for
  cautious re-contact.
- If a dispute involves money or property and talks fail, a family mediator is a better
  next step than escalating — see
  human-services.non-clinical-counselling.conflict-and-mediation-support.informal-mediation-of-relationship-and-neighbour-disputes.

## Verify
- [ ] You've had a one-to-one conversation with each sibling in the last few months.
- [ ] You can describe your sibling's current life without referring to childhood.
- [ ] Any repair conversation used "I felt" language and covered one issue.
- [ ] Parent-care duties are written down and reviewed regularly.
- [ ] No sibling is hearing your grievances second-hand.
