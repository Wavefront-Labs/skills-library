# Getting a group to a decision it will keep

**Goal:** a peer group — choosing a holiday, a club changing its meeting night, a residents'
group spending a small fund — reaches a decision that people actually follow, rather than
endless chat threads or a "decision" half the group ignores.

## 1. Decide how you'll decide — first
Most group-decision pain is ambiguity about the rule. Say it up front:
- **Organiser decides after input** — fast; fine for low-stakes logistics ("I'll pick the
  restaurant from your suggestions by Thursday").
- **Majority vote** — quick, clear; leaves a losing minority. Fine for reversible choices.
- **Consensus** (everyone can live with it) — strong buy-in; slow; one person can block.
- **Consent** (sociocracy-style: no one has a *reasoned, paramount* objection) — faster than
  consensus; "good enough for now, safe enough to try".
- **Fallback rule:** "If we haven't agreed by Sunday, we'll do a majority vote" — prevents stalling.

Match effort to stakes: don't run consensus on pizza toppings.

## 2. Surface options before choosing
- Collect options **separately first** (each person writes theirs, or a shared doc) so the loudest
  or first idea doesn't anchor everyone.
- State the **criteria** the option must meet: budget, dates, accessibility, travel time.
- Expect the **"groan zone"** (Kaner): the confused, frustrating middle where views diverge. It's
  normal, not a sign the process has failed. Summarise where you are and keep going.

## 3. Narrow down
- **Dot voting / multi-voting:** each person gets ~3 votes to spread across options; drop the
  bottom ones.
- **Polls** in group chats (WhatsApp, Messenger, Discord) or date-finders (Doodle, When2meet) for
  scheduling — set a closing time.
- **Combine** options where possible ("cabin in the hills + one day in town").

## 4. Test agreement
- **Fist-to-five:** on a count, everyone shows 0–5 fingers. 0 = block, 1–2 = serious concerns,
  3 = can live with it, 4–5 = support. Ask anyone at 0–2: "What would need to change for you to get to 3?"
- **Thumbs up / sideways / down** — a quicker version for chat (emoji reactions).
- Silence ≠ agreement. Ask quiet members directly, kindly.

## 5. Break a deadlock
- **Go back to interests:** why does each side want their option? Often a third option meets both.
- **Shrink the decision:** trial it for one month, then review.
- **Split it:** decide the date now, the venue later.
- **Use the fallback rule** you agreed in step 1 — this is what it's for.
- **Delegate:** "Can Ali and Jo work this out and we'll go with their pick?"
- **Random choice** for genuinely equal options — a coin flip is fair and ends it.

## 6. Close it
- Say it plainly: "Decision: Saturday hike, meet 9:00 at the station, Sam books the train."
- Write it where people will see it (pinned message, shared doc).
- Name **who does what by when** and when it will be reviewed.
- Thank dissenters for raising concerns, and note any concern that triggers a review.

## Gotchas
- **False consensus:** "Everyone OK? Great." with no real check. Use fist-to-five.
- **Reopening:** people who missed the discussion relitigating. Point to the rule and the review date.
- **Group chat sprawl:** decisions die in 200-message threads. Move to a poll with a deadline.
- **Power behind the scenes:** a friendship clique pre-deciding outside. See
  `relationships-social.group-leadership-and-teamwork.managing-group-dynamics.handling-dominators-cliques-and-free-riders`.
- *Uncertainty:* fist-to-five thresholds vary between groups (some treat 2 as "proceed with
  concerns"); agree your own meaning before using it.

## Verify
- [ ] The decision rule and fallback were stated before options were discussed.
- [ ] Options were collected independently, with criteria.
- [ ] Agreement was explicitly tested, and quiet members were asked.
- [ ] The final decision is written down with owners and a review date.
- [ ] Two weeks later, people are following it.
