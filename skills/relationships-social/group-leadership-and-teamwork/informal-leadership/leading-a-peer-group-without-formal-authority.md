# Leading a peer group without formal authority

**Goal:** get a group of equals — a running club, a community garden, a band, a group trip, a
parents' committee — moving in a shared direction when you can't order anyone to do anything.
Your only tools are credibility, clarity and goodwill.

## 1. Earn the right first
In peer groups, influence is given by the group, not taken. Build it before you need it:
- **Be reliable in small things.** Do what you said, on time. One dropped ball costs more than
  five kept promises earn.
- **Contribute before you ask.** Do the unglamorous jobs — booking, tidying up, the spreadsheet.
- **Know people's "currencies"** (Cohen & Bradford): what each person values — recognition,
  fun, learning, a sense of purpose, social time. People follow when the group gives them what
  they came for.
- **Listen more than you propose,** especially early on. Find out what people want from the group.

## 2. Set direction as a question, then a proposal
- Surface what people care about: "What would make this season great for you?"
- Reflect back a direction that combines it: "Sounds like most of us want to do one bigger
  race and keep Sunday runs social. How about we aim for the half-marathon in May?"
- Make it **concrete and time-bound** — a date, a target, a next step. Vague direction ("let's
  get more organised") goes nowhere.
- Write it somewhere visible (the group chat description, a pinned message).

## 3. Win buy-in
- **Talk to key people one-to-one before the group discussion.** Not to rig it — to hear
  objections early and shape the proposal. Surprises in a group setting create resistance.
- **Find your first followers.** Derek Sivers' point: a leader becomes a movement only when the
  first person joins and others see it's safe. Thank and highlight early adopters publicly.
- **Invite modification:** "What would make this work for you?" People support what they help shape.
- **Make the first step tiny:** "Who's in for a 5k this Saturday?" beats "commit to a 16-week plan".

## 4. Lead from beside, not above
- **Credit the group, own the mistakes.** "Great turnout, everyone" / "I got the time wrong, sorry."
- **Ask, don't order** (see
  `relationships-social.group-leadership-and-teamwork.delegating-among-peers.asking-peers-to-take-on-tasks-and-following-up`).
- **Model the norms** you want — punctuality, inclusion, how you talk about absent members.
- **Decide how decisions are made** rather than deciding everything yourself (see
  `relationships-social.group-leadership-and-teamwork.group-decision-making.getting-a-group-to-a-decision-it-will-keep`).

## 5. Share and hand over the role
- **Rotate tasks** like hosting, organising or chairing; it spreads ownership and prevents burnout.
- **Develop a deputy** so the group survives you being away.
- **Step back deliberately** when someone else has more energy or expertise for the next phase. An
  informal leader who clings on often becomes the thing the group routes around.

## Gotchas
- **The "self-appointed" trap:** announcing yourself as leader in a group of equals usually
  backfires. Let the role emerge from what you do.
- **Doing everything yourself** feels efficient but kills ownership and burns you out.
- **Friendship and leadership tension:** you may need to raise problems with friends. Do it
  privately and about behaviour (see
  `relationships-social.group-leadership-and-teamwork.managing-group-dynamics.handling-dominators-cliques-and-free-riders`).
- **Rivals:** if another person also leads informally, ally with them — split areas — rather than compete.
- **Volunteer groups with legal duties** (money, safeguarding, insurance) need formal roles,
  not just informal leadership; this page doesn't cover governance.

*Uncertainty:* the influence frameworks cited were developed for workplaces; their use in
purely social groups is a reasonable adaptation, not separately tested.

## Verify
- [ ] People can say, in one sentence, where the group is heading and by when.
- [ ] You spoke one-to-one with key members before proposing anything big.
- [ ] At least one other person is visibly co-owning the effort.
- [ ] Credit goes to the group; your mistakes are owned openly.
- [ ] Someone could step in if you were away for a month.
