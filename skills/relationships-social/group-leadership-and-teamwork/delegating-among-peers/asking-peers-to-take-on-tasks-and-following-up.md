# Asking peers to take on tasks — and following up

**Goal:** share the work of a group of equals — organising a party, running a club event, a
volunteer project, a group trip — so that tasks get done by someone other than you, without
sounding like a boss or becoming the group nag.

For delegation where you *are* the manager, see
`business-finance.general-management.executive-leadership.delegation-and-management-cadence`.

## 1. Break the work into ownable chunks
- List everything that needs to happen; group it into tasks **one person can own end to end**
  ("book the venue", not "help with venue stuff").
- For each: **what done looks like**, **deadline**, rough **time needed**, any **budget** or
  constraints.
- Size tasks for volunteers: 1–3 hours is easy to say yes to; "run the whole raffle" is not.

## 2. Match tasks to people
- Look for: skills ("you're good with spreadsheets"), interests (people volunteer more readily
  for things they enjoy), availability, and growth ("you wanted to get into event planning").
- Spread the load. The reliable two people always getting asked is how groups burn out.
- Pair a newcomer with an experienced member on a first task.

## 3. Ask, don't order
- **Ask individually**, not "can someone…" in the group chat — diffusion of responsibility means
  open calls often get no takers. One-to-one asks work far better.
- Shape: **why them + the task + what done looks like + when + an easy out.**
  "You've got a great eye — would you be up for doing the posters? Need them by the 10th, A4 and a
  social-media version. Totally fine to say no."
- **Accept a no gracefully** and ask what they *would* like to do.
- **Get a clear yes.** "Maybe" isn't ownership. "Shall I put you down for it?"
- **Confirm in writing** where everyone can see: a shared list (Google Sheets, Trello, a pinned
  message). Public commitment makes follow-through more likely (Cialdini's commitment and
  consistency principle).

## 4. Hand over, then let go
- Give them what they need: contacts, budget, passwords (via a password manager or secure
  share, not the group chat), previous year's notes.
- **Let them do it their way.** If you specify every detail, you've delegated the typing, not the task.
- Agree how they'll flag problems: "If it looks like it'll slip, just tell me early."

## 5. Follow up without nagging
- **Agree check-in points when you hand it over:** "I'll check in on the 5th." Then a check-in
  is expected, not nagging.
- **Group status round:** a regular "where are we?" in a meeting or the shared list, so you
  aren't chasing individuals.
- **Ask, don't accuse:** "How's the venue going? Anything I can help with?"
- **If it's slipping:** offer help or reassignment without blame — "Would it help if Jo took the
  catering part?"
- **Thank publicly when done**, specifically.

## Gotchas
- **Doing it yourself because it's faster** — it is, once. Then you do it forever.
- **Taking back tasks silently** when someone is slow tells everyone their commitments don't matter.
- **Vague tasks** ("sort out the music") get vague results. Define done.
- **Friend dynamics:** chasing a friend can feel awkward. Agreed check-ins depersonalise it.
- **Chronic non-delivery** is a group-dynamics issue, not a delegation one — see
  `relationships-social.group-leadership-and-teamwork.managing-group-dynamics.handling-dominators-cliques-and-free-riders`.

*Uncertainty:* the claim that individual asks beat open calls rests on bystander-effect research
in other contexts; it fits volunteer-organiser experience but isn't directly measured here.

## Verify
- [ ] Every task has one named owner, a definition of done and a deadline.
- [ ] Asks were made one-to-one with an easy out, and each got a clear yes.
- [ ] Assignments are visible in one shared list.
- [ ] Check-in dates were agreed at hand-over.
- [ ] No single person (including you) holds most of the tasks.
