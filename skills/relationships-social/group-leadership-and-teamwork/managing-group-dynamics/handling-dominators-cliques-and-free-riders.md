# Handling dominators, cliques and free-riders

**Goal:** keep an informal group — club, committee, volunteer crew, study group, friend group —
working when a few people's behaviour is pulling it apart, without public showdowns or losing
the people involved.

## Principles for all of them
1. **Early and small beats late and big.** A light word in week two prevents a blow-up in month six.
2. **Privately first.** Public correction humiliates and rallies sympathy for the person.
3. **Behaviour, not character.** "You've taken most of the airtime in the last few meetings," not
   "You're so domineering."
4. **Assume a reason.** Dominators are often anxious or passionate; free-riders often overloaded
   or unclear on the task; cliques are usually old friends, not plotters.
5. **Group norms do the heavy lifting.** Agreed ground rules let you point to "what we agreed"
   instead of making it personal.

## 1. The dominating member
**In the moment:**
- Use structure: rounds, timed turns, "let's hear from people who haven't spoken yet".
- Acknowledge and redirect: "Thanks, Alex, that's clear — Sam, what do you think?"
- Ask them to write ideas down for later, or give them a role (timekeeper, note-taker).

**Privately:** "You clearly care about this and have loads of ideas. I'm noticing quieter people
aren't getting in. Could you help me draw them out?" Recruiting them as an ally usually works
better than asking them to shut up.

## 2. Cliques
- **Mix structurally:** rotate who sits or works with whom, assign mixed sub-groups, pair newcomers
  with core members.
- **Move decisions into the open:** if a core group decides things over drinks, shift decisions to
  where everyone is (see
  `relationships-social.group-leadership-and-teamwork.group-decision-making.getting-a-group-to-a-decision-it-will-keep`).
- **Talk to the influential member(s)** of the clique: "Newer folk are finding it hard to break in.
  Could you make a point of including them?"
- Close friendships aren't the problem; exclusion is.

## 3. Free-riders
Social psychology calls it **social loafing** — people tend to put in less effort when individual
contributions aren't visible.
- **Make contributions visible:** named tasks, a shared list (see
  `relationships-social.group-leadership-and-teamwork.delegating-among-peers.asking-peers-to-take-on-tasks-and-following-up`).
- **Ask privately and curiously:** "I noticed the flyers didn't happen — what got in the way?"
  Often the task was unclear or their life changed.
- **Right-size their role:** "Would a smaller job suit you better right now?" A smaller job done
  beats a big one ignored.
- **If it persists,** be direct about the impact on others, and agree a clear change or step back
  from the role.

## 4. Tension between two members
- **Don't take sides in public** or carry messages between them.
- **Talk to each separately** to understand, then encourage them to talk directly. Offer to sit
  in as a neutral presence if both want it.
- **Keep the group's work moving** — don't let it become a referendum.
- **Separate them on tasks** for a while if needed.
- If it involves harassment, discrimination or safety, it's not a "dynamics" issue — act on it
  firmly and, where the group has rules or a governing body, use them.

## 5. When nothing works
Some people and groups aren't a fit. A conversation about stepping back, or a group agreeing
ground rules and holding to them, is fairer to everyone than letting one person drive others away.

## Gotchas
- **Avoiding the conversation** is the most common failure; resentment grows silently and good
  members leave quietly.
- **Venting about a member to others** creates a faction. Talk *to* them, not about them.
- **Over-policing** small quirks makes the group joyless. Pick the behaviours that actually harm the group.
- **Cultural differences** in turn-taking and directness can look like dominating or disengagement.
- *Uncertainty:* lab findings on social loafing are robust, but how much they explain volunteer-group
  behaviour versus plain overload is unclear.
