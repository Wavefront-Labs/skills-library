# Small talk — openers, keeping it going, and leaving well

**Goal:** walk up to a stranger or acquaintance, start a light conversation, keep
it going for a few minutes, and either deepen it or exit warmly. Small talk isn't
pointless: it's how people check safety and common ground before anything deeper.
For building rapport deliberately, see
mind-self.persuasion-negotiation-and-deception-detection.rapport-building.building-rapport-quickly-and-genuinely.

## 1. Openers — use the shared situation
The easiest opener comments on or asks about **what you both can see or are doing**:
- "How do you know the host?" (parties) — works almost everywhere.
- "Have you been to one of these before?" (events, classes).
- "Is this line for coffee or registration?" (queues).
- "That looks great — what did you order?" (food).

Then **introduce yourself** within the first exchange or two: "I'm Sam, by the way."
Openers don't need to be clever. Warm and slightly curious beats witty.

Approaching a group: stand at the edge, catch an eye, wait for a pause, then
"Mind if I join you?" Groups of three or more with an open side are easier to
join than a closed pair deep in talk.

## 2. Keeping it going — free information and threads
People offer **free information**: details beyond what you asked. "Just got back
from Lisbon — still jet-lagged." Lisbon and jet lag are both threads. Pick one and
ask about it, or share a related bit of your own.
- **Ask, share, ask.** Alternate a question with a small disclosure so it doesn't
  feel like an interview (see questioning).
- **Safe topic set — FORD:** Family, Occupation, Recreation, Dreams (plans, travel,
  projects). Treat these as directions, not a checklist. Some people prefer not to
  discuss work or family; if an answer is short, move on to another lane.
- **Generally avoid early:** politics, religion, money, health problems, criticism
  of the host or event, and anything that assumes circumstances ("Do you have kids?"
  can be painful — "What keeps you busy outside work?" is safer).
- **Keep answers mid-length:** one or two sentences plus a hook. A one-word answer
  stalls it; a five-minute monologue smothers it.

## 3. If it stalls
Silence for a few seconds is normal. Revive with:
- **Go back to an earlier thread:** "You mentioned Lisbon — was that work or holiday?"
- **Comment on the environment:** music, food, the venue.
- **Bring someone in:** "Have you met Priya? She's just done the same course."
If two revivals don't take, it's a fine time to exit.

## 4. Exiting gracefully
A good exit has three parts: **signal, appreciate, leave.**
- "I'm going to grab another drink — it was really nice talking to you, Alex."
- "I promised I'd say hi to a couple of people — great to meet you."
- "I'll let you get back to it. Good luck with the move!"

Even better, **hand them off**: introduce them to someone before you go. Use their
name in the goodbye. If you want to continue later, say so concretely: "Can I get
your number? I'd love to hear how the course goes."

## Gotchas
- Scanning the room over the person's shoulder while they talk — it's noticed instantly.
- Asking "what do you do?" as the first question everywhere; at social events,
  "what have you been up to lately?" opens more lanes.
- Treating a one-word answer as a challenge; some people don't want to chat. Respect it.
- Holding a person hostage: if they're looking at the exit, help them leave.
- Fake exits ("I need the bathroom") then being seen talking to someone else nearby.

## Verify
- [ ] You opened with a comment or question about the shared situation, then gave your name.
- [ ] You followed at least one piece of free information with a question.
- [ ] Your share-to-ask balance was roughly even.
- [ ] You exited with signal, appreciation and their name — ideally after an introduction.
