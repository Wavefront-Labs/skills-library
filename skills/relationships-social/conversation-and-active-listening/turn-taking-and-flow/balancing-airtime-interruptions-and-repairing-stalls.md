# Turn-taking and flow — airtime, interruptions and stalls

**Goal:** conversations where everyone gets a fair share of talk, nobody feels
steamrolled, and the occasional stall is repaired without awkwardness.

## 1. Airtime: notice your share
- **One-to-one:** aim for roughly balanced talk over the whole conversation, not
  every exchange. If they're telling a big story, listening more is right.
- **The 40-second check (a rule of thumb, not a law):** if you've been talking for
  about 40 seconds without a pause or a sign of interest, wrap up or hand over:
  "…anyway, that's my saga — how's your week been?"
- **Signs you're monologuing:** their responses shrink to "mm", they glance away or
  at their phone, they start a sentence and stop.
- **In groups:** count rough turns. If you've had three turns and someone hasn't had
  one, it's your cue to ask them something.

## 2. Interruptions: supportive or hijacking?
Not all overlap is rude. Linguist Deborah Tannen distinguished **high-involvement**
speakers (overlap shows enthusiasm) from **high-considerateness** speakers (pauses
show respect). Mismatched styles cause each to see the other as rude or cold.
- **Cooperative overlap** — "yes!", "no way", finishing a sentence *they* were
  reaching for, then handing back. Usually fine with high-involvement speakers.
- **Hijacking** — taking the floor and changing the topic or making it about you.
  This is the kind that damages conversations.
If you interrupt by accident: "Sorry — go on, you were saying…" and actually return.

## 3. Being talked over: reclaiming the floor
- Keep going in an even voice for a moment — many interrupters drop off.
- If not: "Hang on, let me just finish this thought," with a small hand gesture.
- In a group, an ally helps: "I think Mai was in the middle of something."
- Do it for others too: "Before we move on — Jordan, you were about to say?"

## 4. Including quiet people
- Ask a specific, easy question, not "Any thoughts?": "Lena, you've been to Japan —
  is it as expensive as people say?"
- Leave a real pause after you ask; don't fill it yourself.
- Some people prefer listening. One invitation is kind; repeated spotlighting isn't.

## 5. Repairing stalls and silences
- **Short silences (a few seconds) are normal** — don't panic-fill them.
- **Revive with a callback:** return to something mentioned earlier. "Wait, you said
  your sister's moving to Canada?"
- **Use the environment:** the food, the music, the event.
- **Offer a small self-disclosure** that invites a response.
- **Change register:** move from facts to opinions — "Do you think it was worth it?"
- **Accept the end:** if two revivals fail, the conversation may have run its course;
  exit warmly (see small-talk).

## 6. Video and phone calls
Lag destroys normal overlap cues. Pause a beat longer before speaking, use names to
hand over turns ("Sam, what do you think?"), and in group calls agree on raised hands
or a facilitator.

## Gotchas
- "Topping" stories — each one bigger than the last — is a slow-motion interruption.
- Waiting for your turn instead of listening (you'll miss the hand-off cue anyway).
- Judging someone as rude for overlapping when it's their cultural or family style.
- Over-correcting into silence; contributing is part of turn-taking.

## Verify
- [ ] You can roughly say who talked most, and it wasn't you by a wide margin.
- [ ] Each time you interrupted, you handed the floor back.
- [ ] Quiet participants got at least one specific, easy invitation.
- [ ] Stalls were repaired with a callback or question rather than rushed filler.
