# Reading social cues — noticing signals and adjusting

**Goal:** notice whether the person you're talking to is engaged, uncomfortable, or
ready to go, and change course in time. The honest caveat: **body language is
ambiguous.** No single gesture has a fixed meaning (crossed arms can mean cold, not
defensive). Read **clusters**, against the person's **baseline**, in **context** —
and when in doubt, ask.

## 1. Baseline first
In the first minute, notice how this person normally is: loud or soft, animated or
still, lots of eye contact or little. A change from their baseline tells you far
more than comparing them to an imagined average. A naturally quiet person going
quiet isn't a signal; an animated one going flat is.

## 2. Common clusters
**Engaged / interested**
- Turned towards you (feet and torso, not just head)
- Asks follow-ups, adds details, laughs easily
- Leans in; expressions track what you say
- Answers lengthen

**Uncomfortable / topic is unwelcome**
- Shorter answers on this topic specifically, then normal again when it changes
- A laugh that doesn't reach the eyes, a tight smile
- Self-touching (neck, face, arms), shifting weight
- Changing the subject or giving a vague non-answer

**Ready to leave / losing interest**
- Feet or torso angled away, towards the door or another person
- Glances at phone, watch, or across the room
- "Anyway…", "Well…", summarising statements, gathering belongings
- Answers shrink to "yeah", "mm", "true"

**Wanting to speak**
- Small inhale, mouth opening, leaning forward, a raised finger

## 3. Tone and words
- **Pitch and pace:** flatter and slower can mean tired or down; faster and higher
  can mean excited or anxious. Again, compare to baseline.
- **What's not said:** a question deflected, a topic dropped, an unusually brief reply.
- **Mismatch** between words and tone ("No, it's *fine*") usually means the tone is
  the truer signal.
- **Text messages** lose most cues; don't over-read punctuation or reply speed.

## 4. Adjusting
| Signal | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Engaged | Keep going; it's fine to go deeper. |
| Uncomfortable on a topic | Lighten or change it: "Anyway — how's the new place?" |
| Losing interest | Shorten your turns, hand over, or offer an exit. |
| Ready to leave | Help them: "I won't keep you — great to catch up." |
| Wants to speak | Pause and let them in. |

## 5. When you can't tell — check in
Asking is not a failure of social skill; it is one.
- "Am I rambling? Tell me if I'm going on."
- "Is this okay to talk about?"
- "You seem a bit quiet — all good?"
- "Do you need to head off?"

## Neurodiversity and difference
Autistic and many other people may show or read cues differently — less eye contact
while listening intently, flat affect while very engaged, missing hints. Direct words
help everybody: say plainly what you mean ("I have to go in five minutes"), and if
someone tells you how they communicate, take them at their word.

## Gotchas
- Over-reading single cues (one glance at a phone, crossed arms in a cold room).
- Confirmation bias: once you think they're bored, everything looks like boredom.
- Cultural variation in eye contact, touch, personal space and expressiveness.
- Ignoring a clear signal because you want the conversation to continue.

## Verify
- [ ] You noted the person's baseline in the first minute.
- [ ] You based any read on two or more cues together, not one.
- [ ] You changed something (topic, length, exit) in response to what you saw.
- [ ] When unsure, you checked in out loud rather than guessing.
