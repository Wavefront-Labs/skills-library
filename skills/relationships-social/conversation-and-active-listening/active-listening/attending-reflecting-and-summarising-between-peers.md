# Active listening — making someone feel heard in everyday conversation

**Goal:** when someone is telling you something that matters to them, leave them
feeling understood, not managed. This is the peer version — between friends, partners,
family, colleagues. For the structured volunteer/helper version, see
human-services.non-clinical-counselling.supportive-listening.active-listening-reflection-and-holding-silence.

## 1. Attend: show you're with them
- **Put the phone face down or away.** Turn your body towards them.
- **Eye contact that's comfortable** — steady, with natural breaks; less intense
  side-by-side (walking, driving) often helps people open up.
- **Minimal encouragers:** "mm", "yeah", "go on", a nod. Enough to show you're
  tracking; not so many it sounds like hurrying.
- **Let pauses sit.** Most people fill a 3–5 second silence with the important part.

## 2. Reflect: hand back what you heard
Three levels, in rising depth:
1. **Content paraphrase** — the facts in your words: "So they moved the deadline up a
   whole week, and nobody told you."
2. **Feeling reflection** — the emotion you sense: "That sounds really frustrating" /
   "You sound pretty worn out by it." Name it tentatively; let them correct you.
3. **Meaning reflection** — why it matters: "It's not just the work — it feels like
   they don't respect your time."

Keep reflections **short** and **tentative** ("sounds like…", "I'm guessing…"). A
wrong guess that gets corrected still helps; it shows you're trying to understand.

## 3. Summarise: tie it together
After a longer stretch: "So — the deadline moved, your manager didn't flag it, and
you're wondering if it's worth raising. Have I got that right?" Summaries show the
whole picture landed and give them a chance to add what's missing.

## 4. Ask before switching to advice
The single biggest failure is jumping to solutions. Ask:
- "Do you want ideas, or do you mostly need to vent?"
- "What would help most right now?"
If they want advice, offer it as options, not instructions.

## 5. Avoid the shift response
Sociologist Charles Derber described **"conversational narcissism"**: the habit of
turning the conversation to yourself with a **shift response** ("That happened to me
too — my boss…") instead of a **support response** ("What did you do?"). An occasional
"me too" can build connection; repeated shifts make the other person feel unheard.
Rule: one support response, ideally two, before any self-reference.

## What not to do
- Interrupt to finish their sentence.
- "At least…" (minimising), "You should…" (fixing), "Calm down" (dismissing).
- Rehearse your reply while they talk — you'll miss the key line.
- Fake attention ("uh-huh") while reading a screen.
- Probe hard for details they're not offering.

## Worked example
Friend: "I didn't get the flat. Third one this month."
- Weak: "Ugh, the market is crazy. When I was looking, I had to see twelve!" (shift)
- Better: "Third one? That's exhausting. Was this one you really wanted?" (feeling +
  support question)

## Gotchas
- Mechanical paraphrasing ("What I hear you saying is…") every sentence sounds scripted;
  vary it and use your own words.
- Cultural and individual differences in eye contact and silence; adapt to the person.
- Active listening is not agreement. You can understand without endorsing.
- If someone discloses risk of harm to themselves or others, listening isn't enough —
  help them reach appropriate support.

## Verify
- [ ] Your phone was away and you let at least one silence run.
- [ ] You reflected a feeling, not just the facts.
- [ ] You summarised and asked "have I got that right?"
- [ ] You asked whether they wanted advice before giving any.
- [ ] You used support responses, not shift responses, for the first few turns.
