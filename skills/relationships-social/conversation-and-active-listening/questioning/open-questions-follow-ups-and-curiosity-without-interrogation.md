# Questioning — asking questions that open a conversation up

**Goal:** use questions to learn about someone and give them room to talk, without the
conversation feeling like a job interview or a cross-examination.

## 1. Open vs closed — and when each is right
- **Closed** questions invite a word or two: "Did you like it?", "Where are you from?"
  Useful for facts, quick checks and getting started.
- **Open** questions invite a story or opinion: "What was it like?", "How did you end
  up doing that?", "What's the best part of it?"
- Convert closed to open by starting with **what / how / tell me about**:
  - "Did you enjoy the trip?" → "What was the highlight of the trip?"
  - "Do you like your job?" → "What's keeping you busy at work these days?"
- A closed question followed by an open one is natural: "Have you lived here long?
  …What brought you here?"

## 2. Follow-ups are the real skill
There's evidence from research on conversations (e.g. Huang and colleagues' 2017
study on question-asking and liking; details unconfirmed here) that people who ask
more questions — especially **follow-up questions** — tend to be liked more. A follow-up
shows you heard the last answer.
- **Pick up a specific word:** "I've been learning to sail." → "Sailing! Where do you
  go out?"
- **Ask about the feeling or the choice:** "What made you decide to go for it?"
- **Ask for the next step:** "And then what happened?"
- **Ask for an example:** "What's a typical day like?"

The first question gets the topic; the second and third get the person.

## 3. Softening "why"
"Why did you do that?" can sound like a demand for justification. Softer versions:
- "What drew you to that?"
- "How did you decide?"
- "What was going through your mind?"

## 4. Avoiding the interview feel
- **Share, then ask.** After their answer, offer a small piece of your own before the
  next question: "I tried sailing once and capsized in about four minutes — is it hard
  to learn?"
- **One question at a time.** Stacking ("Where do you work, do you like it, how long
  have you been there?") lets them answer only the easiest.
- **React before you ask.** "Wow." "That sounds stressful." Then the question.
- **Watch the energy.** Short answers, looking away or turning the question back to you
  repeatedly mean they'd rather talk about something else, or listen.

## 5. Depth: going one level further
When someone seems open, questions about values, feelings and change go deeper:
- "What's something you've changed your mind about recently?"
- "What are you looking forward to this year?"
- "What's been the hardest part?"
Match depth to the relationship; jumping to "what's your biggest regret?" with a new
acquaintance feels invasive. (For deliberate graduated disclosure, see
friendship-and-social-bonding.)

## Gotchas
- Leading questions ("Don't you think that's unfair?") push your view instead of learning theirs.
- "Gotcha" questions designed to win a point end curiosity.
- Questions about sensitive status (marriage, children, salary, health, immigration)
  can hurt; let people volunteer those.
- Asking a question and then not listening to the answer — worse than not asking.
- Cultural norms: in some settings, personal questions early are friendly; in others intrusive.

## Verify
- [ ] Most of your questions began with what, how or tell me about.
- [ ] You asked at least two follow-ups that used their exact words or details.
- [ ] You shared something of your own between questions.
- [ ] You asked one question at a time and reacted before the next.
