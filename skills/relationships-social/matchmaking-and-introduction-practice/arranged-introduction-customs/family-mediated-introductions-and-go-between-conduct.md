# Arranged-introduction customs — family-mediated introductions and the go-between

**Goal:** navigate a formal, family- or community-mediated introduction — as one of the
two people, a family member, or the go-between — respectfully and with both people's
free consent at the centre. Traditions differ enormously by culture, religion,
community and family; the specifics below are commonly described patterns, not a
definitive account of any tradition. Ask people within the community for their norms.

## 1. The shape these traditions share
Across many traditions — for example South Asian *rishta* introductions, Japanese
*omiai*, Orthodox Jewish *shidduch* dating with a *shadchan* (matchmaker), and
family-arranged introductions in many Middle Eastern, African and diaspora communities —
a common pattern recurs:
1. **Intent is stated up front:** the introduction is for marriage (or serious courtship),
   not casual dating.
2. **An intermediary** — a parent, relative, community elder, religious figure or
   professional matchmaker — proposes the match.
3. **Information is exchanged first:** a profile (often called a biodata or marriage CV
   in South Asian contexts) or a verbal description: education, work, family background,
   religious practice, values.
4. **Families may meet** before or alongside the two people.
5. **A small number of meetings,** sometimes chaperoned or in a public place, lead to a
   decision, with the intermediary relaying answers.
Modern practice in many communities blends this with individual choice: families
"introduce", the two people date and decide.

## 2. The go-between's role and conduct
If you're the intermediary:
- **Know both parties' real wishes,** not just their families'. Ask each person privately.
- **Represent honestly.** Don't hide significant facts (health, previous marriage,
  religious observance level) that the other side would want to know; but share
  sensitive details only with the person's permission.
- **Relay decisions tactfully.** A "no" passed through you protects both sides' dignity —
  phrase it as fit ("they felt they were looking for something different"), never as a
  verdict on the person.
- **Keep confidences.** Information shared during matchmaking doesn't go back into
  community gossip.
- **Don't pressure.** Your reputation depends on matches that both people chose.

## 3. As one of the two people
- **Prepare your profile** honestly and specifically: what you value, what you're
  looking for, non-negotiables.
- **Prepare questions** for the first meeting on the things that matter most: faith and
  practice, children, where to live, careers, roles at home, relationship with in-laws.
- **First meetings** are usually short and polite; follow the setting's norms on dress,
  chaperones and contact (some traditions avoid touching before marriage).
- **Ask for more time or another meeting** if you're unsure — that's normal.

## 4. Declining gracefully
- Decline through the intermediary where that's the custom, promptly and kindly.
- Give a fit-based reason or none; avoid criticism of the person or family.
- Families should accept a no without retaliation or repeated pressure.

## 5. Consent: arranged is not forced
An **arranged** introduction gives both people the final say. A **forced** marriage —
one where either person doesn't or can't freely consent, including through pressure,
threats or emotional coercion — is a human-rights abuse and a crime in many countries
(for example, the UK has a dedicated Forced Marriage Unit; check local law). If you or
someone you know is under that pressure, contact local police, a forced-marriage
helpline or a domestic-abuse service.

## Gotchas
- Treating the profile as the person; meet before deciding.
- Families negotiating while the two people haven't spoken privately.
- Assuming one community's customs (e.g. dowry, chaperoning) apply to another.
- Mocking or exoticising a tradition; most participants see it as care, not constraint.

## Verify
- [ ] Both people independently confirmed they wanted the introduction.
- [ ] The profile or description shared was honest and approved by its subject.
- [ ] Questions on the key life issues were prepared for the first meetings.
- [ ] Any no was relayed tactfully, fit-based, and accepted without pressure.
- [ ] At no point did anyone proceed without free consent.
