# Social connecting — mixing guest lists and bridging groups

**Goal:** be the person whose gatherings create new friendships and connections across
your separate circles — work, school friends, neighbours, hobby groups — rather than
hosting each circle in its own silo.

## 1. Why bridging is worth doing
Sociologist Mark Granovetter's "strength of weak ties" work (1973) showed that
acquaintances outside our close circle are disproportionately the source of new
information and opportunities, such as jobs. A connector who links circles creates value
for everyone in them — and usually becomes more valued in each.

## 2. Building a mixed guest list
- **The ratio:** a mix where no single circle dominates, but nobody is the only person
  from theirs. Two or three from each of three or four circles works well for a
  dinner or small party (this is a host's heuristic, not a researched number).
- **Seed with connectors:** include a few socially easy people who'll talk to anyone.
- **Plan specific pairings:** before the event, write down two or three pairs you want
  to meet and why ("Ana and Kofi — both thinking about leaving law").
- **Avoid known friction:** exes, people mid-conflict, or one person who'd dominate.
- **Size:** 6–10 for a dinner where everyone can talk; 15–40 for a mingling party.

## 3. Briefing and setting the tone
- **In the invitation,** say it's a mix: "It'll be a mix of friends from different
  parts of my life — I think you'll like each other."
- **Brief a few guests** on who's coming: "Kofi will be there — he made the move into
  teaching you were asking about."
- **Name tags** only for larger or semi-formal gatherings; at home, introductions do the job.

## 4. During: introduce with hooks
- **Greet each arrival and immediately introduce them** to someone, with a hook: "Ana,
  this is Kofi — you both escaped corporate law. Kofi, Ana's thinking of doing it."
  (See etiquette-manners-and-social-norms.greetings-and-address.)
- **Seat for mixing** at a dinner: separate people who already know each other, put
  your planned pairs side by side, and don't seat a shy guest at the end next to the
  quietest person.
- **Structured moments help mixed groups:** a shared activity (make-your-own pizzas,
  a quiz, a toast where each person says how they know the host) gives strangers a
  reason to talk.
- **Circulate:** spot anyone alone for more than a minute or two and bring them into a group.
- **Rescue stalls:** if a pairing isn't working, introduce a third person and move on.

## 5. After: make the links stick
- **Share contacts** where both are keen: "You two were deep in conversation — want me
  to share your numbers?" (Ask first.)
- **Invite the new combinations again,** in smaller settings.
- **Create a standing format** if it works: a quarterly dinner, a monthly walk, an
  open-house afternoon where the mix keeps refreshing.
- **Notice what grows** — new friendships, collaborations — and don't take credit loudly.

## Gotchas
- Inviting one person who knows nobody and leaving them to it.
- Circles that have nothing in common and no structure to bridge them.
- Over-scheduling the night with games people didn't want.
- Spending the whole event cooking or with your closest friends.
- Sharing someone's details without permission because "you'd get on".

## Verify
- [ ] Your guest list mixes circles with no lone outsider and a few natural connectors.
- [ ] You wrote down at least two intended pairings before the event.
- [ ] Every arrival was introduced to someone with a hook within minutes.
- [ ] Seating or an activity actively mixed people.
- [ ] After the event, you offered to connect pairs that clicked and invited them again.
