# Romantic matchmaking — setting up friends as a friend

**Goal:** introduce two people you know who might be good together, in a way that
respects both of them, doesn't damage your friendships if it doesn't work, and gives
it a fair chance if it might. This is informal matchmaking by a friend or relative,
not professional matchmaking services.

## 1. Check they want to be set up at all
Before you think about who, ask: "Would you be up for me setting you up if I knew
someone?" Some people are deliberately single, recently out of a relationship,
private about their love life, or dislike set-ups. A "no" ends it.

## 2. Judging compatibility — beyond "you're both single"
Being single and likeable isn't a match. Check, as far as you know:
- **Relationship goals:** casual dating vs. a long-term partner; marriage; children.
  A mismatch here sinks it faster than anything.
- **Dealbreakers** each has mentioned: smoking, religion, pets, wanting to relocate.
- **Orientation and stated preferences** — never assume; ask if you don't know.
- **Life stage and logistics:** where they live, schedules, age range they'd consider.
- **Values and lifestyle:** social vs. homebody, ambition, how they spend weekends.
- **Something specific in common** you can name: "you both do long-distance cycling
  and hate small talk."
- **Your honest read:** would *you* want to spend an evening with them as a pair?
Don't set someone up on the strength of one shared trait or because you want two
friends to be a couple.

## 3. Double opt-in, separately
Pitch each one privately, with a fair, short description:
"I know someone I think you'd really get on with — Mia, she's a vet, very funny,
big into hiking, looking for something serious. Want me to connect you?"
- Share a photo only if both are okay with that.
- **Only introduce once both say yes.** If one declines, the other need never know.
- Don't oversell ("you'll be perfect together") — it creates pressure and disappointment.

## 4. Making the introduction
Options, from lowest to higher pressure:
1. **Group setting** — invite both to a dinner or party with others. Low stakes, easy
   exit, they can see each other in their natural habitat.
2. **Number swap** — a short message to both: "Mia, meet Dev; Dev, meet Mia. You both
   love hiking and terrible puns. I'll leave you to it!"
3. **Double date** — useful if one is nervous, but you become part of the dynamic.
Then let them arrange the first date themselves.

## 5. After: step back and stay discreet
- **Don't ask for a debrief** the morning after. If they want to tell you, they will.
- **Don't relay** what one said about the other.
- **If it doesn't work,** accept it with no blame on either side: "No worries at all —
  thanks for giving it a go."
- **If it works,** enjoy it quietly; don't claim it as your project.
- **If it goes badly** (one behaves poorly), believe the person who tells you and
  don't set that person up again without care.

## Gotchas
- Setting up two people only because they're your only single friends.
- Revealing someone's personal information (past relationships, health) to "sell" them.
- Surprise set-ups ("I invited someone for you!") without consent embarrass both.
- Pushing after a no, or after a first date that didn't click.
- Being in the middle if they break up later — stay friends with both, take no side.

## Verify
- [ ] Both people said they're open to being set up at all.
- [ ] You matched on goals, dealbreakers and stated preferences, not just being single.
- [ ] Each person said yes separately before any introduction.
- [ ] The introduction was low-pressure (group setting or a brief connecting message).
- [ ] You stepped back afterwards and didn't relay or press for details.
