# Networking introductions — the double opt-in and the intro message

**Goal:** connect two people for mutual benefit — a job seeker and a hiring manager,
a founder and an investor, two specialists who could help each other — so that both
are glad you did it and your reputation as a connector grows. For the asker's side
(requesting introductions), see
business-finance.career-management.professional-networking.informational-interviews-and-referral-outreach.

## 1. Why double opt-in
A cold "I'm cc'ing you both!" email puts the busier person (often the more senior one)
on the spot: replying no feels rude, ignoring it looks worse. **Double opt-in** means
you ask the receiving person first, privately, and only introduce if they agree. It
protects your relationship with both, and makes the introduction land better.

Skipping the opt-in is reasonable when both clearly benefit and expect it (two peers
who've each said "I'd love to meet more people doing X"), or the ask is tiny.

## 2. The process
1. **Requester asks you.** If someone asks for an intro, ask them for a short
   **forwardable blurb**: who they are, what they want, why this person specifically,
   and how small the ask is ("a 20-minute call").
2. **Decide if you'll vouch.** Would you be comfortable with this introduction carrying
   your name? If not, decline kindly.
3. **Ask the receiver privately** (forwarding the blurb): "Would you be open to an intro
   to Priya? Context below — totally fine to say no."
4. **If yes, make the introduction** (section 3). If no, tell the requester gently
   without blaming the receiver: "They're stretched at the moment — I'll keep you in mind."

## 3. The introduction message — short, specific, mutual
Subject: **"Intro: Priya (Acme) <> Dan (Beta Labs)"**

Body:
> Dan, Priya — connecting you as promised.
>
> **Dan**, Priya leads data engineering at Acme and is moving their pipelines to
> stream processing; she'd value 20 minutes on what you learned doing the same at Beta.
>
> **Priya**, Dan built Beta's streaming platform and is generous with advice — and
> he's been looking for teams trying the new tooling, so you may be useful to him too.
>
> I'll let you two take it from here.

Rules:
- **Address each person's paragraph to them,** about the other.
- **Name the specific reason** and the size of the ask.
- **Name what's in it for the receiver,** if anything — even "Priya's a good person to
  know in this space."
- **Under ~120 words.** No life stories.
- **Include links** (profile, site) rather than long bios.

## 4. After
- **The requester replies first** and moves you to BCC: "Thanks, Sam (moving you to BCC)."
  Then proposes times. This is an etiquette convention worth stating to new requesters.
- **Close the loop:** a good requester tells you how it went; a good introducer asks a
  few weeks later if they don't hear.
- **Track it:** note who you introduced to whom, so you don't over-ask the same person.

## Gotchas
- Introducing people you don't really know — your name is the collateral.
- Over-using your senior contacts; ration intros to them.
- Vague intros ("You two should talk!") — neither knows why or what to do next.
- The requester not following up after the receiver agreed. It reflects on you;
  nudge them once.
- Forwarding a request with sensitive information the requester didn't intend to share.

## Verify
- [ ] The receiving person agreed privately before you sent the introduction.
- [ ] Your intro states, for each person, who the other is and why they should meet.
- [ ] The ask is specific and small, and the email is short.
- [ ] The requester replied, moved you to BCC and followed through; you closed the loop.
