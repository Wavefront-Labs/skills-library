# Setting up friendships — introducing people likely to click

**Goal:** connect two people you know who might become friends — a newcomer to town and a
local, two parents at the same stage, two people with a niche interest — so they meet in
good conditions and the friendship can grow without you in the middle. For romantic
set-ups, see romantic-matchmaking; for professional ones, networking-introductions.

## 1. Spotting affinity
Good friend matches usually share **at least two** of these:
- **A specific interest or activity** they'd actually do together (bouldering, a
  certain band, board games, gardening) — more useful than a broad one ("both like music").
- **Life stage:** new parents, recently moved, newly retired, starting out in a career.
- **Availability and appetite** for new friends. People who've just moved, had a big
  life change, or say "I don't know many people here" are often keen.
- **Humour and temperament** that would mesh.
- **Geography** — near enough to meet without a big effort.
Think: "Could I picture them happily doing something together without me?"

## 2. Check both are open
Unlike romantic set-ups, platonic introductions are often welcome without formal
consent — but checking is still kinder:
"My friend Jo just moved here and is into climbing too — would you be up for meeting her?"
Most people say yes; a hesitant answer is your cue not to push.

## 3. Engineer the first meeting
The meeting should be **easy, short and structured**:
- **A three-way coffee or drink** with you there to make introductions and seed
  topics, then find a reason to leave a bit early.
- **An activity:** bring both to your climbing session, a pub quiz, a park run, a
  gig. Activity gives them something to talk about and to do if conversation lulls.
- **A small gathering** where you seat them together or introduce them with a hook:
  "Jo, Sam is the one I told you about who's also obsessed with sourdough."
- **Connecting message** if you can't meet together:
  "Jo, meet Sam — Sam's lived here ten years and climbs at the same gym you've been
  eyeing. Sam, Jo has just moved from Glasgow. I think you'd get on!"

## 4. Seed the conversation
In the first five minutes, give each a reason to talk:
- Name the shared thing explicitly.
- Tell a short, flattering fact about each ("Sam's the best cook I know").
- Ask a question that both can answer.

## 5. Step back
- Encourage a follow-up between *them*: "You two should go to that bouldering night next
  week." Then don't organise it.
- **Don't insist on being included** in every meetup; the aim is a friendship that
  doesn't need you.
- **Don't feel abandoned** if they become close; it's the outcome you wanted.
- If it doesn't take, that's fine; affinity on paper doesn't always become chemistry.

## Gotchas
- Introducing people solely because they share one demographic trait (nationality,
  age) — it can feel reductive.
- Over-hyping one to the other, creating awkward pressure.
- Leaving them alone too early in a large party with nothing in common to start with.
- Sharing personal details about one to the other beyond the hook.
- Trying to engineer it repeatedly after both have shown mild interest only.

## Verify
- [ ] You named at least two genuine points of affinity before introducing.
- [ ] Both were asked or clearly open to meeting.
- [ ] The first meeting had structure — an activity or a hosted coffee with seeded topics.
- [ ] You stepped back and let them make the next plan themselves.
