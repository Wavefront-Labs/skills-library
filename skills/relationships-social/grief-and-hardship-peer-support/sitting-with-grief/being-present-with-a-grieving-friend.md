# Being present with a grieving friend

**Goal:** spend time with someone who has just lost a person they love in a way that leaves
them feeling less alone — not "better". Grief isn't a problem you can fix; the useful thing a
friend brings is company that can stand the pain.

This is peer support. For the professional role and when to refer on, see
`human-services.bereavement-support-and-death-doula-work.grief-support.companioning-a-grieving-person-and-recognising-when-to-refer`.
For your own words, see
`relationships-social.grief-and-hardship-peer-support.what-to-say.condolence-words-and-platitudes-to-avoid`.

## 1. Before you go
- **Ask, don't announce:** "I'd like to come by for an hour tomorrow — would that be OK, or
  would another time be better?" Some people want company immediately; others are overwhelmed
  by visitors in the first days.
- **Bring something low-effort for them:** food that needs no work, tissues, coffee. Not
  flowers that need a vase, unless you'll arrange them.
- **Settle yourself.** If you're also grieving, decide what you can hold today.

## 2. While you're there — presence over fixing
- **Let them set the agenda.** They may want to talk about the person, the death, the
  practicalities, or anything but. Follow.
- **Say the person's name.** Many bereaved people say the silence around the name is one of
  the hardest parts. "Tell me about Sam" is often welcome.
- **Tolerate tears.** Don't rush to hand a tissue or say "don't cry" — both can signal "please
  stop". Let crying run; a hand on the shoulder or just staying put is enough.
- **Tolerate silence.** Sitting quietly together is a complete activity. Resist filling it
  with stories of your own losses or advice.
- **Don't try to make sense of it** ("everything happens for a reason") — explaining a loss
  feels like dismissing it.
- **Do small useful things without being asked:** wash the cups, answer the door, take a
  phone message.

## 3. Ring theory — who comforts whom
Picture the bereaved person at the centre, closest family in the next ring, then friends,
then acquaintances. **Comfort goes in; complaints go out.** You support those closer to the
centre than you and vent only to those further out. So don't tell a widow how upset *you* are
that you didn't get to say goodbye; tell your own partner later.

## 4. Reading when to stay or go
- Signs to wrap up: they glance at the clock, yawn, start tidying, others arrive, they go
  quiet in a closed-off (not companionable) way.
- Keep first visits **short — often under an hour** — unless asked to stay. You can say:
  "I'll head off soon. Would you like me to stay a bit longer?"
- Leave with a concrete next contact: "I'll text Thursday" — then do it.

## 5. Gotchas
- **Grief varies hugely.** Numbness, laughter, anger, intense practicality — all are normal.
  Don't read calm as "coping well" or laughter as "not grieving".
- **Culture and faith** shape mourning: sitting shiva, wakes, specific periods of visiting,
  rules on food and dress. Ask a family member what's expected rather than guessing.
- **Don't take the bad moments personally.** A grieving person may be short with you; it's
  rarely about you.
- **Your own discomfort** is the usual reason people avoid the bereaved. Showing up awkwardly is
  far better than staying away.
- **When to worry:** talk of not wanting to live, not eating or sleeping for many days, or
  inability to function weeks in. Encourage them to see a GP or counsellor, and if they mention
  suicide, contact a crisis line or emergency services with them. *Note:* where "normal" grief
  ends and prolonged grief begins is clinically debated; this page doesn't attempt to judge it.

## Verify
- [ ] You asked before visiting and offered a time.
- [ ] You talked less than they did and said the person's name.
- [ ] You let tears and silences run without rushing to fill or stop them.
- [ ] You vented, if at all, outward, not to the bereaved.
- [ ] You left with a concrete next contact and kept it.
