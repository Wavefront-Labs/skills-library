# Staying in touch through the long tail of grief

**Goal:** keep showing up for a bereaved friend or relative after the funeral, when most people
have drifted back to normal life and the loss is at its loneliest. A few well-timed, low-effort
contacts over a year often matter more than a week of intense help.

## 1. Why the long tail matters
- The crowd thins after 2–4 weeks; the bereaved person often says the **second to sixth months**
  were harder than the first, as numbness wears off.
- Grief doesn't follow neat "stages"; many people now prefer the idea of **continuing bonds** —
  an ongoing relationship with the person who died — rather than "letting go" or "closure".
  Your role is to make room for that, not hurry it on.

## 2. Build a calendar now
Put these in your own diary, with reminders, the week you hear the news:
- Weekly check-ins for the **first two months**, then fortnightly to month six, then monthly.
- **The date of death** — monthly for the first few months, then the one-year anniversary.
- **The person's birthday**, and the bereaved person's own.
- **Holidays and family days:** Christmas, Eid, Diwali, Mother's/Father's Day, wedding anniversary,
  first day of school, the holiday they always took together.
- **Admin milestones** that can hit hard: inquest, probate, scattering ashes, headstone, clearing the house.

Ask a family member for dates you don't know rather than guessing.

## 3. What a check-in looks like
Keep it light and **reply-optional**:
- "Thinking of you and Sam today. No need to reply."
- "It's a year tomorrow. I'll be remembering him with you."
- "Fancy a walk Sunday? Totally fine if not."
- "I saw a red kite and thought of your mum and her birdwatching."

Alternate kinds of contact: texts, a card on the anniversary, a standing coffee date, an
invitation to something normal. Invitations matter: bereaved people are often dropped from
social plans, especially if they were part of a couple.

## 4. Remember the person aloud
Say their name; tell stories; mention them at birthdays and gatherings. Many bereaved people fear
the person will be forgotten, and being the one who keeps remembering is a big kindness. Ask
"Would you like to talk about her?" if you're unsure — and accept "not today".

## 5. Pacing yourself
- Choose a level you can sustain for a year rather than overdoing month one.
- Share the load: if you're part of a friend group, agree who covers which months or dates.
- If they don't reply for weeks, keep sending occasional reply-optional messages. Silence
  usually means overwhelm, not rejection. Stop only if asked.

## 6. Gotchas
- **"Shouldn't they be over it by now?"** — there's no deadline. Expect waves, triggered by
  dates, smells, songs, even years later.
- **Don't push milestones** ("Have you cleared the wardrobe yet?"). They'll do things in their own time.
- **Watch for prolonged difficulty:** if a year on they can barely function, have withdrawn
  from everything, or talk of not wanting to live, gently encourage a GP, grief counsellor or
  bereavement service, and treat any suicide talk as urgent. *Note:* "prolonged grief disorder"
  is now a formal diagnosis (ICD-11, DSM-5-TR), but the line between it and ordinary deep grief
  is a clinician's call, not a friend's.
- **New relationships or apparent cheerfulness** don't mean the grief is finished; keep remembering the dates.

## Verify
- [ ] Key dates (death, birthday, holidays, anniversary) are in your diary with reminders.
- [ ] You have a planned cadence: weekly, then fortnightly, then monthly through year one.
- [ ] Messages are reply-optional and some mention the person by name.
- [ ] You've invited them to at least one ordinary social thing since the funeral.
- [ ] You know what you'd do if they seemed to be stuck or unsafe.
