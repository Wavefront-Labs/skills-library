# Condolence words, and the platitudes to avoid

**Goal:** say something — briefly, sincerely — when a friend or relative is bereaved or hit by
hardship, instead of saying nothing out of fear of saying the wrong thing. Silence is the
most common hurt people report; a clumsy but warm message is almost always better.

The principle: **acknowledge, don't explain.** You're there to recognise the loss, not to make
sense of it, compare it, or cheer it up.

## 1. Lines that work
Pick one or two; you don't need more.
- "I'm so sorry about your dad."
- "I've been thinking about you all day."
- "I don't know what to say, but I'm here." — honest, and widely welcomed.
- "This is awful. I'm sorry you're going through it."
- "I loved Sam's laugh. I'll miss him." — **name the person** and give a specific memory if you
  have one; families often treasure these for years.
- "No need to reply." — removes the burden of answering.

## 2. By channel
- **Text/message (first days):** two or three lines plus "no need to reply". Don't ask
  questions that demand an answer ("How did it happen?").
- **Card/letter:** open with the acknowledgement, add a specific memory or quality of the
  person, close with care. Handwritten matters to many people. For a fuller template, see
  `languages-linguistics.everyday-composition.letter-writing.writing-formal-complaint-request-and-condolence-letters`.
- **In person:** "I'm so sorry" and a hug or hand (if that's your relationship) is enough. Then
  follow their lead (see
  `relationships-social.grief-and-hardship-peer-support.sitting-with-grief.being-present-with-a-grieving-friend`).
- **Social media:** a public comment is fine as an addition, not a replacement for a direct message.

## 3. Platitudes to avoid — and why they hurt

| Avoid | Why it lands badly |
|---|---|
| "Everything happens for a reason." | Implies the loss was meant or deserved. |
| "They're in a better place." | Only fits if you *know* they share that belief; still says "don't be sad". |
| "At least…" (they lived a long life / you can have another baby / you have other children) | "At least" minimises; anything after it is a comparison. |
| "I know how you feel." | You don't — even with a similar loss. "I lost my mum too; I remember how hard it was" is better, briefly. |
| "Be strong" / "You're so strong." | Pressure to perform coping. |
| "Time heals." / "You'll move on." | Suggests a deadline; many people carry grief indefinitely. |
| "Let me know if you need anything." | Puts the work on them (see practical help, below). |
| "God needed another angel." | Can provoke anger in believers and non-believers alike. |
| "How are you?" (as a greeting) | Unanswerable. "How are you today?" is easier. |

## 4. Specific situations
- **Suicide loss:** say "died by suicide", not "committed" (associated with crime). Don't ask
  why or how. Acknowledge it as a death like any other.
- **Miscarriage/stillbirth:** acknowledge it as a real loss of a baby. Use the name if one was
  given. Avoid "it wasn't meant to be" and "at least it was early".
- **Pet loss:** take it seriously; "I know how much Biscuit meant to you" is right.
- **Diagnosis, job loss, divorce:** the same rule — acknowledge, don't explain or silver-line.
  "That's really hard news" before any practical thought.

## 5. After a blunder
If you said a platitude, a short repair is fine later: "I said something clumsy the other day —
I'm sorry. I just wanted you to know I care." Don't make a big thing of it.

## Gotchas
- Avoid **making it about you** — long stories of your own loss, or how shocked you were.
- **Religious language:** match theirs, not yours.
- **Months later**, it's still OK — often more welcome — to say "I've been thinking about your
  dad." See `relationships-social.grief-and-hardship-peer-support.sustained-support.staying-in-touch-through-the-long-tail-of-grief`.
- *Uncertainty:* which phrases offend varies by person and culture; the table reflects broadly
  reported preferences in English-speaking grief literature, not a universal rule.

## Verify
- [ ] You said something within the first days rather than nothing.
- [ ] Your message acknowledged the loss and named the person.
- [ ] No "at least", "reason", "better place" or "let me know if you need anything".
- [ ] You told them no reply was needed.
