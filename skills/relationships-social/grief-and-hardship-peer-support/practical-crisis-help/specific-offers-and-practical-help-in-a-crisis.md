# Specific offers and practical help in a crisis

**Goal:** take real load off a friend or relative in the first days and weeks of a crisis — a
death, a sudden hospitalisation, a house fire, a partner walking out — in ways they can say yes
to without having to think.

## 1. Kill "let me know if you need anything"
People in crisis are overloaded; working out *what* to ask *whom* is itself a task they can't
do. Offer **a specific thing, at a specific time, that's easy to accept or decline:**
- "I'm going to Tesco at 5 — I'll drop milk, bread and fruit on your step. Text 'no' if not."
- "I can take the kids Saturday 10–4. Shall I?"
- "I'll walk Rex every morning this week unless you tell me otherwise."
- "Can I make the calls to your work and the school for you?"

Phrase it so **doing nothing means yes** for low-risk help (food on the doorstep), and needs a
simple yes for anything entering their space.

## 2. What helps most (often overlooked)
- **Food that needs no work:** ready-to-eat or reheat-only, in disposable containers labelled
  with contents and date. Ask about allergies, diet and how many are in the house.
- **The boring logistics:** bins out, laundry, school runs, pet care, picking up prescriptions,
  watering plants, mowing.
- **Admin, if close enough:** phoning people with the news, cancelling appointments, keeping a
  list of who sent what (for thank-yous later), chasing insurers. Always with their say-so.
- **Childcare** — often the single most valuable thing for parents.
- **A gatekeeper:** someone who answers the door and phone, and tactfully moves visitors on.
- **Transport:** lifts to hospital, the funeral home, the airport. Hospital parking money.
- **Household stocks:** toilet paper, coffee, tissues, phone chargers.

## 3. How to help without taking over
- **Ask before rearranging anything** in their home, especially the belongings of someone who
  has died.
- **Don't expect thanks or updates** — hand food over at the door, don't stay unless asked.
- **Return containers is a chore** — use disposable ones or say "keep it".
- **Respect their way** of doing things even if yours is more efficient.
- **Money:** if offering financial help, make it clear it's a gift and easy to refuse; some people
  find cash hard to accept but take a paid-for meal delivery or cleaner more easily.
- Ask once about anything big; don't argue if they decline.

## 4. Coordinating with others
If several people want to help, one person organising avoids five lasagnes on Tuesday and
nothing on Thursday — see
`relationships-social.grief-and-hardship-peer-support.community-rallying.organising-a-meal-train-and-support-roster`.

## 5. After the first wave
Practical help often floods in for two weeks, then stops just as the funeral is over, family
has gone home, and the person is alone with the admin. The second month is often when
help is most needed. Diary it now (see
`relationships-social.grief-and-hardship-peer-support.sustained-support.staying-in-touch-through-the-long-tail-of-grief`).

## Gotchas
- **Not all crises want visitors.** Some people want only food and texts; respect that.
- **Privacy:** don't share their news or their situation with others without permission, even
  to recruit help.
- **Don't promise what you can't sustain.** Two reliable meals beat a week of promised help
  you drop on day three.
- **Look after your own load** — help you can't sustain turns into resentment.
- *Uncertainty:* supermarket and service names above are UK examples; substitute local equivalents.

## Verify
- [ ] Every offer named a specific task and time.
- [ ] Food was labelled, ready-to-eat and in containers they needn't return.
- [ ] You asked before touching anything personal in their home.
- [ ] Help was coordinated with others rather than duplicated.
- [ ] You put a date in your diary for help in the second month.
