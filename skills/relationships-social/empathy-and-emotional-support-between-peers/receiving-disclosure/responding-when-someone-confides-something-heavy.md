# Responding when someone confides something heavy

**Goal:** when a friend tells you something big — an assault, abuse, self-harm, suicidal
thoughts, an addiction, coming out, a diagnosis — your first reaction makes them feel
safe that they told you. Survivors' accounts and disclosure research consistently show
that a negative first reaction (disbelief, blame, shock) can make things worse and stop
people telling anyone else.

## 1. The first 30 seconds
- **Keep your face and voice steady.** Inner shock is normal; don't let it take over
  the room. Breathe out slowly.
- **Thank them:** "Thank you for telling me. I know that can't have been easy."
- **Believe them:** "I believe you." For assault or abuse, this may be the single most
  important sentence.
- **Don't blame:** no "why didn't you…", "what were you wearing", "why did you go back".
- **It's not about you:** don't make it about your anger, your hurt at not being told
  sooner, or your own similar story.

## 2. Listen; don't interrogate
- Let them share at their own pace and depth. You don't need the details.
- Ask open, caring questions: "How are you doing with it now?" "What do you need?"
- Avoid investigative questions ("Who? When exactly? Are you sure?") — that's not your
  role and it can feel like doubt.

## 3. Confidentiality: be honest
- Don't promise **absolute** secrecy before you know what it is. If asked "Promise you
  won't tell?", say: "I'll keep it private. The only thing I'd ever do is get help if I
  thought you or someone else was in danger — and I'd tell you first."
- Then keep it. Don't share with partners or mutual friends "so they can help" without
  permission.
- If you do need to involve someone, involve them in the decision wherever possible.

## 4. Let them stay in control
After many disclosures — especially assault or abuse — control was taken from them.
Offer options, don't decide for them: "Would you want to talk to someone? I could look
up the number with you." Respect a no, unless there's immediate danger.

## 5. When it's beyond peer support
Some disclosures need more than a friend. Get help (with them where possible) if:
- **Suicide or self-harm:** ask directly — "Are you thinking of killing yourself? Do you
  have a plan?" If there's a plan, means or immediate risk, don't leave them alone; call
  emergency services or a crisis line together. Asking directly does not increase risk.
- **A child or vulnerable adult is being harmed:** this usually needs reporting to the
  authorities; rules differ by country — check local child-protection guidance.
- **Ongoing violence or danger** to them: domestic-violence services and safety planning.
- **Medical urgency:** recent assault (forensic and medical care is time-sensitive),
  overdose, injury.
Keep the main crisis numbers for your country saved in your phone in advance.

## 6. Afterwards
- Check in the next day: "I've been thinking about what you told me. How are you?"
- Don't bring it up in front of others or treat them differently in public.
- Look after yourself: hearing something heavy can affect you. You can seek support
  (a helpline, a counsellor) without breaching their identity.

## Gotchas
- **Over-reacting on their behalf** (confronting the abuser, reporting against their wishes when
  no one is at immediate risk) can take away their control and their safety planning.
- **Coming out:** react to the person, not the label. "Thanks for telling me. How do you feel
  about it?" Don't out them to anyone.
- **"I knew it" / "I'm not surprised"** — even if true, it can feel invalidating.

## Verify
- [ ] Your first words thanked and believed them, with no blame.
- [ ] You asked what they need rather than for details.
- [ ] Your confidentiality promise was honest about safety exceptions.
- [ ] You asked directly about suicide if there was any hint of it.
- [ ] You checked in again within a day or two and haven't told anyone without consent.
