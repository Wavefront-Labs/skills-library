# Seeing it from their side before you respond

**Goal:** understand why a reasonable person in *their* position would think, feel or act
as they do — accurately enough that they'd recognise your summary. Psychologists call
this cognitive empathy or perspective-taking; it's distinct from *feeling* what they feel
(affective empathy), and you can do it without agreeing.

## The core habit: pause, imagine, check
### 1. Pause your own frame
Notice your first reaction ("that's ridiculous", "they're being lazy"). Don't act on it
yet. Our default is the **fundamental attribution error**: we explain others' behaviour
by their character and our own by circumstances. Deliberately flip it.

### 2. Imagine from the inside
Work through four questions:
- **Constraints:** What pressures are they under that I can't see? (Money, time, a sick
  child, a boss, health, fear.)
- **History:** What past experiences might make this situation look different to them?
  (Being let down before, a culture where this means something else.)
- **Stakes:** What do they stand to lose or gain? What are they afraid of?
- **Information:** What do they know that I don't, or not know that I do?
Then write or say a version that starts: "If I were them, I'd probably feel ___
because ___."

### 3. Steel-man it
State their position in its *strongest* form — the version they'd endorse — rather than
the weakest (straw man). If you can't make their view sound reasonable, you probably
don't understand it yet.

### 4. Check your guess with them
Imagined perspectives are often wrong; research on "empathic accuracy" finds people
overestimate how well they read others. So ask:
- "My guess is you're worried this will fall on you — is that right?"
- "Help me understand what this looks like from where you're sitting."
- "What am I missing?"
Then listen and update. The act of checking itself signals respect.

## When it's hardest (and most useful)
- **When you're in conflict with them.** Taking their perspective before you argue often
  shrinks the disagreement; see the conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation field.
- **When they're very different from you** — age, culture, class, neurotype. Your
  imagination fills gaps with your own assumptions; ask more.
- **When you're tired or stressed.** Perspective-taking takes effort and drops under load;
  delay important responses if you can.

## Exercises to build the skill
- After a frustrating interaction, write a paragraph from the other person's point of
  view, in first person, as sympathetically as you can.
- In a disagreement, summarise their view until they say "yes, that's it" before giving yours
  (the "speaker–listener" rule used in some couple-communication programmes).
- Read fiction and memoirs from lives unlike yours — studies link fiction reading with
  perspective-taking, though the effect sizes are debated.

## Gotchas
- **Projection:** "If I were them" can slide into "if I were them *with my values and
  history*". Imagine their whole situation, not just their seat.
- **Mind-reading as a weapon:** "I know what you really think" is not perspective-taking.
- **Perspective-taking isn't excusing.** You can understand why someone did something
  hurtful and still hold a boundary.
- **Over-identifying** can exhaust you; understanding doesn't require carrying their feelings.
