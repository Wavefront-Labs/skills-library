# Reaching out to someone who may be struggling

**Goal:** give someone a genuine opening to say they're not okay — and keep the door
open over time. Peer check-ins don't need training or the perfect words; they need
noticing, a private moment, and persistence.

## 1. Notice the signs
Changes from their normal matter more than any single sign:
- Withdrawing: cancelling plans, slow or no replies, missing from the group chat.
- Mood: irritable, flat, tearful, unusually anxious.
- Life events: breakup, job loss, illness, bereavement, exams, a new baby, moving.
- Habits: drinking more, sleeping much more or less, letting things slide.
- Words: "What's the point", "I'm a burden", "I can't do this anymore".
A big life event alone is reason enough to check in, signs or not.

## 2. Pick the moment and the medium
- **Private, unhurried, low stakes.** A walk, a car ride, a coffee. Not in front of others.
- **Text is fine for an opening**, especially for people who find face-to-face hard, but
  aim to move to a call or meeting if something's wrong.
- **Timing:** not immediately before they have to perform (a meeting, an exam).

## 3. Ask so they can answer honestly
The structure used by R U OK? (Australia) is a good default: **Ask → Listen → Encourage
action → Check in** (paraphrased).
- **Be specific about why:** "You've seemed pretty quiet since the restructure. How are
  you really doing?" Specific observations make "fine" harder to default to.
- **Ask twice.** If they say "fine", try once more, gently: "Sure? You don't seem quite
  yourself — no pressure, but I'm happy to listen." Many people only answer honestly the
  second time.
- **Low-pressure text examples:**
  - "Been thinking about you. No need to reply — just wanted you to know I'm around."
  - "Fancy a walk on Saturday? Totally fine if you're not up for it."
  - "How are you going with everything? Honest answer welcome."
- **Don't make it a big deal.** A heavy, formal "We need to talk about your mental
  health" puts people on the defensive.

## 4. Listen
If they open up: listen more than you talk, don't rush to fix, validate. See
relationships-social.empathy-and-emotional-support-between-peers.validating-feelings and
...holding-space. Ask "How long have you felt like this?" and "What's helped before?"

## 5. Encourage action (gently)
- "Is there anything that would make this week a bit easier?"
- If it's been weeks, or it's affecting daily life: "Have you thought about talking to
  your GP? I could help you book." Offer to go with them.
- Respect that they may not be ready. Keep the door open.

## 6. Keep checking in
The follow-up is the skill most people skip.
- **Diary a reminder:** a week later, then regularly. Struggling people rarely reach out.
- **Vary it:** a meme, an invite, a practical offer, a direct "how are you".
- **Don't stop because they didn't reply.** Unanswered messages can still mean a lot.
- **Keep inviting them** to things, even after declines; make it easy to say yes later.

## Gotchas
- **"Let me know if you need anything"** rarely gets taken up — be specific.
- **Interrogating** with lots of questions.
- **Gossiping** about their state to others — check in, don't broadcast.
- **Asking directly about suicide does not plant the idea** (a common worry the research
  doesn't support). If you're concerned, ask plainly: "Are you thinking about suicide?"
  If yes, or they're in danger, contact emergency services or a crisis line with them;
  see relationships-social.empathy-and-emotional-support-between-peers.receiving-disclosure.
- Look after yourself; you can't be someone's only support.

## Verify
- [ ] You reached out within a few days of noticing a change or hearing of a big event.
- [ ] Your opening named something specific and you asked twice.
- [ ] You listened more than you talked.
- [ ] You have a dated reminder for the next check-in.
- [ ] You'd know which crisis line or service to contact if things were serious.
