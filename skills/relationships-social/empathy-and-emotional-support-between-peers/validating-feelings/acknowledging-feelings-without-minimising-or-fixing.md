# Acknowledging feelings without minimising or fixing

**Goal:** the other person leaves feeling *understood*. Validation means communicating
"your feeling makes sense given what's happening" — it does not mean you agree with
their view of the facts, or that you approve of what they plan to do.

## The three moves
1. **Name it (tentatively).** "That sounds really frustrating." "You seem pretty
   shaken." Guess the feeling; let them correct you ("not frustrated — humiliated").
   A wrong guess offered humbly still shows you're trying.
2. **Make sense of it.** "Of course you're angry — you did the work and someone else got
   the credit." Link the feeling to the situation. This is the step people skip.
3. **Stay with it.** Pause. Let them say more. Ask "What's been the hardest part?" rather
   than moving to solutions.
(Marsha Linehan's DBT work describes several graded "levels" of validation, from paying
attention through to radical genuineness; the three moves above are a simplified peer
version.)

## Phrases that tend to work
- "That makes complete sense."
- "No wonder you're exhausted."
- "I'd feel the same in your shoes." (only if true)
- "That sounds so hard."
- "I can hear how much this matters to you."
- "It's okay to feel this way about it."

## What invalidates (even when well-meant)
| Says | What they hear |
|---|---|
| "At least…" ("at least you have a job") | Your pain doesn't count. |
| "Everything happens for a reason." | Your loss is fine, actually. |
| "Don't be sad / don't worry." | Your feeling is wrong. |
| "I know exactly how you feel — when I…" | Now we're talking about me. |
| "You're overreacting." | You can't trust your own experience. |
| "Have you tried…?" (straight away) | Your feeling is a problem to get rid of. |
| "Look on the bright side." | Hurry up and feel better. |
Toxic positivity — insisting on cheerfulness — is the most common form among friends.

## The fix reflex
Most of us jump to advice because their distress makes *us* uncomfortable. Before
offering any solution, ask: **"Do you want ideas, or do you mainly need to vent right
now?"** Respect the answer. Many people come round to solutions themselves once they
feel heard; advice before that point tends to bounce off.

## Validating without endorsing
If a friend's anger leads to a plan you think is unwise: validate the feeling first,
then raise the concern separately. "You've every reason to be furious. I'm worried that
sending that email tonight could backfire — want to sleep on it?"

## Gotchas
- **Parroting** ("So you feel sad.") sounds robotic. Vary it, and add the "because".
- **Validating the invalid:** you don't have to validate factual distortions ("Everyone
  hates me"). Validate the feeling ("It feels like nobody's on your side right now")
  without endorsing the conclusion.
- **Over-intensifying** ("That's DEVASTATING!") when they said "a bit annoyed" — match their level.
- **Text messages:** validation reads colder in writing; add warmth explicitly, or call.

## Verify
- [ ] Your first response named a feeling and linked it to their situation.
- [ ] You used no "at least", "don't worry" or "everything happens for a reason".
- [ ] You asked before giving advice.
- [ ] They kept talking after your response (a sign it landed) rather than going quiet or defensive.
- [ ] They'd say afterwards that you "got it".
