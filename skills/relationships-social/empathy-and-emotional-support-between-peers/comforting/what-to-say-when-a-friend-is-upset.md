# What to say when a friend is upset

**Goal:** give the comfort they actually need, not the kind *you'd* want. People differ —
and the same person needs different things on different days.

## 1. Open simply
- In person: "Hey, you seem upset. What's going on?" or just "Oh no. Come here."
- By text: "Just saw your message — I'm so sorry. Do you want to talk, or want company,
  or neither right now?"
- Don't open with questions that demand detail ("What exactly happened?"); let them choose
  how much to share.

## 2. Work out which kind of support they need
Support research commonly distinguishes types like emotional, practical (instrumental),
informational and companionship support. Read the signals, or just ask: **"What would
help most right now — talking it through, a distraction, or something practical?"**

| They need | Signals | You do |
|---|---|---|
| **To be heard** | Telling the story, emotion high | Listen, validate, few words |
| **Practical help** | Overwhelmed by tasks, mentions logistics | Offer something specific: "I'll pick up the kids tomorrow." |
| **Distraction** | "I don't want to think about it" | A walk, a film, food, normal chat |
| **Advice** | Asks "what would you do?" | Give it — briefly, as an option, not a verdict |
| **Company** | Doesn't want to talk, doesn't want to be alone | Just be there |

## 3. Phrases that comfort
- "I'm so sorry. That's awful."
- "That makes total sense that you're upset."
- "I'm here. You don't have to go through this by yourself."
- "Do you want to tell me about it?"
- "Is it okay if I just sit with you?"
- "You don't have to be okay right now."
For validation phrasing in depth see
relationships-social.empathy-and-emotional-support-between-peers.validating-feelings.

## 4. What to avoid
- **Comparisons:** "My breakup was worse." / "Other people have it harder."
- **Clichés:** "Plenty more fish in the sea", "It'll all work out", "Time heals".
- **Judgement or blame:** "Well, you did kind of see it coming."
- **Vague offers:** "Let me know if you need anything" puts the work on them. Offer
  something concrete instead.
- **Criticising the other person harshly** (an ex, a family member) — they may get back
  together or feel defensive.
- **Turning it into a lesson** while they're still raw.

## 5. Physical comfort
A hug, a hand on the arm, sitting close: powerful for many, unwelcome for some (and
dependent on culture, gender and your relationship). Ask or offer: "Do you want a hug?"
Food and a hot drink are near-universal comfort; bringing them is often better than words.

## 6. Afterwards
- Text the next day: "Thinking of you. How are you doing today?"
- Remember the date of what's coming (results, court, an anniversary) and check in then.
- See relationships-social.empathy-and-emotional-support-between-peers.checking-in.

## Gotchas
- **Their reaction isn't a verdict on you.** If they snap or go quiet, it's the situation.
- **Over-comforting** someone who wants space; respect "I just need to be alone tonight".
- **Your own mood:** if you're upset too, say so briefly rather than faking calm.
- **Group chats:** pile-on sympathy can overwhelm; a private message is often better.
- If the upset is grief, see the grief-and-hardship-peer-support field.
- If they mention self-harm or not wanting to be alive, take it seriously and see
  relationships-social.empathy-and-emotional-support-between-peers.receiving-disclosure.

## Verify
- [ ] You asked (or read) what kind of support they needed, and gave that kind.
- [ ] Your words included no comparisons, clichés or "at least".
- [ ] Any offer of help was specific and you followed through.
- [ ] Physical comfort was offered, not assumed.
- [ ] You followed up within a day or two.
