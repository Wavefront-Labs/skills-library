# Naming and renegotiating limits

**Goal:** make sure both partners know each other's limits around physical and sexual
intimacy — and the agreements around it — so neither has to guess, and so limits can
change over time without drama. Non-graphic relationship-education reference.

A boundary here means *what you will and won't do or have done*, and what you need to
feel safe. It's about your own participation, not about controlling the other
person's life (see
relationships-social.romantic-relationship-and-intimacy-skill.autonomy-and-togetherness.balancing-individual-life-and-coupledom).

## 1. Know your own limits first
Before the conversation, think through:
- **Hard limits:** not open to negotiation, now or probably ever.
- **Soft limits / maybes:** things you might consider under certain conditions, or
  later, or with more trust.
- **Conditions:** what you need to feel comfortable — privacy, sobriety, protection,
  pace, being asked first.
- **Agreements beyond the bedroom:** exclusivity, whether flirting with others is
  okay, sharing intimate photos, what you each tell friends.
It's fine not to know yet. "I'm not sure, I'll tell you when I know" is a valid answer.

## 2. Saying it
- **Clearly and early-ish**, ideally outside an intimate moment, and *before* the
  boundary is likely to come up.
- **Short, unapologetic:** "Something that's a firm no for me is X." "I need to be
  asked before Y." No justification required, though you may choose to share context.
- **Pair with what you do want**, when you can — it keeps the conversation warm (see
  relationships-social.sexuality-and-intimate-communication.expressing-desire.voicing-wants-and-preferences-to-a-partner).
- **In the moment**, a boundary can still be stated at any time: "Not that — can we..."

## 3. Hearing a partner's boundary
- **Thank them** for telling you. It's an act of trust.
- **Don't debate, bargain or ask them to justify it.** A boundary isn't an opening offer.
- **Ask clarifying questions** only to understand ("Does that include...?"), not to
  find loopholes.
- **Remember it**, so they don't have to repeat it.
- **If it's a dealbreaker for you** — say, on monogamy — that's a real incompatibility
  to discuss openly, not a reason to pressure them.

## 4. Renegotiating over time
Boundaries legitimately change with trust, health, pregnancy, age, stress, medication
or experience.
- **Either partner can reopen a topic**, but no one has to change a limit.
- **Check in periodically** — some couples revisit agreements yearly or around big
  life events.
- **Expansion is opt-in; contraction is immediate.** Anyone can narrow a limit at any
  time, and that applies straight away. Loosening a limit needs a fresh, unpressured
  conversation.
- **Agreements about exclusivity or openness** need especially explicit, mutual
  renegotiation; changing them unilaterally is a breach of trust.

## 5. Images and privacy
- Share intimate images only with clear, mutual agreement, and remember they can be
  copied or leaked. Ask before sending; never forward or keep them without consent.
- Sharing intimate images without consent ("image-based abuse") is illegal in many
  places. If it happens to you, services such as StopNCII.org can help remove images
  from participating platforms.
- Never create or share intimate images of anyone under 18 — it is illegal everywhere
  the authors know of, including between teenagers in many jurisdictions.

## 6. When a boundary is crossed
- **If you crossed it:** stop, acknowledge it without excuses, apologise, and ask what
  they need. Don't minimise ("it wasn't a big deal").
- **If yours was crossed:** you can say so in the moment or later. Your feelings are
  legitimate. A partner who repeatedly ignores your limits is showing you something
  important.
- **Serious or non-consensual violations** are sexual assault. Confidential support is
  available from specialist services (e.g. RAINN in the US, Rape Crisis in England and
  Wales, 1800RESPECT in Australia).

## Gotchas
- **"You did it before"** is not a reason; consent and limits apply to each time.
- **Boundaries stated only with sighs or hints** are easy to miss — say them in words.
- **Past trauma** can make some limits feel urgent or unexplainable; trauma-informed
  therapists can help partners work with this together.

## Verify
- [ ] You can list your own hard limits, maybes and conditions.
- [ ] Your partner knows them, in words, and you know theirs.
- [ ] Neither of you debated or bargained over a stated limit.
- [ ] You both understand that narrowing a limit applies immediately.
- [ ] Exclusivity and image-sharing agreements are explicit.
