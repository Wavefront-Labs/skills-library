# Raising STI testing and contraception with a partner

**Goal:** have a calm, practical conversation about sexual health and contraception
*before* it's needed, so both partners can make informed choices. Communication
reference — for medical decisions, consult a doctor or sexual-health clinic; testing
guidance changes and varies by country.

## 1. When to raise it
- **Before sexual contact with a new partner** — ideally not in the heat of the
  moment, when it's hardest to think clearly.
- **Before changing protection** (e.g. stopping condoms when you become exclusive —
  see relationships-social.dating-and-courtship.early-relationship-navigation.pacing-and-the-defining-the-relationship-talk).
- **After a possible exposure**, a new diagnosis, or if the agreement about
  exclusivity changes.
- **Periodically** in ongoing relationships, particularly non-monogamous ones.

## 2. Phrasing that makes it normal
Frame it as routine self-care, not suspicion:
> "Before we get more intimate, I like to talk about sexual health. I was last tested
> in [month] and everything was clear. When were you last tested?"
- **Go first** with your own information — it lowers defensiveness.
- **Keep it matter-of-fact.** Testing is ordinary health care, like a dental check-up.
- **Ask about contraception plans** as a joint question: "What are you using, and what
  do you want us to use?"
- **Accept "I've never been tested"** without judgement, and suggest testing together.

## 3. What testing does and doesn't tell you (check local guidance)
- **No single test covers everything.** Standard panels often include chlamydia,
  gonorrhoea, syphilis and HIV; others (herpes, HPV) may not be routinely tested or
  have limited tests. Ask the clinic what's included.
- **Window periods:** a test can miss a recent infection; some infections take weeks
  to show. A clear result reflects status up to some weeks before the test.
- **Many STIs have no symptoms**, so "I feel fine" isn't evidence.
- **Free or low-cost clinics** exist in many countries (e.g. NHS sexual-health clinics
  in the UK); some offer home testing kits.

## 4. Contraception and protection
- **Agree who is using what**, and the backup if something fails (a split condom,
  missed pills). Know where to get **emergency contraception** — some types are
  available without prescription in many countries and work best the sooner they're taken.
- **Condoms** are the main method that reduces both pregnancy and many STIs.
- **PrEP** (pre-exposure prophylaxis) substantially reduces HIV risk for people at
  higher risk; ask a clinician whether it's suitable.
- **Vaccines** exist for HPV and hepatitis B.
- **Contraception is a shared responsibility** even when one person's body carries it.

## 5. Disclosing a diagnosis
- **Before sexual contact**, and privately. Laws on HIV and other STI disclosure vary
  by jurisdiction; in some, non-disclosure can be a criminal offence.
- **Give facts, then space:**
  > "I want to tell you something before we go further. I have [condition]. It's
  > managed — I take [treatment] / I know how to reduce the risk. I'm happy to answer
  > questions, and I understand if you want time to think."
- Many conditions are very manageable; for example, people with HIV who maintain an
  undetectable viral load on treatment do not transmit HIV sexually ("U=U").
- **If you receive a disclosure:** thank them for their honesty, ask questions, and
  take time to decide. Avoid shaming language ("clean/dirty").

## 6. After a new diagnosis
- **Tell recent partners** so they can be tested. Many clinics offer anonymous
  partner-notification services.
- Follow treatment instructions and recommended waiting periods before resuming
  sexual contact.

## Gotchas
- **"Clean" implies others are dirty** — say "tested negative" or "no STIs on my last test".
- **Lying about contraception or removing a condom without agreement** violates
  consent and is a crime in some jurisdictions (see
  relationships-social.sexuality-and-intimate-communication.consent-negotiation.asking-checking-in-and-respecting-a-no).
- **Reluctance to discuss it at all** is useful information about a partner.
- Medical specifics here are general and may be out of date; verify with a clinic.

## Verify
- [ ] The conversation happened before sexual contact, not during.
- [ ] You shared your own testing date and contraception plan first.
- [ ] Both partners know what their last tests did and didn't cover.
- [ ] You've agreed on protection and know where to get emergency contraception.
- [ ] Any disclosure was met with thanks and time, not shame.
