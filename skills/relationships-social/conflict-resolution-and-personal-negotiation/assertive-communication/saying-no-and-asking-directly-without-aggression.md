# Saying no and asking directly, without aggression

**Goal:** say what you want and what you won't do, clearly enough to be understood the first
time, in a way that respects the other person — and hold that position when they push.

## The three styles (and the fourth)
- **Passive:** hinting, going along, then resenting it. "Oh… I guess I could…"
- **Aggressive:** getting your way at their expense. "No. Are you serious? Ask someone else."
- **Passive-aggressive:** agreeing aloud, resisting in action — "forgetting", sulking, sarcasm.
- **Assertive:** direct, honest, calm. "No, I can't this weekend."
Assertiveness is about *clarity and respect for both sides*, not about winning.

## 1. The assertive no
Shape: **short acknowledgement + clear no + (optional) brief reason or alternative.**
- "Thanks for thinking of me — I'm not going to be able to help with the move."
- "I'd rather not lend the car. I could drive you to the station on Friday though."

Rules of thumb:
- **Say "no" or "I won't" early in the sentence.** Burying it under "Ugh, I'd love to, it's just
  that maybe…" invites negotiation.
- **Keep reasons short and true.** Long justifications give the other person points to argue
  with. You don't owe a reason for every no.
- **Don't fake it.** "I'm busy" when you're not becomes a trap when they offer another date.
  "I'm keeping that weekend free" is honest and unanswerable.
- **Buy time if you need it:** "Let me check and get back to you tomorrow." Then actually do.

## 2. The direct request — DESC
From Bower & Bower's *Asserting Yourself*:
1. **Describe** the specific behaviour, neutrally: "The last three times we split a bill, I paid
   and wasn't paid back."
2. **Express** how it affects you: "I end up out of pocket and it's awkward to chase."
3. **Specify** the change you want: "Can you transfer your share the same day?"
4. **Consequences** — the positive result (or, rarely, what you'll do): "Then I'm happy to keep
   booking things."
Ask for *one* concrete, doable thing. "Be more considerate" is not a request anyone can act on.

## 3. Holding ground under pushback
- **Broken record:** calmly repeat the same short line. "I understand. I'm still not able to."
  Don't add new reasons each round — each one is a new thing to debate.
- **Fogging:** agree with whatever is true in their pushback without changing your answer.
  "You're right, I did help last time. I can't this time."
- **Name the pressure:** "I can hear you're disappointed. My answer's still no."
- **Guilt-tripping** ("after everything I've done for you") — acknowledge the relationship,
  not the obligation: "I do appreciate what you've done. This one's still a no."

## 4. Body and voice
Even, medium volume; steady eye contact (adjusted for cultural norm); no nervous laughing or
smiling through a no — it sends a mixed message. Upward inflection at the end makes a
statement sound like a question.

## 5. When to yield
Assertiveness isn't rigidity. Changing your mind because of *new information* or a fair
point is fine; changing it because of pressure or guilt is what you're trying to stop. If
someone's need is genuinely bigger than your preference, a negotiated yes is legitimate (see
`relationships-social.conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation.compromise-seeking.finding-a-deal-you-both-can-keep`).

## Gotchas
- The first few assertive nos often trigger more pushback, especially from people used to
  your yes. That's a sign it's working, not failing.
- Overcorrecting: people who have been passive sometimes swing to aggressive. Aim for calm.
- Some settings — an abusive relationship, a workplace with real power over you — carry risk
  for assertiveness. Safety and strategy come first there; this page assumes rough equals.
- Cultures differ a lot in how direct a no can be. In high-context settings, a softer
  "that will be difficult" may be the clear no. *Note:* this page is written from a broadly
  Anglo/Western-European norm.

## Verify
- [ ] Your no was in the first sentence and your reason (if any) was one line.
- [ ] Your request named one specific behaviour and one specific change.
- [ ] Under pushback you repeated rather than added new justifications.
- [ ] You didn't apologise more than once or laugh through the no.
- [ ] Any change of mind came from new information, not pressure.
