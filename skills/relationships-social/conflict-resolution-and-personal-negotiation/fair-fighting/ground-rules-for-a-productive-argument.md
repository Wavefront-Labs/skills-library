# Ground rules for a productive argument

**Goal:** disagree — sometimes strongly — with someone close to you in a way that ends with
the issue clearer or settled and the relationship intact. Conflict itself is normal and often
healthy; what damages relationships is *how* it is fought.

Best practice is to agree these rules together in a calm moment, not announce them mid-fight.

## Rule 1 — Pick the time and place
- Not when either of you is hungry, exhausted, drinking, rushing out or in front of others
  (especially children, in-laws or friends).
- Ask for the conversation: "Something's bugging me about money — can we talk tonight?"
  Raising it in passing as they walk out the door guarantees a bad start.
- For recurring hot topics, a scheduled weekly check-in lowers the stakes of raising things.

## Rule 2 — One issue at a time
Name the topic up front and stay on it. **Kitchen-sinking** — piling on every past grievance —
makes the problem unsolvable and the other person defensive. If a second issue comes up,
park it: "That's fair, let's do that one separately."

## Rule 3 — Start softly, with I-statements
Gottman's research found that the first few minutes strongly predict how the conversation
goes; a harsh start-up tends to end badly. Use the shape:
> **When** [specific behaviour], **I feel** [emotion], **because** [why it matters]. **Could we** [request]?

"When plans change on the day without a message, I feel unimportant, because I've
rearranged things. Could you text me as soon as you know?" — versus "You're so
inconsiderate." Note the trap: "I feel that you're selfish" is a criticism dressed as an
I-statement.

## Rule 4 — Ban the four horsemen
Gottman's four patterns most associated with relationship breakdown, and their antidotes:

| Pattern | Looks like | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Criticism** | attacking character: "you always / you never" | complain about the behaviour (Rule 3) |
| **Contempt** | sneering, mockery, eye-rolling, name-calling | describe your own needs; build appreciation outside fights |
| **Defensiveness** | counter-attack, innocent victim, "yes, but" | accept some responsibility, even a small part |
| **Stonewalling** | going blank, turning away, silent treatment | take a declared break and come back |

Contempt is the one to treat as non-negotiable — it signals disgust, not disagreement.

## Rule 5 — Other things that are off-limits
- Threats to leave or end the relationship as a debating move.
- Using things told in confidence as ammunition.
- Bringing in third parties' opinions ("even your friends think…").
- Physical intimidation of any kind: blocking doors, throwing things. This is not a fair-fight
  issue but a safety one.

## Rule 6 — Either person may pause
Agree that anyone can call a timeout, with a return time (see
`relationships-social.conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation.de-escalation.cooling-a-personal-argument-before-it-escalates`).

## Rule 7 — End deliberately
Finish with one of: an agreement ("you'll text when plans change; I'll ask rather than
assume"), a partial agreement and a date to continue, or an explicit "we see this
differently and that's OK". Gottman's work suggests many couple conflicts are *perpetual* —
rooted in personality or values — and the goal there is ongoing dialogue, not a final win.

## Gotchas
- Rules used as weapons ("that's not an I-statement!") break the spirit. Model them; don't police them.
- Equal airtime matters; one person monologuing is not a fight, it's a lecture.
- Rules can't fix a power imbalance or abuse. If one person is afraid of the other, this isn't the right tool.

*Uncertainty:* Gottman's findings come from observational studies of (mostly) married couples;
applying them to friends and housemates is reasonable extrapolation, not a tested result.

## Verify
- [ ] You asked for the conversation at a sensible time, privately.
- [ ] Topic named at the start; extra issues parked.
- [ ] Opened with a behaviour-plus-feeling I-statement, not a character judgement.
- [ ] No contempt, threats or confidences used.
- [ ] It ended with an agreement, a continuation date or an accepted difference.
