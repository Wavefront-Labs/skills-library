# Apologising and repairing after a fight

**Goal:** after an argument with a partner, friend or relative, restore goodwill so the
relationship is not quietly worse off — and make it less likely to repeat. Repair is a
separate conversation from the fight itself; have it once both of you are calm (see
`relationships-social.conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation.de-escalation.cooling-a-personal-argument-before-it-escalates`).

## 1. Timing
- **Not while flooded.** An apology through clenched teeth reads as a tactic.
- **Not too late either.** Hours to a day or two is typical for everyday fights; letting it
  go for weeks lets each side write their own story of what happened.
- Ask: "Can we talk about last night? I want to say some things about my part." That framing
  tells them you're not reopening the battle.

## 2. Own your part first — even if theirs feels bigger
Before the conversation, list honestly what *you* did: the tone, the dig, the thing you said
to win, the slammed door. Most fights are two-sided; leading with your share lowers the other
person's defences and makes it easier for them to own theirs. Do **not** make your apology
conditional on theirs.

## 3. The parts of an apology that land
Research on apology structure (Lewicki et al. tested six components) found that
acknowledging responsibility mattered most, followed by an offer of repair. A practical shape:
1. **Name the specific thing:** "I mocked your job in front of your sister."
2. **Acknowledge the impact:** "That was humiliating, and it made it look like I don't respect you."
3. **Take responsibility, no qualifiers:** "That was wrong of me."
4. **Say sorry plainly:** "I'm sorry."
5. **Say what changes:** "If I'm annoyed, I'll raise it with you later, not with an audience."
6. **Offer repair if there is one:** "Do you want me to say something to her?"

Then **stop and listen.** They may need to tell you more about how it felt. Don't defend.

## 4. Non-apologies to avoid
- "I'm sorry **you feel** that way" — apologises for their reaction, not your act.
- "I'm sorry, **but**…" — the *but* erases everything before it. Explanations, if needed,
  come later and only if asked.
- "**If** I upset you" — you know you did; that's why you're here.
- "Mistakes were made" — passive voice ducks the "I".
- Over-apologising, self-flagellation — it shifts the job onto them to reassure you.
- Apologising to end the discomfort while still privately thinking you were right — people
  usually sense it. If you don't believe you did wrong, say what you *can* honestly own.

## 5. Receiving their part and closing
- If they own something too, accept it simply: "Thank you, that means a lot." Don't use it to
  re-litigate.
- Forgiveness may not be instant. "I hear you, I'm still a bit raw" is a legitimate answer;
  don't demand "so are we OK?".
- Close with a reconnecting act — a hug, a meal together, a normal plan. Goodwill is rebuilt
  by ordinary time together, not only by words.

## 6. The follow-through
The "what changes" promise is the part that is tested later. Breaking it turns the next
apology into noise. If the same fight keeps recurring, the repair conversation needs to
address the underlying issue (see
`relationships-social.conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation.compromise-seeking.finding-a-deal-you-both-can-keep`),
not just the latest instance.

*Uncertainty:* the component ranking comes from studies of written apology scenarios, mostly
in organisational settings; transfer to intimate relationships is plausible but less tested.

## Verify
- [ ] You waited until calm but did not let it drift for weeks.
- [ ] You named a specific act and its impact, with no "but", "if" or "you feel".
- [ ] You said what will change and listened without defending.
- [ ] You did not demand instant forgiveness or make your apology conditional on theirs.
- [ ] A week later, you have kept the promise you made.
