# Finding a deal you both can keep

**Goal:** resolve a concrete disagreement with someone close — chores, money, where to spend the
holidays, noise, a shared car — with an agreement that both of you will still be honouring in
a month. This adapts interest-based ("principled") negotiation from *Getting to Yes* to personal
life, where you'll keep living with the other party.

Prerequisite: both of you are calm. If it's still hot, do
`relationships-social.conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation.de-escalation.cooling-a-personal-argument-before-it-escalates` first.

## 1. Separate positions from interests
A **position** is what someone says they want; an **interest** is why.
- Position: "We're spending Christmas with my parents." Interests: my mum is unwell this year;
  tradition matters to me; I don't want to host.
- Position: "We're staying home." Interests: I'm exhausted; long drives with the kids are
  miserable; we always go to yours.

Ask each other "**what's important to you about that?**" until you reach things that can be
satisfied more than one way. Write both lists down. Positions usually conflict; interests often
don't (here: "mum unwell" and "no long drive" may both be met by a short visit, or by her coming to you).

## 2. Generate options before judging them
- Brainstorm at least 4–5 options, including silly ones. **No evaluating during the
  brainstorm** — criticism shuts generation down.
- Look for ways to **expand the pie**: alternate years, split the day, trade (I take the drive,
  you take the cooking), add a resource (pay for a cleaner instead of fighting over cleaning).
- Look for **differences you can trade**: one of you cares about timing, the other about
  cost — give each what they care about most.

## 3. Choose with fair criteria
When interests genuinely clash, settle it by a standard neither of you controls:
proportional to income for shared costs, a rota for chores, alternating for holidays, coin
flip for trivial ties, "what we'd advise friends to do". Criteria turn "my will vs yours" into
"what's fair".

## 4. Know your walk-away (gently)
*Getting to Yes* calls it the **BATNA** — best alternative to a negotiated agreement. In
personal life it's usually "we each do our own thing" or "status quo continues". Knowing it
stops you agreeing to something worse than no deal; it is *not* something to threaten with.

## 5. Make it durable
Agreements fail on vagueness. Before you finish, pin down:
- **Who** does **what**, **by when** / **how often**: "I clean the bathroom every Saturday; you
  do the kitchen nightly."
- **What happens on a miss:** "If one of us can't, we swap and say so by Friday."
- **A review date:** "Let's check in on this in four weeks."
- Write it down — a shared note or a message. Memory of agreements drifts in each person's favour.

Test with: "Is there anything about this you'd quietly not do?" If yes, it's not settled yet.

## Gotchas
- **Splitting the difference too early** (meeting in the middle on positions) often leaves
  both unhappy and misses better options. Interests first.
- **Agreeing to escape the discomfort** produces deals you later sabotage. A slower yes beats a fake one.
- **Values conflicts** (religion, how to raise kids) don't always reduce to tradeable
  interests. Aim for understanding and a working arrangement, not a "win".
- **Power imbalances** — money, housing, fear — distort personal negotiation. If one person
  can't safely say no, this isn't a fair negotiation; a third-party mediator may be needed (out of
  scope here).
- **Score-keeping** ("I gave in last time") turns the relationship into a ledger. Use criteria, not tallies.

*Uncertainty:* *Getting to Yes* is built from business, legal and diplomatic negotiation;
its use in intimate relationships is widely recommended but less directly studied.

## Verify
- [ ] Each person's interests (not just positions) are written down.
- [ ] You generated several options before evaluating any.
- [ ] Contested points were settled by an outside standard, not by who pushed hardest.
- [ ] The agreement says who, what, when, what-if-missed, and a review date.
- [ ] Both answered "no" to "anything you'd quietly not do?".
