# Responding non-defensively when someone confronts you

**Goal:** when someone close tells you you've hurt or let them down, respond in a way that
makes it safe for them to have raised it, gets you the real information, and leads to a fair
outcome — whether that's an apology, a change, or a respectful disagreement.

People who raise a grievance have usually rehearsed it and are braced for a fight. How you
handle the first minute largely decides whether they bring things to you in future or start
keeping them to themselves.

## 1. The first ten seconds
Your body will want to defend: heat, a rush of counter-arguments, "that's not what happened".
- **Breathe out and say a holding line:** "OK. I want to hear this." or "Thanks for telling me."
- **Don't answer the first sentence.** The opening line is often the sharpest; the real issue
  usually comes in the second or third.
- If you're genuinely too stung to listen, ask for a short delay with a time: "I want to take
  this seriously — can we sit down in half an hour?"

## 2. Listen to the end
- Let them finish. Use small prompts: "Go on." "What else?"
- **Reflect back** what you hear, in your own words, before replying: "So when I cancelled on
  Saturday, it felt like the third time this month I put work first, and you felt like an
  afterthought." Ask: "Did I get that right?"
- Ask for specifics if it's vague — curiously, not as a challenge: "Can you give me an example
  so I understand?"

## 3. Acknowledge impact before discussing intent
*Difficult Conversations* separates **intent** (what you meant) from **impact** (what they
experienced). Both are real, but leading with intent ("I didn't mean it like that") sounds
like "your hurt is invalid".
- First: "I can see that really hurt." / "That makes sense, given how it looked."
- Later, if relevant: "I want you to know I wasn't brushing you off — I was panicking about a
  deadline. But I get that's not how it came across."

## 4. Find your contribution
Even if you think their account is partly wrong, ask yourself what *part* is true. There
almost always is one. Own that part specifically (see
`relationships-social.conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation.repair-conversations.apologising-and-repairing-after-a-fight`).
Owning 20% honestly beats defending 100%.

## 5. Then decide what to do — honestly
You don't have to agree to everything on the spot. Options:
- **Agree and change:** "You're right. I'll stop booking things on our evenings."
- **Agree partly:** "I can see the cancelling was a problem. I'm not sure I can do fewer
  late nights in this job — can we work out something together?"
- **Disagree respectfully:** "I hear how it felt. I see that conversation differently — can I
  tell you how it looked from my side?" — only *after* steps 2–3.
- **Take time:** "I want to think about this properly. Can we come back to it on Sunday?"

Thank them at the end. Raising a grievance is a bid to keep the relationship working.

## Defensive moves to catch in yourself
- **Counter-complaint:** "Well, *you* always…" (save it for another conversation).
- **Correcting minor facts** ("it was Friday, not Saturday") while missing the point.
- **Innocent victim:** "I can't do anything right."
- **Minimising:** "You're overreacting." / "It was a joke."
- **Making them comfort you** — breaking down so they end up reassuring you.

## Gotchas
- A grievance delivered with contempt or abuse is a different situation; you can say "I want to
  hear this but not like this" and pause.
- If the same complaint keeps coming back, the problem is the pattern, not the latest instance —
  treat it as a negotiation (see
  `relationships-social.conflict-resolution-and-personal-negotiation.compromise-seeking.finding-a-deal-you-both-can-keep`).
- Defensiveness often comes from shame about who you are, not what you did. Separating "I did a
  thoughtless thing" from "I'm a bad person" makes listening easier.

## Verify
- [ ] Your first response was a holding line, not a rebuttal.
- [ ] You reflected their point back and they confirmed it.
- [ ] You acknowledged impact before mentioning intent.
- [ ] You named a specific part you own.
- [ ] You ended with a clear decision (or a set time to decide) and thanked them.
