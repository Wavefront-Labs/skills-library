# Keeping gift exchange balanced over time

**Goal:** give and receive in a way that feels fair to everyone — not a ledger, but not
lopsided either — so gifts strengthen relationships instead of creating quiet
resentment or obligation.

## 1. The underlying idea: gifts create (light) obligation
Anthropologists since Marcel Mauss have observed that gifts in most societies carry
three implied duties: to **give**, to **receive**, and to **reciprocate**. Different
relationships run on different kinds of reciprocity (terms after Marshall Sahlins):
- **Generalised reciprocity** — close family, partners, best friends: give freely, don't
  count, trust it evens out over years (or doesn't need to).
- **Balanced reciprocity** — friends, neighbours, colleagues, extended family: roughly
  equal exchange within a reasonable time.
- **Mismatches** cause most problems: one person is "counting" while the other thinks
  you're past counting, or one keeps escalating.

## 2. Match value and effort, roughly
- **Mirror the level** the other person sets in balanced relationships: if a friend
  gives a modest birthday gift, a modest gift back is right; a lavish one can feel
  like one-upmanship or embarrass them.
- **Value includes effort, not only money.** A homemade gift, a carefully chosen book,
  or a day of help can match a pricier purchase.
- **Timing:** reciprocity doesn't have to be instant. Their birthday gift is usually
  matched on *your* gift to them at their next occasion, not by buying something back
  tomorrow (which can make the gift feel like a trade).

## 3. Read what a gift obliges
Ask yourself:
- **Is this an exchange relationship?** (Office secret Santa: yes, at the set level.
  A grandparent's gift to a grandchild: no — a thank-you is the return.)
- **Is the gift disproportionate?** An unexpectedly large gift from someone you're not
  close to may signal hopes (romantic, business, favours) you should consider before
  accepting (see
  relationships-social.gift-exchange-and-reciprocity-practice.receiving-gifts.receiving-graciously-and-handling-unwanted-gifts).
- **Is there a cultural rule?** Some communities expect specific return gifts (e.g. a
  return gift for wedding guests, or a set proportion returned on certain occasions);
  ask someone who knows (examples general and unverified).

## 4. Avoid the gift arms race
Escalation happens when each side nudges the value up to avoid seeming stingy.
Warning signs: you dread the occasion, you're spending more than you can afford, or
gifts feel like a competition.
**Call a truce, kindly and early (before shopping season):**
> "I love that we do presents, but money's tight this year and I'd love to keep it
> simple — how about a £20 limit / just the kids / a secret Santa / doing a day out
> together instead?"
Most people are relieved. Frame it around your situation or a shared benefit, not
their spending.

## 5. Unequal means
- When incomes differ a lot, **equal effort, not equal money**, is the fairer standard.
  The wealthier party can keep gifts modest to avoid creating pressure, or explicitly
  say "please don't feel you need to match this."
- The less wealthy party can reciprocate with time, skills, hospitality or
  thoughtfulness (see
  relationships-social.gift-exchange-and-reciprocity-practice.favour-exchange.asking-for-and-repaying-favours).
- **Children's parties and class gifts:** many parent groups set a pooled contribution
  or low limit; supporting that helps families who'd otherwise feel pressured.

## 6. When it's lopsided
- **You're always giving more:** decide whether that's fine (generous people often are,
  and that's okay) or whether you feel resentful. If resentful, quietly adjust your
  level down rather than confronting the other person about gifts.
- **You're always receiving more:** say thanks warmly, reciprocate with effort and
  attention, and consider "please don't spend so much on me — your company is plenty"
  if it makes you uncomfortable.
- **Never keep or quote a ledger aloud** ("I spent £50 on you last year"). It turns a
  gift into a debt and damages the relationship more than the imbalance did.

## Gotchas
- **Partners and close family** usually run on generalised reciprocity; scorekeeping
  there is often a symptom of something else (feeling unappreciated — see
  relationships-social.romantic-relationship-and-intimacy-skill.expressing-appreciation.building-specific-appreciation-habits).
- **Workplace gifts** up the hierarchy (to the boss) or from suppliers have their own
  rules; check policy.
- **Cash gifts** in some cultures are recorded by families precisely so they can be
  reciprocated at an equivalent level later — the ledger there is the norm, not a faux pas.

## Verify
- [ ] You know which relationships are "don't count" and which are "roughly balanced".
- [ ] Your gifts roughly mirror the level the other person sets in balanced relationships.
- [ ] Any escalation has been addressed with a kind, early truce proposal.
- [ ] Where means differ, reciprocity is by effort, not just price.
- [ ] No ledger has been mentioned aloud.
