# Host gifts and returning invitations

**Goal:** be a guest people want back and a friend whose hospitality is returned —
by bringing the right thing, behaving well at someone else's home, thanking promptly
and reciprocating in a form that suits your life.

## 1. The host gift
- **Bring something to most invitations** to someone's home in many cultures: a dinner,
  a party, a first visit. Small and easy is right; it's a gesture, not payment.
- **Safe choices:** a bottle of wine or other drink (only if they drink), good
  chocolates, a local treat, flowers *already in a vase or as a pot plant* (so the host
  doesn't have to hunt for a vase mid-party), a candle, nice coffee or tea.
- **Don't expect it to be served.** A bottle you bring is a gift for the host; they
  may open it or save it. If you want to contribute to the meal, ask beforehand.
- **Check restrictions:** alcohol-free households, dietary or religious rules, allergies.
- **Cultural notes** (illustrative, check locally): in parts of Europe certain flowers
  are associated with funerals; in some cultures gifts are refused once or twice before
  being accepted; in many places arriving empty-handed to a first visit is a faux pas.

## 2. Being a good guest
- **Reply to invitations promptly** and say clearly whether you're coming; mention
  dietary needs when you accept, not on arrival.
- **Arrive on time** (or within the local norm — "fashionably late" for parties in some
  places, exactly on time for dinners in others).
- **Offer to help** — clearing plates, washing up — and accept a no.
- **Don't overstay.** Watch for the host's cues (tidying up, yawning, "well...").

## 3. House guests (staying over)
- **Agree dates upfront**, arrival and departure times, and stick to them. A common
  saying holds that guests, like fish, go off after three days.
- **Bring a slightly more substantial gift**, or plan to treat hosts to a meal out.
- **Keep your space tidy**, strip the bed on the last morning (ask how they prefer it),
  and replace anything you use up.
- **Contribute:** buy some groceries, cook a meal, help with chores.
- **Fit around their routine** — work hours, bedtimes, children.

## 4. Thanking afterwards
- **Dinner or a party:** a message the next day ("Thank you for a lovely evening —
  that lamb was incredible").
- **After staying over:** a more considered thank-you — a handwritten card (a
  "bread-and-butter letter" in older etiquette) or a small gift sent afterwards.
- Mention something specific you enjoyed.

## 5. Returning the invitation
Reciprocity in hospitality is about **the gesture, not an identical event**.
- **Return within a reasonable time** — a few weeks to a few months, depending on the
  relationship. Repeatedly accepting without ever inviting back is noticed.
- **Choose a form you can manage:**
  - a dinner of your own, however simple;
  - a takeaway night, a barbecue or a potluck;
  - taking them out for a meal or drinks;
  - an outing — a picnic, a show, a walk with lunch.
- **Small spaces, tight budgets, or little time** don't excuse you from reciprocating,
  but they do change the form. Many hosts are simply happy to be invited somewhere.
- **For house stays,** offer your own home in return if feasible, or host them when
  they visit your area.

## 6. Handling uneven hosting
- **Some people love to host** and don't want reciprocal dinners; ask, and reciprocate
  in other ways — bringing a course, washing up, treating them out.
- **If you're always the host** and it's starting to feel one-sided, ask friends to
  bring a dish (potluck), suggest meeting at a restaurant, or invite someone else to
  host next time: "Shall we do the next one at yours?"
- **Group circles** (dinner clubs, families) often rotate hosting explicitly; it keeps
  the load fair.
- See relationships-social.gift-exchange-and-reciprocity-practice.reciprocity-norms.keeping-gift-exchange-balanced-over-time
  for the general principles.

## Gotchas
- **Bringing an uninvited guest** (partner, child, friend) without asking is a common
  and real faux pas.
- **Cancelling late** after a host has shopped and cooked needs a sincere apology and
  ideally an offer to make it up.
- **Hosting obligations in families** (who does Christmas) are a frequent source of
  tension; discuss rotation openly.

## Verify
- [ ] You brought an appropriate host gift and checked restrictions.
- [ ] You RSVP'd promptly with dietary needs, and arrived and left within norms.
- [ ] You thanked the host by the next day (more formally after a stay).
- [ ] You've returned the invitation, in some form, within a reasonable time.
- [ ] Any long-running imbalance has been addressed with an alternative form of reciprocity.
