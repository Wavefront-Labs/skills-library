# Co-regulating tantrums and naming feelings

**Goal:** help a child move through a big feeling and, over years, learn to do it
themselves. Young children can't calm down alone yet — they borrow your calm first
(co-regulation) and internalise it later (self-regulation).

## The emotion-coaching steps
Based on John Gottman's model (paraphrased):
1. **Notice** the emotion early, while it's still small.
2. **See it as a chance to connect and teach**, not an inconvenience to shut down.
3. **Listen and validate:** take the feeling seriously.
4. **Help name it:** "You're frustrated." "That sounds disappointing."
5. **Set limits and problem-solve** — *after* the feeling has passed.

The common failure is jumping straight to step 5 while the child is still flooded.

## In the moment: a tantrum playbook
1. **Regulate yourself first.** Slow exhale, unclench your jaw, lower your voice. Your
   nervous system is the thermostat. If you're about to yell, step back for 10 seconds.
2. **Safety.** Move them or the hazard; stop hitting gently and firmly ("I won't let you
   hit"). Nothing else matters until everyone's safe.
3. **Few words, low and slow.** A flooded brain can't process sentences. "I'm here. You're
   so angry." Then quiet.
4. **Stay close** (or at a distance they tolerate). Some children want a hug; some want
   space with you nearby. Offer, don't force.
5. **Don't reason, bargain or punish mid-storm.** Don't give in to the demand that started
   it either — the limit stands; the feeling is allowed.
6. **When it passes**, reconnect: a cuddle, water, "That was a big feeling."
7. **Later (minutes or hours), talk:** name what happened, what they felt, what they
   could try next time ("squeeze your fists", "come and get me", the calm corner).

## Building feelings vocabulary (calm times)
- Name your own feelings aloud: "I'm feeling frustrated with this traffic, so I'm going
  to take some deep breaths."
- Name characters' feelings in books and shows.
- Go beyond mad/sad/happy: disappointed, embarrassed, jealous, worried, left out, proud.
- A feelings chart or "feelings thermometer" helps 3–8-year-olds point when words fail.
Labelling feelings is associated with reduced emotional intensity in research on
"affect labelling" — the popular phrase is "name it to tame it".

## Validation is not agreement
"You're really sad you can't have another biscuit" accepts the *feeling*; it doesn't
change the *answer*. Avoid dismissing ("Stop crying, it's only a biscuit"), which teaches
the feeling is wrong, and avoid giving in, which teaches the feeling is a lever.

## Gotchas
- **Hungry, tired, overstimulated** — HALT-style basics cause most meltdowns. Prevention
  beats coaching.
- **Distinguish tantrum types loosely:** a frustration meltdown needs comfort; a "this
  might get me the thing" protest needs a calm unchanged limit. Both get calm from you.
- **Public tantrums:** your job is your child, not the onlookers. Move somewhere quieter
  if you can.
- **Boys get coached less** on sadness and fear in many families; check yourself.
- Very frequent, long or violent outbursts beyond what's typical for age, or self-harm,
  warrant a GP or child-health consultation.
