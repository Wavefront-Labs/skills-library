# United-front agreements and separated co-parenting

**Goal:** the child experiences two parents who are predictable and not at war —
whether the parents live together or not. Ongoing exposure to parental conflict is one of
the more consistently reported harms in the family-research literature; the
communication between the adults matters more than the household arrangement.

## Part A — Parents in one household: the united front
1. **Agree the big rules in private:** bedtime, screens, sweets, how you respond to
   hitting, what consequences are on the menu. Write a short list.
2. **"Check with your mum/dad" rule:** if one parent already answered, the other doesn't
   overrule. Children quickly learn to ask the softer parent.
3. **Disagree away from the child.** If your partner handles something in a way you
   dislike, back them in the moment (unless it's unsafe) and discuss later: "I wasn't
   comfortable with how bedtime went — can we talk about it?"
4. **Weekly 10-minute check-in** about the kids: what's working, what isn't, what's coming up.
5. A united front doesn't mean identical styles. Children cope with different personalities;
   they struggle with contradictory rules and visible undermining.

## Part B — Separated or divorced co-parenting
### Treat it as a business partnership
The relationship is over; the joint project (the child) continues. Communicate like
respectful colleagues: brief, factual, polite, child-focused.

### Written messages: BIFF
A widely used format (from Bill Eddy's high-conflict work) — **Brief, Informative,
Friendly, Firm**:
> "Hi — Sam has a dentist appointment Thursday 4pm. I can take him; let me know by
> Tuesday if you'd rather. Thanks."
No history, no blame, no sarcasm, no "as usual". If a message makes you angry, wait a
day before replying (unless it's urgent and about the child's safety).

### Tools
- **One shared channel** for logistics: a shared calendar plus either email or a
  co-parenting app (OurFamilyWizard, TalkingParents, AppClose and similar — features and
  pricing vary by country and change; some courts order or accept specific ones).
  Keeping it in writing reduces "you never told me".
- **A written parenting plan** covering schedule, holidays, decision-making, travel,
  communication rules. Legal formalities differ by jurisdiction — see
  law-government's custody/parenting-plan material and get local advice.

### Handovers
- Be on time; keep it short and cheerful. Handover is not the moment to discuss money or grievances.
- Neutral locations (school, a café) reduce friction if doorsteps get tense.
- Pack the child's things with them; send a brief note on meds, sleep, homework.

### Never put the child in the middle
- No messages via the child ("Tell your father he's late with money").
- No questioning them about the other home.
- No criticising the other parent in their hearing — children hear it as criticism of half of themselves.
- Let them love both homes without guilt.

### When cooperation isn't possible
**Parallel parenting:** minimal direct contact, each parent runs their own home, all
communication written and logistics-only. It's a legitimate model for high-conflict
separations.

## Gotchas
- If there's domestic abuse, coercive control or safety concerns, co-parenting advice
  doesn't apply as written — seek specialist support and legal advice; mediation may be
  unsuitable.
- New partners: introduce them slowly and don't hand them discipline duties early.
- Different house rules are fine; contradicting the other home's rules to the child is not.

## Verify
- [ ] Core rules are written and both parents agree on them.
- [ ] Disagreements this month happened away from the child.
- [ ] Every written message passes the BIFF test before sending.
- [ ] Handovers are short and calm.
- [ ] The child has never been used as a messenger.
