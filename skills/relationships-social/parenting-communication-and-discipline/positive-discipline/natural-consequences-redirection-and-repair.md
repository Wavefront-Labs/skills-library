# Natural consequences, redirection and repair

**Goal:** discipline in its original sense — *teaching* — so misbehaviour falls over time
because the child learns what to do instead, not because they fear you.

## The order of tools (cheapest first)
Think of it as a pyramid: most of your effort goes in the wide base.

### 1. Base: attention and specific praise
Children work for attention; if the reliable way to get it is misbehaving, they'll
misbehave. Catch them being good, several times an hour when you're working on a
behaviour, with **labelled praise**: "You waited for your turn — that was patient,"
not "Good boy." Aim for clearly more positive than corrective comments (a common rule of
thumb is around 5:1; treat the number as a guide, not a measured target).

### 2. Prevent
Routines, warnings before transitions, food and sleep in order, tempting objects out of
reach. Most "bad behaviour" in under-fives is predictable from context.

### 3. Redirect early
At the first sign — not after the third warning — steer: "Balls are for outside; inside
we can roll this one." For toddlers, physically move them or the object. Redirection
works best before emotion takes over.

### 4. Ignore the minor, attention-seeking stuff
Whining, mild sulking, silly noises: withdraw attention calmly and **pay it back the
instant the behaviour stops**. Never ignore unsafe behaviour, hurting others, or damage.

### 5. Consequences
- **Natural consequence:** what happens anyway if you don't step in. Forgets coat → is
  cold. Use only when safe and the lesson lands within the child's understanding.
- **Logical consequence:** you arrange it, and it must be **related, respectful,
  reasonable** (a widely used test from the Adlerian/Positive Discipline tradition).
  Draws on the wall → helps scrub it. Throws the toy → toy has a rest on the shelf until
  after lunch. Not related: "No pudding because you hit your sister."
- **Short and soon.** Consequences that last days lose their teaching value; the child
  stops linking them to the act and resents instead.
- **Time-out / time-in:** a brief calm-down break (roughly a minute per year of age is a
  common guideline) can help; some families prefer a "time-in" where the adult stays
  close. Evidence supports brief, calm, non-humiliating time-out; the debate is mostly
  about tone. Pick one and use it consistently.

### 6. Repair
After the child is calm, the step most families skip:
1. Reconnect ("Come here, let's sort this out").
2. Talk about what happened, briefly, with their version first.
3. **Make it right:** apology if genuine, helping fix what was broken, drawing a card for
   the sibling. Forced, sarcastic "SORRY" teaches little; a repair action teaches more.
4. Plan: "Next time you're that angry, what could you do?"

Model it: when *you* yell, apologise to your child the same way.

## Gotchas
- **Consequence as revenge** — if you're angry, wait. A consequence delivered hot
  becomes punishment.
- **Rewards that inflate:** sticker charts work for a specific new habit; fade them once
  it's established, and don't put everything on a chart.
- **Inconsistent carers** undo each other; agree the plan.
- **Natural consequences that are unsafe** (roads, water, medicine) are never allowed to happen.
- Smacking and shaming (public telling-off, name-calling) are excluded by definition.
- If behaviour problems are severe, persistent across settings, or include harm, an
  evidence-based parenting programme (e.g. Triple P, Incredible Years, PCIT — availability
  varies by country) is the next step; ask your GP or school.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name one behaviour you're praising specifically this week.
- [ ] Every consequence you used was related, respectful, reasonable and short.
- [ ] You redirected before warning three times.
- [ ] Each incident ended with a repair conversation and action.
- [ ] Over 2–4 weeks, the target behaviour is less frequent (keep a rough tally).
