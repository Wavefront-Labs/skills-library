# Staying connected and negotiating autonomy with teenagers

**Goal:** keep a relationship good enough that your teen still tells you things, while
handing over control in steps they can handle. In adolescence, what you know about your
teen's life mostly comes from what they **choose to tell you** — research on parental
"monitoring" suggests disclosure, which depends on the relationship, matters more than
surveillance.

## 1. Expect the shift and don't take it personally
Pulling away, privacy, more time with friends, eye-rolling: developmentally normal.
Withdrawal from *you* is expected; withdrawal from *everything* (friends, interests, food,
sleep) is a warning sign worth acting on.

## 2. Connection: be available, not intrusive
- **Side-by-side beats face-to-face.** Car rides, cooking, walking the dog, late-night
  snacks. Teens talk more when they don't have to make eye contact.
- **Be around at their talking times** — often late evening, inconveniently.
- **Listen more than you advise.** Ask "Do you want my take or do you just want to vent?"
- **Show interest in their world** (games, music, friends) without mocking or co-opting it.
- **Keep a ritual** they still enjoy, even a small one.

## 3. Autonomy: earn-and-extend
Frame freedom as a ladder linked to demonstrated responsibility:
- Agree a **starting level** (curfew, phone rules, going out) and **what earns the next
  step** ("Home on time for a month → curfew moves 30 min later").
- **Negotiate together.** Let them propose first. Non-negotiables (safety: no getting in a
  car with a drunk driver) stay non-negotiable, and you say so plainly.
- **Write it down** — a short family agreement avoids "you never said that".
- When trust is broken, step back down the ladder for a defined time, then let them
  climb again. Don't leave them at the bottom indefinitely.

## 4. Hard and risk conversations
Topics: alcohol, drugs, sex and consent, online safety, mental health, driving.
- **Many small conversations** beat one big talk. Use openings: a news story, a TV plot,
  something a friend did.
- **Ask what they think first.** "What do people at school say about vaping?"
- **Give facts and your values**, briefly, then stop.
- **The exit plan:** "If you're ever somewhere you need to leave, text me [agreed code
  word] and I'll come, no questions that night." Honour the "no questions" part or it
  won't be used twice.
- **Stay calm at disclosures.** If you explode when they tell you something, they'll stop
  telling you. Thank them for telling you, then deal with it.

## 5. Conflict
Pick battles: hair, clothes and room tidiness are low-stakes. Safety, respect and school
matter more. When it escalates, call a pause: "Let's both cool off and talk at 8."
Apologise when you're wrong — it's powerful modelling.

## Gotchas
- **Reading their phone secretly** can wreck trust when discovered. If you monitor, say so
  and say why; scale it down as trust grows. (Reasonable people disagree on how much
  monitoring is appropriate; age and specific risk matter.)
- **Lecturing**: if you've talked for more than a minute without them speaking, stop.
- **Comparing with siblings or your own teenage years.**
- **Signs to act on:** persistent low mood, self-harm, talk of suicide, sharp drop in
  functioning, substance problems, signs of exploitation — contact your GP, school
  counsellor or a crisis line; in immediate danger call emergency services.
- Communication with younger children: see
  relationships-social.parenting-communication-and-discipline.age-appropriate-communication.

## Verify
- [ ] You had at least two unhurried side-by-side moments with your teen this week.
- [ ] Current freedoms and how to earn more are written and agreed.
- [ ] An exit-plan code word exists and has been honoured if used.
- [ ] In your last tough conversation they talked more than you did.
- [ ] You know the warning signs that would make you seek professional help.
