# Child-led play and one-on-one time

**Goal:** a child who feels reliably seen and enjoyed by you. That security is built
from many small, ordinary moments, not from big outings.

## 1. Daily "special time" (the core habit)
- **10–15 minutes, every day or most days, one child at a time.** Name it ("our special
  time") so the child can look forward to it.
- **Phone in another room.** Partial attention reads to a child as no attention.
- **The child chooses** the activity, within simple limits (no screens, nothing that
  needs you to be the referee of a competitive game).
- **You follow.** Your job is to join, not to teach, improve or tidy up.

### What to do while playing
Play-based parent programmes (e.g. the "PRIDE" skills taught in Parent-Child Interaction
Therapy) boil down to:
- **Describe** what they're doing like a sportscaster: "You're putting the red block on
  top. Now the tower's really tall."
- **Reflect** what they say: child: "It's a rocket!" you: "It's a rocket."
- **Imitate** their play.
- **Praise specifically:** "You worked hard on that bridge."
- **Show enjoyment** — warmth in your voice and face.

And avoid, for these minutes: questions (they quietly take the lead from the child),
commands, and criticism. This feels strange for the first few sessions; it gets easier.
(PCIT itself is a clinical therapy; using its play skills at home as a bonding habit is
everyday practice, not treatment.)

## 2. Serve and return all day
Babies and children constantly make small bids — a babble, a point, "look at this!" A
"serve" answered with a "return" (eye contact, a word, a smile, naming what they point
at) is the basic unit of attachment-building that developmental researchers describe.
You can't catch every one; aim to catch many, especially when they're upset.

## 3. Rituals that anchor connection
Small, predictable, repeated: a bedtime story and the same goodnight phrase, a special
handshake at school drop-off, Saturday pancakes. Rituals tell a child "this is ours" and
carry you through busy weeks when special time slips.

## 4. Respond to distress first, teach second
When a child is upset, comfort comes before correction. A child who learns that their
distress brings you closer — not that it gets them sent away — develops the security
attachment research associates with better long-term outcomes. Emotional moments are
covered in relationships-social.parenting-communication-and-discipline.emotion-coaching.

## By age (rough)
- **Babies:** face-to-face time, copying their sounds, holding, responding to cries.
- **Toddlers/preschool:** floor play following their lead.
- **School age:** their hobby, a walk, a board game they pick, cooking together.
- **Teens:** side-by-side time (car rides, a shared show, late-night chats on their
  terms) — see relationships-social.parenting-communication-and-discipline.parenting-teens.

## Gotchas
- **Special time is never withdrawn as a punishment.** Its unconditional nature is the point.
- **Siblings:** one-to-one matters even more with several children; rotate, even if each
  gets 10 minutes twice a week.
- **Turning play into a lesson** ("How many blocks is that? What colour?") — save it
  for another time.
- **Guilt spirals:** missing a day is fine. Consistency over weeks matters, not perfection.
- **Repair after rupture:** every parent snaps. Coming back with "I'm sorry I shouted"
  strengthens the bond; attachment is built on rupture *and* repair.
- If a baby or child seems persistently unresponsive to connection, or you feel unable to
  feel close to them (common with postnatal depression), raise it with your GP or health visitor.

## Verify
- [ ] Special time happened at least 4 days this week, phone away, child leading.
- [ ] During it, you mostly described and praised rather than asked or directed.
- [ ] You have at least one daily connection ritual per child.
- [ ] When your child was upset this week, comfort came before correction.
- [ ] Your child asks for or mentions special time — the clearest sign it's landing.
