# Setting and holding limits without power struggles

**Goal:** a child who knows where the edges are, finds them predictable, and stops
testing them constantly — without you shouting, bargaining, or winning by force.

The broad style this follows is what the parenting literature calls *authoritative*:
warm **and** firm. Warmth without limits and limits without warmth both tend to produce
more conflict, not less.

## 1. Decide the limit before the moment
- **Few rules, clearly worded.** Three to five household rules a child can recite beats
  twenty they can't. Phrase them as what *to* do: "Feet on the floor" not "Don't climb."
- **Sort your limits:** *non-negotiable* (safety: car seat, roads, hitting), *firm but
  explainable* (bedtime, screens), *flexible* (which shirt). Only fight for the first two.
- **Agree with the other carer** first; a rule one parent drops is not a rule
  (see relationships-social.parenting-communication-and-discipline.co-parenting-communication).

## 2. State it once, calmly, close up
Get down to eye level, use their name, say the limit in one sentence:
"Ava, the iPad goes off when the timer rings." Avoid questions that aren't questions
("Shall we go to bed now, okay?") — they invite "no".

## 3. Tools that sidestep the contest
- **When–then:** "When your shoes are on, then we go to the park." States the order of
  events; it is not a bribe or a threat.
- **Two acceptable choices:** "Do you want to walk to the car or hop like a frog?" The car
  is not optional; the route is. Only offer choices you'll honour.
- **Warnings for transitions:** "Five more minutes, then bath." A visual timer helps
  younger children. Transitions are where most daily battles start.
- **Acknowledge the feeling, keep the limit:** "You're really cross that playtime's over.
  It's still bath time." Validation and the limit can sit in the same sentence.

## 4. Follow through — every time, without a speech
If the limit isn't met, carry out the stated consequence quietly and immediately: the
iPad goes on the shelf; you calmly carry the child to the car. **No re-negotiation, no
lecture, no anger.** Each time a limit gives way after enough whining, the child learns
whining works — and intermittent giving-in teaches persistence more strongly than
always giving in. (This is the well-known intermittent-reinforcement effect.)

## 5. Expect testing — it fades
When you start holding a limit consistently, protest usually gets *worse* for a few days
before it improves (an "extinction burst"). This is the sign it's working. Hold steady.

## 6. Repair afterwards
Once calm, reconnect: a hug, "That was hard. I love you. The rule's still the rule."
Holding a limit should never cost the relationship.

## Gotchas
- **Empty threats** ("We'll never go to the park again") destroy credibility. Only state
  consequences you will actually carry out today.
- **Too many words.** Explaining for the fifth time *is* negotiating.
- **Tired, hungry, overstimulated children** can't comply well; fix the state first.
- **Limits for teenagers** shift toward negotiated agreements — see
  relationships-social.parenting-communication-and-discipline.parenting-teens.
- Physical punishment is advised against by major paediatric bodies and is illegal in many
  countries; it is not part of this method.
- If limits are routinely met with aggression or meltdowns far beyond peers, ask a GP or
  child-health service for advice rather than escalating firmness.

## Verify
- [ ] You can list your house rules in under 30 seconds, and the other carer agrees with them.
- [ ] Each limit was stated once, in one sentence, with any choices genuine.
- [ ] The consequence happened as stated, without a lecture.
- [ ] You reconnected warmly afterwards.
- [ ] Over two weeks, the number of re-tests on that limit is dropping.
