# Writing empirical economics — structure, robustness, referees

**Goal:** a reader understands your question, answer and why they should believe it
from the first two pages; a referee finds every check they would ask for already there.

## 1. The introduction does most of the work
A widely used five-part structure (often called "the introduction formula"):
1. **Hook** — the question and why it matters (one paragraph, no throat-clearing).
2. **Question** — stated precisely, ideally as one sentence.
3. **Antecedents** — the two or three closest papers and what they leave open (not a survey).
4. **Value-added** — what *you* do: data, design, result. Lead with the **main number**
   ("a 10% rise in X raises Y by 2.3%, about a third of the gap…").
5. **Roadmap** — optional, one sentence; many editors would rather you cut it.
Cochrane's advice: state the central contribution in the first paragraphs; most readers
never get past the introduction.

## 2. Body structure for an empirical paper
1. **Background/institutions** — only what the identification argument needs.
2. **Data** — sources, sample construction, key variables, summary statistics table.
3. **Empirical strategy** — estimating equation, the identifying assumption in words,
   threats and how you address them.
4. **Results** — main table first, then figures; interpret magnitudes in meaningful units
   (elasticities, SD changes, cost per outcome), not just significance.
5. **Robustness** — summarised in the text, details in the appendix.
6. **Mechanisms / heterogeneity** — clearly labelled as more exploratory if not pre-specified.
7. **Conclusion** — short: what we learned, limitations, policy relevance. No new results.

## 3. Tables and figures
- Every table self-contained: title says what is estimated; notes give sample, SE type and
  clustering, FE, controls, and significance codes.
- Report point estimates with SEs (or CIs) and the dependent-variable mean so readers can
  judge magnitude.
- Prefer figures for event studies, RD plots, and heterogeneity (coefficient plots).
- Number of decimals matched to precision (2–3 typically).
- Generated by code — see social-sciences.economics.stata.do-file-pipelines-estimation-and-tables.

## 4. Presenting robustness
- Group checks by the threat they address (alternative controls; sample restrictions;
  alternative estimators; placebo outcomes/timing; inference variants).
- A **specification curve** or one big robustness table can replace dozens of tables.
- Put pre-registered vs exploratory analyses in different sections.
- Don't hide failed checks; explain them.

## 5. Seminar and conference talks
- Slide 1–3: question, answer, why believe it. Results by slide 10 in a 60–90 minute
  seminar; economics audiences interrupt — prepare backup slides for likely identification
  challenges.
- 15-minute conference slot: one main result, one key figure, one robustness check.

## 6. Responding to referees (R&R)
- Response letter: thank briefly, then **every comment quoted** with a numbered
  response stating what changed and where (page/table). Group shared concerns across
  referees in a summary at the top for the editor.
- Do the analysis requested unless it is infeasible; if you disagree, give evidence (often
  a new appendix table), politely.
- Track changes or a diff for the editor; keep tone factual.
- Prioritise the editor's letter — it tells you which comments are binding.

## Gotchas
- Literature review in the introduction that delays the result by three pages.
- "Significant" used to mean "large".
- Causal language for correlational results; match verbs to your design.
- Abstract without the main finding or number.

## Verify
- [ ] Main question, answer and number appear on page 1.
- [ ] Identification assumption stated in words in the strategy section.
- [ ] Every table/figure has complete notes and magnitudes interpreted.
- [ ] Robustness grouped by threat; appendix referenced from the text.
- [ ] Response letter addresses every comment with a location in the revision.
