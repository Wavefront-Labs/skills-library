# Coding fieldnotes and writing thick description

**Goal:** turn hundreds of pages of fieldnotes into an argued ethnographic text where
every claim rests on a vivid, situated scene and the reader can see how you know it.

## 1. Read everything first
Before coding, read the whole corpus straight through as a dataset, not as memories.
Mark surprises, repeated phrases, conflicts, and moments where people corrected you —
these are where local logics show.

## 2. Two passes of coding
1. **Open coding:** line-by-line, many small descriptive and *in vivo* codes (local
   words — "hustling", "respect money"). Do not prune yet. Paper and highlighters or
   NVivo / ATLAS.ti / MAXQDA all work; software helps retrieval, not thinking.
2. **Focused coding:** pick the handful of themes that recur, connect, and answer a
   question you care about. Recode the corpus for those only. Expect to drop most
   open codes — that is normal.

Track code definitions in a codebook as you go; codes drift silently over weeks.

## 3. Analytic memos
Write short memos (a page) whenever a pattern clicks: name it, list 3–5 incidents,
note exceptions and what would contradict it. *Integrative memos* link themes into a
storyline. Most of a first draft can be assembled from memos.

## 4. The excerpt–commentary unit
The core building block of ethnographic prose:
1. **Analytic point** (one or two sentences — what this shows).
2. **Orienting information** (who, where, why the reader should care).
3. **Excerpt** — the fieldnote scene or quote, lightly edited for readability.
4. **Analytic commentary** — interpret the excerpt; point to specific words/actions.
5. **Tie back** to the larger argument.
Gotcha: dumping an excerpt without commentary, or commentary that only paraphrases
it. Commentary must say something the excerpt can't say for itself.

## 5. Thick description
Following Geertz, thick description renders not just behaviour but the layered
meanings that make it intelligible ("a wink versus a twitch"). In practice:
- Sequence and context: what led up to the act, how others responded.
- Participants' own categories and evaluations, quoted.
- The stakes: what could go wrong for them, and what it signals.
Avoid "thin" generalisations ("people value family") with no scene attached.

## 6. Voice, reflexivity and representation
- Write yourself in where your presence shaped the scene; skip navel-gazing elsewhere.
- Present multiple local perspectives, including dissent.
- Anonymise beyond names: composite places, altered identifying details. Say in a
  methods note that you have done so (and that composites are composites).
- Consider sharing drafts with participants where safe — it catches errors and harms.

## 7. Structure
- **Article:** a puzzle up front, short field/methods section, 3–4 excerpt-commentary
  sections building one argument, conclusion that speaks to theory.
- **Monograph:** chapters by theme or by social arena; each chapter a mini-argument.
  An opening vignette is conventional but must connect to the argument.

*Uncertainty:* coding schemes here follow the grounded-theory-inflected Emerson et al.
approach; many anthropologists code far less formally. Choose explicitly and describe it.

## Verify
- [ ] Every analytic claim has at least one situated excerpt behind it.
- [ ] Each excerpt is followed by commentary that adds interpretation.
- [ ] Codebook definitions written down and dated.
- [ ] Counter-examples are discussed, not just the cases that fit.
- [ ] Identifying details altered and the alteration disclosed.
- [ ] Your position in key scenes is visible where it mattered.
