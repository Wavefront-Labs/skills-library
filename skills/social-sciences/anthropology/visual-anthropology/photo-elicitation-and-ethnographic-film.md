# Photo-elicitation and ethnographic film

**Goal:** use images both to *produce* knowledge (prompting talk, sharing authorship)
and to *present* it (film, photo essays), without exploiting the people pictured.

## 1. Photo-elicitation
Inserting a photo into an interview tends to evoke more detail, memory and emotion
than words alone (Harper). Steps:
1. Choose images: yours from the field, archival photos, or the participant's own.
2. Ask open prompts: "What's happening here?", "What's missing from this picture?",
   "Who would take a photo like this?". Avoid naming what you think the image shows.
3. Record the conversation *and* which image was on screen (number images; say the
   number aloud for the audio).
4. Note misreadings — when participants see something different from you, that gap is
   often the finding.
Archival photos work especially well with elders and for contested histories, but
check rights and whether pictured people/descendants should be consulted.

## 2. Participatory photography (photovoice)
Participants photograph their own lives around a prompt ("what makes this
neighbourhood unsafe?"), then discuss in groups. Practical points:
- Train on camera/phone use *and* ethics: asking before photographing others, avoiding
  identifiable faces of non-consenting people and children, risks of photographing
  police or illegal activity.
- Use a selection step: participants choose 2–3 images and caption them (the SHOWeD
  prompts are commonly used — *wording varies across sources*).
- Agree upfront who owns images, where they'll be exhibited, and credit lines.

## 3. Ethnographic filming
- **Observational style:** long takes, minimal intervention, camera at the level of
  action; let events unfold. Shoot sequences (wide, medium, close, cutaways) so they
  can be edited.
- **Sound is half the film** — use a shotgun or lavalier mic; on-camera mics fail in
  noisy settings. Record room tone.
- Log footage daily: clip, time, people, content, consent status. Untagged hours of
  footage are unusable.
- **Shared anthropology** (Rouch): screen rushes back to participants and incorporate
  their responses; this catches harms early and is itself data.
- Know the presence effect: people perform for cameras. Note when it happens; long
  acquaintance reduces it.

## 4. Consent and image ethics
- Separate consents for: being recorded for research, being shown in academic
  presentations, being shown publicly/online. People often say yes to the first only.
- Get model releases for any public distribution; respect withdrawals even late.
- Faces, homes, licence plates and tattoos identify people; blur where needed.
- Some communities restrict images of the dead or of ceremonies — ask.
- Online publication is effectively permanent; think about who could misuse the image.

## 5. Archiving
Keep masters (RAW photos, original video files), export access copies, and attach
metadata (date, place, creator, consent level). Deposit with a repository that
honours access restrictions.

- [ ] Each image/clip has logged consent level (research / presentation / public).
- [ ] Elicitation interviews record which image prompted which talk.
- [ ] Participant-made images have agreed ownership and credit terms.
- [ ] Separate audio capture used for film; footage logged daily.
- [ ] Rushes or selections reviewed with participants before public release.
