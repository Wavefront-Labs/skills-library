# Participant observation and fieldnotes

**Goal:** spend sustained time inside a community's everyday life and come out with
dense, dated, analysable fieldnotes — gathered ethically, with your own influence on
the data written down rather than hidden.

## 1. Before you go
- **Ethics approval first.** University IRB/HREC approval is required before any data
  collection; plan for *process consent* (consent renegotiated over months) rather
  than one signature. Oral consent is often more appropriate where written forms
  signal bureaucracy or danger; justify this in the protocol.
- **Know the local rules:** research visas, national research permits (many countries
  require one), community or tribal review boards. Requirements vary — check per country.
- **Plan data security:** encrypted laptop, pseudonym key stored separately, no
  names in file titles.

## 2. Entering the field
- **Gatekeepers** (elders, managers, NGO staff) open doors but colour how people see
  you. Being introduced by the boss of a workplace can make workers treat you as a
  spy; diversify your entry points early.
- Have a **one-sentence self-explanation** in plain language ("I'm writing a book
  about how people here make a living"). You'll say it hundreds of times.
- Expect weeks of awkwardness. Make yourself useful in low-stakes ways (helping,
  learning a skill); competence in local tasks builds rapport faster than interviews.
- Watch the pull of **key informants**: the people most eager to talk are often
  marginal or have an agenda. Note whose voices you are *not* hearing.

## 3. The daily fieldnote routine
1. **Jottings in the moment** — keywords, quotes, numbers on a small notebook or
   phone. Be open about note-taking where it is accepted; step away to jot where it
   would disrupt.
2. **Full fieldnotes the same day**, ideally within hours. Memory decays fast;
   a 24-hour delay loses most verbatim speech. Budget roughly as long writing as you
   spent observing — often 2–3 hours per evening.
3. Write **concretely**: who, where, sequence of actions, exact words (mark verbatim
   quotes vs paraphrase), sensory detail. Replace "she was angry" with what she did and said.
4. Keep **separate layers**: descriptive notes; analytic memos (emerging ideas,
   labelled e.g. `[OC]` observer comment); a personal diary (your feelings, health,
   conflicts). Mixing them makes later coding harder.
5. Header every entry: date, time, place, people present (pseudonyms), your role.
6. Back up nightly to two locations.

## 4. Positionality and reflexivity
Record how gender, age, race, nationality, class, religion and funding shape what
people show you. Write down mistakes and moments of rejection — they are data about
local norms. Reflexivity is evidence-handling, not confession.

## 5. Ethical gotchas
- People forget you are researching once rapport is good. Remind them, especially
  before sensitive conversations.
- Overheard or "off the record" material: default to excluding it unless re-consented.
- Photos, social media and WhatsApp groups need explicit consent decisions.
- Risk to participants outweighs your data — the AAA principle "do no harm" is
  primary; some politically sensitive findings must be withheld or heavily disguised.
- Protect yourself too: a safety plan, a check-in contact, attention to burnout.

## 6. Leaving the field
Signal departure in advance, honour promises (copies, reports, reciprocity), and
agree how you'll stay in contact. Exit is part of the relationship, not an afterthought.

*Uncertainty:* time norms (e.g. "a year in the field") are disciplinary conventions in
cultural anthropology; applied and organisational ethnography often uses shorter,
repeated visits. Check your committee's expectations.

## Verify
- [ ] Ethics approval and any national/community permits obtained before data collection.
- [ ] Full fieldnotes written the same day, with date/place/people headers.
- [ ] Description, analytic memos and personal diary kept as separate layers.
- [ ] Pseudonym key stored apart from notes; data backed up in two places.
- [ ] You can name whose perspectives are under-represented and why.
- [ ] Exit and follow-up commitments made explicitly.
