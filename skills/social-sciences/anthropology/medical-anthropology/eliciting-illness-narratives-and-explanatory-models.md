# Eliciting illness narratives and explanatory models

**Goal:** understand how people experience, name, explain and seek help for suffering
— and how health systems shape that — without reducing it to biomedical categories.

## 1. Key distinctions to hold in your head
- **Disease** (biomedical malfunction) vs **illness** (the lived experience) vs
  **sickness** (the social role and its recognition). Your interviews target illness
  and sickness; don't correct biomedical "errors" in the moment.
- **Explanatory models (EMs):** the notions about an episode held by everyone
  involved — patient, family, healers, clinicians. They often conflict.

## 2. Kleinman's EM questions (paraphrase, adapt to local idiom)
1. What do you call this problem?
2. What do you think caused it?
3. Why did it start when it did?
4. What does it do to you/your body? How does it work?
5. How severe is it? Will it be short or long?
6. What are you most afraid of about it?
7. What are the chief problems it has caused you?
8. What kind of treatment do you think you should receive? What results do you hope for?
Gotcha: asked as a rigid list these feel like a quiz; weave them into a narrative
interview that starts with "Tell me about when this began." The DSM-5 **Cultural
Formulation Interview** is a structured clinical cousin; useful for clinical settings.

## 3. Narrative interviewing
- Let the story run chronologically before probing. Record turning points, metaphors,
  and moral language ("I did everything right").
- Ask about others: who noticed first, who decided what to do, who paid.
- Re-interview: narratives change as illness evolves — longitudinal visits show this.

## 4. Mapping therapy-seeking pathways
Plot each episode as a timeline: first symptom → home remedies → pharmacist/drug
seller → religious or traditional healer → clinic → hospital (or any order). For each
step record who chose it (Janzen's **therapy-managing group**), cost, distance, and
why it was left. Aggregated pathways reveal the real health system, which rarely
matches the official referral chart. **Medical pluralism** is normal — people combine
systems simultaneously.

## 5. Health systems ethnography
Observe clinics as workplaces: waiting times, triage, stock-outs, informal payments,
staff workloads and paperwork. Interview workers too; frontline staff explanations
of "non-compliance" are themselves data. Link individual suffering to political
economy (**structural violence**) with evidence, not slogans.

## 6. Ethics specific to health
- Never imply you can provide care or influence it; clarify your role, especially
  if you are clinically trained.
- Clinical sites need both university and facility approval, sometimes ministry approval.
- Distress protocols: know where to refer someone in crisis before you interview.
- **Idioms of distress** (e.g. *nervios*, "thinking too much") are not to be
  translated straight into diagnoses.

*Uncertainty:* question wording above paraphrases Kleinman's 1978/1988 set; published
versions vary slightly in number and order.

## Verify
- [ ] Each case records local illness name, cause, and expected course in the person's words.
- [ ] EMs of at least two actors (e.g. patient and healer/clinician) compared.
- [ ] Therapy pathway timeline with decision-makers and costs per step.
- [ ] Clinical/facility approvals and a distress-referral plan in place.
- [ ] No local idiom silently converted into a biomedical diagnosis.
