# Focus groups for research — design, moderation, group-level analysis

**Goal:** use focus groups for what they are good at — how people talk about a topic
together, the language and reasoning they use, where they agree and disagree — not as a
cheap way to interview eight people at once.

## 1. Is a focus group the right tool?
Use groups for: shared norms, vocabulary, reactions to materials, generating survey items,
explaining a survey finding. Avoid them for: sensitive personal histories, topics where
status differences silence people, or anything you will report as a percentage.

## 2. Design
- **Segment** so each group is homogeneous on the characteristic that matters (e.g. users
  vs non-users; managers and staff never together). Compare *across* groups.
- **Group size** 6–8 is typical for research (Krueger suggests 5–8); larger groups
  fragment. Over-recruit by ~20–30% for no-shows (this varies a lot by population).
- **Number of groups:** plan at least 2–3 per segment; stop when new groups in a segment
  add little (saturation at group level).
- **Length** 90–120 minutes in person; online video groups work better shorter (60–90 min)
  and smaller (4–6). Asynchronous text "bulletin board" groups run over days.
- **Screener:** behavioural questions that qualify ("In the last 3 months, did you…"),
  with disguised purpose where knowing it would bias answers; exclude professional
  respondents and people who work in the sector.

## 3. Moderator guide — a funnel
1. **Opening** — round-robin, factual, easy (name and something about their use of X).
2. **Introductory** — "When you think of X, what comes to mind?"
3. **Transition** — link to the focus ("Think back to the last time you…").
4. **Key questions** — 2–5, taking most of the time; open, one-dimensional, "how/what",
   rarely "why" (it sounds like a challenge).
5. **Ending** — summary check ("Did I miss anything?") and an all-things-considered question.
Budget minutes per section and mark which questions are expendable if time runs short.

## 4. Moderating
- Set ground rules: no right answers, disagreement welcome, one at a time, confidentiality
  within the group (you cannot guarantee it — say so in consent).
- **Dominant talker:** break eye contact, "Thanks — let's hear from others", seat them
  beside the moderator. **Quiet participant:** call by name with an easy question.
- **Probes:** "Say more", "Can you give an example?", the 5-second pause.
- Do not express opinions, nod at "good" answers, or correct factual errors mid-discussion
  (note and correct at the end if it matters for welfare).
- Assistant moderator takes notes: speaker order, nonverbal reactions, key quotes with
  time stamps, and runs recording.

## 5. Analysis at group level
- Transcribe with speaker IDs (S1, S2) so you can tell five people agreeing from one person
  repeating themselves.
- Debrief within the hour: top 3 themes, surprises, how this group differed from others.
- Code as in the qualitative coding skill
  (social-sciences.social-research-methods.qualitative-coding-analysis.thematic-analysis-codebooks-and-saturation),
  but keep the **group as the unit**: report themes per segment, extent (how many
  groups, how widely within groups), intensity, and whether views emerged through
  interaction (someone changed their position, consensus formed or collapsed).
- Watch for **conformity**: a view that appeared only after a strong first speaker is weaker
  evidence than one raised independently.

## Gotchas
- Clients or stakeholders watching from behind glass or on video change the moderator's
  behaviour; brief them not to message during the group.
- Showing stimulus materials too early anchors the whole discussion.
- Online groups: test everyone's audio before starting; plan a phone fallback.

## Verify
- [ ] Clear reason why groups (not interviews or a survey) answer the question.
- [ ] Segments defined; ≥2 groups per segment; screener tested.
- [ ] Funnel guide with timings and expendable questions marked.
- [ ] Consent covers recording and the limits of group confidentiality.
- [ ] Analysis reports by segment with speaker-level attribution and interaction notes.
- [ ] No percentages of "participants who said X" presented as prevalence.
