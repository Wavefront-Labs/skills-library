# Running design-based research cycles

**Goal:** develop a learning environment (a unit, tool, or activity structure) *and*
theory about how learning happens in it, through repeated design–enact–analyse cycles
in real settings — while producing claims that survive the classic critique that DBR
"just shows the designers liked their design".

## 1. Before cycle one
- **Problem and theory.** State the learning problem and the theoretical commitments
  you start from (e.g. a learning trajectory for proportional reasoning, a theory of
  argumentation).
- **Conjecture map** (Sandoval 2014). Write out:
  - *High-level conjecture* — how learning should be supported in this setting;
  - *Embodiment* — the concrete design: tools/materials, task structures, participant
    structures, discursive practices;
  - *Mediating processes* — what learners should observably do (e.g. compare
    representations, justify claims);
  - *Outcomes* — what learning results.
  Two families of conjectures follow: **design conjectures** (embodiment → mediating
  processes) and **theoretical conjectures** (mediating processes → outcomes). Each
  needs its own evidence.
- **Partnership.** Co-design with teachers; agree roles, time demands, who can modify
  the design mid-cycle, and authorship/credit. Research–practice partnerships often
  outlast single studies.
- **Ethics.** Classroom consent from school, parents and pupils (assent), with an
  opt-out that doesn't deny instruction (e.g. data simply not collected).

## 2. During a cycle
- **Data aligned to conjectures**: classroom video (whole class + focal groups), student
  artefacts, pre/post assessments, interviews, teacher debriefs, field notes.
- **Daily/weekly debriefs** with the team: what happened, which conjectures held,
  what to change tomorrow. **Log every change with its rationale** — this design log
  is your audit trail and part of the evidence.
- Distinguish minor tweaks within a cycle from redesigns between cycles.

## 3. Between cycles: retrospective analysis
- Systematic pass over the whole dataset (Cobb et al.), not just memorable episodes:
  index video, code for the mediating processes, and look for disconfirming cases.
- Test **both** conjecture types: did the embodiment produce the intended processes?
  Did those processes lead to outcomes? A failure in either tells you what to redesign
  or what theory to revise.
- Revise the conjecture map; plan the next enactment (often a new class, teacher or school
  to test transferability).

## 4. Claims and reporting
- Aim for **"humble theory"**: claims about mechanisms of learning in a specified kind
  of setting, with conditions — not universal efficacy.
- Report the design in enough detail to replicate, the cycles and what changed, the
  analysis process, and the evidence chain for each conjecture.
- Efficacy claims need comparative designs later (see
  social-sciences.education-research.education-policy-evaluation.cluster-randomised-trials-in-schools).

## Gotchas
- **Too much data**: hours of video with no analytic plan. Decide focal groups and
  coding approach per conjecture before filming.
- Designer bias — involve someone outside the design team in coding/inter-rater checks.
- Treating pre/post gains as proof of the mechanism (no counterfactual).
- Teacher burnout and school calendar disruptions; plan buffer time.
- Uncertain: the number of cycles "needed" is a judgement; justify it by conjecture stability.

## Verify
- [ ] Conjecture map with both design and theoretical conjectures written before cycle one.
- [ ] Each conjecture has matched data sources.
- [ ] Design log records every change and why.
- [ ] Retrospective analysis was systematic and sought disconfirming evidence.
- [ ] Claims scoped to conditions; efficacy not over-claimed.
