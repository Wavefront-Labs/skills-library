# Structured classroom observation and lesson video

**Goal:** produce trustworthy data about what happens in lessons — instructional
quality, interaction, pupil engagement — through live observation or video, while
meeting the ethical and practical demands of working inside schools.

## 1. Pick or build the protocol
- **Established protocols** (licensing/training often required — check terms):
  - *CLASS* (Teachstone): emotional support, classroom organisation, instructional support.
  - *Framework for Teaching (Danielson)*: general teaching practice, often used in appraisal.
  - *MQI* (mathematics), *PLATO* (English language arts): subject-specific quality.
  - *ICALT/ISTOF* and others are used in international work.
- **Own protocol**: only when nothing fits. Define each code with examples and
  non-examples, choose the unit (whole lesson rating vs time-sampled segments, e.g.
  every 7.5 or 15 min), and pilot.
- Decide: high-inference ratings (quality judgements) vs low-inference counts
  (questions asked, wait time). Low-inference codes are more reliable but say less.

## 2. Access and ethics in schools
- Layers of permission: authority/district research approval where required,
  headteacher, teacher (who may feel evaluated — say clearly the data are not for
  appraisal), parents (opt-in or opt-out per your ethics board and local law), and
  **pupil assent** in age-appropriate language.
- **Filming**: position cameras to exclude non-consenting pupils (seat them out of
  shot, not out of the lesson); agree on data storage, who views footage, retention
  and use in presentations (separate consent for showing clips publicly).
- Follow safeguarding procedures: DBS/background checks where required, never alone
  with pupils, know the school's designated safeguarding lead and reporting route.
- Data protection (e.g. GDPR): video of children is personal data; encrypted storage,
  access logs.

## 3. Rater training and reliability
- Train with master-coded videos; require **certification** (e.g. ≥ 80% exact/adjacent
  agreement with master codes) before live coding.
- **Double-code** a sample (often 15–25%) throughout, not only at the start; run
  calibration sessions to catch **rater drift**.
- Report agreement appropriate to the scale: Cohen's/weighted kappa or ICC; exact and
  adjacent agreement for rubric scales.
- The MET project and Hill et al. show that rater agreement is not enough: scores vary
  across **lessons** and **raters**, so a single observed lesson is a weak measure of a
  teacher. A **generalizability (G) study** tells you how many lessons and raters you
  need for a target reliability — often several lessons per teacher.

## 4. Filming practicalities
- Two cameras (teacher-facing and class-facing) or one wide + teacher lavalier mic;
  audio is the usual failure — test in the real room.
- Record full lessons; log date, class, topic and anything unusual (supply teacher,
  fire drill).
- Video tools: ELAN, Transana, BORIS or NVivo for time-linked coding.

## 5. Complementary data
Pair observations with teacher interviews (lesson intentions, stimulated recall using
clips), pupil work, and short pupil surveys. Interview pupils in small groups in a
visible space, with a staff member nearby.

## Gotchas
- **Observer effect**: first visits are atypical; plan habituation lessons that you
  don't code.
- Rating lessons on different topics/days of week confounds teacher with context —
  sample systematically.
- Raters who know the study's treatment condition bias ratings; code blind where possible.
- Uncertain: certification thresholds and licensing conditions differ by protocol and change.

## Verify
- [ ] Protocol fits the construct; licence/training obtained if required.
- [ ] All consent layers secured; filming plan excludes non-consenting pupils.
- [ ] Raters certified; ongoing double-coding and drift checks recorded.
- [ ] Number of lessons/raters per teacher justified (ideally a G study).
- [ ] Coders blind to condition where the study is evaluative.
