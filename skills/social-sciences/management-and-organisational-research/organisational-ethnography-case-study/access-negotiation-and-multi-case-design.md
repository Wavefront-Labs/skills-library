# Access negotiation and multi-case design in organisations

**Goal:** get sustained, honest access to organisations and turn it into a case or
ethnographic study whose theory a sceptical management reviewer will believe.

## 1. Choose the logic first
- **Single deep case / ethnography** — revelatory, extreme or longitudinal settings;
  theory comes from depth and process. Process studies (Langley) track events over time.
- **Multi-case (Eisenhardt-style)** — typically 4–10 cases chosen by **theoretical
  sampling** (polar types: high vs low performers, adopters vs non-adopters), analysed
  with **replication logic**: each case tests the emerging propositions.
- **Yin-style** — more deductive; propositions and a case protocol set up front.
Say which tradition you follow; mixing vocabularies (e.g. claiming "saturation" in a
replication design) is a common reviewer complaint.

## 2. Negotiating access
- **Find a sponsor** high enough to open doors (a director, not just HR) and a
  day-to-day contact. Warm introductions via alumni, executive-education contacts or
  industry associations beat cold emails.
- **The pitch (one page):** the problem in managers' language, what you need (people,
  hours, documents, observation days), what they get (a feedback session or short
  report, benchmarking across cases), and confidentiality terms.
- **Written access agreement:** scope, who can be approached, right to withdraw,
  anonymisation, data storage, and — crucially — that the organisation may *comment on
  factual accuracy* of drafts but not veto findings. Get this cleared by your ethics
  board and, if asked, the firm's legal team; an NDA that restricts publication is a red flag.
- **Ask small, then expand:** start with interviews, earn trust, then request meeting
  observation or shadowing.
- Expect delays: reorganisations and sponsor departures kill access. Have backup sites.

## 3. In the field
- **Interview protocol** that evolves; record and transcribe with consent. Interview
  across levels and functions to triangulate; collect documents and archival data.
- **Field notes** the same day: jottings during, full notes within 24 hours, separating
  description from interpretation (a margin or bracket for analytic memos).
- **Reflexivity**: note your role (insider? consultant-like?) and how informants
  position you. Beware being captured by the sponsor's narrative.
- **Case database**: keep a chain of evidence (Yin) — every claim traceable to data.

## 4. Analysis
- **Within-case**: write a case history/timeline first. For process work, use
  temporal bracketing or visual mapping.
- **Coding**: first-order informant codes → second-order themes → aggregate
  dimensions (Gioia data structure), in NVivo, ATLAS.ti or similar.
- **Cross-case**: tables comparing cases on each construct with evidence quotes; look
  for patterns that hold across polar types; iterate between data and literature.
- Report: case selection rationale, data inventory (interviews, hours of observation,
  documents), and representative quotes per theme.

## Gotchas
- **Anonymity is harder than it looks.** Industry + size + country + a notable event
  can identify a firm; disguise non-essential details and tell informants the residual risk.
- Gatekeepers may steer you to "showcase" units; ask explicitly for a contrasting site.
- Interview-only "ethnography" will be challenged; call it an interview study.
- Uncertain: norms on case numbers and data-structure figures vary by journal; check
  recent issues of your target.

## Verify
- [ ] Design tradition (ethnography / Eisenhardt / Yin / process) named and followed.
- [ ] Signed access agreement protects your right to publish and informants' anonymity.
- [ ] Case selection is theoretically justified, not just convenience.
- [ ] Data inventory and chain of evidence available for every key claim.
- [ ] Draft checked for re-identification risk before submission.
