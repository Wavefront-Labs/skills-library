# Contextual interpretation and normative argument

**Goal:** produce a political theory paper whose interpretive claims about texts are
defensible and whose normative claims are argued, not asserted.

## 1. Decide what kind of paper it is
- **Interpretive / history of political thought:** what did Hobbes mean, and why does
  it matter?
- **Conceptual:** what is domination / legitimacy / representation, and which
  conception is best?
- **Normative/applied:** what should we do about X (borders, voting age, AI governance)?
Mixing is common, but each claim type needs its own kind of support. Say which one
the thesis is.

## 2. Reading canonical texts
- **Contextualist (Cambridge School, Skinner):** recover what an author was *doing* in
  writing — whom they answered, what vocabulary was available. Read the pamphlets and
  rivals of the time, not just the canon. Guard against the "mythology of doctrines"
  (crediting authors with views on debates they couldn't have known).
- **Textualist/close reading:** the argument's internal logic, tensions, and structure.
- **Straussian** esoteric reading (hidden meanings under persecution) is contested;
  if used, show textual evidence, not just suspicion.
- Practical: cite standard editions and section/paragraph numbers (Leviathan chapter,
  Second Treatise §), and quote the original language for disputed terms.
- Engage the secondary literature: identify the 2–3 interpretive camps and locate
  yourself.

## 3. Conceptual analysis
- Distinguish the **concept** (shared core) from **conceptions** (rival specifications).
- Test definitions against clear cases and counterexamples; check for
  over- and under-inclusiveness.
- Many political concepts are **essentially contested** (Gallie): disagreement is not
  a sign you defined them wrong. Argue for a conception by what it lets us explain or
  justify.

## 4. Building a normative argument
1. State the thesis precisely, including scope ("in liberal democracies...").
2. Lay out premises; separate empirical premises (cite evidence) from normative ones.
3. Support normative premises via principles, analogies and **considered judgements**,
   adjusting both until they cohere (**reflective equilibrium**).
4. Use thought experiments carefully: specify what they isolate, and check whether
   intuitions change when irrelevant details change.
5. **Objections and replies:** present the strongest opposing view (steelman), reply,
   and concede where needed. Reviewers look for this section first.
6. Clarify **ideal vs non-ideal** status: is this the just arrangement under full
   compliance, or guidance for our actual world? Realists will challenge feasibility —
   pre-empt it.

## 5. Gotchas
- Name-dropping theorists in place of arguments.
- Equivocating on a key term between sections.
- Treating your own intuitions as universal; flag where reasonable people differ.
- Anachronistic praise/blame of historical authors.

## Verify
- [ ] Thesis is one sentence and its type (interpretive/conceptual/normative) is clear.
- [ ] Interpretive claims cite primary text locations and engage rival readings.
- [ ] Key concept defined with tested cases.
- [ ] Empirical and normative premises are separated and each supported.
- [ ] Strongest objection stated and answered.
