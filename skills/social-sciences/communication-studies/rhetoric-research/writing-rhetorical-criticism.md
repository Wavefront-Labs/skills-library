# Writing rhetorical criticism

**Goal:** produce an essay that explains *how* a persuasive artifact works and why
that matters for rhetorical theory — not a summary or a verdict on whether it's good.

## 1. Choose and bound the artifact
Speeches, campaign ads, protest signs, memorials, policy documents, viral threads,
films — anything symbolic intended to influence. Fix the **text**: which version
(as delivered vs prepared text; transcript vs video), date and full source. For
public address, check delivered text against video; prepared texts often differ.

## 2. Situate it
- **Rhetorical situation** (Bitzer): the **exigence** (problem speech can address),
  **audience** (those capable of acting), **constraints** (beliefs, facts, traditions).
  Vatz's counterpoint: rhetors create exigence through framing. Use whichever helps.
- Historical context from primary sources: press coverage, archival drafts,
  audience responses.

## 3. Pick a method that answers your question
- **Neo-Aristotelian:** ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), logos (reasoning),
  arrangement, style, delivery, effects. Systematic but risks a checklist essay.
- **Burkean/dramatistic:** the **pentad** (act, scene, agent, agency, purpose) and
  which **ratio** dominates (scene-act: "circumstances forced us"); **identification**
  and division ("we" vs "they"); terministic screens.
- **Genre criticism:** recurring situational demands produce recurring forms
  (eulogies, inaugurals, apologia). Compare to the genre's conventions and show where
  the artifact conforms or innovates.
- **Ideological criticism:** **ideographs** (McGee: high-order abstractions like
  `<liberty>`, `<equality>` that summon collective commitment), whose interests are
  served, what is silenced; feminist, critical race and postcolonial approaches.
- **Narrative paradigm** (Fisher): narrative probability (coherence) and fidelity
  (rings true with audience experience).
- Methods can be generated inductively from the artifact (Foss's "generative
  criticism") — useful when no existing method fits.

## 4. Argumentation analysis
For argument-heavy texts:
- **Toulmin model:** claim, data/grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal.
  Map key arguments in diagrams; unstated warrants are often where ideology lives.
- **Pragma-dialectics:** reconstruct the discussion as a critical exchange; identify
  strategic manoeuvring and fallacies as rule violations.

## 5. Structure of the essay
1. Introduction with a research question and its stakes for theory.
2. Context/situation.
3. Method and why it fits.
4. Analysis — the bulk, organised by insight, quoting the text closely.
5. Implications: what this case teaches about rhetoric (a concept refined, a genre
   extended).
Gotchas: long description with thin analysis; claiming effects on audiences without
reception evidence (use press/audience responses or limit claims to the text's
*invited* reading); praising or condemning instead of explaining.

## Verify
- [ ] Artifact version and source precisely cited.
- [ ] Situation/context supported with primary sources.
- [ ] One named method (or generative method) applied consistently.
- [ ] Every analytic claim tied to quoted language or specific visual elements.
- [ ] Contribution to rhetorical theory stated in the conclusion.
