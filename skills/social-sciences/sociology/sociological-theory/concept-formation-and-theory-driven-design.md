# Concept formation and theory-driven research design

**Goal:** make theory do work in a study — shaping the question, the concepts and
what counts as evidence — instead of a "theoretical framework" section nobody uses.

## 1. Know what theory is for in your study
- **Explanatory/deductive:** theory predicts a relationship you test (e.g. network
  closure → trust). Needs clear propositions.
- **Interpretive/sensitising:** theory points attention to processes you then explore
  (e.g. Goffman's face-work in an ethnography). Blumer's "sensitising concepts".
- **Theory-building:** you aim to produce new concepts or mechanisms (grounded
  theory, abductive analysis — Timmermans & Tavory).
State which one you are doing; reviewers judge them differently.

## 2. Choose the level and scope
Merton's **middle-range theory** is the practical sweet spot: claims about a class of
phenomena (reference groups, status attainment, weak ties) rather than society as a
whole. Grand frameworks (Parsons, Luhmann, Giddens' structuration) inform but rarely
yield testable propositions directly.

Common families and what they make you look at:
| Tradition | Directs attention to |
|---|---|
| Conflict / Marxian | Interests, exploitation, class relations |
| Weberian | Status, legitimacy, rationalisation, ideal types |
| Durkheimian | Integration, norms, collective representations |
| Bourdieusian | Field, capital (economic, cultural, social), habitus |
| Symbolic interactionism | Meaning-making, situations, identity |
| Rational choice / analytical | Individual action, incentives, mechanisms (Hedström) |
| New institutionalism | Isomorphism, legitimacy, organisational fields |

## 3. Build concepts properly
Use a three-level structure (Goertz, *Social Science Concepts*):
1. **Basic level** — the concept (e.g. "social capital").
2. **Secondary level** — its constitutive dimensions (network size, resources
   accessible, trust).
3. **Indicator level** — observable measures for each dimension.
Decide the logic of aggregation: necessary-and-sufficient (all dimensions required —
AND) vs family resemblance (enough of them — OR / substitutable). This choice changes
how you measure.

Watch for **conceptual stretching** (Sartori): applying a concept to cases where its
defining attributes don't hold. Lower the "ladder of abstraction" (fewer attributes)
if you need broader coverage, and say so.

**Ideal types** (Weber) are deliberate one-sided accentuations used as yardsticks —
cases are compared to them, not expected to match.

## 4. Derive claims or mechanisms
Write the causal story as a mechanism: situational (macro→micro), action-formation
(micro→micro), transformational (micro→macro) — Coleman's "boat". For each link, ask
what you'd observe if it were operating, and what would contradict it. Those are your
hypotheses or your fieldwork prompts.

## 5. Literature synthesis
Organise the review around the debate your study adjudicates, not author by author.
End with the gap: a contradiction, an untested scope condition, a mechanism assumed
but never observed.

## 6. Theory → design alignment
- Concept at the individual level? Don't measure it only at the aggregate (ecological fallacy).
- Process claims need temporal data or process tracing.
- Meaning claims need data that capture actors' own accounts.

## Gotchas
- Name-dropping several incompatible frameworks ("drawing on Bourdieu, Foucault and
  rational choice") signals no framework.
- Reifying concepts: "the system decided" — specify the actors.
- Uncertain: classifications of theorists into schools are simplifications; check
  the specific work you cite.

## Verify
- [ ] Theory's role (test / sensitise / build) stated.
- [ ] Each key concept has dimensions, indicators and an aggregation rule.
- [ ] At least one observable implication per mechanism link.
- [ ] Unit of analysis matches the level of the concept.
- [ ] Literature organised around a debate with a clear gap.
