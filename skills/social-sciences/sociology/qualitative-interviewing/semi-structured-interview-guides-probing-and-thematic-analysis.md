# Semi-structured interviews: guides, probing and thematic analysis

**Goal:** get detailed accounts of experience and meaning from people, and turn hours
of talk into defensible themes rather than a set of nice quotes.

## 1. The guide
- 6–10 **main questions** ordered from easy/descriptive to sensitive/abstract, ending
  with "Is there anything I haven't asked that you think matters?"
- Ask about **concrete episodes**, not generalities: "Tell me about the last time you
  asked your neighbours for help" beats "Do you trust your neighbours?"
- Under each question, list **planned probes** (what you hope to hear about) — used
  only if the participant doesn't raise them.
- Avoid "why" as a first move (it invites justification); "how did that come about?"
  works better. Avoid double questions and loaded wording.
- Pilot with 1–2 people similar to your sample and revise.

## 2. In the interview
- Open with consent (recording, anonymity, right to stop/skip) and warm-up talk.
- Listen more than talk; tolerate silence — a 5-second pause often brings the best material.
- **Probe types:** elaboration ("Can you say more about that?"), clarification ("What
  do you mean by 'proper job'?"), example ("Can you give me an example?"), echo (repeat
  their last words), and contrast ("Was it different before you moved?").
- Don't supply words or agree/disagree with opinions; neutral acknowledgements only.
- Note non-verbal context and your impressions in a memo **right after** each interview.
- Typical length 45–90 minutes. Distress: pause, offer to stop, have support
  information ready.

## 3. Sample size and saturation
Purposive or theoretical sampling to capture variation that matters for the question.
Saturation is a judgement, not a number; in fairly homogeneous samples, most themes
often appear within ~12 interviews (Guest et al. 2006), but heterogeneous or
comparative designs need more. Justify by the analytic aim, not a quota.

## 4. Transcription
- Verbatim for most sociological analysis (include hesitations if analysing talk-as-action;
  conversation analysis needs Jefferson notation).
- Automated transcription (e.g. Whisper-based tools) speeds things up but must be
  checked against audio; consent and data-protection rules may forbid cloud services.
- Pseudonymise as you go: replace names, places, employers with tags like [CITY].

## 5. Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke)
1. **Familiarise** — read all transcripts, note initial ideas.
2. **Code** — label meaningful segments (semantic and latent codes) in NVivo, ATLAS.ti,
   MAXQDA, or on paper.
3. **Generate initial themes** — cluster codes around a central organising concept.
4. **Review themes** against coded extracts and the full dataset.
5. **Define and name** — each theme's scope and story in a sentence or two.
6. **Write up** — analytic narrative with extracts as evidence, not as the analysis itself.
Themes are patterns of shared meaning, not topic summaries ("Experiences of work" is a
topic; "Work as moral proof of worth" is a theme). In reflexive TA, inter-coder kappa is
not expected; in codebook approaches it may be — be consistent with your chosen approach.

## 6. Ethics and data
Store audio encrypted; separate consent forms from transcripts; delete recordings per
your data plan. Check whether consent covers archiving (e.g. UK Data Service) and reuse.

## Gotchas
- Interviews produce **accounts**, shaped by the interview situation — not transparent
  windows onto behaviour. Triangulate if you claim what people do.
- "Themes emerged" hides your role; describe your analytic decisions and positionality.
- Uncertain: saturation benchmarks come from specific studies; don't treat them as rules.

## Verify
- [ ] Guide piloted; questions ask for concrete episodes.
- [ ] Probes non-leading; memos written after each interview.
- [ ] Transcripts checked against audio and pseudonymised.
- [ ] Themes are claims about meaning, each supported across several participants.
- [ ] Consent covers storage, quotation and any archiving.
