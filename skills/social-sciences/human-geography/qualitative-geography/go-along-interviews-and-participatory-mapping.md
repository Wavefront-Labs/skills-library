# Go-along interviews and participatory mapping

**Goal:** understand how people experience, use and value places — in their terms —
and capture it in a form that can be both analysed and mapped.

## 1. Go-along (walking) interviews
Interviewer accompanies participants on routes they actually use (walk to school,
commute, a "tour" of the neighbourhood). Places prompt memories and evaluations that
seated interviews miss.
- **Participant-led routes** reveal everyday geographies; **researcher-set routes**
  allow comparison across participants. Say which you used.
- **Kit:** a clip-on lavalier mic for each person (wind noise ruins handheld
  recordings — use furry windshields), plus a GPS track from a phone app or logger.
  Sync start times so talk can be linked to location.
- Take notes/photos of places mentioned; jot timestamps.
- **Safety and comfort:** traffic, weather, accessibility; offer a seated alternative
  for those who can't walk and consider ride-alongs/bike-alongs.
- **Ethics:** you're visible together in public. Discuss what the participant is
  comfortable with being seen doing; avoid routes that could expose them (e.g. to an
  abusive ex-partner, immigration enforcement).
- Analysis: transcribe with timestamps, join to GPS points (time-matched), then code
  themes and map coded segments — a **qualitative GIS** approach.

## 2. Mental / sketch maps
Ask participants to draw "your neighbourhood" or "the places that matter to you" on
blank paper (or on a minimal base map, which constrains but aids comparison).
- Record the order things are drawn and what's narrated while drawing.
- Analyse with Lynch's elements (paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks) or
  inductively. Note omissions — places people avoid or don't see.
- Drawing ability varies; reassure participants that accuracy isn't the point.

## 3. Participatory mapping workshops
- Groups annotate large printed maps or aerial images with stickers, pens, transparent
  overlays; or digital tools (PPGIS web platforms, QField, Field Papers).
- Structure: icebreaker and orientation to the map → individual marking against
  prompts ("where do you feel safe/unsafe?") → group discussion of patterns → validation.
- Facilitation gotchas: dominant voices; people who can't read maps; elders vs youth;
  gender separating sessions where norms require it.
- Photograph maps immediately at high resolution; digitise with consistent categories.
- **Counter-mapping:** communities mapping to contest official maps (land claims,
  resource rights). Here the community leads; researchers support.

## 4. Data sovereignty and ethics
- Maps of sacred sites, resources or informal settlements can be weaponised (land
  grabs, eviction, looting). Agree what stays community-only.
- For Indigenous communities follow **CARE principles** (Collective benefit, Authority
  to control, Responsibility, Ethics) alongside FAIR.
- Return outputs in usable forms (printed maps, files) to participants.

## 5. Writing place
Combine excerpts, photos and maps; let quotes sit next to the locations they describe.
Avoid treating GPS tracks as "objective" and talk as "subjective" — both are partial.

## Verify
- [ ] Route type (participant-led vs set) stated; audio and GPS time-synced.
- [ ] Safety and visibility risks discussed with each participant.
- [ ] Sketch-map process (order, narration) recorded, not just final drawings.
- [ ] Workshop outputs photographed and digitised with a consistent legend.
- [ ] Community agreement on what geographic data may be published.
