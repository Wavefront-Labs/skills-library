# Demarches and diplomatic notes

**Goal:** convey your government's position to another government accurately, at
the right level and in the right form, and bring back a precise record of the reply.

## 1. The demarche
A demarche is a formal representation — a request, protest, or explanation — usually
on instructions from capital.
1. **Read the instructions closely.** Distinguish what you *must* say from
   background. Note any "do not" lines and whether to leave paper.
2. **Choose the level.** Desk officer, director, deputy minister, minister. Higher is
   louder; protests are often signalled by level (summoning an ambassador to the
   ministry is itself the message). Under VCDR art. 41(2), official business goes
   through the foreign ministry or as agreed with it.
3. **Prepare points to make** in short declaratives, ordered by priority. Add
   anticipated questions and responses ("if asked…" / defensive lines).
4. **Deliver.** Say the key line early and verbatim if instructed. Listen; do not
   negotiate beyond instructions. If pressed, take questions back.
5. **Leave paper if instructed**: a **non-paper** (no letterhead, no attribution,
   lets the host engage without formal commitment) or an **aide-mémoire** (more formal
   summary of points made).
6. **Report the same day**: what was said, by whom, exact phrasing of key replies,
   atmosphere, your comment, recommended follow-up. Separate fact from your analysis.

## 2. The note verbale (third-person note)
The workhorse of diplomatic correspondence — used for requests, notifications,
visa and immunity matters, protests.
Typical structure:
- Opening: "The Embassy of [State A] presents its compliments to the Ministry of
  Foreign Affairs of [State B] and has the honour to…"
- Body: in the third person; numbered paragraphs for complex matters.
- Closing: "The Embassy of [State A] avails itself of this opportunity to renew to the
  Ministry of Foreign Affairs of [State B] the assurances of its highest consideration."
- Place, date, embassy stamp/initials; usually unsigned.
Reference numbers and replying to "Note No. X of [date]" matter for the paper trail.
A **protest note** says so explicitly and may reserve rights ("reserves all its rights
under international law") — omission can be read as acquiescence.

Other forms: first-person **letters/notes** signed by ambassador or minister
(more personal or formal); **exchange of notes** can itself constitute a treaty when
the texts mirror each other and state an intent to be bound.

## 3. Bilateral meetings
Brief the principal with: objectives, three key messages, the counterpart's likely
asks, sensitivities, biography, logistics. Agree a readout line with the other side
if a public statement is expected. Note-taker in every meeting.

## Gotchas
- Wording drift between instruction and delivery is a classic failure — keep the
  key sentence verbatim.
- Something said "in confidence" can still leak; deliver as if it will.
- Uncertain: formula wording and whether notes are signed vary between foreign
  ministries; follow your ministry's style guide.

## Verify
- [ ] Mandatory lines delivered verbatim; nothing promised beyond instructions.
- [ ] Level of delivery chosen deliberately.
- [ ] Paper (non-paper/aide-mémoire/note) left only if instructed.
- [ ] Note uses correct opening/closing formulae and references.
- [ ] Same-day report sent with exact key replies and follow-up recommendation.
