# Running an overseas mission

**Goal:** keep a mission delivering on policy priorities while managing people,
money, buildings and risk in a foreign legal environment, through constant staff turnover.

## 1. Mission strategy
- One short strategy (often multi-year) linking capital's priorities to post
  objectives, with named owners and annual review. Every resource bid should cite it.
- Whole-of-government: other agencies at post (defence, trade, aid, law enforcement)
  have their own lines to capital — the head of mission's coordinating role needs a
  regular country-team meeting and agreed priorities.

## 2. Staffing and rotations
- Diplomats rotate every 2–4 years; institutional memory sits with locally employed
  staff and with written handovers.
- **Handover notes**: key contacts (with relationship history), live issues and
  deadlines, files and drives, local quirks, pending HR/finance matters. Overlap in
  person where budget allows.
- The receiving state may limit mission size (VCDR art. 11) and must be notified of
  arrivals and departures of staff (art. 10).

## 3. Locally employed staff (LES/LE staff)
- Usually employed under **host-country labour law** (contracts, minimum terms,
  social security, termination rules) unless a specific arrangement applies; the
  mission's immunity from suit for employment claims is narrowing in many courts
  (restrictive immunity — e.g. European Court of Human Rights cases on embassy
  employees). Treat compliance as real legal risk.
- Benchmark pay with comparator employers; document classification; have a written
  grievance procedure.
- LES often hold the deepest local knowledge; involve them in planning, and protect
  them in crises — they are vulnerable to host-state pressure.

## 4. Money and assets
Annual budget against strategy; procurement rules (home rules plus host law);
segregation of duties for cash; property and lease management; inventory of
vehicles and equipment; regular audit readiness. Diplomatic tax exemptions apply to
specified items — misuse is a reputational hazard.

## 5. Security and continuity
- Premises are inviolable (art. 22) but the host state has the duty to protect them —
  maintain liaison with host police.
- **Emergency action plan**: tripwires (events that trigger actions), drawdown
  levels (authorised then ordered departure of dependants/non-essential staff),
  destruction of classified material, communications fallbacks, assembly points.
  Exercise it at least yearly.
- Duty officer rota and after-hours consular cover.
- Cyber hygiene and information-handling rules for all staff.

## 6. People and wellbeing
Hardship posts, family issues, isolation: track leave, R&R, and mental-health
support. Misconduct allegations follow home-service procedures; immunity from local
jurisdiction does not remove disciplinary responsibility.

## Gotchas
- Assuming immunity shields LES employment disputes is a common and costly error.
- Plans not exercised fail on the day — run tabletop drills.
- Uncertain: structures, titles and authorities differ between foreign services;
  map them to your own service's rules.

## Verify
- [ ] Current mission strategy with owners and review date.
- [ ] Handover notes exist for every rotating position.
- [ ] LES contracts and procedures checked against host labour law.
- [ ] Budget, inventory and cash controls audit-ready.
- [ ] Emergency action plan exercised in the last 12 months.
