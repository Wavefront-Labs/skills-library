# Recruiting, leading and retaining a volunteer mod team

**Goal:** a community kept healthy by unpaid moderators who agree with each other, don't
burn out, and can be replaced without the place collapsing.

## 1. Rules and norms first
- **Public rules:** 5–10 short, numbered rules with one-line examples. Each one should
  map to a removal reason so users can be told exactly what they broke. Must sit within
  the host platform's terms (Reddit, Discord, Mastodon instance rules, etc.).
- **Mod handbook** (private): how each rule is interpreted, standard responses
  (removal reasons, warnings), the penalty ladder (remove → warn → temp ban → permanent
  ban), what needs a second opinion, how to escalate to the platform (threats,
  doxxing, child safety, self-harm).
- Revisit norms with the community periodically via a **meta thread** or survey;
  people follow rules they had a say in.

## 2. Recruitment
- Recruit from **active, respected members** who already behave like moderators
  (report well, de-escalate). Public application forms work for big communities;
  invitations for small ones.
- Application questions: time zone and availability, why they want it, scenario
  questions ("how would you handle X?"), conflicts of interest (do they run a rival
  community or a business that benefits?).
- Cover gaps: time zones, languages, topic expertise, demographics of the membership.
- **Trial period** (e.g. 2–4 weeks) with limited permissions and a mentor; review
  their actions in the mod log before granting full rights.
- Grant **least privilege**: not everyone needs to change settings, ban permanently or
  manage other mods. Keep 2+ trusted people with top permissions (and 2FA required).

## 3. Running the team
- One private **mod channel** with threads for: discussion of hard cases, announcements,
  rule-change proposals.
- **Mod log** and modmail used for everything — no "I banned them in DMs".
- Automation (AutoModerator, Discord AutoMod, bots) for spam, link filters, new-account
  limits — reviewed regularly for false positives.
- Monthly **calibration**: walk through a few contentious decisions.
- **Recusal** when a mod is in the dispute.
- Back each other publicly; disagree privately; reverse a teammate's decision only after
  talking to them (unless it's harmful).

## 4. Conflict handling
- With users: respond calmly, cite the rule, one explanation then disengage; move
  appeals to modmail, decided by a different mod.
- **Brigading / raids:** temporarily restrict posting (approved users, slow mode,
  account-age filters), lock threads, document and report to the platform.
- Within the team: name the disagreement, decide by the agreed process (head mod or
  vote), document the new interpretation in the handbook.
- **Harassment of moderators** (doxxing, threats): never handle alone; report to the
  platform; offer the mod a break; consider anonymising mod actions.

## 5. Burnout and succession
- Expect turnover; norms that every mod must be online daily drive it.
- Rotation of the worst queues; explicit permission to take breaks; recognition.
- Inactivity policy (e.g. remove permissions after 60–90 days inactive, with thanks).
- **Succession:** at least two people able to run everything; documented settings,
  bots and passwords via a shared vault; a plan if the founder leaves.

## Gotchas
- Moderators recruited during a crisis from loud supporters can entrench factions.
- Private mod chats leak; write as if they'll be screenshotted.
- Platforms may intervene in or replace mod teams; know their policies.

## Verify
- [ ] Public rules map to removal reasons; handbook covers interpretations and escalation.
- [ ] New mods trialled with least privilege and a mentor.
- [ ] All actions logged; appeals decided by a different mod.
- [ ] Raid and harassment playbooks exist.
- [ ] Two or more people can run every function; inactivity policy applied.
