# Settlement: case valuation, offers and releases

**Goal:** settle at a number that beats the client's realistic alternative, on paper that
actually ends the dispute. Most civil cases settle; the craft is valuing the case honestly
and documenting the deal without leaving open doors. Rules on offers and costs vary by
jurisdiction.

## 1. Value the case
Build a simple **decision tree**:
- Probability of winning liability × likely damages range (low / mid / high, with
  probabilities) = expected award.
- Add interest and recoverable costs/fees; subtract the **cost to get there** (fees,
  experts, client time) and time value of money.
- Adjust for **collectability** (insurance limits, defendant solvency) and appeal risk.
- Compare to the client's **BATNA** (best alternative to agreement) — usually trial —
  to set a **walk-away number** before negotiating.
Write down the key uncertainties (a motion pending, a witness's credibility) — valuation
should be updated when they resolve.

## 2. Timing
Settlement value changes at inflection points: after a dispositive-motion ruling, key
depositions, expert reports, before trial costs ramp up. Mediation often works best after
enough discovery that both sides understand the risks.

## 3. Offer strategy
- **Opening:** anchor credibly — aggressive but explainable; an absurd anchor ends talks.
- Give reasons with each move; concessions should shrink as you approach your limit.
- Use **brackets** in mediation to break stalemates ("we'll go to 400 if you go to 600").
- **Fee/cost-shifting offers** change the maths: US Rule 68 offers of judgment (served at least 14 days before trial) make a
  rejecting party who does no better pay the costs incurred after the offer; England & Wales CPR Part 36 offers carry
  costs and enhanced-interest consequences (CPR 36.17). Know the local rules and
  formal requirements exactly.
- Everything is conveyed to the client: the lawyer must communicate offers and the
  decision to settle is the client's.
- Settlement communications are generally inadmissible to prove liability (e.g. FRE 408),
  with exceptions.

## 4. Mediation
Prepare a confidential mediation statement (strengths, weaknesses you acknowledge,
settlement history). Bring decision-makers with authority, and the insurer. Expect to
spend most time in caucus. If you reach a deal, **sign a term sheet before leaving**.

## 5. Drafting the settlement agreement
Checklist:
- **Parties** — including affiliates, insurers, successors, as appropriate.
- **Payment** — amount, timing, method, tax treatment and reporting (allocation between
  wages, damages, fees can matter), consequences of late payment.
- **Release** — scope (general or limited to claims in the action), known and unknown
  claims (check any statutory waiver rules), mutual
  or one-way, carve-outs (obligations under the agreement itself).
- **Dismissal** with prejudice, and who files it, and when (often after payment clears).
- **Liens** — medical, Medicare/Medicaid, attorney liens: identify and resolve.
- Confidentiality, non-disparagement, no-admission clauses — check enforceability limits
  (some laws restrict confidentiality in harassment or public-interest matters).
- **Enforcement:** court retains jurisdiction or a consent judgment; governing law;
  dispute resolution.
- Court approval where required (minors, class actions, some employment claims).

## Gotchas
- A release that doesn't name the right entities lets claims come back via an affiliate.
- Agreeing "material terms" by email can create a binding settlement before the formal
  document — be explicit about whether it's subject to contract.
- Tax and lien issues discovered after signing are the client's surprise.
