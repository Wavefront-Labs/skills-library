# Legal research platforms: case-law search and citator checking

**Goal:** find the controlling law on a question quickly and be certain every authority
you cite is still good law. Applies to Westlaw and Lexis (the two dominant commercial
platforms), Bloomberg Law, and free sources (CourtListener, Google Scholar, BAILII,
CanLII, AustLII). Interface names change — the method doesn't.

## 1. Frame the question
Write: jurisdiction, court level, the legal issue in one sentence, key facts, and the
procedural posture. Jurisdiction narrows everything: a California state claim is governed
by California appellate authority; a federal court applies its own circuit on federal
questions.

## 2. Start with secondary sources
Treatises, practice guides, law reviews, annotated codes, encyclopaedias (Am. Jur.,
C.J.S., Halsbury's). They give the rule, the leading cases and the vocabulary courts use.
Thirty minutes here saves hours of case searching.

## 3. Statutes and regulations
Use the **annotated** code: notes of decisions point to cases interpreting each section.
Check the effective date version that applies to your facts; check for pending amendments
and regulations implementing the statute.

## 4. Searching case law
- **Natural-language search** is good for exploring; **Boolean / terms-and-connectors** for
  precision. Typical syntax (check platform help):
  - `"summary judgment"` phrase; `!` or `*` root expanders (`negligen!`)
  - `/s` same sentence, `/p` same paragraph, `/n` within n words
  - `&` AND, space or `OR`, `%` or `AND NOT` exclusion
  - Example: `"economic loss" /p (rule doctrine) /s (contract! tort!) & da(aft 2015)`
- Filter by jurisdiction, court, date; sort by relevance then check the most recent.
- **Headnotes and topics/key numbers** (Westlaw's West Key Number System; Lexis topics)
  let you jump from one good case to all cases on that point. Headnotes are editorial —
  never cite them; read the opinion.
- **One good case** → its citing references and the authorities it cites (forward and
  backward). This is usually faster than new searches.

## 5. Citator checking (non-negotiable)
Run **KeyCite** (Westlaw) or **Shepard's** (Lexis) on every authority before relying on it:
- Red flag / red stop sign: reversed, overruled, or no longer good law for at least one point.
  Read *why* — it may be on a different point.
- Yellow flag / caution: negative treatment — read it.
- Check depth of treatment and whether courts in your jurisdiction have followed it.
- For statutes: amendments, repeals, constitutional challenges.
Free alternatives exist (CourtListener citation tools, Google Scholar "How cited") but are
less complete — weigh that risk.

## 6. AI-assisted research
Platforms now include generative AI research tools. Treat output as leads: **open and read
every cited source** and run the citator. Courts in multiple jurisdictions have sanctioned
lawyers for citing non-existent cases produced by AI tools, and some courts require
disclosure or certification of AI use — check local orders.

## 7. Keep a research log
Record searches run, sources checked, key authorities with pinpoint cites, and date of
citator check. It makes updates fast and supports billing.

## Gotchas
- Unpublished/non-precedential opinions may have limited citation value — check court rules.
- Pinpoint citations must match the reporter page used.
- Citator results go stale; re-run before filing.
- Cost: commercial platforms charge differently for out-of-plan content — know your firm's plan.

## Verify
- [ ] Jurisdiction and posture defined before searching.
- [ ] At least one secondary source consulted for the rule and leading cases.
- [ ] Every cited authority read in full and citator-checked on the filing date.
- [ ] Research log saved with searches and results.
