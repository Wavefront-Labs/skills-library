# Dispositive motions and brief writing

**Goal:** win (or narrow) the case on paper by filing the right motion under the right
standard, with a brief the judge can grant in one reading. US federal practice is the
model; check the court's local rules and the judge's individual practices every time —
they override habit.

## 1. Pick the motion and know its standard
| Motion | Standard (US federal) | Evidence? |
|---|---|---|
| Dismiss for failure to state a claim (12(b)(6)) | Accept well-pleaded facts as true; is a claim plausible? | Pleadings + documents incorporated or judicially noticeable only |
| Judgment on the pleadings (12(c)) | Same as 12(b)(6), after pleadings close | Pleadings |
| Summary judgment (Rule 56) | No genuine dispute of material fact; entitled to judgment as a matter of law; inferences favour the non-movant | Admissible evidence in the record |
| Preliminary injunction | Likely success, irreparable harm, balance of equities, public interest (*Winter*, 2008) | Declarations, evidence; sometimes a hearing |
| TRO | Similar, plus urgency; without notice only on specific facts showing immediate and irreparable injury and the attorney's certification of notice efforts; expires within 14 days unless extended (Rule 65(b)) | Sworn facts |
Ask: what's the realistic payoff? A partial win that narrows discovery can be worth it;
a weak motion educates the other side and costs credibility.

## 2. Brief structure
1. **Introduction (½–1 page):** what you want and the two or three reasons why. Many
   judges decide their leaning here.
2. **Background:** facts with a pinpoint citation for **every** factual sentence (record
   cite, exhibit and page/line). Unsupported facts are ignored or count against you.
3. **Legal standard:** short, accurate, from controlling authority.
4. **Argument:** headings that are complete sentences stating your conclusion
   ("Plaintiff's fraud claim fails because it identifies no misstatement"). Within each:
   rule → controlling authority → application to these facts → conclusion (CREAC/IRAC).
   Lead with your strongest point.
5. **Conclusion:** the precise relief, matching the proposed order.

## 3. Writing habits that win
- Use **controlling** authority first (your circuit, your state's highest court); flag
  persuasive authority as such.
- Confront adverse controlling authority and distinguish it.
- Quote the record and the rule precisely; paraphrase everything else.
- Short sentences, active voice, defined terms used consistently.
- Check every citation with a citator (see legal-research-platforms skill) — overruled
  authority is an embarrassment and potentially sanctionable. Verify any AI-assisted
  research against the actual sources; courts have sanctioned fabricated citations.

## 4. Procedural mechanics
- **Meet and confer** requirements before many motions; certify compliance.
- Page/word limits, font, formatting, tables of contents/authorities above a length.
- Briefing schedule: response and reply deadlines; whether a hearing must be requested.
- File a **proposed order**.
- Summary judgment: comply with the local statement-of-facts format; on opposition,
  respond to each fact with a record cite or use Rule 56(d) to seek discovery if facts
  are unavailable.

## 5. Reply and oral argument
- Reply only to the opposition's points; new arguments in reply are usually disregarded.
- For argument: a two-minute opening that answers "why should I grant this?", then
  anticipate the judge's three hardest questions. Answer questions directly — yes/no
  first, then explain. Know the record cites by heart.

## Gotchas
- Attaching outside evidence to a 12(b)(6) or 12(c) motion can convert it into a summary judgment motion (Rule 12(d)).
- Filing a dispositive motion after the scheduling-order deadline without leave.
- Burying your best argument on page 18.
