# Discovery: plan, requests, privilege and depositions

**Goal:** get the evidence you need and give what's required — proportionally, on time,
without waiving privilege or getting sanctioned. US federal rules (FRCP 26–37, 45) are
the textbook model; disclosure systems elsewhere are narrower (see end).

## 1. Preservation (day one, both sides)
- Send a **litigation hold** to the client's custodians and IT as soon as litigation is
  reasonably anticipated: identify custodians, data sources (email, chat, phones, shared
  drives, cloud apps), suspend auto-deletion. Re-send periodically and document it.
- Send a preservation letter to the opponent and key third parties.
- Spoliation of ESI can lead to sanctions; the most severe (adverse inference, dismissal)
  require intent to deprive (Rule 37(e)).

## 2. The discovery plan (Rule 26(f) conference)
Agree or fight early on: custodians, date ranges, search methods (terms vs technology-
assisted review), ESI production format, privilege-log format, a **Rule 502(d) order**
(so inadvertent production doesn't waive privilege), protective order for confidential
information, deposition limits, and phasing. Then **initial disclosures** — witnesses,
documents, damages computation, insurance (Rule 26(a)(1)(A)).

## 3. Written discovery
- **Requests for production:** describe categories with reasonable particularity; tie each
  to a claim or defence. Proportionality factors (Rule 26(b)(1)): importance, amount in
  controversy, access, resources, burden vs benefit.
- **Interrogatories:** 25 including subparts by default; use them for facts that are hard
  to get otherwise (identities, contentions, computations).
- **Requests for admission:** narrow the case; unanswered RFAs are deemed admitted.
- **Responses and objections:** must be specific — state whether documents are being
  withheld on the basis of each objection. Boilerplate "overly broad and unduly
  burdensome" objections are routinely rejected. Respond within 30 days unless extended.

## 4. Privilege
- **Attorney-client privilege** (confidential communication for legal advice) and
  **work product** (prepared in anticipation of litigation) — scopes differ by jurisdiction.
- Log withheld documents with enough detail (date, author, recipients, subject matter,
  basis) for the other side to assess the claim, unless a categorical log is agreed.
- In-house counsel communications: test whether the primary purpose was legal advice.

## 5. Depositions
**Taking:** build an outline from documents, by topic; lock down facts with short,
leading questions at the end of each topic; exhaust ("anything else?"); use exhibits
pre-marked. For an organisation, a **30(b)(6)** notice with specific topics requires the designee to testify to information known or
reasonably available to the organisation. Default limit: 10 depositions per side, 7 hours each.
**Defending:** prepare the witness on documents and demeanour (listen, pause, answer only
the question, don't guess); object concisely to form; instruct not to answer only for
privilege, a court-ordered limit, or to present a motion.

## 6. Disputes
Meet and confer in good faith (in writing, then by phone), narrow issues, then a motion to
compel or for protective order. Many judges require a pre-motion letter or conference.
Fee shifting is the default for the loser of a motion to compel unless substantially
justified.

## Gotchas
- Producing without reviewing for privilege — always get the 502(d) order first.
- Deadlines in the scheduling order are hard; extending them needs good cause.
- Third-party subpoenas need notice to the other parties and must avoid undue burden.

## Other systems
England & Wales: standard disclosure under CPR 31.6 in most cases, with a separate regime in the Business and Property
Courts (check its current practice direction); depositions are rare.
Civil-law systems have little party-driven discovery.
