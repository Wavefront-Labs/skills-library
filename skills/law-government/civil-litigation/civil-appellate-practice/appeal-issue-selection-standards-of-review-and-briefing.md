# Civil appeals: issue selection, standards of review and briefing

**Goal:** turn a trial-court loss (or defend a win) into the strongest appellate case by
choosing issues the appellate court can actually reverse on, and presenting them cleanly.
US federal practice (FRAP) is the textbook model; state and foreign rules differ —
especially deadlines and whether permission to appeal is needed.

## 1. Jurisdiction and deadlines first
- **Appealable order?** Generally a **final judgment** disposing of all claims for all
  parties. Exceptions: Rule 54(b) certification, interlocutory appeals by statute
  (injunction orders; §1292(b) certification), collateral-order doctrine.
- **Notice of appeal** deadline is short and in civil cases often jurisdictional (US
  federal: 30 days, 60 if the US, a federal agency or officer is a party, FRAP 4(a)(1)). Certain post-judgment motions toll it — but
  only if timely. Diary from the date of *entry* of judgment.
- England & Wales: permission to appeal is required for appeals from County Court and High Court judges (CPR 52.3(1));
  the appellant's notice is due 21 days after the lower court's decision unless the court directs otherwise (CPR 52.12(2)). Check locally.

## 2. Preservation
An argument not raised and ruled on below is usually forfeited. Review the record for
where each objection, motion or proposed instruction was made. Unpreserved errors get
only **plain-error** review (rarely granted in civil cases).

## 3. Choose issues by standard of review
| Standard | Applies to | Reversal odds |
|---|---|---|
| De novo | Questions of law, summary judgment, contract interpretation (usually), statutory construction | Best |
| Clear error | Bench-trial fact findings | Low |
| Substantial evidence / sufficiency | Jury verdicts | Low |
| Abuse of discretion | Evidence rulings, case management, injunction balancing, sanctions | Low |
Then ask: is any error **harmless**? Errors that didn't affect the outcome won't reverse.
**Pick two or three issues** — the strongest, ideally reviewed de novo. Weak issues dilute
credibility and space. Lead with the strongest unless logic requires otherwise
(e.g. jurisdiction first).

## 4. The record
Designate the transcripts, exhibits and filings needed; prepare the appendix/excerpts per
local circuit rules. Every factual assertion in the brief needs a record citation. You
can't add evidence on appeal.

## 5. Writing the brief
- **Statement of issues:** framed so the answer you want is obvious, with the key fact
  embedded ("Whether a release signed before the injury occurred bars...").
- **Statement of the case:** accurate and fair but told from your perspective; judges
  check the record and punish spin.
- **Summary of argument:** a page the judge could rely on alone.
- **Argument:** state the standard of review for each issue up front. Headings as
  conclusions. Rule → controlling authority → application → why the error mattered.
- **Appellee:** you can defend the judgment on any ground supported by the record;
  emphasise deferential standards and harmlessness.
- Comply exactly with word limits, certificate of compliance, typeface and cover colours
  where required.

## 6. Oral argument
Prepare a 2-minute opening roadmap, then expect questions immediately. Know the record
cold, concede what you must, and answer hypotheticals directly. Have a fallback ("Even
if the court disagrees on X, reversal is required because...").

## 7. After the decision
Rehearing / en banc petitions are for real conflicts or exceptional importance and are
rarely granted; further review (e.g. certiorari) is discretionary and short-deadlined.

## Gotchas
- A premature or late notice of appeal can be fatal.
- Supersedeas bond/stay: the judgment can be enforced during appeal unless stayed.
- Raising new theories on appeal; overloading with issues.
