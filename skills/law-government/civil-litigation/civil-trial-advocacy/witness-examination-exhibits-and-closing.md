# Civil trial: witness examination, exhibits and closing

**Goal:** present a coherent story the fact-finder can adopt, get the evidence admitted,
keep the other side's damaging evidence out, and preserve error for appeal. Evidence
references are to the US Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE); state codes are often similar.
Bench trials are less formal but the same structure applies.

## 1. Case theory and theme
- **Theory:** one paragraph explaining what happened and why your client should win,
  consistent with every undisputed fact. If an undisputed fact doesn't fit, the theory is wrong.
- **Theme:** a short phrase that sums it up ("They chose profit over the promise") used in
  opening, through witnesses, and in closing.
- Work backwards from the **jury instructions**: each element you must prove becomes a
  checklist of witnesses and exhibits.

## 2. Pretrial
Motions in limine to exclude or admit key evidence; exhibit lists and objections;
deposition designations; agreed instructions; witness order and scheduling; trial
technology tested in the actual courtroom.

## 3. Jury selection (voir dire)
Aim to identify and remove people who can't be fair to your case — you're de-selecting.
Ask open questions about experiences and attitudes ("Tell us about any experience with
an insurance claim"), listen, then use cause challenges; save peremptories for those
you can't remove for cause. Peremptory strikes cannot be based on race
(*Batson v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 79 (1986)).

## 4. Opening
Tell the story, not the evidence list: who, what happened, why it matters, what you will
prove. No argument (save it for closing). Don't overpromise — the other side will quote
you in closing.

## 5. Direct examination
- Open, non-leading questions (who, what, where, when, why, describe, explain).
- Headlines to signpost ("Let's talk about the meeting on March 3").
- Draw out weaknesses yourself ("drawing the sting") before cross does.
- Short questions; let the witness be the star; loop key words from the answer into the
  next question.

## 6. Cross-examination
- **Leading questions only**, one fact per question, statements the witness must agree with.
- Don't ask a question you don't know the answer to; don't ask "why".
- Stop when you have the point — save the conclusion for closing.
- **Impeachment** with a prior inconsistent statement: commit (confirm today's testimony),
  credit (establish the prior statement — deposition under oath, date, read and signed),
  confront (read it exactly).

## 7. Exhibits: foundation
Mark → show to opposing counsel → show to witness → establish **relevance** and
**authentication** (witness knows what it is; FRE 901) → address **hearsay** (e.g.
business records via custodian testimony or certification, FRE 803(6); party-opponent
statements aren't hearsay, FRE 801(d)(2)) → offer into evidence → publish. Pre-trial
stipulations to authenticity save hours.

## 8. Objections
Object timely and state the specific ground ("Objection, hearsay"). If overruled on key
evidence, ensure the record is clear. If your evidence is excluded, make an **offer of
proof** so the appellate court can see what was lost. Don't object to everything — juries
notice.

## 9. Closing
Argue: tie the evidence to each instruction element; use the exhibits and the actual
words witnesses said; answer the other side's best point; tell the jury exactly what
verdict form answers and damages figures you're asking for.

## Gotchas
- Forgetting to formally move exhibits into evidence.
- Improper argument (personal opinions on credibility, "golden rule" appeals) can
  cause a mistrial or reversal.
- Failing to renew motions for judgment as a matter of law at the right times (Rule 50: move before the case goes to the jury, renew within 28 days after judgment)
  waives sufficiency arguments.

## Verify
- [ ] Every element of every claim/defence maps to a witness or exhibit.
- [ ] Exhibit foundations are scripted or stipulated.
- [ ] Rule 50 motions and objections to instructions are made on the record.
- [ ] Closing asks for specific verdict answers and amounts.
