# Judicial review: grounds, standing and procedure

**Goal:** decide whether a government decision can be challenged, on what grounds, and
run the claim within strict time and procedural limits. The structure follows the
common-law model as taught in England & Wales (CPR Part 54), with US federal equivalents
(the Administrative Procedure Act, APA). Australia, Canada, India and others share the
grounds but differ on procedure and statute.

## 1. Is there a reviewable decision?
- A **public body** exercising a **public function** (including some private bodies
  performing public functions).
- An actual decision, policy, failure to act or ongoing practice — identify it precisely,
  with date.
- **Alternative remedies:** a statutory appeal or tribunal route usually must be used
  instead (US: exhaustion and finality requirements; APA review is of "final agency action for which there is no other adequate remedy in a court", 5 U.S.C. 704).
- Ousting clauses and non-justiciable subjects (some national security, high policy) limit
  review.

## 2. Grounds (UK classification, *GCHQ* 1985) (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
1. **Illegality** — misdirection in law, acting outside powers, improper purpose,
   fettering discretion, taking into account irrelevant or ignoring relevant considerations,
   unlawful delegation.
2. **Irrationality** — a decision no reasonable decision-maker could reach (*Wednesbury*),
   including flawed reasoning. High bar.
3. **Procedural impropriety** — failure to consult when required, bias, no fair hearing,
   failure to give reasons where required, breach of **legitimate expectation**.
Plus **proportionality** where human rights or EU-retained principles apply, and
human-rights incompatibility under the Human Rights Act 1998.
**US APA §706:** arbitrary and capricious (*State Farm* — the agency must examine relevant
data and articulate a rational connection), contrary to law, in excess of statutory
authority, without required procedure, unsupported by substantial evidence (formal
proceedings). Since *Loper Bright* (2024) courts no longer defer to agency
interpretations of ambiguous statutes under *Chevron*; the "major questions" doctrine
may also apply. This area is moving — check recent decisions.

## 3. Standing
UK: "sufficient interest" — generous for directly affected people and often for
responsible public-interest groups. US: Article III standing (injury in fact,
causation, redressability) plus the statutory "zone of interests". Standing is a frequent
first-line defence in the US.

## 4. Time limits
UK: claim form **promptly and in any event within 3 months** of the grounds arising
(shorter for planning decisions — 6 weeks — and for decisions governed by procurement legislation, where the time limit follows that
legislation; CPR 54.5(5)-(6A)). The limits cannot be extended by agreement between the parties (54.5(2)). "Promptly" can bite earlier.
US APA: generally the six-year federal limitation period (28 U.S.C. 2401(a)) unless a statute sets a shorter
one (e.g. 28 U.S.C. 2344 gives 60 days for the orders it covers). Diary from the decision date, not when you heard of it.

## 5. Procedure (England & Wales)
1. **Pre-action protocol letter** to the defendant, with a reasonable response window
   (normally 14 days under the Pre-Action Protocol for Judicial Review, a default rather than a fixed deadline) — unless urgency prevents it.
2. File claim form with statement of facts and grounds, evidence, and the decision.
3. **Permission stage:** the High Court's leave is required and the applicant needs a sufficient interest in
   the matter (Senior Courts Act 1981 s.31(3)).
4. Substantive hearing on the record — fresh evidence is limited.
5. Costs: take advice on costs risk before issuing.

## 6. Remedies
Quashing order, mandatory order, prohibiting order, declaration, injunction, and damages only where claimed
and available in an ordinary action (Senior Courts Act 1981 s.31(1)-(4)). The High Court must refuse relief (and
leave) where it is highly likely the outcome for the applicant would not have been substantially different, unless
there is an exceptional public interest reason (s.31(2A)-(2C)). US: vacatur and remand, or remand without vacatur;
for the scope of relief see *Trump v. CASA* (decided 27 June 2025).

## Gotchas
- Missing the time limit while negotiating — time keeps running.
- Challenging the wrong decision (the later confirming letter vs the original decision).
- JR reviews lawfulness, not merits: frame grounds as legal errors.
