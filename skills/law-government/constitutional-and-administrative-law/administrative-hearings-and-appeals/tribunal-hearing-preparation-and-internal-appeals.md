# Tribunal hearings and internal appeals

**Goal:** win before an agency or tribunal — benefits, licensing, professional discipline,
tax, employment, immigration — and keep onward appeal routes open. Tribunals are less
formal than courts, but procedure and record still decide most cases. Rules are specific
to each tribunal; read them first.

## 1. Read the governing rules (before anything else)
- The **statute** creating the right or power, the **procedural rules** of the tribunal,
  and the agency's own manuals/guidance (often public and frequently decisive).
- **Deadlines** for requesting reconsideration, appeal and hearing — these vary by tribunal
  and can be strict. Some systems require a first-tier internal review before any
  appeal (e.g. UK "mandatory reconsideration" for many benefits, usually requested within one month of the decision).
- Who bears the **burden of proof** and to what standard (usually balance of probabilities/
  preponderance; some disciplinary schemes differ).
- Evidence rules: tribunals typically admit hearsay and weigh it; formal rules of evidence
  often don't apply.

## 2. Exhaustion strategy
- Most systems require exhausting administrative remedies before going to court; skipping a
  step can forfeit judicial review. Exceptions (futility, inadequate remedy, constitutional
  claims the agency can't decide) are narrow — don't rely on them without research.
- **Issue exhaustion:** raise every argument (including legal and constitutional ones) at the
  administrative level where the rules expect it, so you can rely on it later.
- Request a stay of enforcement pending appeal where available.

## 3. Build the record
- Request the **case file** from the agency early (via the tribunal's disclosure process or an
  access request).
- Gather evidence that maps to each legal criterion: e.g. for a disability benefit, medical
  evidence addressing the specific functional descriptors, not just diagnoses.
- Witness statements in the person's own words, signed and dated.
- Expert or professional letters: ask authors to address the legal test explicitly.
- Submit within the deadline set for evidence; late evidence may be excluded.
- Remember: on later appeal or judicial review, the record is usually closed — what isn't
  in it won't count.

## 4. Written submission
A short skeleton (2–5 pages): the decision under appeal, the legal test, the facts mapped
to each element with page references to the bundle, the outcome sought. Tribunals
appreciate a clear index and paginated bundle.

## 5. The hearing
- Prepare the client: what the panel will ask, honesty about difficulties, how to describe
  bad days. Arrange interpreters and reasonable adjustments in advance.
- Opening: one minute on what you're asking and why.
- Examination: open questions to your witness; respectful, focused questions of the
  agency's witnesses or presenting officer.
- Due-process points: right to be heard, to see the evidence relied on, impartial
  decision-maker, reasons. If something unfair happens, object **on the record** and ask
  for it to be noted.
- Closing: walk through each criterion with the evidence.

## 6. After the decision
Request written reasons if not automatically given (sometimes a precondition to appeal).
Onward appeal is usually limited to **errors of law** (UK: appeal to the Upper Tribunal on a point of law,
Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 s.11(1); US: judicial review under the APA or a specific statute, with
substantial-evidence review in cases subject to 5 U.S.C. 556 and 557).
Calculate the next deadline immediately.

## Gotchas
- Assuming informality means preparation doesn't matter.
- Missing a reconsideration step that is a precondition to appeal.
- Settling on terms the agency lacks power to agree to.
