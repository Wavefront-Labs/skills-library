# Constitutional litigation: rights and structural claims

**Goal:** frame and brief a constitutional claim so the court can rule for you within
its doctrinal framework. US doctrine is the textbook model (with constant change at
the Supreme Court — check the latest terms); proportionality systems (Canada, Germany,
South Africa, the ECHR, UK under the Human Rights Act) are summarised at the end.

## 1. Threshold questions (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
- **State action:** constitutional rights generally bind government, not private
  parties. Identify the governmental actor.
- **Justiciability:** standing, ripeness, mootness, political question. Pre-enforcement
  challenges require a credible threat of enforcement.
- **Vehicle:** §1983 for state officials (see civil-rights-litigation skill), APA or
  equitable action against federal officials, a defence in an enforcement proceeding,
  or a declaratory judgment action.
- **Constitutional avoidance:** courts decide on statutory grounds if they can — plead
  them too.

## 2. Facial vs as-applied (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
- **Facial:** the law is invalid in all (or, for First Amendment overbreadth, a
  substantial number of) applications. Harder to win; broader remedy.
- **As-applied:** the law is unconstitutional applied to this plaintiff/conduct. Usually
  a stronger and narrower path.
Plead both where the record supports it.

## 3. Rights claims: the scrutiny framework (US) (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
| Level | Triggered by (examples) | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Strict | Content-based speech restrictions, racial classifications, fundamental rights | Compelling interest, narrowly tailored / least restrictive means |
| Intermediate | Sex classifications, content-neutral time/place/manner rules, commercial speech | Important interest, substantially related / narrowly tailored, alternative channels |
| Rational basis | Most economic and social legislation | Any conceivable legitimate purpose |
**Second Amendment** uses a text-history-tradition test (*Bruen* 2022, refined in
*Rahimi* 2024) rather than scrutiny — briefs need historical analogues, often with expert
historians. Historical methods are spreading to other areas; expect to argue history.
The fight is often over **which tier applies** — win that and you've often won.

## 4. Structural claims
Separation of powers (removal protections, appointments, non-delegation, major questions),
federalism (commerce power, anti-commandeering, spending-clause coercion, pre-emption).
Ground arguments in text, structure, history and precedent; show practical consequences.

## 5. Interpretive briefing
Courts vary in method (textualism, originalism, purposivism, living constitutionalism).
Write for the bench you have: cover **text**, **original meaning/history**, **precedent**,
and **consequences/structure**, so every judge has a path to your result. Use amicus
briefs to supply history or empirical evidence you can't put in the main brief.

## 6. Building the record
Constitutional cases are often won or lost on facts: evidence of the government's actual
purpose, less restrictive alternatives available, burdens on the plaintiff, historical
practice. Make the factual record at trial level — appellate courts can't add it.

## 7. Remedies
Declaratory judgment, injunctions (scope limited; see *Trump v. CASA*, decided 27 June 2025), severance of the unconstitutional provision (check severability clauses), damages
via other statutes. Consider whether you want narrow or broad relief.

## Proportionality systems (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
Typical steps: legitimate aim → rational connection → minimal impairment/necessity →
proportionality stricto sensu (balance).
UK courts cannot strike down Acts of Parliament; they read legislation compatibly
(HRA s3) or issue a declaration of incompatibility (s4).

## Gotchas
- Notice to the attorney general may be required when challenging a statute's validity
  (e.g. FRCP 5.1: notice to the US or state attorney general, who then has 60 days to intervene).
- Sovereign immunity limits suits against states (use *Ex parte Young* for prospective relief
  against officers). (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
