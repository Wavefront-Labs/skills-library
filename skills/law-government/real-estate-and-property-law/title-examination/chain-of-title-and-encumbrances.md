# Chain of title and encumbrances

**Goal:** confirm the seller can convey what the contract promises, list every right or
burden affecting the land, and cure or price in each defect before the buyer is bound.

## 1. Know which system you're in
- **Registered (Torrens-type) title** (e.g. England and Wales, Australia, much of Canada):
  the register is largely conclusive. Examine the current register (property, proprietorship
  and charges sections or equivalents) and the documents it refers to. Watch for overriding
  interests that bind without appearing on the register (e.g. some short leases, rights of
  people in actual occupation; details vary).
- **Recording system** (most of the US): ownership is proved by the chain of recorded
  instruments. You (or the title insurer) search grantor-grantee or tract indexes back to a
  reliable root, often decades.
- **Unregistered title in a registration jurisdiction:** examine deeds back to a good root of
  title (statutory minimum period).

## 2. Trace the chain (recording/unregistered)
For each link, check: grantor in this deed = grantee in the previous; names match (or
variations are explained); deed was properly executed, acknowledged and recorded; legal
description consistent; marital or spousal rights released where required; estates of dead
owners properly administered; corporate grantors had authority.
**Gaps** (a missing deed, a probate never opened) and **wild deeds** (recorded outside the
chain) are the usual defects.

## 3. List every encumbrance
- **Financial:** mortgages/charges, judgment liens, tax liens, mechanics'/construction
  liens, homeowner association liens. Each must be paid off and released at completion or
  expressly assumed.
- **Use rights:** easements (access, utilities, drainage), profits, rights of way, party wall
  arrangements.
- **Restrictions:** restrictive covenants, conditions, planning agreements, conservation
  easements.
- **Pending matters:** lis pendens, notices of litigation, bankruptcy entries, cautions.
- **Possession:** leases and occupiers' rights. Ask who lives there; inspect.

## 4. Title insurance (where used)
Read the commitment/report: **Schedule A**-type requirements (what must happen to issue the
policy) and **Schedule B**-type exceptions (what the policy will not cover). Push back on
standard exceptions you can remove (survey exception, parties in possession) by providing a
survey or affidavits; ask for endorsements where needed.

## 5. Cure defects
Common cures: obtain and record releases of paid mortgages; corrective deeds for description
or name errors; affidavits of identity or heirship; probate or administration of a dead
owner's estate; boundary agreements; indemnity insurance for low-risk defects (e.g. missing
historic consent); in hard cases, a quiet title or rectification action. Put cures as
conditions in the contract with a deadline.

## Gotchas
- Release recorded for the wrong mortgage or wrong property.
- Assuming a registered title has no unregistered interests: occupiers and short leases.
- Easements benefiting the land (e.g. access to a landlocked plot) are as important as burdens.
- Old restrictive covenants may still be enforceable even if no one remembers who benefits.

## Verify
- [ ] System identified and the right records searched to the right depth.
- [ ] Each link in the chain checked; gaps explained or cured.
- [ ] Encumbrance schedule complete, each marked release / assume / cure / accept.
- [ ] Title insurance exceptions reviewed and removable ones removed.
- [ ] Client told in writing what remains and why it's acceptable.
