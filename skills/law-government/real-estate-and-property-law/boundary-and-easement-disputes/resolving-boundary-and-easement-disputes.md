# Resolving boundary and easement disputes

**Goal:** establish where the line is, or what right exists, on evidence; then resolve it in
a way proportionate to the value at stake. Boundary litigation is notorious for costs that
dwarf the land in dispute.

Rules vary a lot: in England and Wales registered title plans usually show **general
boundaries** only; US deeds carry metes-and-bounds or plat descriptions; adverse possession
periods and requirements range widely by jurisdiction.

## 1. Assemble the evidence in order of weight
1. **Conveyancing documents:** the first conveyance that split the land usually governs;
   later deeds normally repeat it. Read the words before the plan; check whether the plan is
   "for identification only".
2. **Plans and surveys:** a qualified land surveyor's measured survey compared against the
   historic description. Scale off old small-scale plans with caution.
3. **Physical features:** fences, walls, hedges, ditches; their age (historic aerial imagery
   and photographs help); presumptions like the hedge-and-ditch rule in some systems.
4. **Conduct:** who maintained what, for how long; prior agreements or statements.

## 2. Easements
Identify the claimed basis:
- **Express grant/reservation:** read the deed; scope is limited by its words and the
  circumstances at grant.
- **Implied** (necessity, common intention, quasi-easements on division of land).
- **Prescription:** long use "as of right" (without force, secrecy or permission) for the
  statutory or common-law period. Permission defeats prescription, so evidence of
  permission is decisive.
Then the scope: route, width, type of use (foot, vehicle, services), and whether the
dominant land's use has intensified beyond what the easement allows. Obstruction is
actionable only if it substantially interferes.

## 3. Restrictive covenants
Check that the covenant binds the current owner (registered/recorded, touches and concerns
the land) and that the claimant has the benefit. Remedies may include application to a
tribunal to modify or discharge obsolete covenants where available.

## 4. Adverse possession
Elements typically: actual, exclusive, open, continuous possession with intention to possess,
for the period, without permission. Registered land regimes may have special procedures
(e.g. notice to the registered owner who can object). Expect a high evidential bar.

## 5. Resolve proportionately
- Exchange evidence early; jointly instruct a surveyor where possible.
- **Mediation** settles most boundary disputes; courts may penalise refusal in costs.
- Record any settlement as a **boundary agreement** or deed of easement and register it, so
  the next owners don't relitigate.
- Litigation remedies: declaration, quiet title, injunction, damages in lieu, rectification of the register.

## Gotchas
- Relying on a title plan's line as precise when it shows general boundaries.
- Using a disputed access route while negotiating can strengthen or weaken a prescriptive
  claim depending on permission; take advice on what to say in letters.
- Self-help (pulling down a fence) can be trespass or criminal damage.

## Verify
- [ ] Root conveyance and all later descriptions obtained and compared.
- [ ] Measured survey by a qualified surveyor.
- [ ] Easement basis, scope and any permission evidence identified.
- [ ] Costs-versus-value assessment discussed with the client; mediation offered.
- [ ] Any settlement recorded and registered.
