# Negotiating key commercial lease terms

**Goal:** get the economics and the exit right. Most commercial lease disputes come from a
handful of clauses; spend negotiation time on them, not on boilerplate.

Market practice and statutory overlays differ (e.g. statutory renewal rights and the codes
of practice in England and Wales; state retail-lease acts in Australia; largely contractual
regimes in most US states). Check what the statute implies or forbids before drafting.

## 1. Start from heads of terms
Agree a short term sheet first: parties and guarantors, premises (with plan), term, rent and
reviews, rent-free or incentives, service charge, repair, use, alienation, breaks, renewal,
deposit. Mark it "subject to contract". Disputes are cheaper to settle here.

## 2. Rent and rent review
- **Fixed steps, indexation (CPI/RPI with caps and collars), or open-market review.**
- Open-market reviews: the assumptions and disregards decide the number. Check the
  hypothetical term length, whether the hypothetical lease includes the actual rent-free
  period, and that the tenant's improvements are disregarded.
- **Upward-only** reviews are common in some markets and restricted in others.
- Late-review mechanics: is time of the essence? Who can trigger it?

## 3. Outgoings: net vs gross
- **Triple-net / FRI (full repairing and insuring):** tenant bears taxes, insurance and repairs.
- **Service charge / CAM:** check the list of recoverable items, exclusions (capital
  improvements, landlord's financing, void units, structural defects), caps, audit rights
  and reconciliation timing.

## 4. Repair and dilapidations
- Tenant's obligation to "keep in repair" can require putting into repair even if the
  premises were in poor condition at the start. A **schedule of condition** (photos,
  signed) limiting the obligation to that condition is the main protection.
- Clarify responsibility for structure, roof and building systems.
- End-of-term dilapidations claims are often capped by statute or case law at the diminution
  in value of the landlord's reversion (in some jurisdictions); know the rule.

## 5. Alienation (assignment and subletting)
Tenants want flexibility; landlords want covenant strength. Usual compromise: consent not to
be unreasonably withheld, with specified conditions (e.g. guarantee by outgoing tenant).
Statute may impose a reasonableness standard or response deadlines.

## 6. Breaks and renewal
- Break clauses are construed strictly. Conditions such as "vacant possession" or "all rent
  paid" have defeated breaks over trivial sums. Tenants should negotiate conditions down to
  paying the principal rent and giving notice.
- Notices: correct party, correct address, correct method, correct date. Diarise.
- Statutory renewal rights: some regimes grant tenants security of tenure unless properly
  contracted out by a prescribed procedure before the lease is signed.

## 7. Security and guarantees
Rent deposits (held on trust or in a designated account, with top-up obligations) and
guarantors. Watch for guarantees that survive assignment unlimitedly; some statutes limit this.

## Gotchas
- Rent-free periods that inflate a later open-market review.
- Break conditions that make the break unusable in practice.
- Missing the formal contracting-out step where security of tenure exists.
- Use clause too narrow, which depresses rent on review and blocks assignment.

## Verify
- [ ] Heads of terms agreed and reflected clause-by-clause.
- [ ] Review assumptions/disregards checked against the deal.
- [ ] Service charge exclusions and caps listed.
- [ ] Schedule of condition annexed if the tenant needs one.
- [ ] Break conditions minimal; notice provisions clear; renewal position confirmed.
