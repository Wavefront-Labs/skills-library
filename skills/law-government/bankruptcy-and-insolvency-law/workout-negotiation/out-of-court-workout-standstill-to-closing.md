# Out-of-court workout: from standstill to closing

**Goal:** restructure a borrower's debt by agreement, avoiding the cost and value
destruction of formal insolvency, while keeping a court process available as a credible
backstop. The craft is mostly commercial; the law sets the edges. Jurisdiction-neutral craft guidance; the
US look-back periods cited are from 11 U.S.C. §§547-548 (checked 2026-10-24). Not legal advice.

## 1. Triage (week 1)
- **Liquidity runway:** a 13-week cash flow tells you how long you have. If it's weeks,
  plan a formal filing in parallel from day one.
- **Map the capital structure:** every facility, guarantor, collateral package, maturity,
  covenant, cross-default and the **amendment thresholds** (which changes need majority,
  supermajority or each affected lender — "sacred rights" such as pricing, maturity, pro
  rata sharing and release of all collateral).
- **Who holds the debt now?** Distressed paper trades; your counterparties may be funds
  that bought at a discount and have different goals than par lenders.
- Appoint financial advisor and counsel for the company; lender groups organise and
  usually have their advisors' fees paid by the company.

## 2. Standstill / forbearance
A standstill buys time: lenders agree not to accelerate or enforce for a defined period.
Typical terms:
- Defined period, short and extendable; termination events (new defaults, missed
  milestones, insolvency filing, material adverse change).
- **Milestones:** deliver a business plan, appoint a CRO, agree term sheet by date X.
- Information rights: weekly cash flow and variance reports.
- Lender asks: fee, increased margin, additional collateral or guarantees, release of
  claims against lenders. **Check releases and new security for preference/fraudulent-
  transfer risk** if a filing later happens within the look-back period (US: 90 days, or one year for
  insiders, under §547(b); two years under §548(a)(1)).
- Reservation of rights — no waiver of existing defaults.

## 3. Designing the deal
Tools, roughly from least to most invasive:
1. Amend-and-extend (push maturities, reset covenants, raise pricing).
2. New money from existing lenders or sponsor, often with priority.
3. Asset sales with proceeds applied to debt.
4. Partial write-down or discounted buyback.
5. **Debt-for-equity swap:** lenders take ownership; requires valuation, governance
   documents, shareholder approvals, and attention to tax (cancellation-of-debt income
   rules vary) and change-of-control triggers in other contracts.
- **Liability management transactions** (drop-downs, uptiers, double-dips) exploit
  documentation flexibility and have produced heavy litigation. Treat as high risk; check current case law in the governing jurisdiction.

## 4. Getting to yes
- **Holdouts:** a dissenter below a unanimity threshold can block. Levers: exit consents
  that strip covenants from bonds left behind (constrained in some jurisdictions),
  majority-amendment provisions, a pre-packaged court process to bind dissenters
  (for example a US Chapter 11 prepack).
- **Information walls:** lenders receiving material non-public information must be
  restricted from trading or "cleansed" by public disclosure at the end of the period.
- Lock in agreement with a restructuring support agreement (RSA) that commits signatories
  to support the deal, including through a court process.

## 5. Closing
Closing checklist: amended credit agreements, intercreditor changes, new security,
board and shareholder approvals, regulatory consents, tax opinions, releases, and press
or market disclosure where securities are listed.

## Gotchas
- Directors' duties may shift toward creditors as insolvency nears (the "zone of insolvency" analysis differs significantly between jurisdictions).
- Continuing to trade while insolvent can create personal liability for directors in
  some systems; take local advice.
- A standstill that lapses silently can leave lenders free to enforce. Diary every date.
