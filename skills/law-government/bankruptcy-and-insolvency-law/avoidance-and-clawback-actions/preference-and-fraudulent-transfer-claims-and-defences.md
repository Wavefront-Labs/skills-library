# Preference and fraudulent-transfer claims and defences

**Goal:** recover value moved out of a debtor before insolvency — or defend a recipient
— by testing each transfer against the elements and defences. Jurisdiction: United States federal (11 U.S.C.), checked current on 2026-10-24; UK
Insolvency Act 1986 equivalents are noted. Periods and thresholds change; confirm current
figures. Reference content, not legal advice; a qualified bankruptcy lawyer should review any real case.

## 1. Preferences (US §547)
The trustee must show a transfer of the debtor's property:
1. to or for the benefit of a creditor,
2. on account of an **antecedent debt**,
3. made while the debtor was **insolvent** (presumed in the 90 days before filing),
4. within **90 days** before filing (**one year** for insiders),
5. that lets the creditor receive more than it would in a liquidation.
Fully secured creditors usually fail element 5 — check perfection and collateral value.

**Defences** (the recipient's burden):
- **Contemporaneous exchange for new value** — e.g. cash on delivery.
- **Ordinary course of business** — ordinary between these parties (compare payment
  timing and amounts against the pre-preference history) **or** ordinary in the industry.
  Build a payment-days analysis: average days-to-pay in the historical period vs the
  preference period. Sudden pressure, new collection tactics or changed terms defeat it.
- **Subsequent new value** — goods or services supplied after a payment and left unpaid
  offset that payment. Run the calculation transaction by transaction.
- Small-amount floors (§547(c)(8)-(9)): transfers under $600 in a primarily consumer-debt case; $5,000 in other cases
  as written in the statute, indexed every three years (the Code's adjustment note shows $8,575 from 1 April 2025).
- Also: the trustee may avoid a transfer only "based on reasonable due diligence in the circumstances of the
  case and taking into account a party's known or reasonably knowable affirmative defenses" (added by
  Pub. L. 116-54, 2019) — use it in early correspondence.

## 2. Fraudulent transfers (US §548 and state law)
**Actual fraud:** transfer made with intent to hinder, delay or defraud creditors.
Proved circumstantially by **badges of fraud**: transfer to an insider, debtor retained
control, concealment, threatened suit beforehand, substantially all assets moved,
debtor absconded, inadequate consideration, insolvency soon after.
**Constructive fraud:** debtor received **less than reasonably equivalent value** *and*
was insolvent, undercapitalised, or unable to pay debts as they matured. No intent needed.
- Look-back: two years before filing under §548(a)(1); trustees can also use state law (UVTA/UFTA
  enactments, with periods set by each state) via §544(b).
- Common targets: leveraged buyouts, upstream guarantees, dividends while insolvent,
  intra-group transfers, gifts to family, below-market sales to insiders.
- **Defences:** a transferee who takes for value and in good faith has a lien on or may retain the
  interest transferred to the extent of the value given (§548(c)); safe harbours for certain securities,
  swap and settlement payments (§548(d)(2)(B) and related provisions; scope heavily litigated — check
  current law); charitable contributions up to 15% of gross annual income, or consistent with the
  debtor's giving pattern (§548(a)(2)).

## 3. Working the file
1. Pull 90-day and one-year payment registers; flag insiders.
2. Build the ordinary-course and new-value analysis before any demand or response.
3. Solvency evidence: balance-sheet test, cash-flow test, capital adequacy; expect
   dueling expert valuations and hindsight arguments.
4. Most preference matters settle at a discount reflecting defence strength and
   litigation cost — quantify both sides' best case.

## Gotchas
- Recipients often assume being paid for real goods means no exposure. It doesn't — the
  antecedent-debt element is what matters.
- Later transferees can be pursued too (§550(a)(2)), unless they took for value, in good faith and without
  knowledge of voidability (§550(b)); the recovery action must be filed within §550(f) limits.
- Insider status (§101(31)) includes relatives of an individual debtor and the directors, officers and
  persons in control of a corporate debtor; the list is non-exhaustive.
- Limitation periods to bring avoidance actions are short (§546(a): the later of two years after the
  order for relief or one year after the first trustee is appointed, if that is before the two years end).

## Other jurisdictions
UK (Insolvency Act 1986): preferences (s239; the company must have been influenced by a desire to
produce the preferring effect; relevant time 6 months, or 2 years where the recipient is connected
with the company), transactions at an undervalue (s238; 2 years) and transactions defrauding
creditors (s423; the section sets no relevant-time window). Other systems have their own
rules; confirm locally.
