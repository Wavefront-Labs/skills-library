# Consumer bankruptcy: intake, exemptions and discharge

**Goal:** take an individual debtor from first meeting to discharge without losing
assets they could have kept, without a dismissal, and without a debt surviving that
they thought was gone. Jurisdiction: United States federal (11 U.S.C.), checked current on 2026-10-24; other systems
are flagged at the end. This is reference content,
not advice for a particular case.

## 1. Intake: the questions that decide the case
- **All debts**, secured and unsecured, with creditor addresses; pull a credit report
  but don't rely on it (it misses medical, family and private debts).
- **All assets**, including tax refunds owed, inheritances expected, lawsuits the debtor
  could bring, crypto, retirement accounts and anything held "for" a relative.
- **Transfers in the look-back period:** gifts, repayments to family or friends,
  sales below value, title put in someone else's name. In the US, insider payments within
  one year and ordinary payments within 90 days can be recovered by the trustee
  (see avoidance-and-clawback skill), and transfers up to two years back (longer under
  some state laws) can be attacked as fraudulent.
- **Prior filings:** a previous case within a year limits the automatic stay; discharge
  timing bars apply (US §727(a)(8): no Chapter 7 discharge if the earlier case was commenced within 8 years
  before the new filing; §727(a)(9): 6 years after a Chapter 13 discharge, subject to payment-percentage exceptions).
- **Household income for the last six months** (the means-test window) and household size.
- **Goal:** keep a house or car? stop a foreclosure, garnishment or repossession today?

## 2. Choosing the route
| | Liquidation (US Ch. 7) | Repayment plan (US Ch. 13) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Short case, no plan | Plan of 3 years (below-median income, longer only for cause up to 5) or 5 years (§1322(d)) |
| Non-exempt assets | Trustee sells them | Kept; plan must pay creditors at least their value |
| Mortgage arrears | Can't cure | Can cure over the plan |
| Eligibility | Must pass means test (§707(b)) | Regular income; debt limits apply (§109(e): $526,700 unsecured / $1,580,125 secured per the Code's adjustment note from 1 April 2025) |

**Means test (US):** compare current monthly income (average of the six full calendar months before filing, from all sources
except certain excluded benefits such as Social Security, §101(10A)) with the state median for household size. Below median → presumption
of abuse doesn't arise. Above → deduct allowed expenses (IRS standards plus actual secured
payments); excess disposable income may push the case to Chapter 13. Medians change
periodically — always use the current table from the US Trustee Program.

## 3. Exemption planning
- Exemptions are the debtor's shield: homestead, vehicle, tools of trade, household goods,
  retirement accounts, a "wildcard".
- US: some states let debtors choose federal or state exemptions; others force state
  exemptions. Domicile rules (§522(b)(3)(A): where the debtor was domiciled for the 730 days before filing, with a
  180-day fallback test) decide **which state's** list applies — a recent mover may be stuck with the old
  state's law. State homestead exemptions are also capped for property acquired in the 1215 days before
  filing (§522(p)).
- Converting non-exempt assets to exempt ones before filing is sometimes allowed but can
  be treated as fraud if done with intent to hinder creditors. Uncertain territory —
  courts vary; document a legitimate reason and avoid last-minute large conversions.
- Value assets at realistic resale value, not purchase price, with evidence.

## 4. Filing and the automatic stay
- An approved credit counselling briefing must generally have been received within the 180 days before
  filing, subject to listed exceptions (§109(h)(1)).
- The stay stops collection, garnishment, foreclosure sales and most lawsuits on filing.
  Exceptions include criminal proceedings and actions to establish or modify domestic support orders (§362(b)).
- Notify creditors immediately when an emergency filing is used to stop a sale; schedules
  can follow within the short deadline (14 days after the petition under Rule 1007(c)(1)) but missing it risks dismissal.

## 5. Discharge: what survives
Commonly **not** discharged (§523(a)): certain priority taxes and taxes on unfiled, late or fraudulent
returns, domestic support, most educational loans (absent "undue hardship"; standards vary by circuit),
debts for fraud or for wilful and malicious injury (only if the creditor asks the court for a
determination, §523(c)(1)), and debts left off the schedules unless the creditor knew of the case.
Secured liens survive discharge even if personal liability ends.
A valid reaffirmation agreement keeps the debtor personally liable (§524(c)) — review them hard.

## Gotchas
- Omitting an asset or transfer is the fastest route to denial of discharge or a fraud referral.
- Paying a relative back just before filing hands the trustee a preference claim against that relative.
- Joint debts: a co-signer is not protected (except the limited co-debtor stay in Ch. 13, §1301).

## Other jurisdictions
Other systems differ in routes, eligibility and discharge periods — check current figures locally.
