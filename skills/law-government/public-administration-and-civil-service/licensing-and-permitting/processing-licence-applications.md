# Processing licence and permit applications

**Goal:** turn an application into a lawful, reasoned decision with conditions that can
actually be enforced, inside the statutory timescale.

Licensing regimes (alcohol and premises, taxis, trade, environmental, professional) each
have their own statute and guidance. The structure below is common to most; the details
(timescales, who may object, whether silence means approval) are regime-specific and must
be checked every time.

## 1. Validate before you assess
An application is usually not "made" until it is complete. Check on receipt:
- Prescribed form, fee, plans or supporting documents, required notices or advertisement.
- Applicant identity and legal capacity (a company must be the legal entity, not a trading name).
Log the date it became **valid**: statutory clocks and any **deemed-approval** ("silence is
consent") rule usually run from that date. Missing a deadline under a deemed-approval
regime can grant a licence nobody assessed. Tell the applicant in writing what is missing.

## 2. Consult
Send to statutory consultees (police, fire, environmental health, planning) and run any
public notice period. Record who was consulted and when. Objections usually must be
*relevant* to the licensing objectives or criteria; irrelevant ones (competition,
moral objections not tied to criteria) are generally disregarded, but record why.

## 3. Assess against the criteria, not preference
- List the statutory criteria (for example: fit and proper person, suitable premises,
  objectives such as public safety or nuisance prevention).
- Background checks: criminal records, previous licence history, financial standing where required.
- Where there are representations, many regimes require a **hearing**. Give the applicant
  the objections in advance and a fair chance to answer (natural justice).
- Policy can guide, but must not be applied so rigidly that it fetters discretion; consider
  whether the case justifies departing from policy.

## 4. Draft conditions that can be enforced
A good condition is: necessary for a stated objective, precise, within the applicant's
control, and checkable by an inspector.
- Bad: "The premises shall not cause nuisance."
- Better: "Amplified music shall not be audible at the boundary of the nearest residential
  property after 23:00." Even better if a measurable standard exists.
Avoid duplicating obligations already imposed by other law.

## 5. Decide and give reasons
The decision notice states: grant / grant with conditions / refusal, the reasons tied to
each criterion, the conditions with their purpose, duration and renewal date, and the
appeal route and deadline.

## 6. Renewal, variation, suspension, revocation
- Renewals: diarise expiry; some regimes let a licence continue while a timely renewal is pending.
- Suspension and revocation are serious interferences: give notice of the grounds, a chance to
  respond, and a proportionate outcome (warning, extra conditions, suspension before revocation)
  unless there is immediate risk, where an interim suspension power may apply.

## Gotchas
- The valid-application date is the single most important fact on the file.
- Unclear conditions are unenforceable and invite appeals.
- A decision-maker who has pre-judged (public comments, campaign involvement) risks a bias challenge.

## Verify
- [ ] Valid date logged; statutory deadline and any deemed-approval date diarised.
- [ ] All consultees and notices recorded; representations assessed for relevance.
- [ ] Each criterion has a finding; each condition is precise and checkable.
- [ ] Notice includes reasons, duration and appeal rights.
