# Preparing applications and challenging refusals

**Goal:** get a yes the first time by giving the decision-maker everything they need in
the shape they need it — and, if refused, use the right challenge route before its
deadline. Applies to benefits, permits, licences, grants, residence and tax filings.
Examples use England & Wales and US federal practice; every programme has its own forms,
deadlines and appeal routes, often printed on the decision letter itself. For complex or
high-stakes matters (immigration, large planning applications, disability benefits
appeals) get advice from a lawyer, accredited adviser or advice charity.

## 1. Find the actual criteria
1. Locate the **official** guidance: the statute or regulation, the agency's published
   policy or manual, and the form's notes. Ignore forum folklore.
2. Write the criteria as a numbered list. Each is a box the decision-maker must tick.
3. Note fees, deadlines, and whether the clock starts on submission or on completeness.

## 2. Build an evidence matrix
| Criterion | My evidence | Document | Gap / fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| e.g. Resident at address 12 months | Tenancy + utility bills | Exhibits 3–5 | Bill for March missing → bank statement |
| e.g. Income below threshold | Payslips, bank statements | Exhibits 6–9 | — |
- Use official documents over self-statements; recent over old; originals or certified
  copies where required; certified translations for foreign-language documents.
- Explain gaps honestly in a short note rather than leaving them for the officer to guess.

## 3. Submit so it can't be lost
- Fill every field; "N/A" rather than blank. Make answers consistent across forms
  (dates, names, addresses).
- A short **cover letter** mapping each criterion to the exhibit numbers.
- Keep a full copy of everything submitted, and **proof of submission** (online
  reference, screenshot, recorded delivery, stamped receipt).
- Diary: expected decision date (published service standards), and when to chase.

## 4. Chasing
- Chase after the published processing time passes: quote your reference, ask for
  status and expected date. Keep a contact log (date, person, what was said).
- Long delays: formal complaint; then an elected representative's caseworker (often
  very effective); then the ombudsman; in some systems a court can order a decision
  (e.g. mandamus or a US APA claim for unreasonable delay).

## 5. If refused
1. Read the refusal for **reasons** and the **deadline** — calendar it immediately. If
   no reasons, ask for them in writing.
2. Classify: missing evidence (fix and resubmit or seek reconsideration), wrong facts,
   wrong law, or unfair procedure.
3. Use the routes in order, as the law requires:
| Route | When | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh application | Defect easily cured | May lose original date |
| Reconsideration / administrative review | Usually mandatory first step | Short deadlines (often ~1 month) |
| Appeal to tribunal/board | Merits disputes | Strict time limits; bring new evidence if allowed |
| Ombudsman | Maladministration, delay, poor service | Usually after the complaints process |
| Judicial review | Unlawful, irrational or unfair decision with no adequate appeal | E&W: promptly and within 3 months (shorter in some areas) |
4. In the challenge, answer each reason specifically with evidence; don't re-tell the whole story.

## Gotchas
- Missing a reconsideration deadline often blocks every later route.
- Contradictions between forms are treated as credibility problems.
- Sending originals you cannot replace.
- Phone conversations not followed up in writing "never happened".

## Verify
- [ ] Criteria listed from official sources.
- [ ] Every criterion has evidence or an explained gap.
- [ ] Copy of submission and proof of delivery kept.
- [ ] Decision and challenge deadlines in the calendar.
- [ ] Challenge addresses each stated reason with evidence.
