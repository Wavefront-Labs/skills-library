# Eligibility determination and claims processing

**Goal:** reach a decision on an entitlement claim that is correct on the rules,
supported by evidence on file, explained to the claimant, and survives review on appeal.

Rules differ sharply between programmes and jurisdictions (contributory vs means-tested,
statutory vs discretionary). Treat this as the generic decision method; the programme's
legislation and the agency's decision-maker guidance always govern.

## 1. Break the claim into conditions
Every benefit is a list of conditions in the statute or regulations. Write them out as a
checklist before you look at evidence, for example:
- **Identity and status** (residence, immigration status, age).
- **Qualifying event or circumstance** (unemployment, disability, caring, low income).
- **Contribution or means test** (contribution record; income and capital below limits).
- **Procedural conditions** (claim made in time, required forms signed, interview attended).
- **Exclusions** (other benefits that overlap, people in prison or abroad, sanctions in force).
A claim fails if *any* condition fails. Decide each one separately and record which
evidence supports it; this is what an appeal body will examine.

## 2. Gather and weigh evidence
- Use data you already hold (agency records, data matches) before asking the claimant.
- Request missing evidence in writing with a clear deadline and the consequence of not
  replying. Keep the request on file: "failed to provide" decisions fall apart if the
  request can't be shown.
- Standard of proof in most benefits systems is the civil one (more likely than not). You
  do not need certainty; you need a reasoned preference for one version.
- The claimant's own account is evidence. You may reject it, but say why (inconsistency,
  contrary records), not merely "insufficient evidence".

## 3. Write the decision
A defensible decision letter states: the decision; the effective date; the amount and how
it was calculated; each condition and the finding on it; the evidence relied on; the rule
cited; and the claimant's review/appeal right with the deadline. Plain language. A reader
should be able to see exactly what to challenge.

## 4. Changes of circumstances and overpayments
- Overpayment decisions are separate decisions with their own appeal rights. Establish:
  (a) the period, (b) the amount, (c) the cause (claimant failed to disclose, official
  error, or both), and (d) whether it is legally recoverable. In many systems, overpayments
  caused purely by official error are not recoverable, or recovery is discretionary.
- Recovery rates from ongoing benefit are usually capped; check hardship provisions.
- Fraud referrals follow a different track and evidential standard. Do not mix an
  investigation interview into ordinary casework.

## 5. Reviews and appeals
Many systems require an internal reconsideration before an external tribunal. At
reconsideration, look at the claim again from scratch, not just the original reasoning.
If new evidence changes the outcome, revise. Defending a decision you think is wrong
wastes tribunal time and usually loses.

## Gotchas
- **Wrong effective date.** Backdating rules, waiting days and assessment periods cause
  more errors than the eligibility decision itself.
- **Silent conditions.** A decision that doesn't mention a disputed condition reads as no
  decision on it.
- **Data-match hits are leads, not proof.** Verify before acting on a match.
- **Deadlines run from notification.** Record when and how the letter was sent.

## Verify
- [ ] Every statutory condition has a finding and cited evidence on file.
- [ ] Calculation and effective date are shown and checked by a second person on high-value awards.
- [ ] Evidence requests and their deadlines are on file.
- [ ] Overpayment cause and recoverability are decided and recorded separately.
- [ ] The letter states review/appeal rights and the deadline.
