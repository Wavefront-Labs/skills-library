# Costing policy proposals and analysing budgets

**Goal:** know what a proposal really costs or raises over time, how a budget's numbers
fit together, and who gains or loses — and show your working so others can check it.

## 1. Read a budget
- Separate **current/operating** spending from **capital** (investment); many fiscal
  rules treat them differently.
- Distinguish **appropriated/discretionary** spending (voted annually) from
  **mandatory/entitlement** spending (driven by eligibility rules — pensions, benefits)
  and from **tax expenditures** (reliefs, credits — spending through the tax system,
  often hidden).
- Compare like with like: **nominal** vs **real** (deflate by a GDP deflator or relevant
  price index), per capita, and as % of GDP.
- Always ask "**compared with what?**" — last year's outturn, last year's plan, or a
  baseline projection? Announced "increases" are often relative to a baseline that was
  already rising.
- Check multi-year profiles; front- or back-loading tells you a lot.
- Independent fiscal institutions (e.g. CBO in the US, OBR in the UK, parliamentary
  budget offices elsewhere) publish baselines and scoring conventions — use them.

## 2. Cost a proposal: the method
1. **Specify precisely** the policy: who, what amount, start date, conditions,
   interactions with existing programmes. Most costing errors are specification errors.
2. **Baseline:** what would be spent/raised without the policy, over the costing period
   (often 5 or 10 years, by fiscal year).
3. **Static costing:** population affected × take-up × unit cost/amount (or tax base ×
   rate change). Use administrative data or **microsimulation** models on household
   survey data where available.
4. **Behavioural response:** people and firms change behaviour — tax avoidance, labour
   supply, take-up rising over time, demand effects. Apply elasticities from evidence;
   state them.
5. **Phasing:** implementation lags, ramp-up of take-up, payment timing vs accrual.
6. **Admin costs** for the delivering bodies.
7. **Interactions:** effects on other taxes and benefits (e.g. a benefit increase
   changing other means-tested awards).
8. Macroeconomic ("dynamic") effects: some scorekeepers include them for large measures;
   others don't — follow the convention and label it.
9. **Uncertainty:** low/central/high, and a rating of the costing's certainty.
Worked example: a child benefit top-up of 10 per week for 400,000 eligible children,
expected take-up 85% → 10 × 52 × 400,000 × 0.85 ≈ 177 million a year before admin
costs and interactions.

## 3. Revenue options
For each: yield estimate (static and behavioural), incidence (who really pays), effect
on incentives, administrative and compliance costs, legal/treaty constraints, timing of
receipts, and stability of the base.

## 4. Distributional analysis
Show effects by household income decile (in cash and as % of income), by household
type, region, and protected groups where data allow. Combine tax and spending measures
for the net picture; don't present only the flattering half.

## 5. Spending reviews
Set objectives and envelope; ask each programme for baseline, evidence of outcomes,
savings options (e.g. scenarios at −5%/−10%), and pressures; challenge with unit-cost
benchmarks and evaluation evidence; decide priorities; publish allocations with outcomes
expected. Zero-based approaches question the baseline itself but are resource-heavy.

## Gotchas
- Fiscal-year mismatches (programme year vs government fiscal year).
- Double counting savings already in the baseline.
- Treating one-off receipts as recurring.
- Costings conventions **vary by jurisdiction** (what counts, period, dynamic or
  not); state which you followed.

## Verify
- [ ] Policy specified precisely; baseline stated.
- [ ] Static estimate, behavioural adjustment and phasing shown separately.
- [ ] Real vs nominal and fiscal years clearly labelled.
- [ ] Uncertainty range and key assumptions published.
- [ ] Distributional effects shown for the whole package.
