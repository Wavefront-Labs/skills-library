# Theory of change, delivery model and eligibility design

**Goal:** turn an agreed policy intent into a programme that reaches the intended people,
produces the intended outcomes, and can be run by real organisations with real budgets.

## 1. Theory of change / logic model
Work backwards from the long-term outcome:
**Impact ← Outcomes ← Outputs ← Activities ← Inputs**, with the **assumptions** at
every arrow written down.
Example (youth employment grant):
- Inputs: funding, caseworkers, employer partners.
- Activities: coaching, short courses, wage subsidies for placements.
- Outputs: N young people coached; M placements.
- Outcomes: sustained employment at 6 and 12 months.
- Impact: lower long-term unemployment in the target group.
- Assumptions: employers will host placements at the subsidy level; young people can
  reach the venues; placements convert to jobs.
The assumptions are where programmes fail — mark each as evidenced, plausible, or
untested, and design the pilot to test the untested ones. Choose **indicators** for
outputs and outcomes now, with baselines (see the policy-evaluation skill).

## 2. Delivery model
Options: direct delivery by the department, an arm's-length body, local government,
grants to NGOs, contracted providers (fee-for-service, payment-by-results, outcome
contracts), vouchers/cash transfers to users, regulation of private provision.
Choose by:
- **capability and capacity** of the delivery body (and its existing systems);
- **control vs flexibility** (central consistency or local adaptation?);
- **market** — are there capable providers? Will payment-by-results attract them or
  only those who can "cream" easy cases?
- **accountability**: who answers for failures? Map the **delivery chain** from
  minister to front line, and where data flows.

## 3. Eligibility and targeting
- Define the target population in words, then translate into **verifiable criteria**
  (age, income, residency, condition), with the evidence source for each.
- Every rule creates **inclusion errors** (people who shouldn't get it, do) and
  **exclusion errors** (people who should, don't). Strict means-testing cuts the first
  and raises the second, plus administrative cost. Simpler, categorical eligibility often
  achieves better targeting in practice because take-up rises.
- **Take-up** is a design variable: auto-enrolment, data-sharing to pre-fill, short
  forms, assisted digital routes, outreach through trusted organisations. Treat
  unnecessary paperwork ("sludge") as a cost to users.
- Cliff edges: a sharp income cut-off creates work disincentives and unfairness at the
  margin — consider tapers.
- Run eligibility rules against **real case examples** and existing data to estimate
  numbers eligible and cost.
- Check legal powers, equality/non-discrimination duties and data-protection basis —
  **these vary by jurisdiction**.

## 4. User research
Talk to prospective users and front-line staff before finalising: journey maps of how
someone finds, applies, waits, receives and leaves the programme. Prototype the
application form and test it.

## 5. Pilot and implementation plan
- Pilot in a limited area or cohort, ideally designed so it can be evaluated (staggered
  rollout, comparison areas).
- Implementation plan: milestones, critical path (legislation, procurement, IT, hiring,
  guidance), RACI for roles, budget profile, **risk register** with owners, go/no-go
  criteria for scale-up, and communications.
- Plan the **exit or review** — time-limited programmes need a closure plan.

## Gotchas
- Programmes designed around the budget announcement date, not delivery readiness.
- Payment-by-results metrics that reward easy cases over the hardest-to-help.
- IT and procurement lead times routinely underestimated.

## Verify
- [ ] Logic model with assumptions graded and pilot tests for untested ones.
- [ ] Delivery body chosen with explicit capacity and accountability analysis.
- [ ] Eligibility tested on real cases; take-up measures built in.
- [ ] Users and front-line staff tested the journey.
- [ ] Implementation plan has critical path, risk owners and go/no-go criteria.
