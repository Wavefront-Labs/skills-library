# Drafting a bill: structure, definitions, amendments and commencement

**Goal:** turn a policy decision into legal text that does exactly what's intended,
fits the existing statute book, and can be implemented on a known date.

Drafting conventions are **jurisdiction-specific** — follow your office's drafting
manual (e.g. the UK Office of the Parliamentary Counsel's guidance, US House/Senate
Legislative Counsel style manuals, Commonwealth drafting offices' manuals) and the
local **Interpretation Act**, which supplies default meanings (gender, number, time
computation, "may" vs "must").

## 1. Drafting instructions (from the policy team)
A drafter needs: the policy objective, the mischief, who is affected, the precise legal
effect wanted (rights, duties, powers, sanctions), interaction with existing law,
enforcement, appeals, funding, territorial extent, timing, and worked examples of
hard cases. Vague instructions produce vague law — push back with questions.

## 2. Bill architecture
Typical order:
1. Long title (scope; in some legislatures it limits amendments) and short title.
2. **Purpose/overview** clause (optional in some traditions).
3. **Interpretation/definitions** (or definitions at point of use for local terms).
4. Substantive parts: duties, rights, powers, procedures — general before specific,
   main rule before exceptions.
5. Enforcement: offences, civil penalties, inspection powers, appeals.
6. Delegated legislation powers and their procedure.
7. Amendments to other Acts (often in a schedule).
8. Transitional and saving provisions; repeals.
9. Extent, **commencement**, short title.

## 3. Words
- **One term, one meaning, throughout.** Never vary vocabulary for style.
- **Define** only terms used in a special sense; avoid "means and includes" ambiguity —
  "means" is exhaustive, "includes" extends. Don't bury substantive rules in definitions.
- Active voice with a clear legal subject: "The Authority must publish…", not "It shall
  be published…".
- "must" for duties, "may" for powers/discretions, "must not" for prohibitions (many
  offices have moved away from "shall").
- Short sections; one idea per subsection; avoid provisos ("provided that").
- Cross-references by number, sparingly.

## 4. Amending existing law
- **Textual amendment** ("In section 4(2) of the X Act, for 'thirty' substitute
  'sixty'") is preferred in most Westminster systems — the amended Act then reads
  correctly. Non-textual ("section 4 has effect as if…") makes law hard to find.
- Prepare a **Keeling schedule** or tracked-change version showing the Act as amended;
  it catches errors the amending text hides.
- Search the whole statute book for every reference to things you're changing —
  consequential amendments are where mistakes hide.

## 5. Delegated powers
Specify who may make regulations, about what, by which procedure (negative/affirmative
or equivalent legislative oversight), with consultation requirements. Broad powers to
amend primary legislation (so-called Henry VIII clauses) attract scrutiny; justify them.

## 6. Offences and penalties
State the conduct, the mental element (or strict liability, expressly), defences and
the burden of proof, maximum penalties consistent with comparable offences, who
prosecutes, and corporate/officer liability.

## 7. Transition and commencement
- **Commencement:** on Royal Assent/enactment, a fixed date, or by commencement order —
  allow lead time for systems and guidance.
- **Transitional/saving:** what happens to pending applications, existing licences,
  proceedings already started. Retroactive effect needs explicit words and justification.
- **Sunset/review clauses** where the policy is experimental.

## Self-check
Read every provision as (a) a regulated person looking for a loophole, (b) the
enforcing body looking for its power, (c) a court asking what Parliament intended.

## Verify
- [ ] Drafting instructions answered every hard-case question.
- [ ] Each defined term used consistently; no substantive rules hidden in definitions.
- [ ] Keeling schedule produced; consequential references found by full search.
- [ ] Delegated powers specify scope and oversight procedure.
- [ ] Commencement and transitional rules cover pending matters.
