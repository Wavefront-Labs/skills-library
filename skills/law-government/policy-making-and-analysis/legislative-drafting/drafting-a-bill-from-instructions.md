# Drafting a bill from policy instructions

**Goal:** produce a bill (or set of regulations) that does exactly what the policy
intends, fits the existing statute book, and is legally certain. Models used: England &
Wales / Westminster practice (Parliamentary Counsel) and US federal and state practice
(legislative counsel). Conventions differ — numbering, "shall" vs "must", amendment
style — so always follow your jurisdiction's drafting manual and Interpretation Act.

## 1. Get proper drafting instructions
Before drafting, insist on written instructions covering:
- the policy objective and the mischief being addressed;
- who is regulated, what they must or must not do, and the consequences (offences,
  civil penalties, enforcement body);
- existing law affected (list Acts and regulations), and international obligations;
- powers to be delegated to ministers or agencies, and the procedure for them;
- commencement, transitional cases and territorial extent;
- fiscal and human-rights / constitutional implications.
Send back a list of questions; gaps in instructions become gaps in the law.

## 2. Plan the structure
| Part | Contents |
|---|---|
| Long title | Purpose summary (legally relevant in some systems; also the scope limit for amendments in Westminster practice) |
| Parts / chapters | Group operative provisions logically: duties, licensing, enforcement, appeals |
| Interpretation | Definitions — either a single section near the end (UK habit) or near the start (many US and Commonwealth drafts) |
| Amendments of other Acts | Often in a schedule |
| Final provisions | Regulations procedure, financial provisions, extent, commencement, short title |
Write a one-page skeleton and get the instructing officials to agree it before writing clauses.

## 3. Draft the operative provisions
- One idea per section; one obligation per subsection.
- Use the active voice and identify the actor: "The licensing authority must..."
- Use **"must"** for duties and **"may"** for powers (modern UK and much US plain-language
  practice); avoid "shall" unless house style requires it, and never mix.
- Define a term only if used more than once or if its ordinary meaning is wrong; never
  put substantive rules inside a definition.
- Keep conditions in a predictable order: case, condition, subject, action.
- Avoid "and/or", "etc.", and lists without a clear "and" (cumulative) or "or" (alternative).

## 4. Amend existing law precisely
- **Textual amendment** ("In section 5(2), for 'thirty days' substitute 'sixty days'")
  is preferred over non-textual ("section 5 shall have effect as if..."), because the
  amended text can be consolidated.
- Check every cross-reference into and out of the amended provision.
- Prepare a **Keeling schedule** (amended text shown in full with changes marked) for
  reviewers and legislators — it catches errors fast.

## 5. Delegated powers, commencement and transition
- For each regulation-making power: who makes it, scope, procedure (affirmative /
  negative resolution in Westminster; notice-and-comment rulemaking in US federal
  agencies), and any consultation duty. Avoid broad powers to amend primary legislation
  ("Henry VIII powers") unless justified; scrutiny committees will challenge them.
- Commencement: on Royal Assent / enactment, a fixed date, or by commencement order /
  regulations. Stagger if systems need build time.
- Transitional and savings provisions: what happens to pending applications, existing
  licences, ongoing proceedings.
- Consider a sunset or review clause where the policy is experimental.

## 6. Notes and checks
Draft explanatory notes / a section-by-section analysis in plain language. Then run a
full read-through against the instructions, and a separate read for consistency of
defined terms.

## Gotchas
- Defining a word, then using a synonym elsewhere — courts may read a different meaning.
- Creating an offence without stating the mental element, penalty and mode of trial
  (rules vary by jurisdiction).
- Forgetting consequential amendments in other Acts that use the old terminology.
- Retrospective effect without explicit justification; presumptions against it apply in
  most common-law systems.

## Verify
- [ ] Every instruction maps to a clause, and every clause maps to an instruction.
- [ ] Defined terms used consistently; no orphan definitions.
- [ ] All cross-references and amended provisions checked against the current consolidated text.
- [ ] Delegated powers, commencement, transitional provisions and extent covered.
- [ ] Keeling schedule and explanatory notes prepared.
