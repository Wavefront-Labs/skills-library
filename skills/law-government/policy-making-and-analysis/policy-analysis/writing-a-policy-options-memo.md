# Writing a policy options memo

**Goal:** give a decision-maker (minister, mayor, agency head, board) a short memo that
lets them choose between genuine alternatives in one reading, with the evidence,
trade-offs and risks visible. The method is jurisdiction-neutral; the UK Green Book and
US agency practice (OMB-style option analysis) are the reference models. House styles
for length and headings vary by department — follow yours.

## 1. Define the problem before touching solutions
- Write the problem in one sentence with a **number and a population**: "About X% of
  renters in the city spend over half their income on rent, up from Y% five years ago."
  If you cannot quantify it, say what data is missing.
- State **why government should act**: market failure (externality, information
  asymmetry, public good, monopoly), equity objective, or legal duty. No rationale, no
  intervention.
- Separate the problem from a pre-chosen solution ("we lack a rent cap" is a solution
  disguised as a problem).
- Confirm the decision-maker's **actual powers and deadline** — options outside their
  legal competence or budget are distractions.

## 2. Set the baseline (do-nothing / do-minimum)
Project what happens with no new action over the appraisal period (typically 3-10
years), including changes already legislated. Every option is measured against this
counterfactual, not against today.

## 3. Generate 3-5 real options
| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Information / nudge | disclosure, default changes, campaigns |
| Economic instrument | tax, subsidy, voucher, fee |
| Regulation | standards, licensing, bans |
| Direct provision | government delivers the service |
| Enabling / partnership | funding NGOs, devolving to local bodies |
Include at least one non-regulatory option. Avoid the "straw-man trio" (two absurd options
framing a favourite) — reviewers and ministers spot it.

## 4. Choose criteria and score
Agree criteria up front (ideally with the client): effectiveness, cost to government,
cost to business/households, equity/distribution, legal feasibility, administrative
feasibility, political acceptability, time to impact, reversibility. Then build the matrix:

| Criterion | Baseline | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Effect (units) | 0 | +est. (range) | ... | ... |
| Gross cost / yr | 0 | ... | ... | ... |

Use ranges and cite the source for every number. Mark judgement-based scores clearly
(e.g. "High/Med/Low — analyst judgement"). Do not add up unweighted scores into a fake
total; show the trade-off.

## 5. Synthesize the evidence
- Rank evidence by strength: systematic reviews and RCTs > quasi-experimental studies >
  comparative international cases > expert opinion > anecdote.
- Note transferability: a result from a different country or scale may not hold.
- Say what is unknown and whether a pilot would resolve it.

## 6. Write the memo
1. **Purpose / decision required** (one line, with the deadline).
2. **Recommendation** up front (bottom line up front), with the one-sentence reason.
3. **Background / problem** (half page max).
4. **Options and assessment** (the matrix + a short paragraph per option).
5. **Risks and mitigations**, including implementation and legal-challenge risk.
6. **Stakeholder and handling** (who will support or oppose; communications).
7. **Next steps** if agreed.
Typical length: 2-4 pages plus annexes. Plain language; define acronyms once.

## Gotchas
- Advocating instead of analysing: present the best case for options you don't favour.
- Ignoring implementation capacity — the most effective option on paper often fails on
  delivery systems, IT or staffing.
- Double-counting benefits, or comparing gross costs for one option against net for another.
- Forgetting distributional effects (who pays, who gains), which decision-makers ask about first.

## Verify
- [ ] Problem stated with a number, a population and a rationale for intervention.
- [ ] Baseline defined and every option compared against it.
- [ ] At least three genuine options, including a non-regulatory one.
- [ ] Every figure sourced; judgement scores labelled; uncertainty shown as ranges.
- [ ] Recommendation and decision required are on page one.
