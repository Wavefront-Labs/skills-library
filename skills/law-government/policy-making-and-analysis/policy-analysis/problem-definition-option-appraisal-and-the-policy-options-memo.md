# Problem definition, option appraisal and the policy options memo

**Goal:** give a decision-maker a clear choice between realistic options, with honest
trade-offs and a recommendation, on one to three pages they will actually read.

A common scaffolding is Bardach's "eightfold path" (define the problem, assemble
evidence, construct alternatives, select criteria, project outcomes, confront
trade-offs, decide, tell your story); government appraisal manuals (e.g. the UK Green
Book, the US OMB Circular A-4 for regulations) formalise similar steps.

## 1. Define the problem
- State it as a **condition with magnitude**, not as a missing solution: "Around N
  households wait more than 6 months for X, up from M in 2020", not "we lack a
  digital portal".
- Who is affected, how badly, trend, and **why it's a public problem** (market failure,
  equity, rights, government failure, statutory duty).
- Root causes (a simple driver diagram helps); which causes are within this
  decision-maker's control?
- Check the **framing** with stakeholders; a mis-framed problem makes every option wrong.

## 2. Assemble the evidence
- Administrative data, statistics, prior evaluations, academic reviews, other
  jurisdictions' experience, stakeholder and front-line input.
- A **rapid evidence assessment** (structured search, inclusion criteria, quality
  grading) beats a scattered literature skim.
- Grade confidence: strong / moderate / weak / none — and say which.

## 3. Construct options
- 3–5 genuinely distinct options, always including **"do nothing / status quo"** (the
  counterfactual everything is compared to).
- Span the policy toolkit: information/nudges, regulation, taxes/subsidies, direct
  provision, contracting, partnerships, institutional change.
- Avoid straw-man options designed to lose; decision-makers notice.

## 4. Select criteria — before scoring
Typical set: **effectiveness** (how much of the problem it solves), **cost/efficiency**
(cost per outcome), **equity** (who gains/loses, distributional effects, protected
groups), **administrative feasibility** (capacity, IT, timeline), **political/legal
feasibility** (legal powers, stakeholder support), **risk** (implementation, unintended
consequences). Weight them explicitly if you must, but show the unweighted picture too.

## 5. Project outcomes and confront trade-offs
- For each option, estimate impact with ranges and stated assumptions, compared to
  the do-nothing baseline. Use logic models; sensitivity-test the key assumptions.
- **Options matrix:** options × criteria, with short qualitative judgments or numbers.
- Name the trade-off plainly: "Option B is cheaper but reaches only a third of those
  affected."

## 6. The memo
Structure:
1. **Purpose / decision required** (one sentence, plus deadline).
2. **Recommendation** (up front — don't make the reader hunt).
3. **Background and problem** (short, with the key numbers).
4. **Options** (one paragraph each) and the matrix.
5. **Risks, handling and stakeholder views.**
6. **Next steps** if agreed (legal, budget, comms, implementation).
Use plain language; numbers with sources; no unexplained acronyms. Write so it still
works if only the first half-page is read.

## Gotchas
- Analysts who favour an option early tend to gather confirming evidence; have a
  colleague argue the other side.
- "Evidence-based" claims from a single study or other countries need context checks.
- Political feasibility belongs in the memo — but analysis shouldn't be silently bent
  to fit it; flag the tension.

## Verify
- [ ] Problem stated with magnitude, trend, affected groups and causes.
- [ ] Evidence graded for confidence; gaps named.
- [ ] Options include do-nothing and span different tools.
- [ ] Criteria set before scoring; trade-offs stated in plain words.
- [ ] Memo leads with decision and recommendation and fits 1–3 pages.
