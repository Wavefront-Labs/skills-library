# Negotiating a plea agreement

**Goal:** reach (or reject, knowingly) a negotiated resolution whose terms are
written, understood by the defendant, and enforceable — for either side of the table.

## 1. Assess leverage before the first call
- **Trial risk:** realistic conviction probability on each count (use the elements
  matrix and suppression outlook).
- **Exposure gap:** likely sentence after trial vs. under the offer. Include
  mandatory minimums, enhancements, consecutive-sentence risk, and any guilty-plea
  discount (formal in England & Wales — a maximum reduction of one-third at the first stage of
  proceedings, falling to one-quarter later and one-tenth on the first day of trial; informal or guideline-based elsewhere).
- **Prosecution pressures:** witness problems, victim wishes, lab delays, workload.
- **Timing:** offers often worsen after indictment, after suppression rulings, or
  when trial prep starts. Early offers frequently lapse.

## 2. What can be bargained
| Lever | Notes |
|---|---|
| Charge | plead to lesser/fewer counts; dismiss others |
| Sentence | agreed recommendation, cap, or (where allowed) binding sentence (US Rule 11(c)(1)(C)) |
| Facts | stipulated drug weight, loss amount, role — drives guideline ranges |
| Cooperation | substantial-assistance motion (US: 18 U.S.C. 3553(e) lets the court go below a statutory minimum on the government's motion) in return for information/testimony |
| Ancillary | forfeiture scope, restitution amount, custody start date, immigration-safe plea |

## 3. Cooperation agreements
Before a proffer, get a written proffer letter and read its use and derivative-use
terms: most allow the statements to be used to impeach the client, or as rebuttal,
if the client later testifies inconsistently. Cooperation is usually at the
prosecutor's sole discretion — the defendant bears the risk of an unsatisfying result.

## 4. Write it down — checklist
- Exact counts and statutory sections; dismissed counts.
- Sentencing terms and whether the court is bound.
- Agreed facts / factual basis.
- Appeal and collateral-attack waivers and their carve-outs (check what the agreement preserves).
- Breach consequences for both sides.
- Restitution, forfeiture, immigration statements.
- "No other promises" clause — and make sure that is true.

## 5. Duties to the client
Convey every formal offer promptly (*Missouri v. Frye*) and advise competently on it
(*Lafler v. Cooper*). Advise on deportation consequences where they are clear
(*Padilla*). The decision to plead is the client's. Put advice and the client's
decision in writing.

## 6. Prepare the colloquy
The court will confirm the plea is knowing, voluntary and factually based: rights
waived (trial, confrontation, silence), maximum penalties, the agreement terms,
no threats/promises outside it. Rehearse with the client so answers are truthful
and not surprised. An Alford or nolo plea is not available everywhere.

## Gotchas
- A defendant who balks at the factual basis in open court can sink the plea.
- Collateral consequences (registration, licensing, benefits, firearms) are often
  bigger than the sentence — check them.
- Uncertain: sentence-bargaining rules and judicial participation vary by
  jurisdiction (some forbid judges in negotiations; some permit indications of sentence).

- [ ] Written agreement covers counts, sentence terms, facts, waivers, breach.
- [ ] Immigration and other collateral consequences checked.
