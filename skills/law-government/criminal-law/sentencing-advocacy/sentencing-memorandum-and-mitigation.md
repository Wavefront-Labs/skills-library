# Sentencing memorandum and mitigation

**Goal:** give the court a correct starting range and a persuasive, evidenced reason
to land at the lowest (defence) or most appropriate (prosecution) point in or beyond it.

## 1. Get the arithmetic right
Most sentences anchor on a framework:
- **US federal:** advisory Guidelines (base offence level + specific offence
  characteristics + adjustments → offence level; criminal history category →
  range from the sentencing table), then the § 3553(a) factors; the guideline range is one factor the court
  considers (§ 3553(a)(4)) and sentences inside or outside it are reviewed for abuse of discretion (*Gall v. United States*, 2007).
- **England & Wales:** Sentencing Council guidelines apply, including the guilty-plea reduction guideline
  (maximum one-third at the first stage of proceedings).
- **States/other countries:** structured grids, presumptive ranges or broad discretion.
Recompute the range yourself; don't accept the probation/pre-sentence report's math.
Common errors: loss amount, drug quantity, role adjustments, double counting,
stale or mis-scored prior convictions.

## 2. Object to the pre-sentence report
File written objections by the deadline (US: within 14 days of receiving the PSR,
FRCrP 32(f)). Factual errors left uncorrected follow the defendant into custody
classification and programme eligibility.

## 3. Build mitigation evidence
Start at intake, not after verdict.
- Personal history: childhood adversity, trauma, mental health, addiction, cognitive
  impairment — supported by records and, where funded, expert/mitigation-specialist reports.
- Rehabilitation: treatment completed, employment, education, sobriety tests.
- Family impact: dependants, caregiving roles.
- Offence context: minor role, duress-like pressure, aberrant conduct.
- Character letters: specific anecdotes from people who know about the offence;
  generic praise from strangers is ignored. Coach writers to address the judge, show
  awareness of the conviction, and stay under a page.

## 4. The sentencing memorandum
Structure: requested sentence up front → correct range and objections → the person
(narrative, evidenced) → statutory factors one by one (e.g. § 3553(a)) → why the
requested sentence is "sufficient but not greater than necessary" → specific
alternatives (treatment programme, community sentence, designation requests).
Attach exhibits. Be candid about aggravating facts — judges distrust memoranda that ignore them.

## 5. The hearing
- Prepare the client's allocution: brief, genuine, acceptance of responsibility,
  no relitigating guilt, awareness of the victim. Rehearse but don't script it word-for-word.
- Anticipate victim-impact statements; respond respectfully, never attack the victim.
- Ask for specific recommendations (facility, programmes) on the record.
- Prosecutors: explain the requested sentence with reference to the same factors
  and any agreement terms; honour plea-agreement recommendations precisely —
  breaching them is a common appeal ground.

## Gotchas
- Mandatory minimums and statutory maxima cap everything; check statutory exceptions.
- Restitution/compensation and forfeiture have their own proof rules.
- Uncertain: guideline amendments occur regularly (the US Sentencing Commission submits amendments to Congress by 1 May and they take effect on a date it
  sets, no later than 1 November of that year, 28 U.S.C. 994(p)); use the manual in force on the sentencing date unless an
  ex post facto issue applies.
