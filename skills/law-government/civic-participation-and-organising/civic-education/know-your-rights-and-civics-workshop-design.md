# Know-your-rights and civics workshop design

**Goal:** leave participants able to *act* — contact the right official, assert a right
correctly, take part in a process — not just know facts. Content must be accurate for
the participants' jurisdiction; rights and procedures differ by country, state and city.

## 1. Start from the action
Write 2–4 learning outcomes as verbs:
- "Identify who decides on the bus route in our city and how to contact them."
- "State the three things to say if police ask to search your car" (jurisdiction-specific).
- "Submit a comment on a planning application."
If you can't describe what participants will do differently, cut the topic.

## 2. Get the content right
- Use primary/official sources (statutes, government guides, court rules) and reputable
  legal-aid or bar materials. Note the date and jurisdiction on every handout.
- **Know-your-rights content is high stakes:** wrong advice about police stops, immigration
  enforcement or evictions can harm people. Have a lawyer or accredited legal-aid
  organisation review it, and re-review when laws change.
- **Legal information vs legal advice:** explain general rules; don't tell an individual
  what to do in their specific case unless you're qualified and authorised. Have a
  referral list (legal aid, clinics, hotlines) ready.
- Be honest about grey areas: "This varies by state" or "Courts are split" is better than
  false certainty.

## 3. Structure (90 minutes is a good default)
1. **Opening (10 min):** purpose, agreements (confidentiality, respect), and what
   people already know — ask about their experiences.
2. **Core content (25 min):** short input blocks of ≤10 minutes each, with visuals.
3. **Practice (30 min):** role-plays (a doorstep encounter, a call to a councillor),
   scenario cards, or a **mock deliberation** — participants debate a real local issue
   using a structured format, then vote.
4. **Action planning (15 min):** each person writes one concrete next step and a date.
5. **Close and evaluation (10 min):** quick written feedback; resources and referrals.

Popular-education principle: participants' lived experience is part of the content —
facilitators draw it out rather than lecturing.

## 4. Accessibility and trust
- Plain language (aim for roughly a reading age of 11–12); define jargon once.
- Translation/interpretation for the community's languages; large-print handouts.
- Venue people already trust; childcare and food raise attendance.
- For sensitive topics (immigration status, police), don't collect unnecessary personal
  data; avoid photos without consent.

## 5. Participation guides
A good one-page guide: the decision-maker, how to reach them, key dates, what to say
(template), what happens next, and where to get help. Test it on someone outside your
group before printing.

## 6. Evaluation
Pre/post questions on the learning outcomes (two or three items), plus follow-up a month
later: did people take the action? Feed results into the next session.

## Gotchas
- Presenting partisan opinions in a "civics" workshop undermines trust and may breach
  rules for schools, charities or funders.
- Handouts circulate for years; date them and include a "check for updates" note.
- Role-plays involving police or authority figures can retraumatise; offer opt-outs.

## Verify
- [ ] Learning outcomes are written as actions.
- [ ] Content reviewed by a qualified person and dated with jurisdiction.
- [ ] At least a third of session time is active practice.
- [ ] Referral list and evaluation are ready before the session.
