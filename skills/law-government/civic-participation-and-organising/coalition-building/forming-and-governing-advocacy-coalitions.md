# Forming and governing an advocacy coalition

**Goal:** combine organisations' power on a shared aim without the coalition collapsing
over credit, money or the first compromise. Coalitions are usually weaker than members
think on day one and stronger than they think if governance is settled early.

## 1. Should this be a coalition at all?
Form one when no single organisation can win alone **and** potential partners bring
different power (members in key districts, expertise, money, legitimacy with affected
people, media reach). Don't form one just to look big — a coalition costs staff time
in meetings and negotiation.

## 2. Who is in
- **Core members** take on obligations (staff time, money, turnout); **supporters** lend
  their name; **allies** coordinate informally.
- Include organisations led by the people most affected — their absence costs legitimacy
  and opponents will notice.
- Map what each partner wants from the coalition (self-interest is legitimate: members,
  funding, profile, a policy win) and what they will never accept.
- Watch for partners with conflicting funders or political alignments that will force
  an early exit.

## 3. Write it down: the founding document
A short **unity statement or MOU**:
- **Purpose and scope:** the specific goal(s); what the coalition will *not* take
  positions on (members keep independence outside scope).
- **Membership tiers** and obligations.
- **Decision rules:** who decides what, quorum, consensus vs voting, how fast-moving
  decisions (press responses) are made between meetings — e.g. a small steering group
  with a 24-hour silence procedure.
- **Speaking for the coalition:** who may sign letters or speak to press using the
  coalition's name; use of logos.
- **Money:** who holds funds (a fiscal host / backbone organisation), how grants are
  shared, how fundraising competition with members is avoided.
- **Exit and dissolution:** how members leave, and a review or sunset date.

## 4. Running it
- **Coordinator/backbone:** someone paid to keep the work moving — agendas, notes,
  follow-ups, tracking commitments. Unstaffed coalitions drift.
- **Meetings:** short, decision-focused, with action items and owners circulated within
  24 hours.
- **Credit and turf:** rotate spokespeople, name all members in press releases, agree in
  advance who leads on which lane (e.g. legal analysis vs field turnout).
- **Communication:** a shared calendar and a single channel for urgent decisions.

## 5. Handling conflict and compromise
- The hardest moment: the target offers a partial deal. Agree **before** negotiations
  what counts as a win, and a process for deciding whether to accept an offer.
- Separate disagreement about tactics (negotiable) from disagreement about principles
  (may mean a member steps out on that issue without leaving).
- Address conflicts directly in one-to-one conversations before they hit the full group.

## 6. Closing or transforming
After a win or loss, evaluate together, celebrate shared credit, then decide: dissolve,
reset with a new goal, or formalise into a standing alliance. Zombie coalitions that
meet without a goal drain member goodwill.

## Gotchas
- Lowest-common-denominator positions can neuter the coalition; allow member
  organisations to push harder independently where scope permits.
- Shared bank accounts and joint lobbying may create legal and reporting obligations
  for the fiscal host — check charity, tax and lobbying rules in your jurisdiction.
- A dominant member that controls all money will control decisions in practice.

## Verify
- [ ] Written MOU with purpose, decision rules, spokesperson rules and exit terms.
- [ ] Each member's interests and red lines are recorded.
- [ ] A rapid-decision process exists for between-meeting events.
- [ ] Pre-agreed process for accepting or rejecting partial deals.
