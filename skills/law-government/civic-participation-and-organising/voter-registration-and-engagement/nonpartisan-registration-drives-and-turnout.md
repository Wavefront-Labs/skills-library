# Nonpartisan voter registration drives and turnout

**Goal:** help eligible people register and vote, especially groups who vote at lower
rates, while staying strictly nonpartisan and inside the rules for third-party
registration. Requirements vary sharply by country and, in the US, by state; build
your plan from the election authority's current guidance.

## 1. Legal groundwork (before the first event)
- **Who registers voters?** Some places allow anyone to help; others require
  registration of drive organisers, training, or deputisation (e.g. some US states).
- **Form handling deadlines:** collected forms may have to be returned within a set
  number of days (sometimes very short) regardless of the overall registration deadline.
  Missing that window can disenfranchise people and expose organisers to penalties.
- **Online registration:** where available (e.g. UK gov.uk register-to-vote, many US
  states), helping people register on their own device avoids form custody issues.
- **Nonpartisan limits:** charities and tax-exempt organisations typically cannot support
  or oppose candidates or parties. Don't ask party preference, don't target by
  partisanship, don't wear campaign gear.
- **Data:** what may you keep? Many places restrict copying or using personal data from
  forms. Get consent for follow-up contact separately.

## 2. Event design
- Go where unregistered people are: naturalisation ceremonies, campuses, high-school
  senior years, community colleges, housing moves, faith services, transit hubs.
- Staff with trained volunteers who can answer eligibility questions accurately, or say
  "I'll check" — wrong answers about eligibility (criminal record rules, residency,
  students) cause real harm.
- Check forms on the spot for completeness: signature, date, ID number fields, address.
- Offer materials in the community's languages and accessible formats.

## 3. Voter education (neutral content only)
Cover: deadlines, how to check registration, ID requirements, polling place lookup,
early and postal voting options, what to do if your name isn't on the list (provisional
ballots where available), accessibility accommodations. Use official sources and link
to them; nonpartisan guides covering *all* candidates are generally acceptable if
genuinely balanced.

## 4. Turnout
What research consistently supports (effect sizes vary by context):
- **Personal contact beats mass contact:** door knocks and relational asks from people
  you know outperform robocalls and generic mail.
- **Make a plan:** asking people when, where and how they'll vote increases turnout.
- **Reminders** close to the deadline and election day, by text or call, with
  practical details.
- **Pledge cards** returned to the voter before election day.
- Social pressure messages work but can produce backlash — avoid shaming.

## 5. Tracking
Count: forms collected, forms submitted (with dates), online registrations assisted,
contacts made, plans made. Where lawful, match against the voter file later to measure
registration success and turnout.

## Gotchas
- Never discard a completed form, even if you suspect it's incomplete or a duplicate — submit
  it and let officials decide (many jurisdictions require this).
- Non-citizen and ineligible registration attempts must be gently prevented; misinformation
  here harms the person.
- Partisan drift (a volunteer adding "vote for X") jeopardises the organisation's status.
- Polling-place activity is regulated (distance limits, electioneering bans).

## Verify
- [ ] Current rules for third-party drives, form return windows and data use are written down.
- [ ] Volunteers trained and tested on eligibility answers.
- [ ] Every collected form is logged and submitted within the deadline.
- [ ] Materials are nonpartisan, translated and sourced from official information.
