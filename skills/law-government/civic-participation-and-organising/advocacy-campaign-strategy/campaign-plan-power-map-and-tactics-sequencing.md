# Advocacy campaign plan: power map and tactics sequencing

**Goal:** turn "we want X" into a written plan that names who can give you X, what
will move them, and the order of tactics that builds enough pressure by a deadline.
Uses the widely taught strategy-chart approach (popularised by the Midwest Academy).
Lobbying registration and charity/non-profit political limits vary by jurisdiction.

## 1. Goals and demand
- **Long-term goal:** the change in the world ("safe drinking water in every school").
- **Intermediate goal:** this campaign's win ("the district funds lead testing and
  filter replacement in the next budget").
- **Short-term goals:** stepping stones that build capacity (a public hearing date, a
  council member's pledge).
- Your **demand** must be specific, measurable, deliverable by one decision-maker, and
  have a date. "Raise awareness" is not a demand.

## 2. Theory of change
State in one paragraph: *If we do A and B, then target T will do X because of reasons
R* (electoral pressure, reputational cost, cost/benefit, legal obligation, internal
champion). Test it: what does the target care about? What has moved them before?

## 3. Power mapping
1. **Primary target:** the person (not institution) with power to grant the demand.
2. **Secondary targets:** people with power over the primary — donors, voters, party
   leaders, board members, the press, peers.
3. Plot everyone on a grid: **support ↔ opposition** (x-axis) vs **influence over the
   target** (y-axis). Focus on high-influence people who are neutral or soft.
4. **Your own power:** members, voters in the district, money, expertise, allies,
   legitimacy (affected people speaking for themselves).
5. **Opposition:** who loses if you win, and what will they do?

## 4. Tactics sequencing
Tactics are **means of applying power to targets**, chosen for their effect on the
target — not because the group enjoys them.
- Order roughly from low to high escalation: request meeting → delegation with
  constituents → letters/calls/petitions → public hearing testimony → media and op-eds
  → accountability event → lawful protest / direct action → electoral or legal leverage.
- Each tactic should **build** (more people, more leaders, more media) and have a
  **specific ask** of the target.
- Leave room to escalate. Starting at maximum leaves nowhere to go.
- Ask: Does it fit our members' comfort and capacity? Is it legal? Does it risk
  alienating persuadable allies?

## 5. Timeline and decision points
Work backward from the **decision date** (budget vote, rule deadline, election). Put
each tactic on the calendar with owners and turnout targets, and schedule **decision
points** to evaluate: did the target move? Escalate, pivot, or claim a partial win.

## 6. One-page strategy chart
Columns: Goals | Organisational considerations (resources, what you want to gain,
problems) | Constituents, allies, opponents | Targets (primary, secondary) | Tactics.
If you can't fill a column, that's your planning gap.

## Gotchas
- Targeting an institution ("the government") instead of a decision-maker produces
  vague tactics.
- Coalition partners may have different bottom lines — agree in advance what counts as
  a win (see coalition-building skill).
- Paid lobbying, campaign spending near elections, and charity political activity can
  trigger registration or reporting duties. Get advice for your entity type.

## Verify
- [ ] The demand names one decision-maker and a date.
- [ ] Power map identifies at least three secondary targets with a route to each.
- [ ] Tactics escalate and each has an ask, owner, date and turnout target.
- [ ] Decision points are scheduled before the target's decision date.
