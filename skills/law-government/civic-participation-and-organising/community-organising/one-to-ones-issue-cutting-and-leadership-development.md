# Community organising: one-to-ones, issue cutting and leadership

**Goal:** build a durable base of people who act together on shared interests — not
a one-off protest. The method below is the common core of broad-based organising
traditions (Alinsky / Industrial Areas Foundation, Citizens UK, tenant and labour
organising). Laws on assembly, charities' political activity and lobbying vary by
country; check them before public action.

## 1. The one-to-one (relational meeting)
A 30–45 minute, face-to-face conversation whose purpose is to discover what someone
cares about and whether they have energy to act — not to recruit them to your issue.
- **Open with why you asked** ("I'm meeting people on this estate to find out what
  matters to them").
- **Ask about stories, not opinions:** "What brought you here?", "What makes you angry
  about living here?", "Who do you talk to about it?"
- **Listen 70% of the time.** Probe self-interest — the concrete thing in their life,
  not abstract values.
- **Agitate gently:** "What would it take to change that?"
- **Close with a next step** — meet someone else, come to a house meeting — and note
  who has a following (people others listen to).
- Log: name, contact, interests, relationships, follow-up. Organisers aim for a steady
  weekly quota (commonly 10–15) — volume is what builds a base.

## 2. Leaders, not just activists
A **leader** has followers: when they ask, people show up. Test it by giving them a
small task with a turnout target. Develop leaders by:
- Giving real responsibility with support, then **evaluating** afterwards.
- Pairing them with experienced leaders; training on power, self-interest, negotiation.
- Not doing for people what they can do themselves (the "iron rule").

## 3. From problem to issue
A **problem** is broad ("housing is bad"). An **issue** is a specific, winnable demand
with a target who can deliver it ("the landlord fixes the lifts in Block C within 60 days").
Good issues are:
- **Widely and deeply felt** (from your one-to-ones, not your assumptions);
- **Winnable** within a timeframe that keeps momentum;
- **Concrete** with a decision-maker who can say yes;
- **Building**: they grow the organisation, develop leaders, and set up the next fight.
Run a **research action** — leaders meet officials to learn how decisions are made and
who decides — before going public.

## 4. Actions and escalation
- Start with the least confrontational step that could work (a meeting with a clear ask).
- Escalate if ignored: public accountability assembly, media, delegation to a higher
  authority, lawful demonstration (see direct-action skill for safety and legal
  observers).
- Every action has: a target, a specific demand, a turnout goal, roles (chair,
  timekeeper, testimony, negotiator), and a **yes/no ask** of the target in public.
- **Evaluate** immediately after: turnout vs goal, what the target said, how leaders
  performed, how people feel. Evaluation is where learning happens.

## Gotchas
- Organisers who talk more than they listen build mailing lists, not bases.
- Picking an unwinnable first issue burns out new leaders.
- Don't let one charismatic person become the only spokesperson.
- Funding with strings (including some charitable rules) can limit partisan or
  lobbying activity — know the rules for your entity type.

## Verify
- [ ] Your issue came out of documented one-to-ones, not your own list.
- [ ] Every action has a target, a demand, a turnout number and a scheduled evaluation.
- [ ] You can name leaders by their following, not by their enthusiasm.
- [ ] Legal limits on your organisation's political activity are checked.
