# Hearing testimony and comment submissions

**Goal:** put your evidence and view into the official record in a form decision-makers
and staff can use — at a council meeting, legislative hearing, planning hearing, or a
written consultation. Procedures differ by body; always read that body's rules.

## 1. Find the opening
- **Agendas** are usually posted a few days before meetings (often a legal minimum
  notice). Subscribe to the body's notices and check the clerk's web page.
- **Consultations / calls for evidence** (e.g. UK government consultations, parliamentary
  select committees) and **notice-and-comment** rulemaking (e.g. US regulations.gov) list
  a closing date — late comments are usually not considered.
- **Sign-up rules:** speaker cards, advance online registration, deadlines (sometimes
  the day before), whether remote testimony is allowed, and time limits (commonly
  2–3 minutes for public comment, sometimes less when many sign up).
- Local bodies often have a general public-comment slot plus item-specific comment —
  speak on the item if you want to influence that vote.

## 2. Spoken testimony (three minutes)
Structure, roughly 350–400 words read aloud:
1. **Who you are** and why you're affected (resident of X, business owner, nurse). 10–15 s.
2. **Your position** in one sentence, referencing the item number or bill.
3. **One or two concrete reasons** — a personal story or a specific fact. Specifics stick:
   "the bus stop outside my building flooded six times this winter" beats "infrastructure
   is failing".
4. **A specific ask:** vote yes/no, amend section 3, delay until a study is done.
5. Thank them; offer written details.
Practise with a timer. Speak to the members, not the audience. Don't repeat the previous
speaker — say "I agree with the last speaker, and add…".

## 3. Written comments
- Lead with your position and ask; then evidence; then answers to the consultation's
  questions if it poses them (staff summarise by question).
- **Cite the document:** section numbers, page numbers, the docket or reference number.
- Original analysis and data weigh more than mass-produced form letters. Agencies
  typically treat identical comments as one viewpoint, not a vote count.
- For rulemaking, comments that raise specific problems create a record the agency must
  respond to — vague opposition usually doesn't (see rulemaking-participation skill).
- Keep a copy and the submission receipt.

## 4. Deputations and meetings
Some councils allow a formal deputation (a group addressing a committee with more time).
Request it in writing by the deadline; name your spokespeople; bring a one-page handout.
Meeting with members or staff beforehand often matters more than the hearing itself.

## 5. Being effective as a group
Coordinate speakers so each covers a different angle; bring people visibly (stickers,
standing when asked); submit a written summary with the group's names. Respect decorum
rules — disruption can lead to removal and lose sympathy.

## Gotchas
- Testimony becomes part of the public record — including your name and sometimes
  address. Check what's published before submitting personal details.
- Content about third parties can be defamatory; stick to facts you can support.
- Quasi-judicial hearings (e.g. some planning or licensing decisions) restrict contact
  with decision-makers outside the hearing ("ex parte" rules) — check before lobbying.

## Verify
- [ ] You know the deadline and sign-up method for the specific item.
- [ ] Spoken testimony fits the time limit with a timer and ends with a clear ask.
- [ ] Written comment cites the document's sections and reference number.
- [ ] You kept a submission receipt.
