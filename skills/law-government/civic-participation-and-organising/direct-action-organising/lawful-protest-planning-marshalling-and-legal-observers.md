# Lawful protest planning: marshalling and legal observers

**Goal:** run a protest or direct action that is safe, disciplined and achieves its
strategic purpose, with the legal side planned rather than improvised. Protest law
varies widely (and has changed recently in several countries, including the UK); get
current local legal guidance before every action. Deliberate civil disobedience carries
real legal consequences — participants must choose it knowingly.

## 1. Purpose first
Write the action's objective in one line linked to the campaign (see advocacy-campaign
skill): who is the target, what do you want them to see or feel, what's the ask? Pick
the form (rally, march, vigil, sit-in, banner drop, picket) that serves that.

## 2. Legal checklist
- **Notice/permit:** many places require notice of processions or permits for using
  parks, streets or amplification (e.g. advance written notice for UK processions;
  US city permits). Public assemblies may also have conditions imposed.
- **Location rules:** private property, government buildings, schools, hospitals,
  critical infrastructure (some jurisdictions have specific offences), noise limits.
- **Specific tactics** (locking on, blocking roads, masks) may carry specific offences —
  know them.
- If any participants plan arrestable actions, separate them clearly (affinity groups)
  from people who must avoid arrest (non-citizens, people on probation, carers).

## 3. Roles
- **Action lead** — decides, with a clear chain if they're unavailable.
- **Marshals/stewards** (rule of thumb: 1 per 20–50 participants, more for marches)
  in visible vests; trained in route, de-escalation and emergency procedures.
- **Police liaison** — single point of contact; doesn't negotiate away the action's
  purpose without the lead.
- **Legal observers** — independent, identifiable, trained (e.g. National Lawyers Guild
  in the US; various groups elsewhere). They document police conduct, arrests and
  injuries; they do not participate.
- **Medics/first aid**, **media spokesperson**, **accessibility lead**, **jail/arrest
  support** off-site.

## 4. Planning documents
- Route or site map with assembly, dispersal, first-aid and accessible points, toilets,
  exits.
- **Risk assessment:** counter-protesters, vehicle attack risk, heat/cold, crowd density,
  kettling, medical emergencies. Mitigations for each.
- **Communications:** a channel for marshals, signal for dispersal, backup if phones fail.
- **Bust cards:** wallet cards with the legal-support number, basic rights, and advice to
  give only required identity details. Content must be checked for the jurisdiction.

## 5. Nonviolence discipline
Agree and publish guidelines (no violence or threats, no property damage, follow marshal
instructions, no alcohol/drugs). Train in **de-escalation**: calm voice, open posture,
create space, redirect agitators, don't engage counter-protesters, marshals link arms
to separate groups. Run a short training before the action; role-play a provocation.

## 6. Arrest and aftermath
- Arrest support records names (with consent), times, locations, and tracks people
  through custody; arranges lawyers and meets people on release.
- Collect observer notes, video and medical reports within 24 hours for any complaint
  or defence.
- **Debrief** the same day: what worked, injuries, legal follow-ups, wellbeing.

## Gotchas
- Organisers may bear legal responsibility for conditions and permits — read them.
- Livestreams can identify participants; set norms on filming faces.
- Don't let a confrontational fringe redefine your action; marshals must know the plan
  for handling it.

## Verify
- [ ] Notice/permit and location rules confirmed in writing for this action.
- [ ] Every role is assigned with a backup; marshals trained.
- [ ] Legal observers and arrest support briefed with the legal number.
- [ ] Risk assessment and dispersal plan circulated to marshals.
