# Writing the campaign plan: win number, budget and timeline

**Goal:** a written plan that turns "we want to win" into a number of votes, a set of
people to get them from, the money and people needed, and a calendar — so every later
decision can be checked against it.

## 1. Research the race
- Electoral system (first-past-the-post, runoff, ranked-choice, list PR, multi-member
  wards) — it changes the maths entirely.
- Past results for this seat for 3–4 comparable elections: registered voters, turnout,
  vote shares, and how they vary by precinct/ward/polling district.
- Demographics and changes since; the opponent(s) and their base; the candidate's
  strengths and liabilities (do your **own** opposition research on your candidate first).
- Key dates: nomination deadlines, registration deadline, postal/absentee and early
  voting windows, finance reporting dates, election day. **Rules vary by jurisdiction**.

## 2. The win number
1. **Projected turnout** = registered voters × expected turnout (average of comparable
   past elections, adjusted for this year's context).
2. **Win number** (two-way FPTP race) = projected turnout × 50% + 1, plus a safety
   margin (often several points). In multi-candidate races, use the expected winning
   share from history. Example: 60,000 registered × 45% = 27,000 voters → win number
   ≈ 13,501; with a 4-point cushion, aim for ~14,600.
3. **Vote goal breakdown:**
   - **Base**: reliable supporters likely to vote (from past results/voter file models).
   - **Turnout targets**: supporters who vote irregularly — need GOTV.
   - **Persuasion targets**: likely voters who are undecided or soft.
   Base + turnout gain + persuasion gain ≥ vote goal. If not, the plan is unrealistic —
   change targets or tactics, not the arithmetic.
4. Convert to **contacts**: if persuasion conversations move ~1 in N people and doors
   answer at X%, you can compute how many doors you need to knock (see
   voter-contact-and-field).

## 3. Message and strategy summary
One paragraph: why the candidate is running, the contrast with the opponent, and the
2–3 issues for the target voters. (Detail in political-strategy-and-messaging.)

## 4. Budget and cash flow
- Line items: staff, office, voter file/tech, field materials, digital ads, mail,
  broadcast, events, polling, legal/compliance, fundraising costs, election-day
  operations, reserve (5–10%).
- Allocate by what moves **target voters** in this race; local races are mostly field
  and mail, larger ones more paid media.
- **Cash-flow plan**: when money must be raised vs spent; ad reservations and printing
  need cash before the money peaks. Track burn rate weekly.
- Check legal spending limits and reporting categories (see campaign-finance-compliance).

## 5. Organisation
Minimum roles (one person may hold several in small races): candidate, campaign
manager (decides day to day), finance/fundraising, treasurer/compliance (legally
required in many places), field/volunteer coordinator, communications, digital,
data. Weekly meeting with metrics against plan.

## 6. Timeline — work back from election day
GOTV period and postal/early voting window → persuasion phase → ID/organising phase →
launch → setup (team, money, data). Put fundraising targets, volunteer recruitment
goals and contact goals by week. **Candidate time** is the scarcest resource: schedule
call time, doors and events deliberately.

## Gotchas
- Plans built on the candidate's hopes rather than turnout history.
- Spending early on visibility (signs, merchandise) that doesn't move votes.
- Forgetting that postal/early votes are banked weeks before election day.

## Verify
- [ ] Win number derived from documented turnout history with a margin.
- [ ] Base + turnout + persuasion targets add up to the vote goal.
- [ ] Budget has a cash-flow plan and reserve; spending limits checked.
- [ ] Roles assigned, including a legally compliant treasurer where required.
- [ ] Weekly targets on a backwards timeline including early-voting dates.
