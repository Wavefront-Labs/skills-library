# Negotiating a coalition agreement

**Goal:** turn an inconclusive election into a stable government (or a deliberate
decision to stay in opposition) on terms your party can live with and sell to its
members. Models: parliamentary systems with coalition traditions (Germany, the
Netherlands, the Nordic countries, New Zealand, Ireland) and the occasional Westminster
hung parliament. Formation conventions, the head of state's role and time limits are
set by each constitution and by convention — confirm the rules for your system.

## 1. Prepare before polling day
- A small, authorised **negotiating team** (3-5 people) and a mandate from the party's
  governing bodies on how a deal will be ratified.
- Ranked policy priorities: **red lines** (non-negotiable), high priorities, tradeable
  items. Keep red lines few; too many make you unpartnerable.
- Cost your manifesto so that trade-offs can be evaluated quickly.
- Scenario analysis: likely seat outcomes, viable combinations, each partner's red
  lines from their manifesto and history.
- Civil-service support: in many systems officials can provide factual and costing
  support to all parties during formation — find out how to request it.

## 2. Choose the arrangement
| Form | What you give | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| Full coalition | collective responsibility for all decisions | ministries, shared programme |
| Minority government with confidence-and-supply | committed votes on confidence and budgets | policy concessions; freedom elsewhere |
| Issue-by-issue support | little | maximal independence, little influence |
Smaller partners in full coalitions often suffer electorally; weigh that against influence.

## 3. Run the negotiations
1. Agree **process first**: venue, confidentiality, who speaks to the media (ideally
   nobody detailed), timetable, use of policy working groups.
2. Start with areas of agreement to build trust; leave hardest issues to the principals.
3. Trade across issues: concede where you care less in return for priorities.
4. Make every commitment specific enough to test: a measurable outcome, a budget line, a
   date, or a bill.
5. Keep a single negotiating text controlled by one drafter; date and version it.

## 4. Portfolios and offices
Allocate ministries/cabinet seats (a widely observed pattern is roughly in proportion to
seats contributed — "Gamson's law" — with bargaining around it), plus junior ministers,
committee chairs and presiding officers. Ministries matter for delivering your priorities;
match portfolios to the commitments you won.

## 5. Governance mechanisms in the agreement
- A coalition committee of party leaders meeting regularly.
- Rules on collective responsibility and named **agreements to differ** on specific issues.
- Dispute escalation steps, and what happens if they fail.
- Procedures for unforeseen issues not in the programme.
- Review point (e.g. mid-term) and the conditions for exit.

## 6. Ratification and party management
Present the deal to members honestly: wins, concessions and why. Follow the party's
rules for approval (executive vote, special conference or membership ballot). Brief
MPs/legislators first so they hear it from leadership, not the media. Plan
communications so that each partner can claim credible wins.

## 7. Living with the coalition
Track delivery of the agreement publicly and internally; maintain a separate party
identity (distinct messaging, policy development for the next election); manage backbench
dissent through the whip and regular consultation.

## Gotchas
- Vague commitments ("will consider") that the partner never delivers.
- Leaks during talks that box in negotiators.
- Neglecting the junior posts and committee chairs where real influence often lies.
- No exit plan — partners leave messily at the worst possible moment.

## Verify
- [ ] Negotiating team mandated and ratification route agreed before talks.
- [ ] Red lines, priorities and tradeables written and ranked.
- [ ] Every commitment in the text specific and testable.
- [ ] Portfolios match the policy commitments won.
- [ ] Governance, dispute and exit mechanisms in the agreement; party ratification completed.
