# Message box, opposition research, debate prep and rapid response

**Goal:** a clear, repeated argument for why target voters should choose your
candidate, defended against attack and pressed against the opponent.

## 1. The message box (Tully box)
Four quadrants:
| What we say about us | What we say about them |
|---|---|
| **What they say about themselves** | **What they say about us** |
Fill all four honestly. Your message is the **contrast** between top-left and top-
right, prepared for the bottom-right attacks. Then compress into:
- a one-sentence **core message** (why you, why now, why not them);
- 2–3 **proof points** per issue (record, plan, story);
- a stump speech and a 30-second version.
Message discipline: every interview, leaflet and ad returns to the core. Repetition
feels boring to the campaign long before voters notice it.

## 2. Test it
- **Polling** (benchmark poll early; tracking later): vote intention, favourability,
  issue priorities, and message tests — read arguments for both sides, measure movement
  among undecided/target groups.
- **Focus groups** with target voters to hear language and reactions (not for numbers).
- Cheap tests: digital ad A/B variants, canvass scripts with recorded responses.
- Be aware of publication rules for polls and push-polling prohibitions — **these vary
  by jurisdiction**.

## 3. Research: self first, then opposition
**Self-research (vulnerability study):** everything an opponent could find about your
candidate — voting record, public statements, social media history, business and
property records, court filings, financial disclosures, past campaign finance. Prepare
answers before anyone asks.
**Opposition research:** the same on opponents, using **public and lawfully obtained**
sources: legislative votes and attendance, speeches and media, filings, court records,
corporate registries, property records, campaign-finance reports, archived web pages.
Every claim footnoted to a document; a wrong attack is a gift to the opponent and can
be defamatory. Never use hacked, stolen or illegally recorded material; avoid family
members who aren't public figures.

## 4. Debate preparation
- Briefing book: format and rules, opponent's likely lines and attacks, your answers,
  key numbers, 2–3 "moments" you want (a contrast line, a story).
- **Mock debates** with a stand-in who studies the opponent's style; time answers to the
  real limits; record and review.
- Technique: answer briefly then **bridge** back to the message ("The real question
  is…"); have a prepared response to the most likely attack; don't repeat the
  opponent's frame; speak to the camera/audience, not the opponent.
- Plan the spin room and post-debate content (clips, fact-checks) in advance.

## 5. Rapid response
- A small team (research, comms, digital, lawyer on call) monitoring news, social media,
  opponent channels and events in real time.
- **Pre-cleared response library** for predictable attacks.
- Decision rule on whether to respond: does the attack reach target voters and is it
  gaining traction? Responding can amplify a story nobody saw.
- Turnaround in hours (or minutes for live events); accurate beats fast when facts are
  unclear.
- **Inoculate** against known vulnerabilities before the opponent raises them.

## Gotchas
- Message built around what the candidate cares about rather than what target voters
  care about.
- Attack material that isn't sourced to documents.
- Election-period rules on broadcast fairness, imprint/disclaimer requirements on
  materials and online ads, and defamation law differ by jurisdiction.

## Verify
- [ ] Message box complete; one-sentence core message and proof points written.
- [ ] Messages tested with target voters, not only supporters.
- [ ] Self-research done before opposition research; every claim sourced.
- [ ] At least two timed mock debates held.
- [ ] Rapid-response library pre-cleared; response decision rule agreed.
