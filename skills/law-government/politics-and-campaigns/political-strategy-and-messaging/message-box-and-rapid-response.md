# Message box and rapid response

**Goal:** settle what the campaign says about itself and its opponent, test that it
moves the voters who matter, say it consistently, and answer attacks fast without being
knocked off message. Applies to candidate and issue campaigns; examples are drawn from
US and UK practice. Rules on paid advertising, broadcast fairness, imprints/"paid for
by" disclaimers and defamation vary by jurisdiction — involve counsel before attack
material goes out.

## 1. Research first
- **Self-research (vulnerability study):** go through your own candidate's public record,
  votes, statements, finances, business interests, court records and social media
  history as an opponent would. Everything found here gets a prepared answer.
- **Opposition research:** the same on opponents, from **public and lawfully obtained**
  sources only (voting records, filings, court and property records, published
  statements, archived media). Every claim gets a source document; anything you cannot
  document does not get used.
- **Voter research:** polling and focus groups to find what target voters care about and
  which words they use.

## 2. Build the message box
| | About us | About them |
|---|---|---|
| **What we say** | Q1: our case for ourselves | Q2: our contrast/case against them |
| **What they say** | Q3: their attack on us | Q4: their case for themselves |
Fill all four quadrants honestly. The campaign's job is to keep the debate in Q1 and Q2,
pre-empt Q3 with prepared responses, and undermine Q4. Distil Q1 into a one-sentence
message and three proof points (record, plan, values) that target voters believe.

## 3. Test it
- Message testing in polls: split samples hear different messages; compare movement
  among persuadable voters, not the whole sample.
- Focus groups (6-10 people per group, separate groups by key demographic) to hear
  language and reactions — directional, not statistically representative.
- Discard messages that only excite your base unless the plan is mobilisation.

## 4. Message discipline
- A one-page message document all spokespeople use: core message, proof points,
  approved facts with sources, phrases to use and avoid.
- Repeat it — voters hear it far less often than the staff do.
- Every event, ad and press release maps back to Q1 or Q2.

## 5. Debate preparation
1. Briefing book: format, rules, moderators' past questions, opponent's lines.
2. Prepare answers to the 20 most likely questions, each pivoting to the message within
   30 seconds; and 3-5 planned contrast moments.
3. Mock debates with a stand-in for the opponent who has studied them closely; record and
   review; at least two full run-throughs.
4. Prepare a short opening and closing, timed exactly.
5. Post-debate: spokespeople ready in the spin room with agreed lines; social clips cut.

## 6. Rapid response protocol
- Monitoring: opponent channels, media, social platforms, legislative records — assign
  named monitors by shift during intense periods.
- Triage within minutes: Is it true? Is it gaining traction? Does responding amplify it?
- Approval chain: who can clear a response at speed (e.g. comms director plus counsel for
  legal-risk items). Pre-approved responses from the Q3 quadrant go out immediately.
- Respond with documented facts, then pivot to message. Correct your own errors fast.

## Gotchas
- Using unverified oppo — a false attack becomes the story, and can be defamatory.
- Obtaining material unlawfully (hacked data, pretexting, trespass) — illegal and ruinous.
- Testing messages on the whole electorate instead of the persuadable universe.
- Responding to everything; small attacks often die without oxygen.

## Verify
- [ ] Self-research completed, with prepared answers for every vulnerability.
- [ ] Oppo claims each backed by a lawful, citable source document.
- [ ] Message box filled in all four quadrants; core message tested with persuadables.
- [ ] Message document distributed; debate prep includes recorded mock debates.
- [ ] Rapid-response monitors, triage rules and approval chain named in writing.
