# Sitting as arbitrator: procedural orders to award

**Goal:** run an arbitration efficiently and fairly and deliver an award that survives
challenge at the seat and is enforceable abroad. Models: UNCITRAL Model Law seats and
England & Wales (Arbitration Act 1996, as amended), with institutional rules (ICC, LCIA,
ICDR, SIAC etc.) layered on top — the chosen rules and the seat's law govern; deadlines
and formalities differ. For the party-counsel view see
`law-government.international-and-human-rights-law.international-arbitration.running-a-commercial-or-investor-state-arbitration`.

## 1. Appointment and disclosure
1. Run conflicts against both parties, affiliates and counsel; disclose anything that
   might give rise to justifiable doubts (IBA Guidelines' Orange List is a practical
   checklist). Disclosure is a continuing duty.
2. Confirm availability realistically — delay is the most common user complaint.
3. Agree fees/deposits per the rules; sign the statement of independence.

## 2. Jurisdiction
- The tribunal can rule on its own jurisdiction (*kompetenz-kompetenz*). Decide
  objections early as a preliminary issue or join them to the merits — explain why.
- Check: valid agreement, parties bound, dispute within scope, pre-arbitration steps
  (negotiation/mediation tiers) complied with, claim not time-barred where that is jurisdictional.

## 3. First case management conference → Procedural Order No. 1
Cover in PO1 (ICC also requires Terms of Reference):
| Topic | Typical decision |
|---|---|
| Timetable | Memorials or pleadings sequence, dates, hearing window |
| Document production | IBA Rules as guidance; Redfern schedule; narrow and specific requests |
| Witnesses & experts | Written statements stand as evidence-in-chief; expert meetings and joint report |
| Hearing | Venue/virtual, chess-clock time allocation, transcripts, interpreters |
| Communications | Email to all, no ex parte contact |
| Confidentiality, data protection, cybersecurity | Protocol |
| Costs | Submissions after hearing; format of statements |
Consult the parties, then decide — fairness and efficiency are both duties
(e.g. s.33 Arbitration Act 1996).

## 4. Running the hearing
- Pre-hearing conference (2–4 weeks before): agree witness order, bundles, timing.
- Keep the chess clock; intervene to focus cross-examination on issues that matter.
- Ask your questions — better now than drawing an inference later the parties never addressed.
- Consider witness conferencing ("hot-tubbing") for experts.
- Close by asking each party to confirm it had a full opportunity to present its case.

## 5. Deliberation and award
1. Deliberate promptly while the evidence is fresh; agree an issues list and draft by
   allocated sections (chair usually holds the pen).
2. Award structure: parties and tribunal → procedural history → agreement and seat →
   jurisdiction → issues → for each issue: positions, analysis, finding → interest →
   costs (with reasons) → **dispositive section** → date, seat, signatures.
3. Decide **every** claim and defence presented (omission is a ground for challenge)
   and nothing not asked for.
4. Don't decide on a point neither party argued; if needed, invite submissions first.
5. Check formal requirements of the seat and rules (writing, signatures, reasons,
   date, seat; institutional scrutiny where applicable).

## Gotchas
- Late disclosure of a relationship is a leading cause of successful challenges.
- Over-generous document production converts arbitration into litigation by another name.
- Ambiguous dispositive wording (currency, interest start date, payer) complicates enforcement.
- Copying large chunks of party submissions into "reasons" invites a lack-of-independence attack.

## Verify
- [ ] Disclosure complete and updated; no ex parte contact logged.
- [ ] Jurisdiction ruled on with reasons.
- [ ] PO1 issued after consultation; timetable kept or variations recorded.
- [ ] Every claim and defence addressed; no surprise reasoning.
- [ ] Award meets seat and rules formalities; dispositive section is precise and enforceable.
