# Supporting elder-council hearings and recording customary settlements

**Goal:** help a customary forum (elders' council, chief's court, village or local
council court) resolve disputes in a way the community accepts **and** the formal
legal system can respect — fair process, a clear record, and referral of what it must
not decide.

This skill is written for a facilitator, community paralegal, clerk or NGO programme
officer working alongside customary authorities, as well as members of those forums.
Customary law is **highly local**: the norms, who may sit, and the forum's legal
status differ by community and state. Learn the specific community's practice and the
national law that recognises (or limits) it before acting.

## 1. Know the boundary with state law
- Find the statute or constitutional provision recognising customary forums, if any
  (some countries have legislated village courts, local council courts or recognised
  traditional authorities; others give them no formal status).
- **Usually outside customary jurisdiction:** serious crime (homicide, rape, serious
  assault), cases involving children's welfare, gender-based violence, and anything
  where the law requires a court. Have a written referral path to police, social
  services and the formal court, and use it.
- Know whether outcomes can be registered, appealed or enforced in state courts.

## 2. Intake
Record: parties, their relationship, the dispute in their words, prior attempts to
resolve it, whether any court or police case is open, and any risk of violence. Ask
each party who should speak for them — in many customs a relative or elder speaks for a
party; make sure the party themself is also heard.

## 3. The hearing
- Fix a date and place both parties can reach, and give notice to both (and to
  affected family heads where custom requires).
- Check the panel: anyone related to a party or with an interest should step aside.
  Encourage inclusion of women and youth members where the forum allows; their
  absence is a common reason women's claims (land, inheritance) are lost.
- Each side presents in turn, with witnesses; panel questions; site visit for land
  and boundary disputes (walk the boundary with neighbours, note landmarks, sketch).
- Deliberation follows local practice (consensus, elder judgment). The facilitator
  does not decide; they help keep turns fair and note what is said.

## 4. Outcomes
Customary outcomes emphasise **restoring relationships**: acknowledgement, apology,
compensation (money, livestock, goods), boundary re-marking, a reconciliation
ceremony or shared meal. Check the outcome against minimum safeguards:
- no corporal punishment, banishment or forced marriage;
- no outcome that strips a woman or child of property or rights the national law gives
  them;
- compensation proportionate and payable.
If an outcome fails these, record the concern and refer onward.

## 5. The settlement record
Write in the local language and, where useful, the official one: date, place, panel
members, parties, the dispute, evidence summary, the decision/agreement, payment or
action deadlines, who confirms completion, and signatures or thumbprints of parties and
panel. Give each party a copy and keep one in a register. For land, include the sketch
and landmarks. A clear record prevents the same dispute returning and helps if it ever
reaches a court.

## Gotchas
- Don't import a lawyer's adversarial model; legitimacy comes from local procedure.
- Equally, "custom" is sometimes invoked to justify discrimination; national
  constitutional rights normally prevail.
- Uncertain: registrability of customary settlements in formal land registries varies widely.

## Verify
- [ ] Case checked against the jurisdiction boundary; excluded matters referred.
- [ ] Both parties notified and personally heard; conflicted panel members stood down.
- [ ] Outcome checked against the minimum safeguards.
- [ ] Written, signed settlement record issued to both parties and registered.
