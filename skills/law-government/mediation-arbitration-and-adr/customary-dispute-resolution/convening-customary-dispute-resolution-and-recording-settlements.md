# Convening customary dispute resolution and recording settlements

**Goal:** resolve a dispute through a forum the community already trusts — elders,
chiefs, a jirga or shura, a village court — in a way that restores relationships, is
fair to weaker parties, and produces a record that holds up if the state system is
later involved. Most disputes in many countries never reach a state court. Examples
draw on East African, South Asian and Pacific practice; whether and how the state
recognises customary outcomes varies (e.g. Kenya's Constitution recognises traditional
mechanisms subject to the Bill of Rights). Each community's custom governs its own procedure.

## 1. Is the forum appropriate for this dispute?
| Usually suitable | Usually unsuitable (refer to the state system) |
|---|---|
| Boundary, grazing and water disputes; debts; minor assaults; family quarrels; inheritance distribution among willing heirs | Serious crimes (homicide may involve customary compensation **and** state prosecution); sexual violence; child protection; disputes with the state or powerful outsiders |
Check state law: some offences cannot be settled privately, and a customary settlement
may not bar prosecution.

## 2. Convening
1. Confirm both parties (and their families or clans, where custom makes the dispute
   collective) accept the forum and its members. Get recusal of elders related to a party.
2. Agree venue, date and who speaks for each side; ensure women, youth or minority
   parties can speak directly or through a chosen representative.
3. Set out the procedure custom prescribes (opening rituals, order of speaking,
   witnesses, oaths) and any agreed adaptations — written down if possible.

## 3. The hearing and deliberation
- Each side states its case and calls witnesses; elders question; community members
  may contribute as custom allows.
- Elders deliberate (sometimes privately), weighing custom, precedent remembered from
  earlier cases, and the goal of restoring relations.
- Outcomes typically combine: acknowledgment and apology, compensation (livestock,
  money, labour), restoration of property, a reconciliation ceremony, future undertakings,
  and guarantors who vouch for compliance.

## 4. Fairness checks (a facilitator's or support NGO's role)
- No party coerced into attending or accepting; consent re-confirmed before the outcome.
- No "settlement" that transfers a woman or girl in marriage or as compensation — this
  breaches human rights and state law nearly everywhere.
- No corporal punishment or banishment contrary to state law.
- Women's property and inheritance rights not traded away by male relatives without
  the woman's own consent.

## 5. Recording the settlement
Write a short settlement record in the local language (with translation if needed):
| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Forum | Name, place, date, presiding elders |
| Parties | Names and representatives (and clans/families if collective) |
| Dispute | Brief description |
| Terms | Each obligation, amount, deadline, guarantor |
| Consent | Statement that parties accept voluntarily; signatures or thumbprints; witnesses |
| Follow-up | Who checks compliance and when |
Keep copies with each party, the forum, and (where a scheme exists) a local registry or
court. Some jurisdictions allow a customary settlement to be adopted or registered by a
state court; check the procedure.

## Gotchas
- Treating the most powerful elder's view as the community's view.
- Collective compensation that a poor family cannot pay, creating new conflict.
- Settlements that resolve the public dispute but bury a private abuse.
- State actors later ignoring an unwritten outcome — write it down.

## Verify
- [ ] Dispute type is within what the forum may lawfully settle.
- [ ] Forum members accepted by both sides; conflicts recused.
- [ ] Weaker parties heard directly; consent freely given.
- [ ] Terms comply with state law and human rights minimums.
- [ ] Written record signed, witnessed, copied; follow-up date set.
