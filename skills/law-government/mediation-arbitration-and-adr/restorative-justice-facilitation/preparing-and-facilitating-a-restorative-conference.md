# Preparing and facilitating a restorative conference

**Goal:** bring the person harmed and the person responsible (with supporters)
together safely so the harm is acknowledged, questions are answered and a realistic
repair agreement is made and kept. Models: UK Restorative Justice Council standards,
UNODC handbook principles, NZ family group conferencing. Where restorative justice
sits in the criminal process — diversion, pre-sentence, post-sentence, in prison —
and its legal effect differ by jurisdiction; confirm the referral pathway and any
reporting duty to the court or police.

Rule zero: **preparation is the work**. Most of a successful conference happens in the
meetings before it.

## 1. Suitability and risk (on referral)
- Responsible person admits (or does not deny) responsibility for the harm. RJ is not
  a forum for deciding guilt.
- Both parties participate voluntarily and can withdraw at any point.
- Risk assessment: ongoing threat, domestic abuse or coercive control (many services
  exclude or apply specialist protocols), sexual harm (specialist only), mental health,
  substance use, literacy and language needs, age and capacity.
- Check timing: too soon after the offence can re-traumatise; ask the person harmed.

## 2. Preparation meetings (separately, often 2+ each)
1. Explain the process, who will attend, what will be asked, confidentiality and its
   limits (safeguarding, new disclosures of offences).
2. Hear their story; ask the restorative questions in private first so nothing on the
   day is a shock to the facilitator.
3. Explore what the harmed person wants: answers, apology, reparation, reassurance it
   won't happen again — and what they don't want.
4. Prepare the responsible person to speak without minimising or blaming; check they
   understand they may hear painful things.
5. Agree supporters for each side; brief them too.
6. Decide the format: face-to-face conference, shuttle (indirect) communication,
   letter exchange, or video — indirect options are legitimate outcomes.

## 3. The conference (typically 1–2 hours)
| Stage | Facilitator prompts (paraphrase the script, don't read stiffly) |
|---|---|
| Welcome & ground rules | Purpose, respect, anyone may take a break |
| Responsible person | What happened? What were you thinking at the time? What have you thought about since? Who has been affected and how? |
| Harmed person | What did you think when it happened? How has it affected you and others? What has been hardest? |
| Supporters | How has this affected you? |
| Responsible person response | What would you like to say now? |
| Repair | What needs to happen to make things as right as possible? |
| Agreement | Draft specific, achievable actions |
| Close | Informal refreshments time — often where reconciliation happens |
The facilitator stays neutral, speaks little, and stops the conference if it becomes
harmful.

## 4. Outcome agreement and follow-through
- SMART actions: apology letter by date, reparation (work, money in instalments),
  attendance at a programme, agreed behaviour (avoidance of contact or of areas).
- Who monitors each item, and how the harmed person learns of completion.
- Record and report per the referral route (court, youth panel, police) — only what
  the parties agreed may be shared, plus any mandatory reporting.
- Follow-up contact with both parties at e.g. 1 and 3 months.

## Gotchas
- Pressuring a harmed person to attend "for the offender's benefit".
- Agreements that look good in the room but are unaffordable or unachievable.
- Under-preparing supporters, who then derail the conference.
- Treating an apology as compulsory — a forced apology harms.

## Verify
- [ ] Suitability and risk assessment recorded; exclusions applied.
- [ ] Each participant prepared separately and consents freely.
- [ ] Format chosen to fit the harmed person's wishes.
- [ ] Agreement is specific, achievable and has monitors and dates.
- [ ] Follow-up done and outcome reported per the referral pathway.
