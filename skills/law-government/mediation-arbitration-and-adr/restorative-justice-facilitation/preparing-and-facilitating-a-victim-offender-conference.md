# Preparing and facilitating a victim-offender conference

**Goal:** bring the person harmed and the person responsible (plus supporters) together
safely so the harm is acknowledged, questions answered and repair agreed — without
re-victimising anyone.

## 1. Referral and suitability
Referrals come from police, courts, youth justice, prisons, schools or self-referral.
Minimum conditions in most practice standards:
- the person responsible **admits** responsibility (not necessarily guilty in law, but
  does not deny the core facts);
- **both participate voluntarily** and can withdraw at any time;
- a **risk assessment** finds it safe (current threats, domestic or sexual abuse
  dynamics, mental health, substance use, gang context, ongoing proceedings).
Domestic abuse and sexual offences are restricted or excluded in many services and
need specialist facilitators — follow your scheme's policy, which **varies by
jurisdiction and provider**.

## 2. Preparation — the bulk of the work (often 2–4 meetings each)
Meet each side separately, usually in person:
- **Person harmed:** what happened for them, effects since, what they want to ask or
  say, what would help repair, who they want as support, their fears. Offer all
  options: face-to-face, **indirect** (letters, recorded messages, shuttle), or nothing.
  Their choice of "not now" is a legitimate outcome.
- **Person responsible:** their account, who has been affected, readiness to hear
  the impact without defending, realistic reparation, supporters.
- Agree ground rules, seating, breaks, who speaks first, and a safety plan (separate
  waiting rooms, arrival times, exit routes).
- Don't proceed if the person responsible is likely to minimise or blame; it harms the
  victim. Postpone and prepare more.

## 3. The conference
A common scripted sequence (e.g. the widely used restorative conference script),
adapted to the people present:
1. Welcome, introductions, purpose; it is not about deciding guilt.
2. **Person responsible:** What happened? What were you thinking at the time? What
   have you thought about since? Who has been affected and how?
3. **Person harmed:** What did you think when it happened? How has it affected you
   and others? What has been the hardest thing?
4. **Supporters** of the person harmed, then of the person responsible: same impact questions.
5. Back to the person responsible: anything you want to say now? (An apology is
   welcome but must not be scripted or demanded.)
6. **Person harmed:** what would you like to come out of today?
7. Discussion to an **outcome agreement**; then informal time (refreshments) — often
   where the most repair happens.
The facilitator stays out of the content: short prompts, silence, and intervention
only to keep it safe.

## 4. Outcome agreement and follow-through
Specific, achievable, measurable: an apology letter by a date, repair or community
work, compensation in instalments, attending a programme, staying away from a place.
Name who monitors, and **tell the person harmed** whether it was completed — failed
follow-through undoes the conference. Report to the referring body only what was agreed.

## Gotchas
- Confidentiality limits: disclosures of new offences or safeguarding risk must be
  reported; say so at preparation.
- In court-linked schemes, check how (and whether) participation affects sentencing
  before promising anything.
- Don't let supporters escalate; brief them as carefully as principals.
- Co-facilitation is good practice for serious harm.

## Verify
- [ ] Admission, voluntariness and risk assessment documented.
- [ ] Each participant prepared separately and offered indirect options.
- [ ] Safety plan in place for arrival, breaks and departure.
- [ ] Outcome agreement is specific, with a named monitor and deadline.
- [ ] Person harmed informed of completion or non-completion.
