# Grievance mediation and collective bargaining conciliation

**Goal:** resolve workplace disputes, from two colleagues who no longer speak to a
union and employer heading for a strike, with an outcome people keep after you leave.

## A. Individual and interpersonal mediation
**Who commissions it matters.** The employer usually pays, so state in writing that you
are neutral, that HR/management receive only the fact that mediation happened and
(if parties agree) the agreement, and that the content is confidential.

Typical one-day structure:
1. **Separate meetings** (60–90 min each): their story, the impact on them, what they
   need to be able to work together. Check it is suitable — not a live disciplinary
   investigation, not harassment that requires a formal finding, no safety risk.
2. **Joint meeting:** uninterrupted opening statements; you summarise each; build an
   agenda of issues in neutral language ("communication about rota changes", not
   "her rudeness").
3. **Problem-solve issue by issue** — concrete behaviours: how they'll greet, how
   requests get made, what happens in team meetings, how future friction is raised.
4. **Written agreement** in their words, signed by both, with a review date (4–8
   weeks). It is normally *not* a contract and not a disciplinary record.

Mediation is **not** appropriate in place of a required investigation (e.g. a formal
discrimination or whistleblowing complaint) unless the complainant genuinely chooses
it after advice; pressure to mediate can itself become a grievance.

## B. Employment-claim settlement / statutory conciliation
Where a legal claim exists, state conciliation services often sit between the parties
(e.g. ACAS early conciliation in Great Britain, mandatory before most tribunal claims;
EEOC mediation in the US; equivalents elsewhere). Settlements typically need a specific
form to waive statutory rights (in Great Britain a COT3 via ACAS, or a settlement
agreement with independent advice). **Rules vary by jurisdiction**; check the waiver
formalities before anyone signs.

## C. Collective disputes and bargaining
**Conciliation of an impasse** (FMCS in the US, ACAS collective conciliation, labour
department conciliators elsewhere):
- Meet each side's bargaining team separately; map every open item with each side's
  last position and the real priority behind it.
- Establish what each side's constituency (members, board) will accept, and the
  ratification route — a deal the members vote down is a failure.
- Package items rather than trading one at a time; use side-letters, phased
  implementation, joint working groups and review clauses to bridge gaps.
- Keep a single "conciliator's draft" text and control versions yourself.
- Watch the legal clock: notice of industrial action, cooling-off periods, ballot
  mandates and contract expiries vary by jurisdiction and drive timing.

**Interest-based bargaining (IBB) facilitation** (before impasse): joint training,
shared ground rules, issues framed as problems, interests listed per issue, options
brainstormed jointly, then objective criteria (comparator wages, CPI, affordability
data) to choose. IBB fails if one team still holds back a positional "real" offer.

## Gotchas
- Never carry a threat of industrial action or dismissal as your own message.
- Union reps are both advocates and employees; protect their standing with members.
- Keep notes minimal and destroy per your retention policy — they can be sought in later litigation.
- A grievance "resolved" by mediation can resurface; build the review date in.

## Verify
- [ ] Suitability checked: no required investigation or safety issue bypassed.
- [ ] Confidentiality and reporting back to the employer agreed in writing.
- [ ] Agreement lists concrete behaviours or terms, with a review date.
- [ ] Statutory waiver formalities checked before any claim is settled.
- [ ] Collective deal has a ratification route and a single controlled text.
