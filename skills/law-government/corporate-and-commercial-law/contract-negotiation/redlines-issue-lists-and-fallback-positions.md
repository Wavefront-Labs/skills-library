# Redlines, issues lists and fallback positions

**Goal:** get from the other side's draft (or yours) to a signed contract that protects
the client's priorities, quickly, without losing track of what was conceded. The process
is the same whatever the governing law.

## 1. Prepare before you mark up
- **Learn the deal:** commercial terms, why the client wants it, timing pressure, the
  relationship (one-off vs strategic), the client's real risk tolerance.
- **Playbook:** for each key clause (liability cap, indemnities, IP, payment, termination,
  data, warranties), write: **ideal position → acceptable fallback(s) → walk-away**.
  In-house teams often have standard playbooks; outside counsel should ask for them.
- **Know market:** what's typical for this deal type and party size — but "market" claims
  are a negotiating tactic, not law.
- **Paper:** whoever drafts first sets the structure and defaults. Offer to draft when you can.

## 2. Marking up
- Work in the native document with **tracked changes**; when exchanging, also send a
  **comparison (blackline)** against the last version *received*, generated by compare
  software — never trust the other side's tracked changes alone.
- **Prioritise.** Mark up what matters; leaving minor stylistic points alone signals good
  faith and saves rounds. A heavily marked draft for a low-value deal costs the relationship.
- Add brief **margin comments** explaining important changes ("Cap aligned to annual fees as
  discussed") — explanations reduce pushback.
- Don't silently change things; hidden changes destroy trust and can create disputes about
  what was agreed.
- Keep internal notes in a separate document, never as comments in a draft you'll send.

## 3. The issues list
After the first exchange, move open points into an **issues list** (table):
| # | Clause | Issue | Our position | Their position | Status / proposed resolution | Owner |
- Group into: legal points lawyers can close, commercial points the business must decide,
  and dependent points.
- Use it to run calls: go through items in order, record agreements in real time, circulate
  after the call.

## 4. Trading
- **Bundle**: trade concessions across issues ("we'll accept your cap if indemnity for IP
  infringement sits outside it").
- Concede on low-value points to you that matter to them; ask what each side really needs
  (interests, not positions).
- Concessions should get smaller as you approach your limit and always be *conditional*
  until the whole deal is agreed ("nothing is agreed until everything is agreed").
- **Escalate** commercially stuck points to business principals with a short note of options.
- Get client sign-off on any move beyond pre-agreed fallbacks.

## 5. Closing the redline
- Produce a **clean execution version**, plus a final compare against the last agreed draft
  to catch late changes.
- Check schedules, exhibits, signatory names and capacities, and dates.
- Confirm signing method (wet ink, e-signature platform, counterparts) is valid for the
  document type and jurisdiction (deeds and some filings have formal requirements).
- Archive the final version, the issues list and key correspondence — they are evidence of
  intent if a dispute arises.

## Gotchas
- Version chaos: name files with date, party and version number; one person controls the
  master.
- Agreeing in email "subject to contract" vs binding — be explicit.
- Metadata in documents (comments, earlier versions) can reveal positions; scrub before sending.

## Verify
- [ ] Playbook positions and walk-aways agreed with the client before markup.
- [ ] Every round includes an independently generated blackline.
- [ ] Issues list current, with owners and status.
- [ ] Execution version compared against the last agreed draft before signing.
