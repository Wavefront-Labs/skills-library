# Jurisdiction, governing law, service abroad and judgment enforcement

**Goal:** win in a court whose judgment can actually be enforced where the defendant's
assets are. Work backwards from enforcement. Private international law is mostly
national or regional (EU regulations), stitched together by Hague Conventions with
uneven ratification — check which instruments bind each state involved, and their
current status (e.g. post-Brexit UK is outside Brussels Ia).

## 1. Start from the assets
Where does the defendant hold assets (bank accounts, receivables, real estate, shares,
ships, IP)? The best forum is often the one whose judgments are easily enforced there,
or the asset state itself. Consider early **freezing orders** (worldwide in some
common-law courts) or attachment.

## 2. Jurisdiction
- **Agreed forum**: exclusive choice-of-court clauses are generally respected (EU
  Brussels Ia Art 25; Hague Choice of Court Convention 2005 among parties to it).
  Check whether it's exclusive or non-exclusive, and its scope.
- **Default rules**: EU — defendant's domicile, plus special rules (place of performance
  for contracts, place of harmful event for torts, consumer and employment protections).
  Common-law courts — presence/service in the jurisdiction, or service out with
  permission through "gateways", plus **forum non conveniens** discretion. US — personal
  jurisdiction under due process (general vs specific jurisdiction).
- **Parallel proceedings**: EU *lis pendens* (first court seised decides jurisdiction,
  with an exception favouring chosen courts); elsewhere anti-suit injunctions and
  stays. Race to file can matter.

## 3. Governing law
- Contract: party choice respected (EU **Rome I**; most systems), subject to overriding
  mandatory rules and consumer/employment protections. Absent choice: characteristic
  performer's habitual residence (Rome I default) or "closest connection"/ "most
  significant relationship" (common law, US Restatement).
- Tort: EU **Rome II** — law of the country where damage occurs, with exceptions;
  common law varies (double actionability historically in some systems; lex loci delicti
  or interest analysis in US states).
- Foreign law is usually a **fact to be proved** by expert evidence in common-law courts;
  civil-law courts may ascertain it themselves.

## 4. Service abroad
- Use the method the destination state accepts, or the judgment may be unenforceable
  there. **Hague Service Convention**: central authority route; some states object to
  postal service or direct service through agents (check the state's declarations).
- EU: Service Regulation for intra-EU service. Non-convention states: letters rogatory via
  diplomatic channels — slow (months to a year+).
- Translations into the official language often required. Build service time into the
  limitation and procedural timetable.

## 5. Evidence abroad
Hague Evidence Convention letters of request; many states declared they won't execute
requests for pre-trial discovery of documents (Art 23 reservations). US courts may order
discovery under their own rules regardless; foreign blocking statutes may conflict.
Under US 28 U.S.C. §1782, parties to foreign proceedings can seek discovery in the US.

## 6. Recognition and enforcement
- Treaty/regional regime: EU Brussels Ia (no exequatur; limited refusal grounds),
  Lugano, Hague Choice of Court 2005, **Hague Judgments Convention 2019** (in force among
  a growing group — check parties), bilateral treaties.
- Otherwise national law: common-law action on the judgment (debt, final and conclusive,
  competent court in the international sense, no fraud/public-policy/natural-justice
  defence); US states' uniform recognition acts; civil-law exequatur often requiring
  reciprocity.
- Typical refusal grounds: lack of jurisdiction of the original court (by the enforcing
  state's standards), no proper notice, fraud, public policy, irreconcilable judgments.
  Non-money judgments, penal and revenue judgments are often not enforceable.
- Arbitration awards are generally easier to enforce (New York Convention) — a reason to
  choose arbitration at contract stage.

## Gotchas
- Defective service is the most common enforcement killer.
- Limitation periods differ and may be characterised as substantive or procedural.
- Currency conversion date and interest rules differ at enforcement.

- [ ] Jurisdiction basis analysed in both the forum and enforcing state.
- [ ] Governing law determined; foreign-law expert lined up if needed.
- [ ] Service method complies with the destination state's declarations.
- [ ] Recognition grounds checked for each enforcement state.
