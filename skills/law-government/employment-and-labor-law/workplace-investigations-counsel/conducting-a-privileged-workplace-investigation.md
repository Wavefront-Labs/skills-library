# Conducting a privileged workplace investigation

**Goal:** establish what probably happened, fairly and promptly, in a way that
supports a lawful decision and survives scrutiny in later litigation.

## 1. Triage and interim measures
- Log the complaint; assess immediate risk (safety, ongoing harassment, evidence
  destruction).
- Interim measures that don't punish the complainant (e.g. change reporting lines,
  paid leave for the respondent). Moving the complainant against their wishes can look retaliatory.
- Issue a **document preservation notice**; secure email, chat, device data and CCTV.

## 2. Scope and structure
- Write a **scope memo**: allegations to determine, policies at issue, timeframe,
  who decides outcomes. Expanding scope later is fine; drifting silently is not.
- **Investigator choice**: HR, in-house counsel, or external investigator — impartial,
  trained, senior enough. External for senior respondents or high-risk matters.
- **Privilege**: if legal advice is the purpose, have counsel retain the investigator
  and direct the work. Know the trade-off — if the employer later relies on the
  investigation as a defence (e.g. Faragher/Ellerth), privilege over it may be waived.
  Some teams separate a fact-finding investigator (non-privileged report) from
  counsel giving advice.

## 3. Interviews
Order: complainant → witnesses → respondent (then re-interview as needed).
- Opening: purpose, confidentiality (as far as possible — never promise absolute
  confidentiality), no retaliation, duty to tell the truth.
- When counsel interviews: give an **Upjohn warning** — counsel represents the
  company, not the employee; privilege belongs to the company, which may waive it.
- Open questions first, then specifics; ask for documents and other witnesses.
- Put each allegation and key evidence fully to the respondent; record the response.
- Union members (US) may request a representative in investigatory interviews they
  reasonably believe could lead to discipline (*Weingarten*). In GB, there is a
  statutory right to be accompanied at disciplinary/grievance *hearings*; at
  investigation meetings it depends on policy.
- Notes: contemporaneous, near-verbatim, dated, signed; consider giving
  interviewees the chance to review a summary.

## 4. Weighing evidence
- Standard: usually **preponderance** (more likely than not).
- Credibility factors: inherent plausibility, corroboration, consistency, motive to
  falsify, demeanour (weight lightly), past record.
- Lack of a witness is not lack of evidence — make findings on credibility.

## 5. The report
Allegations → process (who, when, documents reviewed) → evidence summary per
allegation → credibility analysis → findings (substantiated / not substantiated /
inconclusive) → policy conclusions. Leave discipline to the decision-maker unless
the scope says otherwise. Avoid legal conclusions ("this was unlawful harassment")
in a non-privileged report.

## 6. Close-out
Tell the complainant and respondent the outcome at an appropriate level of detail;
remind all of anti-retaliation; monitor for retaliation; fix systemic issues found.

## Gotchas
- Delay is itself evidence of indifference — set a timeline.
- Data-protection law (e.g. GDPR) gives subjects access rights to some investigation
  records; write notes accordingly.
- Uncertain: privilege rules differ (US vs England's narrower legal-advice privilege
  for employees' communications after *SFO v ENRC*); structure with local counsel.

## Verify
- [ ] Preservation notice issued and evidence secured.
- [ ] Scope memo and privilege structure documented.
- [ ] Upjohn warning given in counsel-led interviews.
- [ ] Respondent heard on every allegation.
- [ ] Findings tied to evidence with reasoned credibility analysis.
- [ ] Retaliation monitoring after close-out.
