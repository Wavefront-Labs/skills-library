# Screening, scoping and adequacy challenges

**Goal:** get the impact-assessment process legally right so the decision survives
judicial review — or find the defect that makes it vulnerable.

## 1. Is assessment required? (screening)
- **US NEPA**: applies to "major federal actions" (federal funding, permits, land).
  Three tracks: **categorical exclusion** (with extraordinary-circumstances check) →
  **environmental assessment** + FONSI → full **EIS** + record of decision. The 2023
  amendments set page limits (EA 75, EIS 150/300 pages) and deadlines (1 and 2 years).
  CEQ rescinded its NEPA regulations by an interim final rule effective 11 April 2025 and agencies issue their
  own procedures — check the lead agency's current rules.
- **EU/UK**: Annex I projects always need EIA; Annex II projects need a **screening
  opinion** applying selection criteria (characteristics, location, potential impact).
  Screening decisions must give reasons.
Salami-slicing (splitting a project to avoid thresholds) is a classic ground of challenge.

## 2. Scoping
Define issues, study area, methods and **alternatives**. Request a scoping opinion
(EU/UK) where available — it limits later arguments about what was required.
NEPA requires a reasonable range of alternatives including no-action;
EU law requires a description of reasonable alternatives studied and the main reasons
for the choice.

## 3. Content that courts examine
- Baseline and methodology; direct, indirect and **cumulative** effects.
- Mitigation — enough detail to judge effectiveness (a mitigation "plan to make a
  plan" is vulnerable).
- Scope of upstream/downstream effects: *Seven County* (2025) told courts to give
  agencies substantial deference on how far to trace effects of separate projects.
- Climate: greenhouse-gas assessment expectations differ — UK Supreme Court's
  *Finch* (2024) required assessment of combustion (scope 3) emissions from extracted oil.

## 4. Consultation
Public notice, availability of documents, adequate time, consultation of statutory
bodies, transboundary consultation (Espoo Convention) where effects cross borders.
Late material changes may require re-consultation (supplementary EIS / further information).

## 5. Challenging or defending
- **Standing and timing**: short windows (England & Wales planning JR: 6 weeks;
  NEPA claims on federal highway/transit approvals: 150 days after notice under
  23 U.S.C. § 139(l), 2 years for FAST-41 covered projects; generally 6 years
  under the APA otherwise). Aarhus cost-protection rules limit costs for claimants in UK/EU.
- **US standard**: "hard look" / arbitrary-and-capricious review — the agency must
  have considered relevant factors; courts don't substitute judgement.
- **EU/UK**: Was the screening rational and reasoned? Was the information adequate
  for an informed decision? Relief is discretionary — courts may refuse where the
  outcome would be the same.
- Defence side: build the administrative record as you go; respond to every
  substantive comment; document why alternatives were rejected.

## Gotchas
- An EIA is procedural — it doesn't require choosing the least-harmful option
  (NEPA; similar in EU law) — but the reasons must be rational.
- Mitigation relied on in the FONSI or decision must be enforceable (conditions).
- Uncertain: NEPA's procedural framework is in flux after 2023–2025 changes; verify
  current agency procedures and case law.

## Verify
- [ ] Screening decision reasoned and project not split to avoid thresholds.
- [ ] Alternatives, including no-action/do-nothing, documented with reasons.
- [ ] Cumulative and indirect effects addressed within the applicable scope.
- [ ] Consultation steps and timings evidenced.
- [ ] Challenge deadlines and cost-protection rules identified.
