# Water rights and extraction licensing

**Goal:** establish what water (or other natural resource) a client may lawfully
take, how secure that right is, and how to transfer, defend or contest it.

## 1. Identify the regime
Water law is intensely jurisdiction-specific. First question: which doctrine governs
surface water and which governs groundwater?

**Surface water (US)**
- **Riparian** (most eastern states): owners of land touching a watercourse share a
  right to *reasonable use*; rights aren't lost by non-use; many states overlay
  permit systems ("regulated riparianism").
- **Prior appropriation** (western states): "first in time, first in right" — a
  **priority date**, a quantity, a point of diversion, a place and purpose of
  **beneficial use**. In shortage, senior rights are satisfied fully before juniors get
  anything ("calls"). Rights can be lost through **abandonment** (intent) or statutory
  **forfeiture** (non-use for a statutory period set by each state).
- Hybrid states (e.g. California) recognise both.

**Groundwater (US)**: rule of capture (Texas, with groundwater district regulation),
reasonable use, correlative rights, or prior appropriation; California's SGMA requires
basin sustainability plans that can limit pumping.

**Licensing systems**: England & Wales require an **abstraction licence** (generally
over 20 m³/day) from the Environment Agency, with time-limited licences and conditions
(hands-off flows); Australia separates water entitlements from land and trades them
under basin plans.

## 2. Due diligence on a water right
- Chain of title and decree/permit/licence documents.
- Priority date (and whether adjudicated).
- Historic **actual use** — the measure of a transferable right is often consumptive use
  actually made, not paper quantity.
- Forfeiture/abandonment risk from non-use periods.
- Conditions, expiry, and pending adjudications or curtailment orders.
- Federal or tribal reserved rights in the basin (*Winters* rights have early priority dates
  and can be large and unquantified). *Arizona v. Navajo Nation* (2023): the US has no
  affirmative treaty duty to secure water for the tribe absent express terms.

## 3. Transfers and changes
Change of point of diversion, place or purpose of use usually requires an
application and a **no-injury** showing to other rights holders (including juniors who
rely on return flows). Expect objections; engineering reports on historic consumptive
use are central. Licences in permit systems may be transferable only with regulator consent.

## 4. Disputes
General stream adjudications, curtailment and calls, interstate compacts and equitable
apportionment (original jurisdiction cases in the US Supreme Court, e.g. *Texas v. New
Mexico*), public trust challenges (*National Audubon*, Cal. 1983), environmental-flow
requirements under endangered-species law.

## 5. Other natural resources
Mineral, oil and gas rights often sever from surface title; check reservations in
deeds, leases (royalty, Pugh clauses, continuous-operation terms), state spacing and
pooling orders, and national licensing regimes where the state owns subsurface resources.

## Gotchas
- Paper rights exceed wet water: transfers are often cut to historic consumptive use.
- Non-use during a sale or bankruptcy can forfeit a right.
- Uncertain: forfeiture periods, de minimis exemptions and licence thresholds vary by
  state/country and change; check current statute.
