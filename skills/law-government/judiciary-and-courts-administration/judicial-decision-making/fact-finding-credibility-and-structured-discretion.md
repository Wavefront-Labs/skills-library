# Fact-finding, credibility and structured discretion

**Goal:** reach a decision that is correct, fair, and visibly reasoned — one that the
losing party can understand and an appellate court can review. Written for judges,
magistrates and tribunal members in adversarial systems; inquisitorial systems give the
judge a more active investigative role, and standards of proof and evidentiary rules
differ by jurisdiction and case type.

## 1. Frame the issues early
- Read the pleadings/charges and identify the **elements** that must be proved for each
  claim or offence, and the defences.
- Build an **issues list**: which facts are agreed, which are disputed, which legal
  questions arise. Share it with parties where procedure allows — it focuses evidence
  and argument.
- For each disputed issue note: who bears the **burden**, and the **standard** (civil:
  balance of probabilities / preponderance; criminal: beyond reasonable doubt;
  intermediate standards such as clear and convincing evidence for some matters in some
  systems).

## 2. Weighing evidence
Work issue by issue, not witness by witness.
- Start with **objective anchors**: contemporaneous documents, records, physical evidence,
  admitted facts. They are generally more reliable than recollection years later.
- Test each account against: internal consistency, consistency with other evidence and
  with the anchors, inherent probability, opportunity to observe, motive and interest,
  and whether it was given against interest.
- **Demeanour** is a weak guide to truthfulness — nervousness, cultural communication
  styles, interpretation and trauma all distort it. Research and appellate guidance in
  several systems caution against relying on it heavily.
- Memory is reconstructive: honest witnesses can be wrong; a mistaken detail doesn't
  necessarily destroy credibility on core matters. Distinguish honesty from reliability.
- Expert evidence: check qualifications, the factual assumptions it rests on, method,
  and whether the expert stayed within expertise; prefer reasoning over conclusions.
- Absence of evidence a party could reasonably have called may support an inference
  (rules vary).

## 3. Guarding against bias
- Name the risks: **anchoring** on the first account or a proposed figure, **confirmation
  bias** after an early impression, hindsight bias, sympathy and familiarity effects.
- Techniques: articulate the strongest case for the party you're inclined to rule against;
  write findings before deciding the outcome where possible; check whether the same
  reasoning would apply if the parties were swapped.
- Bench books and equal-treatment guidance help with self-represented litigants,
  interpreters, disability and cultural differences.

## 4. Applying the law
- Identify the governing rule, binding precedent and its limits, and statutory text;
  explain why distinguishing or following an authority is right.
- Decide only what's necessary; flag issues not decided.
- Natural justice: if you're minded to decide on a point neither party argued, invite
  submissions first.

## 5. Structured discretion
Discretionary decisions (sentencing, costs, interim relief, case management, custody
arrangements) are reviewable for error of principle, irrelevant considerations, failure
to consider relevant ones, or plain unreasonableness.
- Identify the statutory purposes/criteria and any guidelines (e.g. sentencing guidelines).
- List relevant factors on each side; give each a weight and say why.
- State the result and how the factors produced it; for sentencing, show the starting
  point, adjustments and any discount steps.
- Consistency matters: check comparable decisions.

## 6. Ruling from the bench
Ex tempore rulings: short structure — issue, relevant law, findings on key disputed
facts, conclusion. Note them carefully; transcripts become the reasons on appeal.

## Gotchas
- Deciding credibility globally ("I prefer X's evidence") without engaging with the
  specific contradicting evidence — a frequent appellate ground.
- Reversing the burden inadvertently in criminal cases (e.g. criticising the defendant
  for not explaining).
- Treating a party's lies as proof of guilt without the cautions many systems require.

## Verify
- [ ] Every element/issue has an identified burden and standard.
- [ ] Each disputed fact finding cites the evidence relied on and addresses contrary evidence.
- [ ] Credibility assessments rest on more than demeanour.
- [ ] Discretionary factors listed, weighed and explained.
- [ ] Parties had a fair chance to address any point the decision turns on.
