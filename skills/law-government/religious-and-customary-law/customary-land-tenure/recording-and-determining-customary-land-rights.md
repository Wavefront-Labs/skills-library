# Recording and determining customary land rights

**Goal:** establish who holds what rights in land under custom, resolve disputes before the
appropriate forum, and record the rights so they are secure without freezing or distorting
the customary system.

Customary tenure covers a large share of land in sub-Saharan Africa, the Pacific and parts of
Asia and the Americas. Legal recognition ranges from constitutional protection with
customary land boards to statutes that allow conversion into individual title. International
guidance: FAO Voluntary Guidelines on the Responsible Governance of Tenure (VGGT). Each
country's statute and each community's custom differ; this is a method outline only.
Status: draft.

## 1. Understand the legal frame
- What does state law recognise: customary ownership by a group, individual use rights within
  it, both? Who has jurisdiction (traditional authority, customary land board, land tribunal,
  ordinary courts on appeal)?
- What processes exist to record rights (certificates of customary ownership, group
  registration, village land registers)?
- Does recording change the nature of the right (e.g. conversion to freehold)? That has
  consequences for alienability and the group's control.

## 2. Identify the right-holders and the rules
- The **right-holding unit**: clan, lineage, family, village, or individual within a group.
  Ask how membership is acquired (descent, marriage, allocation, adoption).
- **Bundle of rights:** allocation, use, transfer within the group, inheritance, grazing,
  gathering, passage, seasonal use by others. Many disputes come from collapsing these into
  "ownership".
- **Secondary rights:** tenants, migrants, pastoralists, women married into the group,
  widows. Recording schemes that list only primary holders can erase these rights.
- Gather evidence from several sources: elders, multiple families, neighbouring groups,
  oral histories, genealogies, previous records, aerial imagery.

## 3. Map boundaries participatively
- Boundary walks with representatives of each neighbouring group; record agreed points with
  GPS and photographs; note disagreements rather than resolving them silently.
- Public display of draft maps and lists for a notice period with an objection process.
- Use local names and landmarks alongside coordinates.

## 4. Resolving disputes
- Start with the customary forum where the law gives it jurisdiction; respect its procedure
  (hearing elders, witnesses, oaths where customary).
- Ensure basic fairness the state law and constitution require: notice, hearing both sides,
  non-discrimination (women's inheritance rights are a common constitutional flashpoint).
- Record decisions in writing with reasons and witnesses for later recognition.

## 5. Dealing with outsiders (investors, state projects)
Leases or acquisitions of customary land usually require the group's consent through its
recognised decision process; where indigenous peoples are concerned, **free, prior and
informed consent** standards apply under international norms and some national laws.
Confirm who has authority to sign; a single chief signing for a group without mandate is a
classic source of later conflict.

## Gotchas
- Recording only male household heads.
- Treating a traditional leader as owner rather than trustee or allocator.
- Rushed mapping that skips neighbouring groups.
- Conversion to individual title enabling distress sales and loss of communal land.

## Verify
- [ ] Legal recognition and forum identified.
- [ ] Right-holding unit and full rights bundle recorded, including secondary rights.
- [ ] Boundaries walked with neighbours; drafts publicly displayed.
- [ ] Decisions written with reasons; fairness requirements met.
- [ ] Any dealing with outsiders authorised by the group's decision process.
