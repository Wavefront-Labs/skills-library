# Recognition of religious rulings and dual-compliant drafting

**Goal:** give a client one coherent answer to "what is my legal position?" when a
religious system and the state both claim the same marriage, divorce, estate or
contract — and draft documents that work in both. Models: England & Wales and US
(secular states recognising religion through contract and arbitration) and plural
personal-status systems (e.g. Israel, India, many Muslim-majority states) where the
state itself applies religious law. Results turn on the specific forum, so always run
the analysis for **each** state where the document may be relied on.

## 1. Map the two systems first
For every issue write two columns: status under the religious law; status under each
relevant state law. Typical mismatches:
| Situation | Religious view | Possible state view (E&W/US examples) |
|---|---|---|
| Religious-only marriage (nikah, unregistered ceremony) | Married | Possibly no marriage at all (E&W "non-qualifying ceremony", *Akhter v Khan* 2020) → no divorce/financial remedies |
| Civil divorce, no get/talaq | Still married | Divorced |
| Religious divorce, no civil divorce | Divorced | Still married |
| Foreign talaq | Valid | E&W: recognised only under the Family Law Act 1986 tests (proceedings vs non-proceedings divorces); a bare talaq pronounced in England is not recognised |
| Islamic or halakhic estate shares | Binding | Only if in a valid will or agreed by heirs; family-provision claims may override |

## 2. Recognition analysis for a religious ruling
1. **What is it legally?** A judgment of a state religious court (foreign judgment
   rules), an arbitral award (arbitration law), or a private religious act (no direct
   legal effect; maybe evidence of agreement).
2. **Jurisdiction and connection:** domicile, nationality, habitual residence at the time.
3. **Procedure:** notice, opportunity to be heard, independence — weighed at recognition.
4. **Public policy:** sex-discriminatory procedure, coercion, or outcomes contrary to
   child welfare can block recognition even when other tests are met.
5. **Arbitrability:** family arbitration rules vary (e.g. Ontario requires family
   arbitration to apply Canadian law since 2006); custody always remains reviewable.

## 3. Dual-compliant drafting
**Contracts (e.g. Islamic finance):**
- Choose a **state** governing law (English courts held Sharia cannot be the governing
  law of a contract under the conflicts rules — *Shamil Bank v Beximco*, 2004).
- Incorporate the religious requirements as **express contractual terms**, not as a
  choice of law (e.g. no interest, asset-backed profit, defined late-payment charity).
- Make Sharia-board approval a condition precedent; include a non-reliance clause so
  a later fatwa doesn't become a defence to payment.
- Disputes: arbitration with a state seat; optionally a religious expert panel.

**Marriage:**
- Advise couples to have a **civil registration** as well as the religious ceremony.
- Halakhic or Islamic prenuptial agreements: draft as civil contracts (dower terms,
  get-cooperation obligations, arbitration clause); enforceability varies by state.

**Wills:**
- Execute under local formalities; express the religious distribution as specific
  gifts or fractions; include a scholar-interpretation clause for ambiguities; warn
  of family-provision / forced-heirship exposure.

## Gotchas
- Assuming a religious marriage gives property or pension rights it doesn't.
- Clients holding talaq or get certificates think they are free to remarry civilly.
- "Governed by Sharia" or "Jewish law" clauses can fail and leave the contract governed
  by default rules nobody expected.
- In plural systems (personal-status law), conversion or forum shopping can change the law applied.
