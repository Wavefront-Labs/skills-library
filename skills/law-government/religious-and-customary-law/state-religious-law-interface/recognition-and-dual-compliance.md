# Recognition and dual compliance

**Goal:** make sure a client's legal position is secure in **both** systems they live under:
the religious one they observe and the state one that courts enforce, without one silently
undoing the other.

How states treat religious law varies: plural personal-status systems (e.g. India, Israel,
Lebanon, Malaysia), state recognition of some religious marriages as civil marriages
(conditions vary), and secular states where religious acts have civil effect only through
general law (contract, arbitration, registration). Always identify the state rules first.
Status: draft.

## 1. Map the issue in both systems
For each matter, write two columns:
- **Religious law:** what's needed (e.g. valid nikah, get, canonical form) and what follows.
- **State law:** what's needed (registration, civil ceremony, court order) and what follows.
Gaps between columns are the risks: e.g. a religiously married couple who are not civilly
married may have no state divorce, maintenance or inheritance rights in some jurisdictions.

## 2. Marriage and divorce
- **Formation:** is the religious ceremony also a valid civil marriage (registered officiant,
  registered building, notice requirements)? If not, advise civil registration.
- **Dower (mahr) and ketubah obligations:** many civil courts will enforce them as contracts
  if terms are clear and not contrary to public policy; approaches differ between jurisdictions.
- **Religious divorce** without civil divorce leaves civil marriage in place; civil divorce
  without religious divorce can leave a spouse unable to remarry religiously. Coordinate both
  (see `law-government.religious-and-customary-law.jewish-law-practice.religious-divorce-get-process`).
- **Foreign religious divorces** (e.g. talaq pronounced abroad): recognition depends on the
  forum's rules on foreign divorces, proceedings, notice and public policy.

## 3. Religious tribunals and arbitration
- Religious bodies' decisions bind civilly only through a recognised mechanism, usually
  arbitration law. Check arbitrability (family status, child matters often excluded),
  procedural fairness, equal treatment, and that the award doesn't violate public policy;
  courts refuse enforcement otherwise.
- Settlements reached in a religious forum can be turned into consent orders in family
  courts, which then scrutinise fairness.

## 4. Dual-compliant contracts
Islamic finance, kosher supervision agreements, church property trusts and similar documents
must work under state law while satisfying religious requirements:
- **Choice of law:** most state courts won't apply "Sharia" or "halakha" as a governing law of
  a contract; choose state law and incorporate religious requirements as terms.
- Define religious concepts in the document; name the board or scholar who certifies
  compliance, and what happens if certification is withdrawn.
- Ensure the contract's economic substance is clear for tax and consumer law (e.g.
  murabaha or ijara treated as financing).

## 5. Inheritance
A religious distribution usually takes effect only through a valid will or heirs' agreement;
forced-heirship and family-provision laws may override.

## Gotchas
- Assuming a religious ceremony created a civil marriage.
- Choosing a religious system as governing law and having the clause ignored.
- Arbitration awards on non-arbitrable family matters.
