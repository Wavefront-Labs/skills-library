# Conducting a hearing and writing a religious-court ruling

**Goal:** decide a dispute in a religious forum (beth din, Sharia council or qadi
court, church tribunal) so that the decision is faithful to the religious sources,
procedurally fair, and — where the parties will need it — enforceable or at least
respected by the state. Models: voluntary religious tribunals in the US and England &
Wales, where authority rests on consent and arbitration law; in states with official
religious courts (e.g. Israel's rabbinical courts, Sharia courts in several Muslim-majority
states) statutory procedure governs instead. Each tradition has its own procedural
law — apply it; the steps below are the common professional core.

## 1. Before the hearing: jurisdiction
1. Identify the basis of authority: statute, or the parties' written **submission to
   arbitration** (e.g. a *shtar borerut*). Without valid consent a voluntary tribunal
   has only moral authority.
2. Confirm scope: which issues, which law (e.g. "Jewish law and custom", "the Hanafi
   school"), whether the panel may decide by compromise (*pesharah*) as well as by strict law.
3. Exclude what the state reserves: in most secular states criminal matters, child
   custody (courts retain best-interests review), and status effects for civil law
   cannot be decided bindingly by a private tribunal.
4. Check panel composition and conflicts; disclose and recuse where a reasonable
   observer would doubt impartiality.

## 2. Running the hearing
- Give written notice of date, place, panel and issues; allow reasonable time
  (often 14+ days) to prepare.
- Both parties present together; each may speak, call witnesses, produce documents
  and respond to the other's case. Permit an advocate or supporter if the forum's
  rules allow; consider interpreters.
- Safeguard vulnerable parties: separate waiting areas, attention to coercion or
  domestic abuse, refusal to pressure anyone into waiving civil rights.
- Apply the forum's evidence rules (e.g. witness competence rules) but record any
  departures from state-law fairness expectations — these matter at enforcement.
- Keep a written record: attendance, exhibits, summary of testimony.

## 3. Reasoning from the sources
1. State the question precisely.
2. Set out the governing sources in their recognised hierarchy (scripture, authoritative
   codes or schools, responsa or precedents, custom).
3. Find the facts, explaining which evidence was believed and why.
4. Apply the sources to the facts; address the losing party's strongest argument.
5. Where authorities disagree, say which view the panel follows and why.

## 4. Drafting the ruling
| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Heading | Forum, panel, parties, date, case reference |
| Jurisdiction | Basis of authority and scope agreed |
| Procedure | Notice, hearings held, who attended, documents received |
| Facts found | Numbered findings |
| Reasons | Sources and application |
| Orders | Precise, performable obligations with deadlines and amounts |
| Signatures | All panel members; dissent noted if the rules allow |
Write orders that a state court could enforce as an arbitral award: definite sums,
dates, no ambiguity.

## 5. After the ruling
- Serve it on both parties; explain any internal appeal or reconsideration.
- Enforcement: voluntary compliance and community sanctions, or confirmation as an
  arbitration award (e.g. US FAA/state arbitration acts; England & Wales Arbitration
  Act 1996) where the matter is arbitrable and procedure was fair.

## Gotchas
- A ruling issued without the other party's genuine opportunity to be heard is
  vulnerable both religiously and in state court.
- Unequal procedural treatment by sex can make an award unenforceable or contrary to
  public policy in many states.
- Purporting to decide child custody or criminal liability bindingly.
- Ambiguous orders ("shall make appropriate payment") cannot be enforced.

## Verify
- [ ] Signed submission or statutory jurisdiction confirmed; scope recorded.
- [ ] Conflicts checked and disclosed.
- [ ] Both parties heard on every issue decided.
- [ ] Reasons cite sources and address the losing side's case.
- [ ] Orders are specific, dated and enforceable.
