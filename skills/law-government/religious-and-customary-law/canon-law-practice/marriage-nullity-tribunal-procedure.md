# Marriage nullity tribunal procedure (Catholic canon law)

**Goal:** understand and work through the process by which a Catholic tribunal decides
whether a marriage was invalid from the start, so that a petition is properly grounded and
evidenced.

Framework: the 1983 Code of Canon Law (Latin Church) and the Code of Canons of the Eastern
Churches, as amended by the 2015 reform *Mitis Iudex Dominus Iesus* (and its Eastern
counterpart), plus local tribunal norms. Tribunals differ in practice, fees and timescales.
This is an outline for orientation; advocates should be approved by the tribunal, and a
canonical decision has **no civil-law effect**: civil divorce or annulment is separate.
Status: draft; confirm procedure against the tribunal's current norms.

## 1. What a declaration of nullity is
Not a "Catholic divorce": it declares that a valid marriage bond never came into being
because of an impediment, a defect of consent or a defect of canonical form at the time of
the wedding. Marriage enjoys the **favor of law**: validity is presumed until the contrary is
proven with moral certainty.

## 2. Main grounds (with their canons, Latin code)
- **Defect of form** (e.g. a Catholic married outside the Church without dispensation),
  often handled by a documentary process.
- **Impediments** (e.g. prior bond, consanguinity, age, holy orders) without dispensation.
- **Defects of consent:** grave lack of discretion of judgement or incapacity to assume
  essential obligations for psychological reasons (c. 1095); error about a quality directly
  intended; fraud/deceit (c. 1098); simulation, i.e. exclusion of marriage itself or an
  essential property such as permanence, fidelity or openness to children (c. 1101);
  force or fear (c. 1103); conditions.
Pick the ground(s) the facts actually support; tribunals assess each separately.

## 3. The petition (libellus)
States the tribunal's competence (place of marriage, domicile of a party, or where most
evidence is), the parties, the ground(s), and a summary of facts and evidence. The other
party (respondent) is cited and has the right to participate, receive information and give
evidence; refusal doesn't stop the case.

## 4. Instruction (evidence)
- Testimony of both parties, separately.
- **Witnesses** who knew the parties before and around the wedding: their knowledge must be
  specific and ideally from that time.
- Documents: marriage records, letters, medical or counselling records with consent.
- Expert opinion (psychological) is ordinarily required in c. 1095 cases unless clearly
  unnecessary.
The **defender of the bond** argues for validity; the advocate may review the acts and submit
a brief before decision.

## 5. Decision and appeal
- Ordinary process: decided by a judge or college of judges. Since 2015, one affirmative
  sentence can become executive if no appeal is lodged within the time limit (the previous
  automatic second review was removed).
- **Briefer process before the bishop:** where both parties petition or consent and the
  nullity is manifest from evidence that needs no lengthy inquiry.
- Appeals go to the appellate tribunal and ultimately the Roman Rota.
- A sentence may attach a **prohibition (vetitum)** on a future marriage until conditions are met.

## Gotchas
- Mistaking civil grounds (breakdown) for canonical grounds.
- Witnesses who only know about the later breakdown, not the time of consent.
- Expecting civil effects from the decree.

## Verify
- [ ] Tribunal competence established.
- [ ] Ground(s) matched to specific facts at the time of the wedding.
- [ ] Respondent properly cited.
- [ ] Witnesses and documents speak to the pre-marriage period.
- [ ] Party told the decision is canonical only.
