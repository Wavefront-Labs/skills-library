# Preparing a marriage nullity case

**Goal:** take a petitioner from first interview to an executable sentence on whether a
marriage was invalid from the start, by choosing grounds the evidence can actually prove
and running the case cleanly through the tribunal. Model: Latin Church under the 1983
Code (CIC) as reformed by *Mitis Iudex* (2015). Eastern Catholic Churches use the CCEO
with parallel but differently numbered canons; each tribunal also has its own norms,
fees and forms — check them.

Key mindset: a declaration of nullity is not a "Catholic divorce". Marriage enjoys the
favour of the law (can. 1060): validity is presumed until the contrary is proven to
**moral certainty** (can. 1608).

## 1. Intake and screening (first interview)
1. Confirm a civil divorce is final or that reconciliation is impossible; most tribunals
   require it before accepting a petition.
2. Take a full history: courtship, the wedding (where, which rite, which officiant,
   any dispensation), early married life, when problems began, children, prior
   marriages of either party.
3. Collect documents: baptismal certificates (recent issue, with notations), church
   marriage certificate, civil marriage and divorce records.
4. Screen for **documentary** cases first — they are fast and need no full trial.

## 2. Choose the route and the grounds
| Route | When | Decided by |
|---|---|---|
| Documentary process (cann. 1688-1690) | Nullity proven by an unchallengeable document: impediment never dispensed (e.g. prior bond), or lack of canonical form for a Catholic | Bishop / judicial vicar |
| Briefer process before the bishop (*processus brevior*) | Both spouses petition or consent, **and** nullity is manifest from evidence | Diocesan bishop |
| Ordinary formal process | Everything else | Collegiate tribunal or sole judge as permitted |

Common grounds (plead only what the witnesses can support):
- **Defect of consent:** grave lack of discretion of judgement or incapacity to assume
  essential obligations from a psychic cause (can. 1095, often needs an expert);
  error about a quality directly intended (1097); fraud (1098); total or partial
  simulation, e.g. excluding children, fidelity or permanence (1101); force or fear (1103).
- **Impediments** (cann. 1083-1094): prior bond, age, consanguinity, etc.
- **Defect of form** (can. 1108 ff.).

## 3. Competence and the libellus
- Competent tribunals (can. 1672): place of celebration, domicile or quasi-domicile of
  either party, or where most proofs will be gathered.
- Draft the **libellus** (petition): parties, the marriage, the tribunal and basis of
  competence, the specific ground(s), a concise factual summary showing how the facts
  fit each ground, the proofs offered (witness names and what each knows), and signature.
- The respondent must be cited and may participate, oppose or remain absent.

## 4. Instruction (proof phase)
1. The judicial vicar accepts the libellus and the **formula of doubt** is fixed (e.g.
   "Has the nullity of this marriage been proven on the ground of simulation by the man?").
2. Depositions of the parties and witnesses — ideally people who knew the couple
   **before and at the time** of the wedding, not only after the breakdown.
3. Expert report where can. 1095 is pleaded; the tribunal appoints or approves the expert.
4. The **defender of the bond** participates throughout and argues for validity.
5. **Publication of the acts** (can. 1598): parties and advocates may inspect and add
   proofs; then the case is concluded and written briefs are exchanged.

## 5. Sentence, appeal, execution
- The judges must reach moral certainty from the acts; the sentence states the law,
  facts and reasoning.
- Since 2015 a single affirmative sentence becomes executive if no appeal is lodged
  (the former mandatory second review was abolished). Appeal deadlines are short
  (traditionally 15 "useful" days to lodge) — check the current norms.
- A **vetitum** (prohibition on a new marriage without permission) may be attached.
- Annotation of the decision in the baptismal registers.

## Gotchas
- Pleading every ground "just in case" dilutes credibility and lengthens the case.
- Witnesses who only heard the petitioner's account later add little.
- Petitioners often expect civil effects; a canonical sentence has none in most
  states (see `law-government.religious-and-customary-law.state-religious-law-interface.recognition-of-religious-rulings-and-dual-compliant-drafting`).
- Pastoral sensitivity: the respondent may be hurt by the petition; neutral wording helps.
- Timelines vary widely by tribunal (months to years); do not promise dates.

## Verify
- [ ] Civil divorce final (or tribunal policy confirmed) and documents gathered.
- [ ] Route chosen: documentary, briefer or ordinary — with reasons.
- [ ] Each ground pleaded has specific facts and at least one corroborating witness.
- [ ] Competence basis stated in the libellus.
- [ ] Publication of the acts reviewed before conclusion; appeal deadlines diarised.
