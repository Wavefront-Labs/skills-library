# Working within indigenous legal orders

**Goal:** act for or alongside an indigenous community in a way that treats its own law
as law — identifying who decides, how, and by what principles — and then present that
law accurately where the state requires proof or recognition. Examples come from
Canada, Aotearoa New Zealand, Australia and US tribal practice; each nation's law is
distinct, and state frameworks (Canada's s.35 and duty to consult, native title in
Australia, tikanga in NZ common law after *Ellis* 2022, US tribal sovereignty) differ
sharply. Never generalise one nation's law to another.

## 1. Start with legitimacy, not paperwork
1. Identify **whose** law applies: nation, clan, house, hapū, band — and whether
   elected (state-created) bodies and hereditary or customary authorities agree on
   who speaks for what. Get this in writing from the community before acting.
2. Ask what protocols govern first contact: introductions (who you are, where you are
   from, who sent you), gifts or offerings, meeting order, language, who may speak.
   **Ask a community liaison** rather than relying on generic cultural guides.
3. Clarify your role: outside counsel takes instructions from the authority the
   community designates, and must manage conflicts between internal factions openly.

## 2. Research the law itself
Indigenous law lives in stories, songs, ceremonies, place names, practices and the
decisions of elders — not codes. A widely used method (developed by the Indigenous Law
Research Unit, Canada) treats each story or precedent like a case:
| Brief field | Question |
|---|---|
| Issue | What problem did the people face? |
| Facts | What happened? |
| Decision | What was done in response? |
| Reasons | Why — explicitly or implicitly? |
| Bracket | What does the story not tell us? |
Synthesise across many stories into principles (authorities, procedures, obligations,
rights, responses to harm) and **test the synthesis with knowledge-keepers** before
using it. Expect variation and internal debate; record it rather than smoothing it out.

## 3. Respect data and knowledge sovereignty
- Agree in writing who owns recordings, transcripts and maps (OCAP: ownership,
  control, access, possession; CARE principles internationally).
- Restricted or sacred knowledge may not be put in public court records — use
  confidentiality orders, sealed evidence or oral-history protocols where available.

## 4. Carrying indigenous law into state processes
| Process | Typical evidence | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Consultation / FPIC on a project | Community laws on land and decision-making; impacts | Consultation is not consent unless law says so |
| Rights or title claims (s.35, native title) | Oral history, genealogy, expert anthropology, continuity of connection | Burdens of proof framed by state law; elders' testimony protocols |
| Self-government / jurisdiction agreements | Written codification of community law | Codification can freeze a living law |
| Tribal or community courts | Community law and procedure | Interaction with state appeals and civil-rights statutes |
Prepare elders for cross-examination with care; seek procedural accommodations
(testimony on territory, ceremony, interpreters).

## Gotchas
- Treating the band council as the only authority when the dispute is about hereditary title.
- Paraphrasing community law into common-law categories and losing what makes it distinct.
- Promising outcomes inside timelines set by the project proponent, not the community.
- Publishing research without community review and consent.

## Verify
- [ ] Authority to instruct confirmed and documented by the community.
- [ ] Protocols for engagement confirmed with a liaison and followed.
- [ ] Legal principles drawn from multiple sources and validated by knowledge-keepers.
- [ ] Data-sovereignty agreement signed before recording or filing.
- [ ] State-law framework for the specific jurisdiction checked; accommodations requested.
