# Safety induction, toolbox talks and culture measurement

**Goal:** make sure every worker is competent for the hazards they face and works in a
culture where reporting, stopping work and following controls are normal — and measure
whether that is true. Applies across jurisdictions: most OHS laws (GB HSWA s.2 information
and training duty, OSHA standard-specific training requirements, EU Framework Directive)
require training but leave design to the employer. Mandatory course content, refresher
intervals and certification (e.g. forklift, scaffolding, first aid, confined space) are
set per regime — check them.

## 1. Training needs analysis (TNA)
1. List roles and the tasks/hazards each one meets (from risk assessments).
2. For each, define the competence required: knowledge, skill, and any legal certificate.
3. Build a **training matrix**: roles × required courses, with refresher intervals; mark statutory ones.
4. Gap-check current staff; plan. Supervisors and managers need their own module: legal duties, how to run a talk, how to respond to concerns.

## 2. Induction design
- Before work starts, short and site-specific: emergency arrangements, site rules, the top 5 hazards and their controls, how to report, stop-work authority, welfare.
- Under 60–90 minutes; deeper task training follows.
- Check understanding (practical demo or short quiz, not a signature alone).
- Handle language and literacy: translated material, pictures, interpreters, buddy system.
- Follow up after the first week — new starters have elevated injury rates.

## 3. Toolbox talks that work
- 5–15 minutes, at the work location, before the task.
- One topic tied to today's work or a recent incident/near miss.
- Structure: hazard → what could happen (real example) → controls → ask the crew ("what would go wrong here?") → actions.
- Leader talks less than half the time.
- Record attendees, topic and any issues raised; close the issues and tell the crew.

## 4. Behavioural safety programmes
- Peer or leader observations of defined safe behaviours, followed by a conversation: praise safe acts, ask about at-risk ones, learn what drives them.
- Feed data into **system** fixes (if people bypass a guard, why — productivity, design?).
- Avoid blame or quotas; programmes that punish kill reporting. Behavioural approaches complement, never replace, engineering controls.

## 5. Leadership routines
- Planned leadership safety walks (e.g. monthly per manager) with open questions, not inspections.
- Visible response to concerns raised within an agreed time.
- Leaders stop work themselves when needed, and thank others who do.

## 6. Measure culture and training effectiveness
| Measure | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Safety climate survey (validated questionnaire, anonymous, repeated every 12–24 months) | Perceptions of management commitment, priority, reporting, involvement |
| Near-miss/hazard report rate and close-out time | Willingness to report; trust in response |
| Observation and conversation counts/quality | Engagement |
| Kirkpatrick levels: reaction → learning → behaviour → results | Whether training changed practice |
| Maturity model (e.g. pathological → reactive → calculative → proactive → generative) | Shared language for where you are |
Share results with the workforce and act on two or three themes — surveys without action make culture worse.

## Gotchas
- Attendance records mistaken for competence.
- Generic, purchased e-learning in place of site-specific content.
- Toolbox talks read from a script to a silent crew.
- Celebrating "zero injuries" while near-miss reporting falls.

## Verify
- [ ] Training matrix maps every role to hazard-based requirements and refresher dates.
- [ ] Induction checks understanding and is followed up.
- [ ] Toolbox talks are task-specific and issues raised get closed.
- [ ] Observation programme feeds system fixes, not discipline.
- [ ] Culture measured on a cycle with visible actions taken.
