# Investigating a workplace incident: root cause and corrective actions

**Goal:** understand why an incident happened at a system level and change things so it
doesn't recur — while meeting legal reporting duties and not destroying evidence.

## 1. First hour
1. **Make safe and care for people:** first aid, emergency services, stop the process,
   isolate energy, keep others out.
2. **Preserve the scene:** barricade, don't move equipment unless needed for safety;
   photograph and video from wide to close, with scale; sketch with measurements. Secure
   CCTV, data loggers, permits, logbooks, maintenance records before they're overwritten.
3. **Notify:** internal escalation, insurer, and the regulator where required. Many
   jurisdictions mandate prompt reporting of deaths, specified serious injuries and
   dangerous occurrences (e.g. RIDDOR in Great Britain; OSHA requires reporting a fatality
   within 8 hours and an in-patient hospitalisation, amputation or eye loss within 24
   hours; Australian WHS "notifiable incidents" with scene-preservation duties). Exact
   triggers and deadlines **vary by jurisdiction** — keep a one-page reporting guide.
   Separately, record it in the injury log (e.g. OSHA 300) if recordable.
4. Consider **legal privilege** for serious incidents: some organisations run the
   investigation under counsel's direction. Don't let this delay safety fixes.

## 2. Team and scope
Lead investigator independent of the area's line management, plus someone who knows the
task, a worker or safety rep, and specialists (engineering, human factors) as needed.
Scale effort to the **potential** severity, not the actual — a near miss that could have
killed deserves a full investigation.

## 3. Gather evidence
- **Interviews** as soon as practical, separately, in a neutral place; witness may bring
  a representative. Open questions first ("talk me through your shift from the start"),
  then specifics. Explain the purpose is learning. Avoid leading questions and blame.
- **Documents:** risk assessments, procedures, permits, training records, maintenance
  and inspection history, shift rosters (fatigue), previous similar incidents.
- **Physical:** failed parts (tag and retain), PPE worn, environmental conditions.

## 4. Analyse
1. **Timeline** of events and conditions leading up to it — minute by minute near the event.
2. Identify the **immediate causes** (unsafe acts and conditions).
3. Dig to **underlying and root causes** with a structured method: 5 Whys (quick, but
   tends to stop at a person), fishbone/Ishikawa (people, equipment, methods, materials,
   environment, management), or more rigorous systems methods (ICAM, TapRooT-class,
   bowtie barrier analysis).
4. **Human error is a starting point, not a conclusion.** Ask why the action made
   sense to the person at the time: design, procedures, time pressure, training,
   supervision, staffing, conflicting goals. A just-culture approach distinguishes
   error, at-risk behaviour and reckless conduct.
5. Check: which **barriers/controls** should have stopped this, and why did each fail?

## 5. Corrective actions
- One or more actions per root cause, prioritised by the hierarchy of controls —
  "retrain the operator" alone is a weak action.
- **SMART**: specific, owner, due date, resources; plus a check of effectiveness
  after implementation.
- Share lessons across sites that have the same equipment or task.

## 6. Report
Summary; what happened (facts, timeline); why (causal analysis); what we'll change
(actions table); appendices (photos, diagrams, statements). Write factually — reports
are likely disclosable in litigation and regulatory proceedings.

## Verify
- [ ] Regulator notification assessed against the jurisdiction's triggers and deadline.
- [ ] Scene and perishable evidence (CCTV, logs) secured.
- [ ] Analysis goes beyond the individual to system causes and failed barriers.
- [ ] Every root cause has an owned, dated action above "retrain" in the hierarchy.
- [ ] Effectiveness check scheduled; lessons shared.
