# Building a safety management system: procedures, permits and committees

**Goal:** a system that makes safe work the default and catches drift before it hurts
someone — sized to the organisation, not a 400-page manual nobody reads.

## 1. Frame: Plan–Do–Check–Act
Most recognised frameworks — **ISO 45001**, ILO-OSH 2001, the UK's HSG65, OSHA's
Recommended Practices for Safety and Health Programs — share this cycle:
- **Plan:** policy, legal register, risk assessments, objectives.
- **Do:** roles, competence, procedures, permits, contractor control, emergency plans,
  consultation.
- **Check:** inspections, incident investigation, monitoring, audits.
- **Act:** management review, corrective actions, improvement.
Certification to ISO 45001 is optional in most places; legal duties are not.

## 2. Plan
- **Policy** (one page) signed by the top executive: commitment, responsibilities,
  consultation, resources. The legal duty-holder is usually the employer/PCBU and its
  officers, not the safety manager — make that explicit.
- **Legal register:** the OHS laws, regulations and codes that apply, by site; who
  checks for changes. These **vary by jurisdiction** and sector.
- **Roles:** board/officer duties, line-manager duties (the real safety managers),
  competent safety advisers, worker duties. Write them into job descriptions.
- **Objectives** with measures, e.g. "100% of high-risk tasks have a current JSA by Q2".

## 3. Do: procedures and permits
**Safe work procedures** (SWPs/method statements) for high-risk or complex tasks only.
Write them with the operators, step by step, with the hazard and control at each step,
pictures where possible, one to two pages. Test by watching someone follow it.

**Permit-to-work (PTW)** for work where normal controls are suspended: hot work,
confined space entry, work on energised/process systems, breaking containment, work at
height in specific areas, excavation. Essentials:
1. Defined permit types and **who may issue, accept and close** (named, trained,
   authorised people).
2. Hazard assessment and precautions on the permit itself.
3. **Isolation** of energy — lockout/tagout with personal locks, isolation
   certificates, and **verification of zero energy** ("try" step).
4. Gas testing where relevant, with readings recorded.
5. Fixed validity period and shift-handover rules.
6. Cross-referencing of conflicting permits in the same area (a permit board or
   electronic system).
7. Formal hand-back: area left safe, isolations removed, permit closed and filed.
8. Audit live permits in the field — most PTW failures are paper-correct, field-wrong.

## 4. Consultation and committees
Many jurisdictions require worker consultation and give elected **safety
representatives** rights (inspect, investigate, receive information). A safety
committee works when it has: a terms of reference, management and worker members (often
at least half workers), a monthly agenda (incidents, actions, inspections, upcoming
changes), and an **action log with owners** that's reviewed each time.

## 5. Check and Act
- **Leading indicators:** inspections completed, actions closed on time, permit audits
  passed, near-miss reports, training currency.
- **Lagging indicators:** lost-time and recordable injury rates (e.g. per 200,000 or
  1,000,000 hours — state which), serious incidents, occupational illness.
- Internal audit annually against your own system; **management review** with the top
  executive deciding on resources and objectives.

## Gotchas
- A low injury rate can mean under-reporting; watch near-miss reporting trends.
- Procedures written by consultants without operators get ignored.
- PTW issuers become a bottleneck; train enough of them across shifts.

## Verify
- [ ] Signed policy, legal register and named role holders exist.
- [ ] SWPs exist for high-risk tasks and were field-tested.
- [ ] PTW covers issue, isolation verification, handover and close-out; field audits done.
- [ ] Safety committee has worker members and a live action log.
- [ ] Leading and lagging indicators reported to top management.
