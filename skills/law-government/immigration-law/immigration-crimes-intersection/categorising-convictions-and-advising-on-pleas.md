# Categorising convictions and advising on pleas

**Goal:** tell a non-citizen defendant (and their criminal lawyer) what each possible
plea does to their immigration status, and steer toward dispositions that preserve
status or relief. Heavily US-specific: US criminal defence counsel must advise on
deportation consequences (*Padilla v. Kentucky*). Other countries have their own
deportation-on-conviction regimes (e.g. UK automatic deportation thresholds tied to
sentence length) — the "conviction + sentence → consequence" mapping exercise is the same.

## 1. Gather the client facts that change the answer
- Exact immigration status (citizen? permanent resident? visa holder? undocumented?) and
  date of admission — some grounds depend on how long after admission the offence occurred.
- Prior convictions (count matters for some grounds).
- Relief they might need later (cancellation, asylum, adjustment) — some convictions
  are bars only to relief.
- Possibility the client is actually a citizen (derived/acquired) — check before panicking.

## 2. Immigration definition of "conviction" and "sentence"
In US immigration law, a conviction includes many dispositions that state law treats as
non-convictions (e.g. some deferred adjudications where a plea or admission was entered
and some punishment imposed). **Sentence** counts the term imposed, even if suspended.
A 364-day vs 365-day sentence can be the difference between an aggravated felony and not
for some offence types, because the crime-of-violence and theft categories require a term of imprisonment of at least one year (INA 101(a)(43)(F), (G)).

## 3. The categorical approach
Courts compare the **elements** of the statute of conviction to the federal generic
definition — not the client's actual conduct.
1. Identify the exact statute and subsection.
2. Find the least culpable conduct the statute covers (with a realistic probability of
   prosecution). If that minimum conduct falls outside the generic definition, the
   conviction doesn't match — **no consequence**, whatever the person actually did.
3. If the statute lists alternative elements (**divisible**), the **modified categorical
   approach** lets the adjudicator consult the limited "record of conviction" (charging
   document, plea agreement, plea colloquy, judgment) to see which alternative was the
   basis. Alternative *means* of committing one element don't make it divisible (*Mathis*).
Some grounds use a **circumstance-specific** approach instead (e.g. loss amount for
fraud aggravated felonies), where actual facts matter.

## 4. The main screens
- **Aggravated felony**: a long statutory list (e.g. drug trafficking, theft or crime of
  violence with a one-year sentence, fraud with loss over a threshold). For permanent
  residents it is close to a death sentence: removability plus bars to most relief.
- **Crime involving moral turpitude (CIMT)**: fraud, intent to steal, certain intent to
  harm. Check the statutory exceptions for inadmissibility and deportability.
- **Controlled substance offences**: check the statutory exceptions. Mismatches between
  federal and state drug schedules can defeat the match.
- **Firearms, domestic violence, stalking, child abuse, protection-order violations**.

## 5. Plea strategy
- Aim for a statute or subsection outside the generic definition, a **vague or
  element-neutral record** (plead to the statute without admitting the disqualifying
  alternative), or a sentence below the trigger.
- Keep factual admissions out of the plea colloquy and documents that make up the record.
- Put the advice in writing and have criminal counsel document that it was given.

## 6. Post-conviction relief
Whether a vacatur, expungement or sentence modification is recognised in immigration
proceedings depends on its ground and on current circuit and Board precedent; check
before relying on it.

## Gotchas
- Precedent shifts by circuit and over time; a statute safe in one circuit may not be
  in another. Check current case law for the specific statute.

## Verify
- [ ] Exact statute, subsection and sentence identified from certified records.
- [ ] Categorical analysis written out for each ground (aggravated felony, CIMT, drugs, others).
- [ ] Consequences mapped both for deportability and for eligibility for relief.
- [ ] Safer plea alternatives proposed and record-of-conviction language reviewed.
- [ ] Advice delivered in writing to client and criminal counsel.
