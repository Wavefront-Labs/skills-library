# Best-interests advocacy and parenting plans

**Goal:** secure parenting arrangements that a court will find in the child's best
interests and that the family can actually follow — while getting jurisdiction right.

## 1. Jurisdiction first
- **US interstate: UCCJEA** — initial jurisdiction belongs to the child's **home state**
  (where the child lived with a parent for 6 consecutive months before filing; the federal PKPA, 28 USC 1738A(b)(4), uses the same six-month definition); that
  court keeps **exclusive continuing jurisdiction** until no one significant remains
  there. Temporary emergency jurisdiction is narrow.
- **International**: the 1980 **Hague Abduction Convention** requires prompt return of
  a child wrongfully removed from their habitual residence (applications ideally
  within one year; limited defences such as grave risk, settled after one year,
  child's objections, consent/acquiescence). Return is not a custody decision.

## 2. The legal standard
Courts decide on the **best interests of the child** (US) or the child's **welfare as
paramount** (England & Wales, with the s.1(3) welfare checklist). Typical factors:
child's wishes (weighted by age and maturity); physical and emotional needs;
each parent's capacity and past caregiving; stability and continuity; sibling and
extended-family relationships; willingness to support the child's relationship with
the other parent; domestic abuse, substance use, mental health; harm or risk of harm.
Many US states presume joint legal custody absent abuse; a presumption *against*
custody to a perpetrator of domestic violence exists in many states. England & Wales
has a presumption of parental involvement unless that would put the child at risk.

## 3. Vocabulary
- **Legal custody**: decision-making (education, health, religion) — joint or sole,
  sometimes split by domain.
- **Physical custody / parenting time**: where the child lives and when.
- England & Wales: **child arrangements orders** (who the child lives with/spends time
  with), specific issue and prohibited steps orders.

## 4. Drafting a workable parenting plan
Include: regular schedule (e.g. 2-2-3, alternating weeks, every-other-weekend +
midweek) suited to the child's age; holidays, birthdays, school breaks by year
parity; exchange times and locations (neutral/school exchanges cut conflict);
transportation; communication with the child (calls/video); decision-making
process and tie-breaks; information sharing (school/medical portals); co-parenting
app use; travel and passport consent; right of first refusal; relocation notice;
dispute resolution (mediation before court). Specificity prevents litigation.

## 5. Evidence
- **Custody evaluation** (psychologist/social worker) or court welfare reports (Cafcass
  in England): prepare clients to be candid and child-focused; review methodology
  and collateral sources; depose or cross-examine evaluators on bias and data gaps.
- **Guardian ad litem**/child's lawyer represents best interests or wishes depending on role.
- Documentary evidence: school and medical records, communications, calendars of
  actual caregiving. Coach clients: what they post and text will be exhibits.

## 6. Relocation (move-away)
Most jurisdictions require notice and court permission (or consent) for moves that
affect the schedule. Factors: reason for move, effect on the relationship with the
other parent, feasibility of revised schedule, child's benefit. Burdens differ by state.

## Gotchas
- Alienation/"gatekeeping" claims cut both ways; focus on child-centred evidence.
- Agreements between parents are reviewable by the court for the child's interests.
- Uncertain: presumptions and factor lists differ by state and are amended often.
