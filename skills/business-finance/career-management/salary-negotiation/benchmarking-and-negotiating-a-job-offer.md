# Benchmarking and negotiating a job offer

**Goal:** finish with the best realistic package for the role without damaging the
relationship with your future manager. Most employers expect a counter; few
withdraw an offer over a polite, evidence-based one (rare, but it happens, mainly
with small employers or rigid public-sector scales).

## 1. Benchmark before the first salary conversation
- Posted ranges: pay-transparency laws in several US states, the EU (Pay Transparency
  Directive, national transposition due 2026) and elsewhere mean many postings now
  show a band. Check exact local rules; coverage varies.
- Data sources: Levels.fyi (tech, strong on equity), Glassdoor/Indeed salaries, Payscale,
  government wage statistics (e.g. US BLS OES), recruiter conversations, peers.
- Build a **range for this exact title, level and location**, and pick three numbers:
  walk-away floor, realistic target, ambitious anchor (top quartile you can justify).

## 2. Deflect early, anchor late
When asked "what are you looking for?" in a first screen: "I'd like to learn more about
the role first; what's the budgeted range?" If pressed, give a researched **range whose
bottom is your target**, not your floor. Don't disclose current salary where asking is
unlawful; elsewhere you may decline politely.

## 3. Value the whole offer
Put it in a spreadsheet, annualised:
- base, target bonus (and how often it actually pays out),
- equity: RSUs at current price ÷ vesting years; options are worth far less than
  headline (strike price, liquidity, dilution). Ask the share count, strike, vesting
  schedule, cliff and the latest valuation/preferred price; treat startup options as
  a lottery ticket,
- pension/401(k) match, health cover cost, paid leave, remote stipend,
- signing bonus (often with a clawback if you leave within 12 months: read it).
Compare like-for-like with your current package and any competing offer.

## 4. The counter (by phone or video, confirmed in writing)
Script outline:
1. Enthusiasm: "I'm excited about this and want to make it work."
2. Specific number with reasoning: "Based on the market for this level in <city> and
   the scope we discussed, I was expecting a base of 128,000."
3. Silence. Let them respond.
Use a **specific** number (128,000, not "around 130"); precise anchors read as
researched. Ask for one coherent package, not drip-fed demands over five calls.

## 5. If base is capped, trade other terms
Signing bonus, earlier review date (in writing), higher level/title, extra leave,
remote days, equity refresh, relocation, training budget, start date. HR bands often
cap base but have flexibility on one-off cash.

## 6. Competing offers and your BATNA
Your BATNA (best alternative) is your real leverage: a competing offer, a good current
job, or runway. Never invent an offer; recruiters do check. If you have one, say so
factually and give a decision deadline.

## 7. Counter-offers from your current employer
When you resign and they match: ask why it took a resignation. The reasons you looked
usually remain. Practitioner experience (not solid data) says many who accept leave
within a year or two anyway.

## 8. Raises and promotions internally
Same method: market data + documented impact + the specific ask, timed before the
budget cycle closes (often 1-2 months before the review), not at the review.

## Gotchas
- Accepting verbally then negotiating looks like bad faith; negotiate before "yes".
- Get the final terms in a revised written offer before resigning anywhere.
- Exploding offers (24-48 h deadlines) are a pressure tactic; asking for a few more
  days is normal.

## Verify
- [ ] Floor, target and anchor written down from at least two data sources.
- [ ] Total-comp spreadsheet built for each offer.
- [ ] Counter made with one specific number and a reason.
- [ ] Final agreed terms in a written offer before you resign.
