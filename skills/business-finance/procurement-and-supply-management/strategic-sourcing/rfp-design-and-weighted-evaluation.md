# Designing an RFP and evaluating bids with weighted scoring

**Goal:** run a request for proposal that gets comparable, competitive bids and an
award decision you can defend to stakeholders, auditors and losing suppliers.

*Public-sector buyers: procurement law (thresholds, mandatory procedures, standstill
periods, published criteria) adds binding rules not covered here.*

## 1. Choose the right RFx
- **RFI** — learn the market, shortlist suppliers; non-binding.
- **RFQ** — requirement fully specified, decision mostly on price (commodities, spares).
- **RFP** — solution or service where approach, quality and price all matter.
Don't use an RFP when an RFQ would do; it costs both sides weeks.

## 2. Requirements before suppliers
- Gather with stakeholders and classify each requirement **must-have** (pass/fail) or
  **scored**. Keep must-haves few and truly essential; each one can eliminate a good bid.
- Write **outcome-based** requirements where possible ("99.5% monthly availability")
  rather than prescribing the solution.
- Freeze requirements before issuing; late changes need a formal addendum to all bidders.

## 3. Set evaluation criteria and weights *before* issue
Typical structure:
| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Technical / solution fit | 35% |
| Delivery & implementation approach | 15% |
| Supplier capability & references | 10% |
| Commercial (price / TCO) | 30% |
| Risk, compliance, ESG | 10% |
- Define a **scoring scale with descriptors** (e.g. 0 = not addressed, 5 = fully meets
  with evidence, 10 = exceeds with clear added value) so evaluators score consistently.
- Decide how price converts to points, e.g. lowest price ÷ bid price × max points.
  Use **TCO** over the contract term, not unit price.
- Record the weights and scoring method in the file before opening bids (and in the
  RFP itself where transparency is expected).

## 4. The RFP document
Background, scope, requirements (numbered), response format (questionnaire + pricing
template so bids are comparable), timeline, Q&A process, evaluation approach,
draft contract terms, confidentiality, and the right not to award.
Give a **pricing template** with fixed line items and volumes; free-format pricing
can't be compared.

## 5. Run the process
- Clarification questions answered **to all bidders**, anonymised, by a deadline.
- Single point of contact; stakeholders don't talk to bidders during the event.
- Late bids handled per the stated rule.

## 6. Evaluate
1. Compliance check (complete? must-haves met?).
2. **Independent scoring** by each evaluator first, with written justification per score.
3. **Moderation meeting** to reconcile large divergences — agree the score, record why.
4. Price evaluation, ideally by a separate person, after or separately from quality scoring
   to limit anchoring.
5. Clarifications/presentations/references for the shortlist; negotiate (BAFO if used).
6. Sensitivity check: would small weight changes change the winner? If yes, note it.

## 7. Award and debrief
Approval memo with scores and rationale; notify unsuccessful bidders and offer a
debrief focused on their own scores.

## Gotchas
- Weights set after seeing bids = biased outcome (and in public procurement, challengeable).
- **Conflict-of-interest declarations** from every evaluator before scoring.
- Over-specified requirements written around the incumbent's product.
- Scoring "price" twice (in commercial and inside "value" criteria).

## Verify
- [ ] Requirements classified must-have vs scored and frozen before issue.
- [ ] Weights, scale descriptors and price formula recorded before bids opened.
- [ ] Pricing template forces comparable TCO.
- [ ] Independent scores with justifications plus moderation record exist.
- [ ] Conflict declarations signed; award memo and debriefs done.
