# Writing findings and tracking remediation — make the audit change something

**Goal:** turn test results into findings that a busy process owner understands, agrees
with and fixes — and then prove the fix happened. An internal audit that produces a
report but no change has failed, however good the testing was.

Framework: the IIA's Global Internal Audit Standards (2024 release, effective 9 January 2025, replacing the 2017 IPPF Standards) expect findings to be evaluated for significance, agreed actions to be
documented, and implementation monitored. *Check your function's charter and methodology
for its exact rating scale and follow-up rules.*

## 1. Structure every finding the same way (the "5 Cs")
- **Criteria** — what should be happening: the policy, regulation, contract or good
  practice. Cite it.
- **Condition** — what you found, with numbers: "In 14 of 40 sampled supplier changes
  (35%), bank details were changed without the required call-back to a known number."
- **Cause** — *why* it happened. This is where weak findings fail. "Staff didn't follow
  the policy" is not a cause; "the call-back step isn't in the ERP workflow and new
  starters aren't trained on it" is.
- **Consequence / effect** — the risk in business terms: exposure to payment-redirection
  fraud on €X of monthly supplier payments. Quantify where you can.
- **Corrective action** — what management will do, addressing the cause, not just the
  14 instances.

## 2. Rate consistently
Use the function's scale (e.g. High / Medium / Low or 1–4) with written definitions tied
to impact and likelihood. Calibrate across the team; inconsistent ratings destroy
credibility with the audit committee faster than anything else. Consider aggregation:
several low findings in one process may add up to a higher overall process rating.

## 3. Agree the facts before the report
- Hold a closing meeting with the process owner; walk through each condition with the
  evidence. Factual corrections are welcome; disputes about *rating* are a separate conversation.
- Never surprise management in a written draft with something they haven't heard verbally.
- Where management disagrees with a finding or accepts the risk, record their position
  and escalate risk acceptance to the right level — a manager can't accept a risk above
  their authority.

## 4. Action plans that actually close
Each action needs: a specific deliverable (not "will review the process"), a **named
owner** (a person, not a department), a realistic **due date**, and the evidence that will
show it's done. Push back on dates that are vague or a year away for a high-rated issue.

## 5. Track and validate
- Keep an issues log: finding, rating, owner, due date, status, evidence received, date validated.
- **Validate** closure for medium/high findings — re-perform a small test, don't just
  accept "done" by email.
- Report to the audit committee: open items by rating and age, overdue items with owners,
  and repeat findings. Escalate repeatedly extended dates.
- Track **repeat findings** from prior audits; they signal a culture or resource problem.

## Gotchas
- Findings that describe symptoms ("14 exceptions") without cause get fixed one exception
  at a time and recur next year.
- Overloading a report with low-risk observations buries the two issues that matter;
  move minor points to a management letter.
- Words like "always", "never", "no controls exist" invite rebuttal; be precise to the sample.
- Root cause techniques (5 whys, fishbone) help, but talk to the people who do the work.
- Independence: auditors recommend; management designs and owns the fix. Don't take on
  the implementation yourself.

## Verify
- [ ] Every finding has criteria, condition (with numbers), cause, effect and action.
- [ ] The cause explains *why*, and the action addresses it.
- [ ] Ratings follow written definitions and were calibrated with a second auditor.
- [ ] Each action has a named owner, a date and defined closure evidence.
- [ ] High/medium closures were validated by re-testing; overdue items are reported upward.
