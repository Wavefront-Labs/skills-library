# Customer discovery and MVP tests — learn cheaply before you build

**Goal:** find out whether the problem is real, painful and worth paying for — using
conversations and small experiments that take days, not a product that takes months.

## 1. Write down the assumptions and rank them
List what must be true for the venture to work: *this segment has this problem*, *it
happens often*, *they already spend money or time on it*, *they'll switch to us*, *we can
reach them for under €X*. Rank by **risk × impact**. Test the riskiest one first —
usually desirability ("do they care?"), not feasibility ("can we build it?").

Write each as a falsifiable hypothesis with a threshold **before** testing:
> "At least 6 of 15 operations managers at 50–200-person logistics firms describe manual
> shift scheduling as a top-3 weekly headache and have tried a workaround."

## 2. Run problem interviews that don't lie to you
People are polite; they will tell you your idea is great. So:
- **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Ask about the last time the problem happened,
  what they did, what it cost, what they tried. Don't pitch until the end, if at all.
- **Ask for past behaviour, not future intent.** "Would you use…?" and "How much would you
  pay?" produce fiction. "What did you do last time?" and "What are you paying now?" produce data.
- **Dig for workarounds and spend.** A problem nobody has tried to solve is rarely painful enough.
- **Listen for commitment, not compliments.** A meeting with their boss, a pilot, a deposit,
  an introduction — those are signals. "Cool idea" is not.
- 15–20 interviews in one segment usually shows the pattern; if every interview sounds
  different, your segment is too broad.
- Take notes verbatim; one person interviews, one writes, if you can.

## 3. Pick the cheapest MVP that tests the hypothesis
| MVP type | What it tests | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Landing page / smoke test | Demand, messaging | Page + "Join waitlist" or "Pre-order"; measure conversion from targeted traffic |
| Fake door | Interest in a feature | Button in an existing product that leads to "coming soon" |
| Concierge | Value of the outcome | Deliver the service by hand to a few customers |
| Wizard of Oz | Value with a real-looking interface | The front end looks automated; humans do the work behind it |
| Pre-sale / letter of intent | Willingness to pay | Ask for money or a signed LOI before building |

Set the metric and pass threshold up front (e.g. "≥ 4% of visitors from the targeted
ad click Pre-order at €29"). Decide what you'll do if it passes, and if it fails.

## 4. Pivot, persevere or kill
After each test:
- **Clear pass** → persevere; move to the next riskiest assumption.
- **Clear fail** → pivot one element (segment, problem, channel, revenue model) and
  re-test, or stop. Pivot deliberately, one variable at a time, so you learn what changed.
- **Ambiguous** → usually the test was badly designed (too little traffic, vague
  threshold). Fix the test rather than rationalising the result.

## Gotchas
- **Friends and family** are a biased sample; interview strangers in the target segment.
- **Confirmation bias** — moving the threshold after seeing the data. Write it down first.
- **Vanity metrics** (page views, sign-ups with no follow-up) are not demand. Measure an
  action that costs the user something: time, money, data, reputation.
- **Too little traffic** — 40 visitors cannot distinguish 2% from 6% conversion. Get a
  few hundred targeted visitors or accept the test is directional only.
- A concierge MVP proves the outcome is valued, not that you can automate it profitably.
- Consumer-protection rules apply to pre-orders: if you take money, say clearly when
  it ships or refund it. *Rules vary by jurisdiction — check before charging.*

## Verify
- [ ] Assumptions listed and ranked; the one being tested is the riskiest.
- [ ] Hypothesis, metric and pass/fail threshold were written before the test.
- [ ] Interview notes contain past behaviour and current spend, not opinions about your idea.
- [ ] The MVP measured an action with real cost to the user.
- [ ] A pivot/persevere/kill decision was recorded with the evidence behind it.
