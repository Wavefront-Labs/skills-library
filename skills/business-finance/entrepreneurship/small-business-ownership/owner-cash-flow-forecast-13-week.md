# Owner's 13-week cash-flow forecast — see the cash crunch before it arrives

**Goal:** know, every week, how much cash the business will have for the next quarter, so
you can act (chase invoices, delay a purchase, arrange credit) while there's still time.
Profitable small businesses fail from running out of cash, not from losses on paper.

## 1. Why cash, not profit
Profit counts a sale when you invoice; cash arrives when the customer pays — maybe 30–90
days later. Stock bought today is cash out now and a cost later. Tax, loan repayments
and owner drawings hit the bank but aren't in "profit". A business can grow profitably
straight into an overdraft.

## 2. Build the sheet (one hour the first time, 20 minutes weekly after)
Columns: **13 weeks**, starting this week. Rows:

**Opening cash** (actual bank balance, all accounts, minus uncleared cheques)

**Receipts**
- Collections from existing invoices — list the big ones individually by expected pay
  date, based on how that customer *actually* pays, not the terms on the invoice.
- New sales collected within the window (cash sales, card takings net of fees, deposits).
- Other: tax refunds, loan drawdowns, owner injections.

**Payments**
- Payroll and payroll taxes on their exact dates.
- Rent, utilities, insurance, subscriptions.
- Supplier payments — by due date for big ones.
- **VAT / sales tax / estimated income tax** — on the filing deadline, for the amount
  already accrued. This is the classic surprise: you collected it, but it was never yours.
- Loan and lease repayments, owner drawings, planned capital spending.

**Net cash flow** = receipts − payments; **Closing cash** = opening + net; next week's
opening = this week's closing.

Add a **minimum cash line** — e.g. one payroll plus one month of fixed costs — and
highlight any week that dips below it.

## 3. The weekly rhythm
1. Replace last week's forecast with actuals; note the biggest variances and why.
2. Roll forward one week (add week 13).
3. Update the receivables list from your accounting system's aged-debtors report.
4. Look at the lowest closing-cash week. If it's under the minimum line, act now.

## 4. Levers when a gap appears (roughly in order of cost)
- **Collect faster:** invoice the day work is done, chase on day 1 overdue, phone the
  top five debtors, offer card/direct-debit payment, ask for deposits or stage payments.
- **Slow outflows honestly:** agree extended terms with suppliers *before* you're late;
  defer non-essential purchases; reduce stock you don't need.
- **Owner drawings:** reduce them in the tight weeks.
- **Arrange credit early:** an overdraft or line of credit is far easier to get when you
  don't urgently need it. Invoice finance is an option for B2B businesses with good debtors.
- **Talk to the tax authority** before missing a deadline; many offer time-to-pay
  arrangements, but penalties and interest usually still apply. *Terms vary by country.*

## Gotchas
- Forecasting collections at invoice terms when customers really pay 20 days late.
- Forgetting annual or quarterly items: insurance renewals, annual software, quarterly
  tax instalments, bonuses, a 13th payroll month in some countries.
- Seasonal businesses: a 13-week view can miss the low season; keep a rough 12-month
  monthly view alongside it.
- Counting card takings gross — the processor deducts fees and may hold funds.
- Mixing personal and business accounts makes the forecast meaningless; separate them.
- Big customer concentration: if one customer is over ~25% of receipts, model them paying late.

## Verify
- [ ] Opening cash matches the bank to the unit.
- [ ] Every tax, payroll and loan date for the next 13 weeks is in the sheet.
- [ ] Receivables forecast by actual customer payment behaviour.
- [ ] Last week's forecast vs actual compared, variances explained.
- [ ] The lowest-cash week is known, and if it breaches the minimum line there's a named action.
