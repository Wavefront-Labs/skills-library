# Delegation and management cadence — running a unit through other managers

**Goal:** as a general manager, get decisions made at the right level and see
problems early, without doing your reports' jobs or losing control.

## 1. Decide what to delegate, and how much
Sort recurring decisions into a simple **decision-rights table** (a lightweight
RACI or "DACI"): decision · who decides · who must be consulted · who is informed.
Then set the delegation level per item — a useful five-step scale:
1. Do what I say (rare; crises, new staff).
2. Look into it and report options; I decide.
3. Recommend; I approve.
4. Decide and tell me afterwards.
5. Decide; no need to tell me.
Most dysfunction comes from mismatched levels: the manager thinks it's level 4, the
report thinks it's level 2, and the decision stalls. **Say the level out loud**.

Keep for yourself: hiring/firing of your direct reports, setting priorities between
teams, anything above your stated financial authority, and the few
"one-way door" decisions that are expensive to reverse.

## 2. Delegate outcomes, not tasks
A good handover states: the outcome and why it matters, the constraints (budget,
deadline, non-negotiables), the delegation level, check-in points, and what "done"
looks like. Then **don't take it back** when it gets hard — coach instead
("what options do you see?").

## 3. The management cadence
| Rhythm | Format | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly 1:1 (30–45 min) per direct report | Their agenda first, then yours | Unblock, coach, early warnings |
| Weekly staff meeting (60–90 min) | Metrics → cross-team issues → decisions | Coordination; decisions that need several people |
| Monthly business review | Dashboard vs plan, deep dive on 1–2 topics | Performance and course correction |
| Quarterly planning | Priorities, resourcing, targets | Re-allocation |
Protect it: cancelling 1:1s is the fastest way to lose early warning.

## 4. Managing managers specifically
- Judge them on their **team's** results and health (attrition, engagement,
  succession), not their personal output.
- Hold **skip-levels** (meet their reports) quarterly, framed as listening, never as
  going around the manager. Feed themes back without naming individuals.
- Watch for managers who escalate everything (level too low or low confidence) or
  nothing (hiding problems). Both show up as surprises.

## 5. Information without micromanaging
Agree a small set of **leading indicators** per team that you review weekly. If those
are healthy, stay out. Ask for problems early with a "no-surprises" rule: bad news
travels fast and is never punished for being early.

## Gotchas
- **Upward delegation:** a report brings a problem and leaves it with you. Reply with
  "what do you recommend?" and hand it back at level 3.
- **Delegating without authority:** giving accountability but not budget, access or
  decision rights sets people up to fail.
- **Bottleneck GM:** if your calendar is the constraint on decisions, your delegation
  levels are too low.
- The five-level scale is a common management-training heuristic, not a formal
  standard; adapt the wording. *(Draft.)*

## Verify
- [ ] A written decision-rights list exists and each report can state their levels.
- [ ] Every delegated item has outcome, constraints, level and check-ins.
- [ ] 1:1s and staff meetings ran on schedule for the last month.
- [ ] You learned of the last significant problem from the owner, not from a third party.
- [ ] Skip-level themes were gathered this quarter and acted on.
