# Payout rate and spending policy

**Goal:** decide how much the foundation spends each year, how that amount is smoothed
against market swings, how it's split across programmes, and how much of future years
is already committed — consistent with the board's intent on lifespan. Legal minimums
and what counts toward them are jurisdiction-specific (e.g. the US private-foundation
minimum investment return of 5% of net investment assets under IRC 4942(e), with qualifying distributions
including grants and part of reasonable administrative expenses (4942(g)); Australian private ancillary funds must
distribute at least 5% of net assets at the end of the previous year, or $11,000 if greater under the 2019 guidelines,
with a 4% rate for some eligible funds in 2021-23 per ATO; other regimes differ or have none) — **confirm with counsel and
current regulations**; figures below are illustrative.

## 1. First decide lifespan
- **Perpetuity:** preserve real (inflation-adjusted) value forever. Sustainable spend
  ≈ expected long-run nominal return − inflation − investment costs. E.g. 7% nominal −
  2.5% inflation − 0.5% costs ≈ 4% real capacity. This is why many perpetual foundations
  sit near the legal minimum.
- **Spend-down / time-limited:** spend everything over a set period (e.g. 10–25 years).
  Permits much higher payout and bigger bets now; requires an exit plan for grantees.
- **Hybrid:** perpetual core plus a time-limited tranche, or elevated payout for a
  defined period (some foundations raised payout temporarily during crises).
This is a values decision about **intergenerational equity**: is a dollar for today's
problem worth more than a dollar in 30 years? Record the reasoning.

## 2. Choose a spending rule
Payout = rate × a **smoothed** asset base, so grant budgets don't whipsaw with markets:
- **Simple moving average:** rate × average of the last 12–20 quarter-end values (3–5 years).
- **Inflation-linked (hybrid / "Yale-style") rule:** spending = w × (last year's spending
  × (1 + inflation)) + (1 − w) × (rate × current asset value), with w = 0.8 in Yale's published policy
  (80% prior spending, 20% long-term rate of 5.25% on the prior year's beginning value, inflation-adjusted; weights vary by institution). Very smooth; adjusts slowly.
- **Bands:** collar the year-on-year change (e.g. ±10%) to protect grantees.
Then check the result against the **legal minimum** in that year; if smoothing
produces less than the minimum after a strong market year, the minimum binds.
Know what counts as qualifying distributions (grants, often reasonable administrative
costs of charitable activities, programme-related investments — *rules vary*) and any
carry-forward of excess distributions (IRC 4942(i) applies excess qualifying distributions of up to 5 preceding years against the current distributable amount).

## 3. Model before adopting
Run a simple simulation (spreadsheet or Monte Carlo) over 20–50 years with the
investment policy's expected return and volatility: probability that real value falls
below, say, 75% of today; volatility of the annual grant budget; legal-minimum shortfall
years. Present trade-offs to the board as charts, not a single number.

## 4. From payout to programme budgets
1. Start from the total distributable amount.
2. Deduct operating costs that count toward payout (staff, evaluation) per policy.
3. Deduct **existing multi-year commitments** falling due this year.
4. Allocate the remaining **new-grant capacity** across programmes using strategy
   weights, with a small **opportunity/discretionary reserve** (e.g. 5–10%) for
   emergencies and unplanned opportunities.

## 5. Multi-year commitments
Multi-year grants are good for grantees but consume future capacity. Set a policy cap:
e.g. **committed future payments ≤ a set % of next year's projected budget**, tapering
for later years. Maintain a **commitment ladder** (by year, by programme) and update it
quarterly. In a market drawdown, commitments are honoured first, so new grantmaking
absorbs the shock — the cap prevents years of zero new grants.

## 6. Governance
A written spending policy approved by the board, reviewed every 3–5 years or after
major market or mission changes; alignment with the investment policy statement
(return target must support the payout); annual board report showing payout vs rule,
legal minimum, commitments and real-value trend. Remember the US private foundation
excise tax on net investment income (IRC 4940; confirm the current rate) and similar costs elsewhere in the net-return maths.

## Verify
- [ ] Lifespan decision (perpetual / spend-down / hybrid) is documented with rationale.
- [ ] Spending rule written, with smoothing method and rate, and simulated over long horizons.
- [ ] Annual payout checked against the applicable legal minimum and its counting rules.
- [ ] Commitment ladder exists and respects the board's cap.
- [ ] Investment return target is consistent with the payout rate plus inflation and costs.
