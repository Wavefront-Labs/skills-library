# Policy rollout, training and attestation — from document to behaviour

**Goal:** staff know what the policy requires of *them*, can apply it in a real situation,
and the firm can prove it — to a regulator, an auditor or a court assessing whether the
compliance programme was effective (prosecutors' guidance such as the US DOJ's *Evaluation
of Corporate Compliance Programs* asks exactly this).

## 1. Draft a policy people can use
- **Structure:** purpose, scope (who and what), the rules, roles and responsibilities,
  escalation/approval routes, consequences of breach, related documents, owner, approval
  date, next review date.
- **Policy vs procedure:** the policy states *what* and *why* and is approved at senior level;
  procedures describe *how* and are owned by the business. Don't put workflow steps in
  board-approved policies — they go stale and need re-approval to fix.
- **Plain language:** short sentences, "you must / you must not", concrete examples and
  thresholds ("gifts above €100 must be pre-approved in the gifts register").
- **Consult** the people who'll operate it; a policy that conflicts with how work is actually
  done will be ignored or worked around.
- Record the obligations it implements (link to the obligations register).

## 2. Approve and publish
Approval at the level the policy framework sets; version control; one authoritative
location (intranet/policy portal), old versions archived with dates. Announce changes with
a short summary of **what's different** — nobody re-reads a 20-page policy.

## 3. Training that fits the risk
- **Training needs analysis:** which roles face which risks? Sales staff and procurement need
  deep anti-bribery training; everyone needs the basics.
- **Role-based** modules with realistic scenarios from your own business ("a distributor
  asks for an extra 5% 'marketing fee' in cash") beat generic e-learning.
- Include where to get help and how to speak up.
- Keep core modules short (15–30 min); test understanding with scenario questions, not
  recall of definitions. Set a pass mark and allow retakes.
- Higher-risk groups: live sessions where people can ask questions.
- New joiners: within a set period of starting (e.g. 30 days); refresher cycle (often annual
  for high-risk topics).

## 4. Attestation and tracking
- Staff **attest** they have read, understood and will comply (and, for some policies,
  that they've complied in the past year — e.g. conflicts declarations).
- Track completion in the LMS by person, team and manager. Chase at set intervals; escalate
  overdue completion to line managers and, persistently, to HR with consequences (bonus,
  performance review). Report completion rates to senior management.
- Keep records for the retention period — they're your evidence.

## 5. Measure effectiveness, not just completion
- Test scores and the questions most often failed (fix the training or the policy).
- Volume and quality of questions to compliance, approvals requested, registers used
  (e.g. gifts declared rising after training is often a good sign).
- Monitoring results and breaches in the policy area; speak-up reports.
- Short pulse surveys: do staff know where to go for help?
Completion rates of 98% tell you people clicked through; they don't tell you behaviour changed.

## Gotchas
- Policies written by lawyers for lawyers; staff can't find the rule they need.
- Annual review dates that pass silently — track them.
- Attestation without training, or training without the current policy version.
- Exemptions for senior executives from training — a classic regulator criticism.
- Translations missing for local staff in multinational rollouts.

## Verify
- [ ] Policy has owner, scope, rules with examples, escalation route, approval and review date.
- [ ] Training needs analysis maps roles to modules.
- [ ] Training uses business-specific scenarios and tests understanding.
- [ ] Completion and attestation tracked by person; overdue escalated.
- [ ] At least two effectiveness measures reported beyond completion rate.
