# Debt repayment strategy — list, order, accelerate, protect

**Goal:** get out of consumer debt faster and cheaper with a written plan, without
wrecking your credit file or falling for "debt relief" traps. General education;
lending law, credit scoring and collection rules differ by country — confirm locally
(free government or non-profit credit counselling exists in most places).

## 1. The debt list (do this first, it's half the cure)
For every debt: lender, balance, **APR**, minimum payment, due date, type (revolving /
instalment / secured), promotional rate expiry, and whether it's in arrears. Pull your
**credit report** (free from the official bureau channel in most countries) to catch
anything you forgot. Total the minimums: that's your floor; everything above it in the
budget is your **attack amount**.

## 2. Choose an order
Pay minimums on everything, put the whole attack amount on one target, and when it's
cleared roll its payment into the next ("rollover" — this is what creates momentum).
- **Avalanche:** highest APR first. Mathematically cheapest. Use when you're
  disciplined and rates differ a lot.
- **Snowball:** smallest balance first. Costs a bit more interest but quick wins
  keep many people going. Use when motivation is the real constraint.
- **Hybrid:** knock out one or two tiny balances, then switch to avalanche.
Overrides: anything in arrears or with collection/legal action, secured debts where you
could lose the home or car, and tax debts usually come first regardless of rate.

Run both orders in a spreadsheet or online calculator (simulated: four debts, 200/month extra, avalanche finished in 47 months with 4,571.91 interest, snowball in 48 months with 5,228.01); if the avalanche saves only a
small amount, pick the one you'll stick with.

## 3. Lower the rate
- **Ask.** Call the card issuer, cite your payment history and a competitor's offer,
  request a lower APR. Works more often than people expect; costs nothing.
- **Balance transfer:** 0% for 6–24 months, usually with a 2–5% transfer fee (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) (a 3% fee on 5,000 is 150; a 12-month promo needs 416.67 a month). Only worth
  it if the debt is cleared *before* the promo ends — divide the balance by the promo
  months and check you can pay that. Don't spend on the new card (purchases may accrue
  at full rate), and don't close the old card on the same day without thinking
  about utilisation (below).
- **Consolidation loan:** one fixed-rate instalment loan replaces several cards. Good if
  the rate is genuinely lower *and* you cut up or freeze the cards; disastrous if the
  cards fill up again. Watch for origination fees and longer terms that lower the
  payment but raise total interest — compare **total cost**, not monthly payment.
- **Avoid** turning unsecured debt into secured debt (home-equity consolidation) unless
  you've weighed the risk to the house.

## 4. Protect the credit score while you pay
Factors (US FICO-style; other countries' models are broadly similar but *not identical*):
myFICO lists payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%, length of history 15%, new credit 10%, credit mix 10%, so
**never miss a minimum**; set autopay for at least the minimum on everything. **Utilisation** (balances ÷ limits)
is part of amounts owed: low use helps and near-maxed cards hurt. The "under 30%" and "under 10%" figures are
rules of thumb, not published FICO cut-offs (myFICO gives none), and the statement-date effect
is unconfirmed here (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked). Closing old cards can raise utilisation and shorten history.
Hard enquiries from many applications dent the score briefly — rate-shop loans within a
short window.

## 5. When you can't pay: negotiate early
1. Contact the creditor **before** you miss payments; ask for the hardship team.
2. Offer what you can actually sustain from your budget — not what feels polite.
3. Ask for: reduced or frozen interest, waived fees, a temporary lower payment, or a
   structured plan. Get every agreement **in writing** before paying.
4. For collectors: request written validation of the debt; check whether it's
   statute-barred in your jurisdiction (and don't make a payment that may restart the
   clock until you know — *rules vary*). Settlements below balance can have tax and
   credit-report consequences.
5. Be wary of fee-charging "debt relief" firms that tell you to stop paying; that's
   where credit damage and fees pile up. Non-profit counselling first.

## Verify
- [ ] Written list with APR, minimum and due date for every debt, cross-checked against the credit report.
- [ ] Autopay set for at least the minimum on each.
- [ ] One named target debt and a fixed monthly attack amount.
- [ ] Any balance transfer has a payoff-by-promo-end figure that fits the budget.
- [ ] Hardship agreements are documented in writing.
- [ ] Utilisation and score checked at least quarterly.
