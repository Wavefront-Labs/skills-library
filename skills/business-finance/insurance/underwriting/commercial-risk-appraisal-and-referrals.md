# Commercial risk appraisal and referral decisions (underwriting)

**Goal:** decide whether to accept a commercial insurance risk, on what terms and at
what price, staying inside your authority and the portfolio's appetite — and know
when to refer instead of deciding.

*Draft: illustrated with commercial property/liability; the method transfers to most
P&C lines. Authority limits and guidelines are insurer-specific.*

## 1. Read the submission against appetite first
Before any analysis, check the risk is one you write: **class of business, territory,
size (sums insured / turnover), occupancy/trade** against the underwriting guidelines'
appetite list and exclusions. Out-of-appetite risks are declined or referred
immediately — don't spend an hour pricing something you can't write.

## 2. Check the submission is complete
Typical minimum for property/casualty: proposal form signed, schedule of values per
location, construction/occupancy/protection/exposure (**COPE**) details, turnover and
wage roll, **5 years of loss history** (insurer-produced loss runs, not just the
broker's summary), current insurer and premium, requested limits and deductibles.
Missing data = ask; don't assume the benign case.

## 3. Appraise the risk
- **Hazard:** what can go wrong and how badly — physical (COPE, nat-cat exposure),
  moral (management quality, financial health, honesty of disclosures), morale
  (carelessness because insured).
- **Loss history:** frequency vs severity, trend, cause of each large loss, and what
  has been done to stop recurrence. A claim with clear risk improvement afterwards
  can be better than a clean but unmanaged record.
- **Maximum foreseeable / probable loss** for large property risks; aggregation with
  what you already hold in that area (accumulation).
- Survey or risk-engineering report for larger risks; read recommendations and
  whether prior ones were completed.

## 4. Shape the terms
Levers besides price: **deductible/excess** size, **sub-limits**, **exclusions** and
**endorsements**, **warranties/conditions precedent** (e.g. alarm maintained, hot-work
permit system), co-insurance or line size (% share), survey requirements.
Use terms to fix specific hazards, price to pay for the remaining risk.

## 5. Price within guidelines
Apply the technical rating tool/rate guide; then judgement loadings/discounts within
permitted bands. Document the gap between **technical price** and **quoted price**
— persistent under-technical writing is how portfolios deteriorate.

## 6. Refer when you must
Refer to a senior underwriter or the referral committee when: above your financial
authority (limit/premium), outside guideline appetite, technical price deviation
beyond your band, unusual wording requests, adverse moral hazard indicators, or
sanctions/compliance flags. A referral note states the risk, your recommendation,
and exactly which rule triggered referral.

## 7. Record the decision
Underwriting file: data received, analysis, terms, technical vs actual price,
referrals and approvals, conditions for renewal. Auditors and peer reviewers read this.

## Gotchas
- **Non-disclosure:** depending on jurisdiction, the insured's duty to disclose (or
  fair presentation) affects your remedies later; ask specific questions rather than
  relying on general duties.
- **Broker pressure on renewal dates** — never bind without required information
  "to be provided later" unless your authority allows it and it's a subject-to.
- Ignoring accumulation: each risk is fine alone, the portfolio isn't.

## Verify
- [ ] Appetite and authority checked before analysis.
- [ ] Loss history covers 5 years from insurer-sourced data; large losses explained.
- [ ] Terms address the specific hazards identified.
- [ ] Technical vs quoted price recorded; deviations within your band or referred.
- [ ] File documents rationale, referrals and subjectivities.
