# Equity research and the investment thesis

**Goal:** a one-to-three-page memo that says what the market believes, why you
disagree, what the stock is worth, what would prove you wrong, and how you'll know —
built from primary documents, not from other people's summaries. Not investment
advice.

## 1. Read the primary sources, in this order
1. **Annual report / 10-K:** business description, segment note, risk factors,
   MD&A, accounting policies, the notes (revenue recognition, leases, contingencies,
   related parties, share-based pay).
2. **Last 4–8 quarterly reports and earnings call transcripts:** what management
   promised vs delivered; language that changed.
3. **Proxy / remuneration report:** what management is paid to maximise (EPS growth
   can be gamed; ROIC and per-share free cash flow are harder).
4. Competitors' filings, industry data, customer and supplier commentary.
Read sell-side research last, to learn the consensus view you're testing.

## 2. Business quality
- **Unit economics** and what drives revenue (price × volume × mix).
- **Return on invested capital** vs cost of capital over a cycle. Persistent
  ROIC > WACC needs a reason — a moat: switching costs, network effects, cost
  advantage, intangible assets, efficient scale. Name it and test it.
- **Reinvestment runway:** can it reinvest at those returns?
- Capital allocation track record: acquisitions, buybacks at what prices, dilution.

## 3. Earnings quality checks
- **Cash conversion:** operating cash flow / net income over several years; free
  cash flow vs reported earnings.
- Receivables or inventory growing faster than revenue (DSO/DIO rising).
- Rising capitalised costs (software, development, contract costs).
- "Adjusted" earnings that exclude recurring items; stock-based compensation excluded
  but very real.
- Auditor changes, restatements, late filings, related-party transactions.

## 4. Valuation — at least two ways
- **DCF** with explicit drivers (see business-finance.corporate-finance.financial-modelling.three-statement-model-build-and-integrity-checks);
  check how much value sits in the terminal value and what growth/ROIC it implies.
- **Multiples** vs history and peers: EV/EBITDA, EV/sales for early-stage, P/E,
  FCF yield. Use the right numerator: EV multiples with pre-interest measures, equity
  multiples with post-interest ones.
- **Reverse DCF:** what growth and margins does today's price imply? Often the most
  useful single test.
- Output: bear / base / bull values with probabilities, not one target.

## 5. Write the thesis
- **Variant perception:** "Consensus thinks X; I think Y because Z evidence."
  Without a variant view there's no edge.
- **Catalysts** and timeframe: what event will make the market see Y?
- **Risks and pre-mortem:** "it's two years later and this lost 40% — why?"
- **Kill criteria:** specific, observable facts that mean the thesis is wrong (e.g.
  net revenue retention below 105% for two quarters). Decide them now.
- Position-size implication: conviction and downside, not excitement.

## Gotchas
- **Anchoring** on a purchase price or a first model.
- **Narrative over numbers:** a great product doesn't make a great stock at any price.
- **Peer sets** cherry-picked to make the stock look cheap.
- **Confirmation bias:** spend real time on the strongest bear argument.
- Using material non-public information (e.g. from an expert network leak) is
  illegal insider dealing — stop and escalate to compliance.

## Verify
- [ ] Memo cites primary filings, not just secondary research.
- [ ] Cash conversion and working-capital trends checked.
- [ ] Two valuation methods plus a reverse DCF; bear/base/bull stated.
- [ ] Variant perception, catalysts, risks and kill criteria written down.
- [ ] Thesis revisited after each quarterly report against kill criteria.
