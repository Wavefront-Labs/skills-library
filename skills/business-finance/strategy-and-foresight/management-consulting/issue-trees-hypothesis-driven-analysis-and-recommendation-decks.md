# Issue trees, hypothesis-driven analysis and recommendation decks

**Goal:** go from an ambiguous client question to a defensible recommendation the
client acts on, in weeks rather than months, by structuring the problem and testing
answers instead of boiling the ocean. Works for internal strategy teams too.

## 1. Problem statement first
One sentence, agreed with the client sponsor in writing: **SMART-ish** (specific,
measurable, with a time frame), naming the decision-maker and the decision.
"What must <client> do to restore EBITDA margin from 8% to 12% within 24 months?"
Add: context, success criteria, scope (in/out), constraints, key stakeholders. Most
failed engagements trace back to an unclear or unshared problem statement.

## 2. Issue tree (MECE)
Break the question into sub-questions that are **Mutually Exclusive and Collectively
Exhaustive**:
```
Restore margin to 12%?
├─ Grow revenue?    ├─ price  ├─ volume (existing customers / new)  └─ mix
└─ Reduce costs?    ├─ COGS (materials, labour, overhead)  └─ SG&A (sales, G&A)
```
Mathematical decompositions (profit = revenue − cost) are naturally MECE; conceptual ones
need checking. Go 3-4 levels until each leaf is answerable with specific analysis.
Prune branches that can't move the answer materially.

## 3. Hypothesis-driven work
- Write a **day-one answer**: your best guess at the recommendation, from interviews and
  experience ("Margin gap is mostly price leakage in mid-market discounts and SKU
  complexity in plant B").
- Turn the tree into a **hypothesis tree**: each branch a testable statement.
- For each hypothesis: the analysis that would confirm/kill it, the data needed, the
  owner, the date. That's your **workplan**.
- Prioritise analyses by impact × ease; do 80/20 cuts first.
- Kill hypotheses quickly when data says so. The risk of hypothesis-driven work is
  confirmation bias: actively look for disconfirming evidence and say so in team meetings.

## 4. Ghost deck and storyline
Early (week 1-2), sketch the final deck with placeholder charts: each slide's **action
title** (a full-sentence conclusion) and what chart would prove it. It shows which
analyses matter and keeps the team from producing charts nobody needs.

## 5. Writing the recommendation (Pyramid Principle)
- Lead with the answer. Opening via **SCQA**: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer.
- Under the answer, 3-5 supporting arguments, each grouped MECE, each supported by data.
- **Action titles**: every slide title is the "so what" in one sentence (≤2 lines);
  the body proves it. A reader of titles only should get the whole story.
- One message per slide; source and date on every chart; label units.
- Executive summary on one page; appendix for detail.
- Include implementation: initiatives, owners, timeline, quick wins, risks, value at stake.

## 6. Engagement management
- Kick-off with sponsor: problem statement, scope, governance, data requests, interview list.
- Weekly sponsor check-ins; **steering committee** every 2-4 weeks for decisions.
- "No surprises": pre-wire key findings with each stakeholder before the big meeting,
  especially bad news for their area.
- Track: workplan status, data requests outstanding, risks, scope changes (put changes
  in writing with time/cost implications).
- Interview well: prepared guide, listen more than talk, write notes the same day.

## 7. Case interviews (for candidates)
The same skills under time pressure: clarify, structure aloud (issue tree), state
hypotheses, do maths cleanly, synthesise to an answer with next steps. Practise with
partners out loud.

## Gotchas
- Trees that mix levels or overlap (e.g. "pricing" and "revenue" as siblings).
- Analysis for its own sake; every chart must answer a branch.
- Topic titles ("Market overview") instead of conclusions.
- Recommendations without owners or a first step don't get implemented.
- Client data confidentiality: anonymise everything reused elsewhere.

## Verify
- [ ] Problem statement signed off by the sponsor.
- [ ] Issue tree passes a MECE check by someone not on the team.
- [ ] Each hypothesis has an analysis, data source, owner and date.
- [ ] Reading only the slide titles tells the full story.
- [ ] Recommendations include owners, timeline and value at stake.
