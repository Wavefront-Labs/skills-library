# Where to play, how to win, and portfolio choices

**Goal:** a strategy that is a set of **choices** (including what you won't do) that
changes how money and people are allocated, not a vision statement plus a list of goals.

## 1. Start with a diagnosis (Rumelt's "kernel")
A good strategy has: a **diagnosis** of the critical challenge, a **guiding policy** for
dealing with it, and **coherent actions** that carry it out. Write the diagnosis first in
a few sentences: what is actually going on, what's the obstacle? ("Our core product is
commoditising and two low-cost entrants are taking the mid-market.")
Warning signs of bad strategy: fluff, goals mistaken for strategy ("grow 20%"), a long
list of unconnected priorities, ignoring the hard problem.

## 2. The choice cascade (Playing to Win)
Answer in order, each consistent with the others:
1. **Winning aspiration**: what winning means, concretely.
2. **Where to play**: which customers, geographies, channels, products, value-chain
   stages. Also where *not* to play.
3. **How to win**: the advantage in those places: lower cost or differentiation (a
   reason customers choose you that competitors can't easily copy).
4. **Capabilities**: the few activities you must be excellent at, reinforcing each other.
5. **Management systems**: measures, structures, incentives, investment processes that
   support the capabilities.
Test: "What would have to be true?" for each choice to be right; then test the riskiest
conditions with data, not debate.

## 3. Growth options
Map options on an **Ansoff matrix** (existing/new products × existing/new markets):
penetration, market development, product development, diversification (riskiest).
For each: market attractiveness, your right to win, investment, time to payoff, risk.
Balance across **three horizons** (core today, emerging businesses, future options),
noting the approach is a heuristic; time frames depend on the industry.
Build vs buy vs partner: acquisitions are fast but integration risk is high and many
deals destroy value for the acquirer (a widely reported pattern in M&A research; quoted
failure rates vary a lot by study and definition).

## 4. Portfolio choices (multi-business companies)
- Plot units on **market attractiveness vs competitive position** (GE-McKinsey style) or
  growth vs relative share (BCG). Treat these as conversation starters; they ignore
  synergies and cash needs.
- Ask the **parenting** question: does our ownership make this business more valuable
  than it would be under another owner (shared customers, capabilities, scale)? If not,
  it's a divestment candidate.
- Decide per unit: invest to grow, maintain, fix, harvest for cash, or exit.
- Make **capital allocation** follow the strategy: compare actual capex/opex/headcount
  by unit against the stated priorities. A strategy that doesn't move budgets isn't one.

## 5. Running a strategic review (practical cadence)
1. Fact base (4-8 weeks): market, customers, competitors (see competitive-analysis),
   financial performance by segment, capabilities.
2. Diagnosis workshop with leadership.
3. Options generation (several genuinely different options, including "status quo
   adjusted"), then what-would-have-to-be-true tests.
4. Choice, then a short strategy document (choices, rationale, resource shifts,
   milestones, KPIs) and communication cascade.
5. Annual refresh; quarterly tracking of milestones and leading indicators.
Stress-test the chosen strategy against scenarios (see scenario-planning).

## Gotchas
- Choosing everything = choosing nothing; force trade-offs.
- Strategy written by a separate team without operating leaders rarely gets executed.
- Benchmarking competitors into the same strategy erodes differentiation.
- Sunk-cost attachment to legacy units delays exits.
