# Horizon scanning, weak signals and futures wheels

**Goal:** see longer-term changes early enough to act on them, and turn observations
into decisions, rather than producing a glossy trends report nobody uses. Foresight is
about widening the range of futures considered; it doesn't predict.

## 1. Set scope and cadence
Define: the organisational question or domain ("future of retail banking customers in
our markets"), time horizon (often 5-20 years), and who will use the output. Scanning
should be **continuous** (a light weekly habit across several people) with periodic
synthesis (quarterly or semi-annual), not a one-off project.

## 2. Horizon scanning: what and where
- Use **STEEP/PESTLE** categories to force breadth beyond your own industry.
- Sources: academic preprints, patents, start-up funding announcements, regulatory
  consultations, niche and fringe media, conferences outside your sector, government
  foresight units' public outputs, futures reports (treat as inputs, not answers),
  conversations with "edge" users and young people.
- Capture each item as a **scan hit** in a shared database: title, one-line summary,
  source link, date, STEEP category, why it might matter to us, scanner name.

## 3. Weak signals vs trends vs megatrends
- **Weak signal**: an early, ambiguous indication of possible change (a novel
  regulation in one small jurisdiction, an odd consumer behaviour in a niche). Low
  evidence, potentially high impact.
- **Emerging issue / trend**: a direction of change with growing evidence across sources.
- **Megatrend**: large, slow, well-evidenced shifts (ageing populations, urbanisation,
  climate change) that will shape most futures.
Cluster scan hits periodically: several weak signals pointing the same way may be an
emerging trend. Record your confidence level and what evidence would raise or lower it.

## 4. Trend radar
Plot clustered trends on a radar: sectors by STEEP category, rings by time to
significant impact (e.g. 0-3, 3-7, 7+ years), dot size or colour for impact on the
organisation. Review and update it each synthesis cycle; changes between versions are
often more informative than the snapshot.

## 5. Futures wheel: explore implications
Put a trend or event in the centre ("half of customers use AI agents to switch
providers automatically"). Ring 1: direct consequences. Ring 2: consequences of those.
Ring 3: further ripples. Run it in a group of 4-8 for 45-60 minutes; mark which
implications are opportunities, threats, or uncertain. Second- and third-order
effects are where non-obvious insights usually appear.

## 6. From futures to decisions
- **Three Horizons** framing: H1 (current system, declining fit), H3 (emerging
  future patterns, seen today in pockets), H2 (transitional innovations). Ask what to
  protect, what to let go, and what to seed.
- **Backcasting**: describe a preferred future state, then work backward to the
  milestones and decisions needed from today.
- Feed the most important uncertainties into scenario planning (see scenario-planning)
  and the strategy cycle; feed signposts into risk registers.
- Output format: a short brief per issue: what's changing, evidence, implications for
  us, confidence, recommended action (watch / explore / act) with an owner.

## 7. Other methods (when to reach for them)
Delphi (structured, anonymous multi-round expert surveys) for contested estimates;
cross-impact analysis to examine how trends interact; visioning workshops for
stakeholder alignment.

## Gotchas
- Scanning only familiar sources reproduces the consensus you already hold.
- Treating a viral prediction as a trend without corroboration.
- Foresight teams disconnected from budget decisions: outputs become decorative.
- Over-precision: dated predictions ("X by 2031") lose credibility; use ranges and conditions.
- Uncertain: there is no agreed standard for horizon-scanning cadence or radar design;
  the formats above are common practice, adapt them.
