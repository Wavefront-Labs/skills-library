# Competitor profiles, five forces and market sizing

**Goal:** a fact base about the market and competitors that answers a specific
decision (enter, price, position, invest), built from sources you can cite, with
estimates labelled as estimates.

## 1. Frame the question first
"How attractive is the UK mid-market payroll software segment, and can we win there?"
beats "do a competitor analysis". The question decides which competitors, which
metrics, and how deep.

## 2. Industry structure: Porter's five forces
For the defined industry, rate each force (low/medium/high) **with evidence**:
1. **Rivalry** among existing competitors: number and balance, growth rate, fixed costs,
   differentiation, exit barriers.
2. **Threat of new entrants**: scale economies, capital needs, regulation, access to
   channels, switching costs, incumbent retaliation.
3. **Threat of substitutes**: other ways to do the same job (including spreadsheets or doing nothing).
4. **Buyer power**: concentration, switching costs, price sensitivity, backward integration.
5. **Supplier power**: concentration, uniqueness, switching costs (cloud providers,
   key components, specialised labour).
Conclude: where is profit captured in this industry, and what's changing? Use it on a
clearly bounded industry; drawn too broadly, every force looks "medium".

## 3. Competitor profiles (one page each)
Fields: offering and pricing, target customers, positioning/messaging, size (revenue,
headcount trend), funding/ownership, channels, strengths and weaknesses, recent moves
(launches, hires, acquisitions), likely next moves.
Sources (public and ethical only): annual reports and filings (SEC EDGAR, Companies
House), investor presentations and earnings calls, websites and pricing pages (use the
Wayback Machine for change history), job postings (reveal priorities and tech stack),
review sites (G2, Capterra, Trustpilot), patents, press, trade shows, former customers.
Never misrepresent yourself to obtain information, use leaked confidential documents,
or ask new hires for their former employer's confidential data.

## 4. Market sizing: do it two ways
- **Top-down**: an industry report figure × the share relevant to your segment. Quick,
  but analyst reports disagree widely and definitions differ.
- **Bottom-up**: number of target customers × adoption/penetration × price per year.
  E.g. roughly 37,000 UK firms with 50-249 staff (government business population
  estimates; check the latest release)
  × 30% buying third-party payroll software × £6,000/yr ≈ £67M (illustrative inputs).
- Express as **TAM** (everyone with the need), **SAM** (those your model can serve) and
  **SOM** (realistically obtainable in 3-5 years given competition and capacity).
- Triangulate: if top-down and bottom-up differ by >2×, find out why before presenting.
  Show assumptions in a table so others can challenge each.

## 5. Positioning map
Pick the two dimensions **customers** use to choose (from interviews or win-loss, not
internal opinion), plot competitors, and look for crowded and empty spaces. Empty spaces
may be empty because nobody wants them; validate.

## 6. Win-loss analysis (the best competitive data you own)
Interview recent wins and losses (ideally by someone outside the sales team, 20-30
minutes, within weeks of the decision). Ask: alternatives considered, decision criteria,
why chosen/not, perceptions of each vendor, price role. Code the reasons; look for
patterns over 15-20+ interviews. Feed sales **battlecards** with what actually wins.

## Gotchas
- Profiling only the competitors you know, missing substitutes and new entrants.
- Treating vendor-reported or analyst numbers as fact; cite and caveat them.
- SWOT lists without implications are decoration; end every analysis with "so what".
- Stale profiles: date every profile and refresh at least twice a year for key rivals.

## Verify
- [ ] The decision question is written at the top.
- [ ] Each force rating has cited evidence.
- [ ] Every competitor profile is dated and sourced.
- [ ] Market size computed top-down and bottom-up with an assumption table.
- [ ] Win-loss reasons come from customer interviews, not only CRM drop-downs.
