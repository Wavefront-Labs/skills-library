# Grading by hand at line speed with consistent judgement

**Goal:** every grader on every shift puts the same item in the same grade, at the speed
the belt runs, and keeps doing so at hour seven. Applies to produce and food grading,
inspection of moulded or machined parts, and sorting recyclables.

## 1. Grade standards you can see
- Words like "minor blemish" are not a standard. Each grade needs **limit samples**
  (physical or high-quality photos) showing the *boundary*: "worst acceptable" and
  "just rejected" for every defect type.
- A **defect library** at the station: defect name, photo, grade it causes, any size
  limit (e.g. bruise ≤ 10 mm diameter allowed in grade 1 — numbers per your customer or
  statutory grade spec).
- Size limits get a reference: a gauge ring, a printed scale on the belt guide, a
  coin-sized template.
- Keep limit samples fresh (real produce decays; photos fade) and controlled by quality.

## 2. Set the station up to make seeing possible
- **Lighting:** bright, even and colour-correct where colour matters. Inspection
  guidance commonly quotes roughly 1000 lux or more for fine visual inspection, with
  high colour rendering (CRI 90+) for colour grading — check your site's spec. No glare
  or shadows across the belt.
- **Belt load:** a single layer, not overlapping. If product piles up, nobody can see
  the underside — ask for the infeed to be throttled rather than trying harder.
- **Rolling/turning:** roller tables or turning devices so each item shows all sides;
  otherwise turn by hand.
- **Ergonomics:** standing height so you look down at the belt without bending; reach
  to the far side within ~0.5 m; anti-fatigue mats.

## 3. Technique at pace
- Use a **fixed scan pattern**: e.g. look upstream at a zone ahead, sweep across the
  belt, follow items into your reach zone. Eyes ahead of hands.
- Work your assigned **lane or zone** only; with several graders, split the belt by
  width or by defect type (one picks rots, the next grades size) so responsibility is
  clear.
- Pick with the hand nearest the item; drop straight into the right chute or tote
  without looking twice.
- When unsure on a borderline item, follow the rule your site sets (usually "if in
  doubt, downgrade") and check it against the limit sample at the next pause.

## 4. Fatigue and vigilance
Detection rates fall noticeably with time on a repetitive visual task; many plants
rotate graders every 20–60 minutes between grading and other tasks, with short breaks.
(Exact intervals vary; follow site practice and watch your own error checks.) Signs
you're drifting: you notice a reject that passed you, or you stop seeing any defects at
all on a batch that should have some.

## 5. Keeping graders consistent
- **Calibration sessions:** at shift start or weekly, grade a set of known samples
  (including borderline ones) and compare with the master answer.
- **Attribute agreement analysis:** several graders grade the same 30–50 mixed items
  twice, blind. Measure agreement with themselves (repeatability), with each other,
  and with the standard. Kappa above roughly 0.75 is usually taken as good, under
  0.4 poor (common rule of thumb). Retrain on the defect types where they disagree.
- **Audit the output:** a quality check of a sample of each grade bin gives a running
  measure of escapes.

## 6. Reject handling
- Rejects into clearly marked, colour-coded containers per reject class
  (waste, rework, lower grade); never on the floor next to good product.
- Foreign bodies or contamination (glass, metal, plastic, pests) are not a grading
  matter — stop and call the supervisor; keep the item.
- Record reject counts per defect type; a jump points to an upstream problem.

## Verify
- [ ] Limit samples at the station for every defect boundary.
- [ ] Lighting and belt load allow every item to be seen, all sides.
- [ ] Rotation schedule followed.
- [ ] Graders pass calibration; attribute agreement measured and acted on.
- [ ] Reject bins segregated; foreign bodies escalated, not sorted.

*Uncertain: lux, rotation intervals and kappa thresholds are common guidance, not fixed
rules.*
