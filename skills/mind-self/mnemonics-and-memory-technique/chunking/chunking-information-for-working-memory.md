# Chunking information for working memory

**Goal:** hold and handle more information at once — a reference number, a sequence of
steps, a set of facts — by grouping items into fewer, bigger, meaningful units.

## Why it works
Working memory holds a small number of **chunks**, not a fixed number of letters or digits.
Miller's famous "seven, plus or minus two" (1956) is now usually revised down: Cowan's
work suggests roughly **3–5 chunks** when rehearsal is prevented. A chunk can be one digit
or a whole familiar phrase — so the trick is to make each chunk carry more.

## 1. Group raw strings into 3–4s
- `4 9 1 7 3 0 2 8 5 6` (10 items) → `491 · 730 · 285 · 6` or `49 17 30 28 56`.
- Read groups with **rhythm** — say them aloud with a pause between groups; the
  pattern helps more than you'd expect.
- Match the grouping that's printed when copying (IBANs and card numbers are already
  in 4s) so you can check back easily.

## 2. Find natural boundaries
Look for structure already there: area codes, year-like numbers (1984), repeating digits
(777), sequences (345), dates, letters that form a word. `0 1 9 6 9 2 0 2 4` →
`0 · 1969 · 2024`. Two years, one leading zero: three chunks.

## 3. Recode into something you know
Recoding converts a chunk into an existing memory:
- Digits → known numbers (your house number, a famous year, a sports score).
- Letters → words or acronyms: `F B I C I A U S A` → FBI · CIA · USA.
- Numbers → words via the Major System (see major-system) for long strings.
- Steps of a procedure → a named phase ("prep", "mix", "rest") each containing 3–4 steps.

## 4. Build hierarchies
Chunks can contain chunks. A 20-step procedure becomes 5 phases × 4 steps; you hold the
5 phase names, then expand one at a time. Outlines, headings and checklists are
hierarchical chunking on paper.

## 5. Chunking as expertise
Experts see larger chunks: chess masters recall real board positions far better than
novices, but not random arrangements (Chase and Simon, 1973). Practising the *patterns*
of a domain — chord shapes, code idioms, common phrases in a language — is how chunks grow.
Deliberate exposure to many examples, with attention to recurring structure, is the method.

## Applying it to learning and presenting
- When teaching or writing instructions, give **3–5 items per group** and label the groups.
- In slides, one chunk per slide region; avoid ten bullet points.
- For a new topic, build a skeleton of 3–5 big ideas first, then attach details.

## Gotchas
- **Too-big chunks** that aren't meaningful (a group of 7 random digits) don't help;
  the chunk must be a *unit* to you.
- **Arbitrary grouping changes** — if you group a number differently each time, you're
  re-learning it.
- **Chunking ≠ long-term memory.** It helps hold information now; to keep it, add review
  or a mnemonic.
- The capacity figures are averages from lab tasks; individual and task variation is large.

## Verify
- [ ] Any string over ~5 items is split into groups of 3–4 with rhythm.
- [ ] You looked for natural boundaries and known patterns before grouping arbitrarily.
- [ ] At least one chunk was recoded into something already familiar.
- [ ] A long procedure or topic is held as 3–5 labelled groups.
- [ ] Test: repeat a 10–12 digit number back correctly after 30 seconds.
