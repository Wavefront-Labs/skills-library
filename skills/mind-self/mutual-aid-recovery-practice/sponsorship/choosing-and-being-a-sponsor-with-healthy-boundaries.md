# Choosing and being a sponsor — with healthy boundaries

**Goal:** a peer-reference account of how sponsorship generally works in 12-step
fellowships: choosing and asking for a sponsor, and later being one, while keeping the
relationship safe. Sponsors are fellow members sharing experience — not counsellors,
doctors, lawyers or bankers. Customs vary by fellowship and group.

## As a sponsee: choosing
Look for someone who:
- has worked all the steps with their own sponsor and still does step work;
- has stable recovery — a year or more of continuous abstinence is a common rule of thumb
  (some groups suggest longer);
- talks in meetings about *how* they stay well, in a way that makes sense to you;
- is available — has time and answers the phone;
- by widespread custom is the **same gender** (or a gender you're not attracted to), to
  keep romance out of it.
A **temporary sponsor** is fine while you look; asking is normal in early recovery.

## Asking
After a meeting: "I've liked what you share. Would you consider sponsoring me?" Most
people are honoured to be asked. A "no" is usually about capacity, not you — ask someone
else. You can change sponsors at any time; a brief, honest conversation is enough.

## The working rhythm
Typical expectations a sponsor might set (they vary):
- a daily or several-times-a-week **check-in call or text** in early recovery;
- reading assigned literature and meeting regularly — weekly — for **step work**;
- going to a home group and taking a service commitment;
- calling *before* acting on a craving or a big decision, not after.

## As a sponsor: guiding the steps
- Share **your own experience** of each step, not opinions on the sponsee's life.
- Work from the fellowship's literature so the sponsee isn't relying on your personal
  version of the programme.
- Pace the steps to the person; don't race or stall them.
- Hear the fifth step in a private setting and **keep it confidential** — the obvious
  exception being an immediate risk to someone's life.
- Encourage the sponsee to build a wider network: other members' numbers, a home group.

## Boundaries a sponsor keeps
- **No romantic or sexual involvement** — "thirteenth stepping" newcomers is widely
  condemned and harmful.
- **No money lending, employment or housing arrangements** with the sponsee; these
  change the relationship.
- **Not their therapist:** suggest professional help for trauma, depression, suicidal
  thoughts, domestic violence, medication questions or withdrawal. Never advise someone
  to stop prescribed medication — that's between them and their doctor, and many
  fellowships say so explicitly.
- **Availability limits:** it's fine to say "call before 10 p.m. unless it's an emergency,
  then call [crisis line] or emergency services."
- **Not responsible for their sobriety.** You share; they choose.
- **Cap your number** of sponsees to what you can actually serve.

## Sponsee check-ins: a simple format
Today: abstinent? Cravings (0–10)? What happened? Any resentments or fears? What's the
next right action? Any step work done? Five minutes is often enough.

## Gotchas
- **Controlling sponsors** who dictate jobs, relationships or medication are a recognised
  problem; a sponsee should feel free to leave.
- **Relapse:** a sponsor's job is to keep the door open and encourage the sponsee back to
  meetings and, where needed, medical care — not to punish.
- **Crisis:** sponsors aren't emergency services. If someone talks about suicide or is
  medically at risk (overdose, severe withdrawal), call local emergency services or a
  crisis line.

## Verify
- [ ] Sponsor chosen for worked steps, stable recovery and availability.
- [ ] Check-in and step-work rhythm agreed in words.
- [ ] Boundaries (no romance, money, therapy, medication advice) understood by both.
- [ ] Sponsee has other contacts and knows crisis numbers.
