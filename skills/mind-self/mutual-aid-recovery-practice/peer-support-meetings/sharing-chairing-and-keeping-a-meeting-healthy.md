# Sharing, chairing and keeping a mutual-aid meeting healthy

**Goal:** know how to take part in a peer-support meeting well and, later, run one. The
wording here is general; each fellowship and each group has its own format card and
customs — follow those.

## Meeting types
- **Open** meetings welcome anyone (family, students); **closed** are for people who
  identify with the problem.
- Formats: speaker (one person tells their story), discussion/topic, step or literature
  study, beginners'. Online meetings follow the same principles plus tech rules (below).

## Sharing
- Speak **from your own experience** ("I…"), not advice ("you should…").
- Keep to the time the chair asks — commonly 3–5 minutes in a busy meeting.
- Stay on the topic loosely; honesty matters more than polish.
- Many fellowships open with name and identifier ("I'm Sam, alcoholic"); you can also just
  say your first name or pass. **Passing is always allowed.**
- Avoid graphic "war stories" of using that could trigger others; talk about what
  happened, what helped, and where you are now.

## Listening without crosstalk
Most 12-step meetings use **no crosstalk**: don't respond to, question, correct or advise
another person's share during the meeting. Nod, listen, and talk afterwards if invited.
It keeps the room safe for honesty. (SMART and some other programmes do allow structured,
facilitated discussion — know which kind of meeting you're in.)

## Anonymity and confidentiality
"Who you see here, what you hear here, let it stay here." Don't name attendees outside,
post photos, or share stories. Online: no screenshots or recording.

## Chairing
A typical sequence: welcome and the group's preamble → moment of silence or a reading →
introduce newcomers (optional, no pressure) → speaker or topic → open sharing, keeping
time → collection (the "seventh tradition" in 12-step groups, where groups are
self-supporting through members' contributions) → announcements → closing.
Chairing tips:
- Start and end **on time**.
- Keep shares roughly even; a gentle "thanks, we have a few minutes left for others" works.
- Leave a few minutes before close for anyone who's new or struggling.

## Service roles
Secretary (format, keys, group records), treasurer (collection, rent, contributions to
the wider service structure), chair rotation, greeter, literature, tea and coffee, and a
representative to the wider service body. Service rotates — often every 6–12 months —
and decisions go through a **group conscience** meeting, not one person.

## Welcoming newcomers
Greet at the door, sit with them, give a meeting list and a few phone numbers (same
gender by custom), and say "keep coming back". Don't overwhelm them with programme jargon
or step talk on day one.

## Handling difficulty
- **Disruptive or intoxicated attendee:** stay calm. A trusted member quietly speaks to
  them outside, offering a seat or coffee and to stay if they can be quiet. Groups often
  have a written policy agreed in group conscience.
- **Predatory behaviour** (pursuing newcomers, harassment): raise it at group conscience;
  groups can and do ask people to leave.
- **Medical emergency or suicide risk:** call emergency services immediately. Members
  aren't clinicians; know the address of the venue and where any naloxone kit is, if
  the group keeps one. *(Draft: naloxone policies vary by group and country.)*
- **Online:** use waiting rooms, mute-on-entry, a co-host to remove disrupters, and no
  public posting of meeting links where possible.

## Verify
- [ ] Shares are first person, within time, no crosstalk.
- [ ] Anonymity respected in person and online.
- [ ] The chair follows the group's format and keeps time.
- [ ] Newcomers are greeted and given contacts.
- [ ] The group has an agreed plan for disruption and emergencies.
