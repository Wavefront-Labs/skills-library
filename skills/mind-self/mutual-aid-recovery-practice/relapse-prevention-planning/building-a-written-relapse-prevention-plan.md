# Building a written relapse-prevention plan

**Goal:** a one-page, written plan — made while you're well — that you, your sponsor or
supporters can follow when risk rises. This is peer-support and self-help reference,
drawn from the published relapse-prevention model. It complements, and doesn't replace,
treatment from a doctor, counsellor or addiction service; ideally build it with one.

## 1. Map triggers and high-risk situations
Write specific, not general, entries:
- **People:** old using contacts, a particular relative.
- **Places and times:** payday, the route past a pub, Friday 6 p.m., home alone.
- **Feelings:** boredom, loneliness, anger, celebration — positive moods are risky too.
- **Body states:** the recovery shorthand **HALT** — hungry, angry, lonely, tired.
- **Things:** paraphernalia, a song, alcohol in the house.
Mark the top three. For each, write what you'll do: avoid, leave, or have a plan to cope.

## 2. Early warning signs
Relapse is often described as a process before the use itself:
- **Emotional:** isolating, skipping meetings, poor sleep and eating, bottling feelings.
- **Mental:** romanticising past use, thinking "just once", planning around opportunities,
  hanging around old places.
- **Physical:** actually using.
List your **own** personal signs (ask people who know you — they often see them first).
The plan should trigger at the emotional stage, not the physical one.

## 3. Coping menu (cravings)
Cravings typically peak and fade within minutes to an hour.
- **Delay:** "not for the next 30 minutes".
- **Urge surfing:** notice where the craving sits in the body, rate it 0–10, breathe and
  watch it crest and fall.
- **Distract:** a walk, shower, call, game, cooking — written as a specific list.
- **Leave** the situation.
- **Play the tape forward:** what happens the morning after, not the first ten minutes.
- **Connect:** call someone on the contact list.
- Physical basics: eat, rest, water.

## 4. Contacts
List at least three people with numbers (sponsor, a friend in recovery, a family member),
your doctor or keyworker, a local or national helpline, and emergency services. Tell the
people that they're on your list and what you'd like them to do.

## 5. Lapse protocol
A **lapse** (one-off use) need not become a **relapse** (return to the old pattern). The
biggest risk after a slip is the **abstinence-violation effect** — "I've failed, so I may
as well keep going." Write, in advance:
1. Stop and get somewhere safe.
2. Contact one person from the list within 24 hours.
3. Get rid of what remains.
4. Go to a meeting or appointment within a day or two.
5. Afterwards, review what led to it and update this plan — without self-punishment.

## Safety essentials
- **Opioids:** tolerance drops quickly after a break, so a lapse at the old dose can be
  fatal. Never use alone; carry **naloxone** where available and make sure someone
  knows how to use it.
- **Alcohol, benzodiazepines:** stopping again after a heavy lapse can cause dangerous
  withdrawal — get medical advice.
- **Suicidal thoughts** are a medical emergency: call emergency services or a crisis line.
- Mixing substances sharply increases overdose risk.

## Keep it alive
One page, kept on the phone and on paper; share a copy with a supporter. Review monthly in
early recovery and after any lapse, big life change or new trigger.

## Verify
- [ ] Top three triggers each have a specific plan.
- [ ] Personal early warning signs are listed, including ones others notice.
- [ ] Coping menu has concrete actions, not "be strong".
- [ ] Three contacts plus professional and emergency numbers, and they know.
- [ ] Lapse protocol and safety essentials are written and reviewed monthly.
