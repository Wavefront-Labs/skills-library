# Recovering depleted attention deliberately

**Goal:** treat focused attention as a resource that runs down and can be
refilled — notice the drain early, refill it with the right kind of break, and
plan rest instead of waiting to crash.

## Background (briefly)
Attention restoration theory (Kaplan & Kaplan) distinguishes effortful *directed*
attention — what you use to concentrate and resist distraction — from effortless
*soft fascination*, the gentle interest of watching clouds, water or leaves.
Directed attention tires with use; soft fascination lets it recover. Studies of
nature walks versus urban walks generally support a modest restorative effect.
*(The size and reliability of the effect are debated; treat it as a useful tool,
not a guarantee. The older "ego depletion" idea that willpower is a finite fuel
has largely failed to replicate — fatigue is real, but the mechanism is unclear.)*

## 1. Detect attention fatigue before it costs you
Early warning signs — learn your own:
- Rereading the same sentence or line of code several times.
- Rising irritability at small interruptions.
- More typos and small slips.
- Reaching for your phone more often; restless switching between tabs.
- Choosing easy tasks and avoiding the hard one.
When two or more show up, a break now will cost less than pushing on.

## 2. Breaks that restore vs breaks that don't
**Restorative (prefer):**
- **Walk outside**, ideally somewhere with trees, water or open sky — even 10–20
  minutes.
- **Look out of a window** at distant greenery or sky. Indoors, rest your eyes on
  something far away; screen workers often use "20-20-20" (every 20 minutes, look
  at something about 20 feet / 6 m away for 20 seconds) for eye strain.
- **Light movement**: stretching, stairs, a few minutes of easy exercise.
- **Doing nothing** — sitting quietly, a cup of tea without a screen.
- **A short nap** of 10–20 minutes, early afternoon, if your schedule allows.
  Longer naps can leave you groggy.
- **Social chat** that's easy and pleasant (not a tense conversation).

**Not restorative (or less so):**
- Social media and news feeds — they use the same directed attention and add
  emotional stimulation.
- Switching to email "as a break" — that's just different work.
- Breaks that run on with no end time.

## 3. Rest across the day
- **Micro-breaks**: every 25–90 minutes of focused work, 3–10 minutes away from
  the screen (see mind-self.attention-and-focus-training.focus-intervals).
- **A real lunch break**, away from the desk.
- **Place hard work early** in your best hours and leave lower-attention tasks for
  the afternoon dip.
- **Wind down** in the last hour before bed: dimmer light, fewer screens.

## 4. Rest across the week
- At least one block (half a day or more) without work and without obligation.
- Something absorbing but undemanding: a hobby, a long walk, time in nature.
- **Sleep is the foundation.** Most adults need around 7–9 hours. No break
  technique compensates for chronic short sleep.

## Gotchas
- **Guilt about resting** pushes people into "productive" breaks that don't
  restore. A restorative break is part of the work.
- **Caffeine masks fatigue**, it doesn't remove it. Late-day caffeine also erodes
  that night's sleep.
- **Weekend recovery can't fully repay** a week of overwork; spread rest through
  the week.
- **When rest doesn't help:** if you're exhausted most days for weeks despite
  sleeping and resting, have trouble concentrating on anything, or feel flat or
  hopeless, see a doctor. Persistent fatigue and brain fog can come from burnout,
  depression, anaemia, thyroid problems, sleep apnoea, long COVID and more.

- [ ] Your breaks today included at least one walk, outdoor view or movement.
- [ ] Breaks have an end time and don't involve feeds or email.
- [ ] Demanding work sits in your best hours.
- [ ] You're getting enough sleep, and you know when fatigue warrants a doctor.
