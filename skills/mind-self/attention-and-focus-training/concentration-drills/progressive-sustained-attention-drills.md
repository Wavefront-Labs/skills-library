# Progressive sustained-attention drills

**Goal:** strengthen your ability to hold attention on one chosen thing, the way
you'd build endurance in a muscle: short daily sets, a clear measure, and slow
increases.

The core skill in every drill is the same loop: **place attention → notice it has
wandered → return it without fuss**. The noticing-and-returning *is* the
repetition. A drill where you wandered twenty times and came back twenty times
was a good session, not a failed one.

## 1. Breath counting (the main drill)
1. Sit upright, eyes closed or lowered. Set a timer (start at 5 minutes).
2. Breathe naturally. Silently count each out-breath: "one" … up to "ten", then
   start again at one.
3. If you lose count, go past ten, or realise you're thinking about something
   else, **start again at one**. No commentary.
4. Afterwards, note roughly how many times you reset.

Variant for beginners: count in-breath and out-breath ("one" in, "one" out).

## 2. Fixed-point gazing
1. Pick a small point at eye level, about 1–2 m away: a dot on paper, a doorknob.
   (The traditional version uses a candle flame — **candle safety applies**:
   stable holder, nothing flammable nearby, never leave it unattended.)
2. Rest your gaze on it softly; blink normally. Don't stare until the eyes water.
3. When you notice your eyes or mind have drifted, return to the point.
4. 2–5 minutes is plenty to start.

Skip gazing drills if you have an eye condition, migraines triggered by
fixation, or photosensitive epilepsy (for candles) — use breath or sound instead.

## 3. Counting and mental-tracking drills
Good for days when sitting still with the breath is hard:
- **Serial subtraction:** count backwards from 300 in sevens, silently and
  accurately. Restart at 300 if you lose your place.
- **Sound anchoring:** listen to a single steady sound (a fan, a clock) and
  return to it when attention drifts.
- **Reading drill:** read a page of dense text slowly, and every time you realise
  you've read a line without taking it in, go back to that line.

## 4. Benchmark and progression
**Benchmark (week 0 and every 4 weeks):** do 5 minutes of breath counting and
note (a) how many full cycles of one-to-ten you completed and (b) roughly how
many resets. A rough self-rated measure — it's noisy day to day, so look at
trends across weeks, not single sessions.

**Progression:**
| Week | Session length | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 5 min | daily |
| 3–4 | 8 min | daily |
| 5–8 | 10–15 min | daily |
| 8+ | 20 min | 5+ days/week |

Only lengthen when the current length feels manageable on most days. Consistency
beats length: 5 minutes daily outperforms 30 minutes once a week.

Keep a one-line log: date, drill, minutes, resets, sleep/energy note. Patterns
(worse after poor sleep, better in the morning) are often the most useful output.

## Gotchas
- **Straining** — clenching the jaw, forcing focus — tires you fast. Aim for
  light, persistent attention.
- **Judging the wandering** adds a second distraction. Reset like you'd tap a
  metronome back into time.
- **Transfer is not automatic.** Improvements on drills don't guarantee better
  focus at work. *(Evidence for broad transfer from attention training is
  mixed.)* Pair drills with environmental changes (see
  mind-self.attention-and-focus-training.distraction-management).
- **Doing it lying down** usually leads to sleep. Sit.
- For some people, especially with a history of trauma, sitting with eyes closed
  and turning attention inward can bring up distress. Keep eyes open, use an
  external anchor (sound, gazing), shorten sessions, and stop if it feels
  overwhelming; consider guidance from a qualified teacher or therapist.

## Verify
- [ ] You can describe the place–notice–return loop and don't count wandering as failure.
- [ ] You have a week-0 benchmark recorded.
- [ ] Sessions are short, daily and logged.
- [ ] You lengthen sessions only after the current length is comfortable.
- [ ] You've rechecked the benchmark after 4 weeks and compared trends.
