# Setting up the conditions for flow

**Goal:** you can't switch flow on, but you can reliably set up the conditions in
which it tends to happen — and stop accidentally breaking it once it starts.

Flow, in Csikszentmihalyi's description, is complete absorption in an activity:
action and awareness merge, self-consciousness drops away, time feels distorted,
and the activity feels worth doing for itself.

## 1. The three conditions you can engineer
**a. Challenge slightly above skill.** Too easy → boredom; too hard → anxiety.
Aim for "a stretch I can just about manage". Adjust in the moment:
- Bored? Add a constraint (a time target, a harder variation, a quality bar).
- Anxious or stuck? Shrink the scope, get a reference, solve a smaller version first.

**b. A clear, near goal.** Not "work on the novel" but "get the characters from
the station to the house in this scene". The goal should be specific enough that
you know what the next action is without deciding.

**c. Immediate feedback.** You need to know moment to moment whether it's going
well. Some activities have it built in (music, sport, code that runs). For others,
build it: run tests often, read the paragraph aloud, compare against the
reference, keep a visible word or rep count.

## 2. Entry ritual
Flow usually needs a warm-up of 10–20 minutes of ordinary, slightly effortful
work before absorption arrives *(rough figure; varies by person and task)*. A
consistent ritual shortens that ramp by acting as a cue:
1. Same place or setup where possible.
2. Clear the field: phone away, notifications off, unrelated tabs closed
   (see mind-self.attention-and-focus-training.distraction-management).
3. Write the session goal on paper.
4. A fixed cue: a particular playlist, a drink, putting on headphones.
5. Start with an easy, concrete action — reread the last paragraph, fix one small
   bug — to get moving.

## 3. Protect the block
- **Book long enough slots**: 90 minutes or more if possible. A 30-minute gap
  between meetings rarely gets past the warm-up.
- **Schedule for your best hours.** Many people find mornings best for demanding
  work, but chronotype varies; check your own log.
- **Defer, don't interrupt.** Keep a capture list for ideas and to-dos that
  arise.
- **Pre-solve friction**: have materials, references and access ready before
  the block starts. Hunting for a password is a classic flow breaker.

## 4. Recognising flow (and when it isn't)
Signs: you lose track of time, stop noticing yourself, the next action is obvious,
and effort feels lighter than it should. Useful to note afterwards in a log —
what task, how long the warm-up was, what triggered it — to learn your own
triggers.

Absorption isn't always flow. Endless scrolling or gaming binges can feel
absorbing but lack a chosen goal and leave you depleted. A good test: did you
choose it, and do you feel better for it afterwards?

## 5. Exiting well
Stop at a point where you know the next step — Hemingway's advice to stop
mid-sentence. Note the next action in one line. Re-entry next time is much faster.

## Gotchas
- **Chasing the feeling** makes it recede. Focus on the task conditions, not on
  "getting into flow".
- **Flow isn't always good judgement.** Absorbed people skip breaks, meals and
  checks. Set a soft alarm for long sessions, and don't rely on flow for
  safety-critical work where fatigue matters.
- **Collaborative flow** exists (bands, teams) but needs shared goals and fast
  mutual feedback; most open-plan office settings work against it.
- **Mismatch persists?** If every task feels either crushing or dull for weeks,
  the issue may be role fit, burnout or low mood rather than technique — worth
  discussing with a manager, doctor or counsellor.

## Verify
- [ ] Each deep session starts with a specific, near goal written down.
- [ ] You know how you'll get feedback during the session.
- [ ] You adjust difficulty when bored or anxious instead of pushing through.
- [ ] You have a repeatable entry ritual and a protected 90-min block in the week.
- [ ] You end sessions with the next action noted.
