# Doing one thing at a time on purpose

**Goal:** replace the reflex of juggling several tasks with a deliberate sequence —
one task in front of you, everything else parked somewhere you trust.

## Why bother: the switch cost
For anything needing thought, people don't truly do two things at once; they
switch rapidly, and each switch has a cost. Lab studies consistently show slower
work and more errors when switching between tasks than when doing them in
sequence. Leroy's "attention residue" work adds that part of your mind stays on
the previous, unfinished task for a while after you switch. *(The exact size of
the cost varies a lot by task; popular figures like "it takes 23 minutes to
refocus" come from specific observational studies and shouldn't be quoted as a
universal number.)*

Practical upshot: the fewer unfinished things you bounce between, the better each
one goes.

## 1. Make a capture list — the key enabler
Most multitasking is driven by *open loops*: "I must remember to reply to that",
"did I book the dentist?". The mind keeps nagging until the item is stored
somewhere trustworthy.
- Keep **one capture point** within reach while working: a notepad, a single
  notes file, a task app inbox. One, not three.
- When a thought intrudes, **write a few words and return** to the task. Do not
  act on it, even if it's a two-second job — two-second jobs breed.
- **Empty the capture list** at a fixed time (end of each focus block, or twice a
  day). If you never process it, you'll stop trusting it and the nagging returns.

## 2. Set up the workspace for one thing
- **One window, one tab** for the current task. Close (don't minimise) the rest.
  Bookmark or list tabs you're afraid to lose.
- Put the current task's name on a sticky note or the top of the screen. It
  makes drift visible.
- Phone out of sight. See
  mind-self.attention-and-focus-training.distraction-management.

## 3. Catch the multitasking reflex
Treat the urge to switch as a signal, not an instruction:
1. Notice it ("I'm reaching for email").
2. Name what's happening — often the current task just got hard or boring.
3. Capture the urge on the list, take one breath, return to the task.
Track how many times you catch yourself per hour for a week. The number usually
drops as the habit forms; if it doesn't, the task may be unclear — define the
next physical action.

## 4. Batch the shallow stuff
Email, messages, small admin and approvals are real work, but they shatter deep
work when sprinkled across the day. Group them into 2–4 set windows (e.g. 11:30,
14:00, 16:30) and tell colleagues roughly when you respond. During a batch,
single-task too: process messages one at a time top to bottom, don't open five.

## 5. When you genuinely must multitask
Pairing one automatic activity with one thinking activity is fine (walking while
on a call, folding laundry with a podcast). Pairing two thinking activities —
writing while in a meeting, texting while driving — is where errors and danger
live. Never treat driving as the "automatic" half.

## Gotchas
- **Partial switches count.** Glancing at a notification without acting still
  breaks the thread.
- **Over-capturing** — writing elaborate notes for every intrusion — becomes its
  own distraction. Three to five words.
- **"Waiting" tasks** (a build running, a reply pending) tempt you to start
  something else. Pick a small, closable filler task deliberately rather than
  opening something big you'll abandon.
- Some roles (reception, on-call support) are interrupt-driven by design. Aim
  for single-tasking within each interruption rather than eliminating them.

## Verify
- [ ] You have exactly one capture point and empty it on a schedule.
- [ ] Only the current task's window/tab is open during focus time.
- [ ] Shallow work happens in set batches, not continuously.
- [ ] You can count how often you caught the switching urge today.
- [ ] Driving and similar safety tasks never get a second thinking task.
