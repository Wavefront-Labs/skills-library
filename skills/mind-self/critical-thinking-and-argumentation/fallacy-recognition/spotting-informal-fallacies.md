# Spotting informal fallacies — name it, explain it, don't overuse it

**Goal:** notice when an argument's *move* fails (not just its facts), say precisely why,
and avoid the common mistake of shouting a fallacy name at every point you dislike.

## The three-step method
1. **Restate the argument** in one or two sentences: "They are saying X because Y."
2. **Ask what connects Y to X.** Is Y actually relevant, sufficient and not just a
   restatement of X?
3. **Name the gap in plain words first**, the Latin label second. "Her income doesn't
   bear on whether the statistic is right" beats "ad hominem!".

## A working set (grouped by what goes wrong)
**Relevance — the reason is about something else**
- *Ad hominem:* attacking the person instead of the claim. **Not** a fallacy when the
  person's credibility is the issue (a witness with a motive to lie).
- *Tu quoque / whataboutism:* "you do it too" — hypocrisy doesn't make the claim false.
- *Red herring:* switching to a related but different topic.
- *Appeal to popularity / tradition:* many people, or long practice, ≠ correct.

**Distortion — the target is wrong**
- *Straw man:* refuting a weaker version than was argued. Check: would the original
  speaker accept your restatement?
- *Motte-and-bailey:* defending a bold claim (bailey) by retreating to a modest one
  (motte) when challenged, then re-advancing. Pin down which claim is on the table.
- *Equivocation:* one word, two meanings mid-argument ("theory", "natural", "free").

**Structure — the inference doesn't carry**
- *False dilemma:* only two options offered when more exist. Ask "what else?"
- *Begging the question:* the conclusion is hidden in a premise.
- *Hasty generalisation:* a few cases → a rule.
- *Post hoc:* B followed A, so A caused B. Ask for a mechanism and a comparison group.
- *Slippery slope:* a chain of "will lead to" steps with no evidence for each link.
  **Not** a fallacy if each step is actually supported.
- *Appeal to authority:* fallacious only when the authority is outside their field, or
  experts genuinely disagree. Deferring to real expert consensus is reasonable.

## Fallacy vs weakness
Many "fallacies" are only *defeasible* moves — sometimes fine, sometimes not. Before
calling foul, ask: in *this* context, is the reason irrelevant or insufficient, or just
less than conclusive? Say "that's a weak reason because…" when it is merely weak.

## The fallacy fallacy
An argument containing a fallacy can still have a true conclusion. Finding one tells
you *this reason* fails, not that the opposite is true.

## Responding in conversation
- Ask a question rather than labelling: "How does that bear on whether X is true?"
- Restate their real claim and invite correction (defuses straw-manning both ways).
- Concede the fair part first.

## Gotchas
- Naming lists vary across textbooks; names here follow common usage, not one standard.
- Online "fallacy bingo" trains labelling, not reasoning — always explain the gap.
- You are most blind to fallacies in arguments for conclusions you already like.
  Run the check on your own side's best argument as practice.

## Verify
- [ ] You can restate the argument in a form its author would accept.
- [ ] You explained the flaw in plain words, not just a label.
- [ ] You checked whether the move is legitimate in this context (credibility, real
      expertise, supported causal chain).
- [ ] You did not conclude the claim is false merely because one argument for it failed.
