# Debiasing your own judgement — procedures, not willpower

**Goal:** reduce the systematic errors in your own estimates and decisions. Key fact:
*knowing about* a bias does little to remove it (you'll spot it in others first).
What helps is changing the procedure you follow.

## 1. Consider the opposite (confirmation bias)
Before settling on a view, write down **two or three specific reasons it could be wrong**
and what evidence you'd expect to see if it were. Then go and look for that evidence.
In research this simple instruction ("consider the opposite") reduced biased assimilation
more than telling people to "be unbiased". Search phrasing matters too: search
"evidence against X" as well as "evidence for X".

## 2. Take the outside view (planning fallacy, overconfidence)
1. Identify a **reference class**: similar past projects, decisions or cases.
2. Find its **base rate**: how long did those take, how often did they succeed?
3. Start from that number, then adjust *modestly* for what's genuinely special about
   your case. The inside view ("our plan is solid") is what makes everyone late.
Example: "Kitchen renovations like ours typically overrun ~X%" — find a real figure
for your class; the number here is deliberately not given because it varies by source.

## 3. Respect base rates (base-rate neglect)
A vivid description pulls you away from how common something is. Ask "out of 100 cases
like this, how many would be X?" before weighing the specifics. Natural frequencies
("8 in 100") are easier to reason with than percentages.

## 4. Reset anchors
Any number you've seen — an asking price, a first estimate, a random figure — pulls
your estimate toward it. Counter-moves: make your own estimate **before** seeing theirs;
generate a high and low bound independently; ask what you'd estimate if the anchor had
been very different.

## 5. Run a premortem (group or solo)
"Imagine it's a year from now and this decision failed badly. Write the story of why."
Prospective hindsight surfaces risks that a "what could go wrong?" list misses, and it
gives dissenters permission to speak.

## 6. Write it down before you know (hindsight bias)
Record your prediction, confidence and reasoning *before* the outcome. Afterwards you
will otherwise remember having been more right than you were. A decision journal also
separates bad decisions from bad luck.

## 7. Sunk cost check
Ask: "If I were starting fresh today with no prior investment, would I choose this?"
Past cost is gone either way; only future costs and benefits count.

## 8. Decouple judgement from the room
In groups, collect estimates independently (silently, in writing) before discussion;
otherwise the first or loudest voice anchors everyone.

## Gotchas
- **Bias-labelling others** is the most common misuse; apply checks to yourself first.
- **Over-correcting:** the outside view is a starting point, not a veto on your evidence.
- **Not every heuristic is a bias** — fast rules of thumb are often right in familiar
  environments with good feedback.
- Some classic bias findings (e.g. parts of priming and ego-depletion research) failed to
  replicate; anchoring, overconfidence and the planning fallacy are among the more robust.

## Verify
- [ ] You wrote reasons you could be wrong and looked for disconfirming evidence.
- [ ] You know a base rate for the reference class and started from it.
- [ ] Your estimate was made before seeing others' numbers.
- [ ] A premortem was run for any significant decision.
- [ ] The prediction and confidence are recorded with a date.
