# Building a sound argument — claim, grounds, warrant, qualifier, rebuttal

**Goal:** produce an argument (for an essay, proposal, meeting or disagreement) that a
fair-minded sceptic would have to engage with rather than dismiss.

## 1. Sharpen the claim
Write the claim in one sentence, then make it **precise enough to be wrong**:
- Vague: "Remote work is better." Precise: "For our 12-person design team, two office days
  a week would cut commuting cost without lowering output."
- Name the type: fact (is), value (is good/bad), or policy (should). Policy claims need
  both a fact basis *and* a value premise — people often supply only one.

## 2. Gather grounds (evidence) before wording
List what you actually have: data, examples, expert testimony, shared principles.
Rate each: how direct, how reliable, how representative? Drop the weak ones — one bad
reason gives an opponent an easy target and lowers trust in the good ones.

## 3. State the warrant out loud
The **warrant** is the general rule connecting grounds to claim: "Output can be measured
by deliverables shipped", "Costs borne by staff matter to retention". Most failed
arguments have fine evidence and a warrant the audience does not share. If your
audience might reject it, give **backing** for the warrant too.

## 4. Qualify honestly
Use the narrowest true quantifier: *all / most / many / some*, *will / likely / may*.
"This will likely reduce costs for teams like ours" is harder to refute than "this
always works", and a refuted overclaim discredits the whole case.

## 5. Pre-empt the strongest objection (steelman it)
Write the best objection a smart opponent would raise — not the weakest. Then:
concede what is true in it, show why the claim survives (or narrow the claim), and
keep the answer shorter than the objection. Naming it first shows good faith and
takes the sting out.

## 6. Order it for the reader
A reliable skeleton for writing or speaking:
1. Claim (and why it matters to *them*).
2. Two or three strongest grounds, each with its warrant made clear.
3. The main objection and your answer.
4. Qualified restatement of the claim plus what would change your mind.

## Burden of proof
Whoever proposes changing the status quo, or asserts the existence of something,
normally carries the burden. Do not try to make the other side prove a negative;
do expect to support every premise they reasonably question.

## Gotchas
- **Fact-value mix-up:** "Sugar taxes reduce consumption, therefore we should have one"
  is missing the value premise (reduced consumption is worth the costs).
- **Evidence dump:** ten weak reasons persuade less than two strong ones.
- **Arguing to win, not to be right:** if you cannot state what evidence would change
  your mind, the argument is probably motivated reasoning (see cognitive-bias-mitigation).
- **Hiding the qualifier at the end:** put it in the claim sentence itself.
- Toulmin's terms vary by textbook (some say "data" for grounds, "reservation" for
  rebuttal); the structure matters more than the labels.

## Verify
- [ ] The claim is one sentence, precise enough to be shown false.
- [ ] Every reason has an explicit or obvious warrant the audience would accept.
- [ ] Policy claims have both a factual and a value premise.
- [ ] The quantifier/modal is as narrow as the evidence supports.
- [ ] The strongest counter-argument is stated fairly and answered.
- [ ] You can say what would make you drop the claim.
