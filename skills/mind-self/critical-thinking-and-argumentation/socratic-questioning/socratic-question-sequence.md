# A Socratic question sequence — testing a belief by asking, not telling

**Goal:** get to the bottom of a claim (yours or someone else's) by asking questions in
a deliberate order, so that weak spots surface on their own instead of being argued over.

## The six question types (a widely used taxonomy, after Paul and Elder / R. W. Paul)
Work roughly in this order; loop back when an answer opens something new.
1. **Clarification** — "What exactly do you mean by *fair* here?" "Can you give an example?"
   Most disagreements shrink by half once key words are pinned down.
2. **Assumptions** — "What are we taking for granted?" "Would this still hold if X weren't
   true?" "Why would someone hold the opposite assumption?"
3. **Reasons and evidence** — "How do you know?" "What would count as evidence against it?"
   "How confident are you, and why that much?"
4. **Viewpoints** — "How would someone who disagrees put it at their strongest?" "Who is
   affected and how would they see it?"
5. **Implications and consequences** — "If that's true, what else must be true?" "Where
   does this lead if applied consistently?" Inconsistency with other beliefs the person
   holds is the classic Socratic finding.
6. **Questions about the question** — "Is this the right question?" "Why does it matter?"
   "What would answering it let us decide?"

## Running it with another person
- **Purpose first:** say you're trying to understand, not to win. The method collapses
  into cross-examination if it feels like a trap.
- **One question at a time**, then listen fully. Summarise their answer back before the
  next question ("So you're saying… is that right?").
- **Follow their words**, not your script — the best next question usually reuses a word
  they just used.
- **Leave silence.** Thinking takes a few seconds; filling the gap answers for them.
- **Stop when the goal is met** — clarity reached, a crux found, or the person is tired.
  Name the crux: "So we disagree mainly about whether X causes Y."

## Running it on yourself
Write the belief at the top of a page and answer each of the six types in writing.
The writing matters: silent self-questioning drifts back to the comfortable answer.
Particularly useful questions: "What would I expect to see if I were wrong?" and "When
did I first come to believe this, and on what basis?"

## Gotchas
- **Leading questions** ("Don't you think that…?") are statements in disguise; people
  notice and dig in.
- **Gotcha-chaining** — stacking questions to trap someone in a contradiction — wins the
  exchange and loses the person. Socrates' own interlocutors were often annoyed, not
  persuaded.
- **Infinite regress:** "how do you know that?" can always be asked again. Stop at
  premises you both accept.
- **Wrong setting:** grief, crisis or high emotion call for listening, not probing.
- The six-type list is a teaching taxonomy, not something Socrates wrote; other lists
  exist and work as well.

## Verify
- [ ] Key terms were clarified before anything else.
- [ ] At least one hidden assumption was surfaced and examined.
- [ ] The strongest opposing viewpoint was stated.
- [ ] An implication was traced and checked for consistency.
- [ ] You can name the crux (the point the disagreement really turns on).
- [ ] The other person (or you) felt questioned, not cross-examined.
