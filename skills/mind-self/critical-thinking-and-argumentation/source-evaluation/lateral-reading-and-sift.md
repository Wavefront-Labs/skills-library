# Lateral reading and SIFT — check a source the way fact-checkers do

**Goal:** decide quickly whether a post, article, statistic or study deserves your
trust, without being fooled by a professional-looking page.

## The core insight: leave the page
Studies of professional fact-checkers (Wineburg and McGrew) found they rarely judged a
site by reading it carefully. They opened **new tabs** and read *about* the source
elsewhere — "lateral reading". A polished design, an "About us" page, a .org domain and
references are all cheap to fake; what others say about the source is not.

## SIFT (Mike Caulfield's four moves)
1. **Stop.** Notice your reaction. Strong emotion ("this is outrageous!") or a claim that
   perfectly confirms what you believe is the cue to check *before* sharing. Also ask:
   what do I actually need — a quick sanity check or a deep dive?
2. **Investigate the source.** In a new tab, search the outlet or author's name (add
   "funding", "bias" or "who owns"). A Wikipedia entry on the outlet is a good first
   stop. You want: who is behind it, what is their expertise, what is their interest?
3. **Find better coverage.** Search the *claim* itself, not the article. If it's real
   and important, reputable outlets or specialist sources will have it. Use the best
   source you find, which may not be the one you started with.
4. **Trace claims, quotes and media to the original.** Follow links back to the study,
   dataset, full speech or original photo. Check that the original actually says what
   the summary claims, in context. For images use reverse-image search (Google Lens,
   TinEye, Bing Visual Search) to find earlier uses.

## Evidence quality, roughly strongest first (for empirical claims)
Systematic reviews / meta-analyses › randomised controlled trials › large cohort or
observational studies › case reports › expert opinion › anecdote. Also:
- **Preprint** = not yet peer reviewed; useful, but treat as provisional.
- **Peer review** filters, it doesn't guarantee; one study is rarely settled science.
- **Press release vs paper:** headlines routinely overstate ("may be linked" → "causes").
- Check sample size, who was studied (mice? 20 students?), and absolute vs relative risk.

## Interests and conflicts
Who benefits if you believe this? Funding, advertising, political affiliation and
product sales don't make a claim false, but they raise the bar for evidence. Look for
disclosed conflicts at the end of papers.

## Gotchas
- **Checking the wrong thing:** a reliable outlet can still misreport one study; a
  partisan outlet can still quote it accurately. Judge the claim's chain, not just the brand.
- **Date blindness:** old stories recirculate as new; check the publish date and whether
  facts have moved on.
- **Screenshot of a "tweet" or headline** is not a source — find the live original.
- **Vertical reading trap:** spending ten minutes on the site's own About page.
- Tool names and features (e.g. reverse-image services) change; verify current options.

## Verify
- [ ] You paused and noted your emotional reaction before judging.
- [ ] You know who is behind the source from somewhere other than the source itself.
- [ ] You found at least one independent report of the claim (or noted there is none).
- [ ] You traced the claim to its original and confirmed it says what's claimed.
- [ ] For a study claim, you know the study type, sample and whether it's peer reviewed.
