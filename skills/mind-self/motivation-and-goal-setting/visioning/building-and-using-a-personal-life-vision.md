# Building and using a personal life vision

**Goal:** get a clear, felt picture of the life you're heading towards — clear
enough to say yes or no to opportunities today — without turning it into
wishful thinking.

## Why it helps (and its limit)
- **Future-self continuity**: Hershfield's research suggests people who feel more
  connected to their future selves make more future-friendly choices (saving,
  health). Vivid, concrete pictures of that self seem to help.
- **Best-possible-self writing**: studies (from King, 2001, onward) show small,
  short-term boosts in optimism and mood.
- **Limit**: pure positive fantasy can sap effort (see
  mind-self.motivation-and-goal-setting.mental-contrasting). A vision is a
  compass, not a substitute for planning around obstacles.

## 1. Best-possible-self writing (20 minutes, once or several days running)
Imagine it's 5 or 10 years from now and things have gone as well as they
realistically could. Write about that life in the present tense, in detail:
work, relationships, health, where you live, what a normal Tuesday looks like,
how you feel. Specific scenes beat abstractions ("I cycle to a studio I rent with
two others", not "I'm successful").

## 2. Future-self letter
Write a letter **from** your future self (5–10 years on) **to** you now. What do
they thank you for starting? What do they wish you'd stopped? What reassurance do
they offer about today's worries? Then optionally a reply. This tends to surface
priorities more honestly than a vision list.

## 3. Odyssey plans (three alternative futures)
From *Designing Your Life*: sketch three different 5-year paths.
1. **Plan A** — the life you're currently heading towards.
2. **Plan B** — what you'd do if Plan A suddenly vanished.
3. **Plan C** — what you'd do if money and others' opinions didn't matter.
For each: a timeline, a six-word title, questions it raises, and gut ratings for
resources, likeability, confidence and coherence. Three plans break the
illusion that there's one right answer and often reveal elements worth testing.

## 4. Distil the vision
From your writing, extract:
- **Themes / values**: 3–5 words that recur (freedom, craft, family, health,
  community).
- **Vision statement**: a short paragraph, present tense.
- **Non-negotiables**: what must be true (e.g. "home by 6 most days").
Keep it on one page. Optional: a vision board with images — fine as a reminder,
but don't let making it stand in for action.

## 5. Use it as a filter
When a decision comes up — a job offer, a commitment, a purchase, how to spend a
Saturday — ask:
- Does this move me towards or away from the vision?
- Does it honour or violate my non-negotiables?
- Would my future self thank me?
And translate the vision into this quarter's 1–3 goals (see
mind-self.motivation-and-goal-setting.goal-setting-frameworks).

## 6. Revisit
Reread monthly; rewrite yearly or after big life changes. Visions should evolve
as you learn what you actually enjoy — prototype pieces (a short course, a
conversation with someone living part of it) before betting big.

## Gotchas
- **Borrowed visions**: check whether the picture is yours or a parent's,
  partner's or social media's. Ask "who would I be impressing?"
- **Comparison and despair**: if the gap between vision and now feels crushing,
  shrink the horizon (one year), or focus on values you can live today.
- **Rigidity**: the vision should guide, not trap. Revise it freely.
- **Grief or major loss** can make future-picturing painful; it's okay to wait.

## Verify
- [ ] You've written a best-possible-self piece or a future-self letter with concrete scenes.
- [ ] You've sketched three odyssey plans.
- [ ] You have a one-page vision with 3–5 values and some non-negotiables.
- [ ] You've used the filter questions on at least one real decision.
- [ ] Current quarterly goals trace back to the vision.
