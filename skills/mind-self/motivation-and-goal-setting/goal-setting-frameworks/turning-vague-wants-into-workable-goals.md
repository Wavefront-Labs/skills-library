# Turning vague wants into workable goals

**Goal:** take "I want to get fit / write more / sort my finances" and turn it
into a small set of goals specific enough to act on this week and honest enough
that you'll keep them.

## Background
Goal-setting research (Locke & Latham) consistently finds that **specific,
challenging goals** outperform "do your best", provided you're committed, get
feedback, and the task isn't so complex that a narrow target distorts behaviour.

## 1. Make it SMART (the baseline)
| Letter | Question | Vague → workable |
|---|---|---|
| **S**pecific | What exactly? | "Get fit" → "Run 5 km without stopping" |
| **M**easurable | How will I know? | Time/distance tracked in an app |
| **A**chievable | Realistic from where I am? | Currently run 1 km; 12 weeks is plausible |
| **R**elevant | Does it matter to me, now? | Want energy to keep up with kids |
| **T**ime-bound | By when? | By 31 March |

*(Letters vary by source — "A" is sometimes "agreed", "R" sometimes "realistic".
The substance matters, not the acronym.)*

## 2. Pair outcome goals with process goals
- **Outcome goal**: the result ("run 5 km by March", "save £3,000"). Motivating,
  but partly outside your control and only checkable at the end.
- **Process goal**: the behaviour you control ("run Mon/Wed/Sat", "auto-transfer
  £250 on payday"). This is what you actually do each week.
- Optionally a **performance goal** between them ("run 3 km by end of January").
Track the process goal weekly; it's your leading indicator. If you hit the
process consistently and the outcome still isn't moving, change the process,
not your commitment.

## 3. Ladder the milestones
Work backwards from the deadline:
1. Final outcome and date.
2. Halfway milestone — what must be true by then?
3. Monthly or fortnightly milestones.
4. **This week's first action**, small and concrete ("download a couch-to-5k
   plan, schedule Monday's run in the calendar").
If you can't name this week's action, the goal is still too vague.

## 4. Triage: few and honest
Most people set too many goals and keep none.
1. Brain-dump every goal you're considering.
2. For each, ask: How much does this matter (1–5)? Do I have the time and energy
   for it this quarter (yes/no)? What would it displace?
3. Keep **one to three active goals** per period (quarter is a good unit). Move
   the rest to a "later" list — not deleted, just not now.
4. Check for conflicts: "train for a marathon" and "launch a side business" in
   the same quarter may compete for the same evenings.

## 5. Write it down and review
Write each active goal in one sentence with its process goal and next action.
Review weekly (15 minutes): did I do the process? What got in the way? What's
next week's action? Monthly: still relevant? adjust targets?

## Gotchas
- **Too easy or too hard**: aim for a stretch you judge has perhaps a 50–70%
  chance of success *(rule of thumb, not a research figure)*.
- **Goal tunnel vision**: narrow targets can push you to cut corners (injuring
  yourself chasing a run time, crash dieting for a weight target). Add a
  guardrail: "…without pain over 3/10" or "…while eating at least three meals".
- **Avoidance goals** ("stop being lazy") are harder to act on than approach goals
  ("walk 20 minutes after lunch").
- **Health and money goals**: aggressive weight-loss, training or debt targets
  are worth checking with a doctor or a qualified financial/debt adviser.
- Pair with mind-self.motivation-and-goal-setting.mental-contrasting to plan for
  obstacles.

## Verify
- [ ] Each active goal passes all five SMART questions.
- [ ] Each outcome goal has a process goal you control.
- [ ] You can name this week's first action for every goal.
- [ ] You have three or fewer active goals; the rest are on a "later" list.
- [ ] A weekly review is in your calendar.
