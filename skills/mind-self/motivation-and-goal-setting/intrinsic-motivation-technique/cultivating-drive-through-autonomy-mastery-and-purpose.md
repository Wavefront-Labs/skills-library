# Cultivating drive through autonomy, mastery and purpose

**Goal:** make more of your activity something you *want* to do, or at least
endorse, so that motivation doesn't depend on deadlines, rewards and guilt.

## Background
Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan) says people are most self-motivated
when three needs are met: **autonomy** (feeling you choose), **competence**
(feeling effective and growing) and **relatedness** (feeling connected to
others). Motivation runs on a spectrum from fully external ("or I'll be fired")
through "I value this" to "I enjoy this for itself". You can't make every task
enjoyable, but you can move many from "have to" towards "choose to". Pink's
*Drive* popularised a similar triad: autonomy, mastery, purpose.

## 1. Autonomy framing
- **Reframe "have to" as "choose to, because…"**: "I have to do the report" →
  "I'm choosing to do the report because I want the project funded." It sounds
  trivial; noticing the real reason often shifts the feel.
- **Find the choices inside the task**: order, method, time, tools, place,
  style. Even small choices increase ownership.
- **Ask for latitude** at work: "Can I decide how I hit this target?"
- **Cut "should" language** in self-talk; it signals pressure.

## 2. Mastery tracking
Competence grows when progress is visible.
- **Keep a progress log**: what you can do now that you couldn't a month ago.
  Amabile's research on "the progress principle" found that small wins on
  meaningful work were among the strongest boosts to motivation.
- **Set skill goals alongside output goals**: "learn keyboard shortcuts for my
  editor" alongside "finish the chapter".
- **Seek just-right challenge** (see
  mind-self.attention-and-focus-training.flow-state-cultivation).
- **Get informative feedback** — specific, about the work — rather than only
  praise or ratings.

## 3. Purpose and relatedness linking
- **Trace the line** from the task to someone who benefits: a customer, a
  colleague, your family, a cause. Write it down. Studies of fundraisers who met
  a scholarship beneficiary (Grant and colleagues) found they raised more.
- **Connect with people**: work alongside others, share progress, help a
  colleague. Relatedness is easy to neglect when working alone.
- **Values check**: which of your values does this task serve? If none, that's
  worth knowing.

## 4. Job crafting your own work
Wrzesniewski and Dutton describe three levers:
- **Task crafting**: change the scope or type of tasks — take on more of what
  energises you, trade or streamline what drains you.
- **Relational crafting**: change who you work with and how often.
- **Cognitive crafting**: change how you think about the job's purpose (a
  hospital cleaner seeing their role as part of patient care).
Exercise: list your tasks, mark each energising/neutral/draining, and pick one
change per lever this month.

## 5. Protect interest from over-rewarding
The **overjustification effect**: offering an expected, tangible reward for
something already enjoyed can reduce interest once the reward stops (classic
studies with children's drawing). Debate continues on how strong and general this
is, but sensible practice:
- Don't pay yourself or your kids for activities that are already fun.
- Prefer unexpected or informational rewards ("that was clever"), not
  controlling ones ("you'll get X if…").
- Use external rewards for genuinely boring tasks — that's where they help.

## Gotchas
- **Not everything can be intrinsic.** Some tasks simply need doing; aim for
  "I endorse this" rather than "I love this".
- **Passion myths**: interest often follows competence, not the reverse. Give
  new things time.
- **Workplace limits**: autonomy may be genuinely restricted. Focus on the levers
  you control and raise the rest directly.
- **Persistent loss of interest** in almost everything, for weeks, can be a sign
  of depression or burnout — worth discussing with a GP.

## Verify
- [ ] You've rewritten at least one "have to" as "choose to, because…".
- [ ] You keep a progress log and look at it weekly.
- [ ] Each major task has a written link to someone who benefits.
- [ ] You've made one job-crafting change.
- [ ] Enjoyable activities aren't tied to controlling rewards.
