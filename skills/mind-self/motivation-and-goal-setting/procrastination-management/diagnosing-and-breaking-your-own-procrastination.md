# Diagnosing and breaking your own procrastination

**Goal:** stop treating procrastination as laziness. Work out *why* this task is
being avoided, then apply the fix that matches the cause.

## The key idea
Research (Pychyl, Sirois and others) frames procrastination mainly as **short-term
mood repair**: the task brings up an unpleasant feeling — boredom, anxiety,
confusion, self-doubt — and avoiding it gives instant relief. Steel's temporal
motivation theory adds that we delay more when the reward is distant, the task
feels unlikely to go well, and we're easily distracted. So the fix is usually
emotional and structural, not more self-criticism.

## 1. Aversion diagnosis (2 minutes)
Pick the task you're avoiding and rate how strongly each applies (0–3):
| Cause | Sounds like | Fix to try first |
|---|---|---|
| **Vague** | "Sort out the house stuff" | Define the next physical action |
| **Too big** | "Write the thesis" | Shrink the first step (section 2) |
| **Boring** | "Expenses again" | Temptation bundling, timer, batching |
| **Fear of judgement / perfectionism** | "It has to be brilliant" | Deliberate "bad first draft", lower the bar for v1 |
| **Confusion / skill gap** | "I don't know how" | Ask, find an example, spend 15 min learning |
| **Distant reward** | Deadline is weeks away | Create a near-term deadline or check-in |
| **Resentment** | "Why is this my job?" | Address the fairness issue separately; or accept and do it |
| **Low energy** | Everything feels heavy | Rest, food, sleep; schedule for best hours |
The top one or two scores tell you where to start.

## 2. Shrink the first step
Rewrite the task until the first step is so small it seems silly:
"Write report" → "Open the document and write the headings" → "Open the
document". The first step should take under 5 minutes and need no decisions.

## 3. Two-minute starts
Commit to just **two minutes** (or five) on the task, with explicit permission to
stop after. Starting is usually the hardest part; most of the time you'll carry
on. If you genuinely stop, you've still broken the avoidance and the next start is
easier.

## 4. Temptation-proof scheduling
- **Put the task in the calendar** at a specific time, ideally your best hours.
- **Decide the "where" too**: "at 9:00 at the kitchen table with the laptop, phone
  in the bedroom".
- **Remove the escape routes** in advance: close tabs, block sites (see
  mind-self.attention-and-focus-training.distraction-management).
- **Temptation bundling**: allow a treat only while doing the dreaded task
  (a favourite podcast only during cleaning). Some evidence supports this
  (Milkman and colleagues, gym study).
- **Artificial deadlines** with someone else in the loop (see accountability
  systems).

## 5. Your unstick protocol
Write this on a card for when you're stuck in avoidance:
1. Notice: "I'm avoiding X." No self-blame.
2. Name the feeling: bored? anxious? confused?
3. Ask: what's the smallest next action?
4. Set a 5-minute timer and do only that.
5. Still stuck? Change one thing: location, time, tool, or ask someone.
6. Afterwards: one line on what helped.

## 6. Self-forgiveness
A study of students (Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett, 2010) found those who forgave
themselves for procrastinating on the first exam procrastinated less on the next.
Beating yourself up adds to the bad feeling you're trying to escape.

## Gotchas
- **Productive procrastination** — cleaning, organising, doing easier tasks — is
  still avoidance if it displaces the key task.
- **Planning as avoidance**: elaborate systems and new apps can become the
  procrastination.
- **Chronic, lifelong, across everything**: if procrastination has seriously hurt
  your work, finances or relationships for years, despite effort, it may be
  worth seeing a doctor about ADHD, depression or anxiety, which can drive it.

## Verify
- [ ] You've rated the causes for one avoided task and picked a matching fix.
- [ ] The first step is under 5 minutes with no decisions.
- [ ] The task has a calendar slot with time and place.
- [ ] Your unstick protocol is written down and accessible.
- [ ] You respond to slips with self-forgiveness and a next step.
