# Daily smell training with an aroma kit

**Goal:** sharpen your sense of smell — to name and remember more odours, or to support
recovery after smell loss — using a short, structured daily routine. For trade-level
material evaluation, see
`handcrafts.perfumery.perfumery-materials.smelling-and-evaluating-raw-materials`.

## First: when to see a doctor
Sudden or one-sided smell loss, loss with nosebleeds, blocked nose on one side, headaches,
or other neurological symptoms, or loss lasting beyond a few weeks after a cold — see a
GP or ENT specialist before or alongside training. Smell training is a recognised,
low-risk support for post-viral loss, but it doesn't rule out other causes.

## Build the kit
The classic research kit used four odours from distinct families:
- **Rose** (floral), **lemon** (fruity), **clove** (spicy), **eucalyptus** (resinous).
Later studies rotated in new sets every few months, with some evidence that changing
odours helps. *(Draft: summary, not re-checked.)* Practical version:
- Small dark-glass jars with lids, each with a cotton pad and **a few drops** of essential
  oil, or real materials (lemon peel, cloves, coffee beans, a rose).
- Label each jar. Replace pads every few weeks as they fade.
- Keep oils away from children and pets, and off skin; some oils irritate.

For general acuity (not recovery), build a wider set of 10–20 with everyday odours:
vanilla, coffee, cinnamon, mint, smoke, cut grass (cis-3-hexenol or fresh cuttings),
banana, garlic, leather, soap.

## The routine (twice a day)
1. Sit calmly. Hold a jar a few centimetres below the nose.
2. Take **gentle, short sniffs** ("bunny sniffs") for about **15–20 seconds**, not one huge
   inhale — big inhales drag the odour past without much contact.
3. While sniffing, **picture the source** vividly and recall memories of it: real lemons,
   cutting one, the taste. Attention and imagery are part of the training.
4. Rest ~10 seconds, rinse your attention (smell your own sleeve or plain air), next jar.
5. Total a few minutes, **morning and evening, every day for at least 12 weeks.**
Recovery-focused training often continues for several months; improvement can be slow
and uneven.

## Naming and memory drills (general acuity)
- **Blind identification:** a helper hands you unlabelled jars; name each, then check.
  Score out of 10 weekly.
- **Pairs:** distinguish two close odours (lemon vs lime, orange vs mandarin, spearmint vs
  peppermint).
- **Families:** sort jars into floral, citrus, woody, spicy, green, gourmand.
- **Describe before naming:** sweet/sharp, warm/cool, heavy/light — words bridge the
  famous "tip of the nose" gap where you know it but can't name it.
- **Smell walks:** pause outdoors and name five odours.

## Track progress
Daily: score each jar 0–10 for strength and note any distortion. Weekly: blind
identification score. Write down qualitative changes ("clove came back as clove,
not burnt").

## Gotchas
- **Olfactory fatigue:** a scent fades after continuous exposure; short sniffs and gaps
  avoid it.
- **Parosmia** (familiar things smelling wrong — rotten, burnt, chemical) is common during
  post-viral recovery and often a sign of nerve regeneration. It's distressing; keep
  training with odours you tolerate and seek specialist support if it affects eating.
- **Safety:** if your sense of smell is reduced, fit smoke and gas alarms, use date labels
  on food, and don't rely on smell for gas leaks or spoilage.
- Don't sniff jars right after strong foods, perfume or smoking.

## Verify
- [ ] Any red-flag symptoms checked with a doctor.
- [ ] Kit of 4+ distinct odours, refreshed regularly.
- [ ] Twice-daily sessions with gentle sniffs and active imagery, logged.
- [ ] Weekly blind identification score is recorded and trending.
