# Basic-taste threshold and triangle-test training

**Goal:** sharpen taste as a general skill — detect faint basic tastes, tell close
samples apart blind, and describe what you taste in words. Trade-specific panels and
exams (wine, coffee cupping) build on this; see
`hospitality-culinary.sensory-evaluation.tasting-panel-methods.running-triangle-tests-and-trained-panels`.

## Set-up
- Bottled or filtered still water (tap chlorine interferes), identical small cups, a
  measuring scale (0.1 g if possible), masking tape for codes, a spittoon.
- Taste mid-morning or mid-afternoon: not just after meals, coffee, toothpaste or smoking.
- No perfume; neutral-smelling room.

## 1. Dilution series (finding thresholds)
Make a stock solution and dilute by halves to get 6–8 steps for each taste:
| Taste | Substance | Rough top concentration |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet | table sugar (sucrose) | ~20 g per litre |
| Salty | table salt | ~4 g per litre |
| Sour | citric acid (food grade) | ~1 g per litre |
| Umami | MSG | ~2 g per litre |
| Bitter | tonic water (quinine) diluted in water | 50% tonic |
*(Draft: these are practical approximations chosen to start clearly tasteable; they are
not the ISO 3972 reference values.)* For bitter, use tonic water rather than caffeine or
quinine powder — pure caffeine powder is easy to overdose on.

**Procedure:** taste from weakest to strongest with a water cup between each. Sip, hold
5 seconds, move around the mouth, spit. Note the step where you first notice *something*
different from water (**detection threshold**) and where you can *name* the taste
(**recognition threshold**). Repeat on another day — thresholds wobble with time of day,
hunger and illness. Recognition typically sits a step or two above detection.

Expect sour and bitter to be confused at first, and umami to be hard to name until you've
tasted it alongside the others.

## 2. Triangle tests (discrimination)
A helper prepares three coded cups: two identical, one different (e.g. 5 g vs 6 g sugar
per litre). You find the odd one out.
- Randomise positions and use three-digit codes so neither of you can guess.
- Chance of guessing right is 1 in 3, so one correct answer means little. Run sets of 10+;
  scoring well above a third (e.g. 7/10) suggests you really discriminate. *(Use a
  binomial table for proper significance.)*
- Narrow the difference as you improve. Then move to real products: two similar apple
  juices, two brands of salted crackers, coffee brewed at different strengths.

## 3. Comparative tasting discipline
- **Same conditions:** same temperature, volume and cup for each sample.
- **Order effects:** first samples and those after a strong one are judged differently;
  rinse with water (and plain cracker for fatty foods) and pause ~30 s between.
- **Taste blind** where you can — labels and prices change what you report.
- **Limit numbers:** fatigue sets in after ~6–8 samples of strong items.
- Pinch your nose for one sip: what's left is mostly basic taste; what returns when you
  release is smell (retronasal aroma). It's the clearest way to feel the difference.

## 4. Vocabulary
Keep a notebook. For each tasting write: basic tastes and their intensity (0–5),
mouthfeel (astringent, oily, carbonated, hot), aromas, aftertaste and length. Borrow
words from flavour wheels for coffee, wine or beer. Writing it down is what turns
impressions into a calibrated palate.

## Gotchas
- Colds, some medicines and COVID can blunt taste and smell temporarily; persistent
  changes are worth seeing a doctor about.
- Don't swallow repeated samples of salt or sour solutions; spit.
- People with dietary restrictions (e.g. low-salt diets, diabetes) should adapt or spit
  everything.

- [ ] Samples tasted under matched, blind, rinsed conditions.
