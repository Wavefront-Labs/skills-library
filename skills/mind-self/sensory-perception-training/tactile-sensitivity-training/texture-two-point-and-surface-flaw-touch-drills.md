# Texture, two-point and surface-flaw touch drills

**Goal:** sharpen touch as a general skill: tell finer textures apart, sense smaller
details and pressure differences, and find surface defects by hand. Skills like Braille
reading or medical sensory re-education after nerve injury have specialist programmes —
see a therapist for those.

## Before training
- **Numbness, tingling, burning, or loss of feeling** — especially in both feet or hands,
  or after an injury — can be a sign of neuropathy (e.g. diabetes, vitamin B12 deficiency)
  or nerve compression. See a doctor; don't just train around it.
- Warm hands work better: cold fingers lose sensitivity markedly. Wash hands, no lotion.

## How to touch: active exploration
Different movements pick up different properties (the "exploratory procedures" in
perception research):
| Property | Movement |
|---|---|
| Texture | lateral sliding, light pressure |
| Hardness | pressing |
| Temperature | static contact, whole fingertip |
| Shape | enclosing, following contours |
| Weight | unsupported holding |
Use the right one for the question, and **slow down** — speed and heavy pressure blur
fine texture. Fingertips (especially the index and middle finger pads) are the most
sensitive skin.

## 1. Texture grading
- **Sandpaper ladder:** cut squares of 60, 80, 120, 180, 240, 320, 400, 600 grit (and
  finer if you can get it). Label the back. Blindfolded (or eyes closed), arrange them
  coarse to fine. Score misplacements.
- Progress by removing the coarse end and working with adjacent fine grits (e.g. 320 vs 400).
- Repeat with **fabrics** (cotton weights, linens, wool blends) and **papers** (printer
  paper, writing papers of different weights, card).

## 2. Blind material identification
A helper hands you objects: wood types, metals, plastics, stone, glass, leather. Identify
by texture, hardness, weight and temperature. Metals feel colder than wood at the same room
temperature because they conduct heat away from your skin faster — a useful identification
cue, and a demonstration that touch senses heat flow, not just temperature.

## 3. Two-point discrimination
Unfold a paperclip into a U and set the tips a known distance apart (use a ruler). A
helper randomly touches you with **one or two points**, light and simultaneous; you say
"one" or "two" with eyes closed.
- Typical thresholds: fingertips roughly 2–5 mm; palm about 10 mm; forearm and back
  several centimetres. *(Approximate textbook ranges.)*
- Narrow the gap until you score near chance; record the smallest reliable gap. Expect
  modest, practice-specific improvement.
- Don't press hard enough to hurt or mark the skin.

## 4. Fine pressure
- Use hairs or fishing lines of different diameters (a home stand-in for von Frey
  filaments): press each perpendicular until it just bends, and report whether you felt it.
- Detect a single hair or grain of salt under a sheet of paper by sliding a fingertip over it.

## 5. Surface-flaw detection
- **Feel through a thin cloth or cotton glove** over a smooth surface (a painted panel, a
  planed board). It smooths out skin friction and reveals dents, high spots and ridges
  your bare hand misses. Sweep in long overlapping strokes, flat-handed, in two directions.
- **Fingernail drag:** a scratch that catches a fingernail is generally deeper than a
  surface scuff — a common rough test before polishing or refinishing.
- Mark found flaws with a pencil or tape, then check by eye under raking light.

## Safety
Check surfaces for sharp edges, splinters, burrs and swarf by eye first; never feel
along a freshly cut metal edge. Don't touch hot or chemically treated surfaces. People
with reduced sensation should take extra care with heat.
