# Pitch, timbre and localisation ear drills

**Goal:** sharpen hearing as a general faculty — finer pitch differences, better
identification of sound colour, more accurate sense of where sounds come from — with
short, measurable drills. Formal musical ear training (intervals, chords, dictation)
belongs with music.

## Safety first
- Train at **comfortable, moderate volume** — conversation level. Louder doesn't
  sharpen discrimination and risks damage.
- Limit headphone sessions to 15–20 minutes; take breaks.
- If you notice ringing (tinnitus), muffled hearing, or one ear worse than the other,
  get a hearing test from an audiologist or GP before training.

## Tools
- A tone generator: Audacity (*Generate › Tone*, sine wave, set frequency and 1 s
  duration) or any reputable online/phone tone app.
- Closed-back headphones or decent speakers in a quiet room.
- A helper, or software that plays randomised trials and records answers.

## 1. Pitch discrimination (adaptive staircase)
Pitch differences are measured in **cents** (100 cents = one semitone).
1. Use a reference around 500–1000 Hz. Play reference then comparison; you answer
   "higher or lower?" (a forced choice; the helper randomises which).
2. Start with a large difference: 50 cents.
3. **Two-down, one-up:** after two correct answers in a row, halve the difference; after
   any wrong answer, increase it one step. This settles on the difference you get right
   about 71% of the time — your current threshold.
4. Run ~30–40 trials, then log the average of the last few turning points.
Untrained adults often land somewhere around 10–30 cents; trained listeners can reach
a few cents. Practice helps quickly at first, then slowly. *(Draft: typical ranges from
the literature, not re-checked.)* Vary the reference frequency so you don't simply
memorise one tone.

## 2. Timbre and frequency-band identification
- **Instrument/voice ID:** a helper plays short clips of similar sources (oboe vs clarinet,
  two singers, two cars starting); name them blind.
- **EQ band training:** play pink noise or music; a helper (or a training app) boosts one
  octave band by +6 to +12 dB (e.g. 125, 250, 500 Hz, 1, 2, 4, 8 kHz). Identify which.
  Start with 3 bands, widely spaced, and large boosts; add bands and reduce the boost
  as you improve.
- **Describe:** link bands to words — boomy (~100–250 Hz), boxy (~500 Hz), nasal
  (~1 kHz), harsh (~2–4 kHz), sibilant (~6–8 kHz). *(Approximate, varies by source.)*

## 3. Sound localisation
- Sit blindfolded in a quiet room. A helper makes a soft click (or taps a glass) at
  different positions around you; you point. They note the error.
- Include positions directly in front and behind — **front-back confusion** is common
  because both ears receive nearly the same signal; small head turns resolve it, so
  practise both with your head still and with turning allowed.
- Vary height and distance. Progress: quieter sounds, a background noise source.

## 4. Environmental listening
Five minutes, eyes closed, anywhere: list every sound, its direction and distance, and
its source. Repeat in the same place on different days and compare lists. Then try
naming similar sounds: different birds, car engines, footsteps on different surfaces.

## Logging
Per session: date, drill, threshold or score, conditions (headphones, room, fatigue).
Improvement is often specific to what you trained — vary the material to generalise.

## Gotchas
- **Fatigue** shows up fast in pitch work; stop when scores drop.
- **Cheating cues:** helpers' timing, clicks in the audio when switching tones, or louder
  comparison tones. Match loudness and use software where possible.
- Absolute pitch is a different, largely early-developed ability; don't measure progress
  against it.

## Verify
- [ ] Volume moderate; any hearing concern checked by a professional.
- [ ] Pitch threshold measured with a staircase and logged in cents.
- [ ] EQ-band or timbre ID scored blind.
- [ ] Localisation errors recorded with front-back trials included.
