# Colour, detail and rapid-recognition drills

**Goal:** see more, faster and more accurately — finer colour and shade differences,
small details and changes, quick recognition — and observe a scene systematically.
These drills train attention and judgement; they don't change the optics of your eyes.

## Before training
- **Eye test:** if you haven't had one in two years, get one. Uncorrected focus problems
  limit every drill here.
- **Colour vision screen:** a quick Ishihara-style plate test (optometrists do it; online
  versions are rough because screens vary). Around 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a
  red-green colour vision deficiency; training won't remove it, but you can still sharpen
  lightness and saturation discrimination. *(Commonly cited figures for people of
  northern European descent; vary by population.)*
- New or changing vision symptoms — flashes, floaters, blurring, field loss — see an
  optometrist or doctor promptly.

## 1. Colour and shade discrimination
- **Lighting matters most:** use daylight-balanced light (around 5000–6500 K, high CRI)
  or indirect north daylight; avoid warm bulbs and mixed sources. Neutral grey
  surroundings help.
- **Hue ordering:** cut paint chips or print swatches in a fine gradient (e.g. 15–20 steps
  from blue to green), shuffle, and arrange in order. Score the number of misplacements.
  This is the idea behind the Farnsworth-Munsell hue test.
- **Shade matching:** a helper picks one chip; find its match among close neighbours.
- **Naming:** describe colours by hue, lightness and saturation ("dark, greyish blue-green")
  rather than single words.
- On screens: calibrate brightness, disable night-light filters, and remember displays
  differ — scores on one screen don't transfer exactly.

## 2. Detail spotting and visual search
- **Spot-the-difference** pairs: time yourself; then hunt systematically — scan in a grid
  or by quadrants rather than wandering.
- **Change detection:** a helper moves or removes one small object in a room while you
  look away; spot what changed.
- **Visual search:** find a target letter or shape in a dense field; progress to targets
  that differ only subtly from distractors.

## 3. Rapid recognition
- **Flash cards:** a helper (or slideshow) shows an image for ~0.5–1 second; you name or
  describe it. Shorten exposure as you improve.
- **Digit or word flashes:** recall a string of 4–7 digits shown briefly.
- **Silhouettes:** birds, aircraft, cars, leaves — recognise category from outline.
Practice is domain-specific: recognising birds well doesn't much speed up car recognition.

## 4. Kim's game and structured observation
- **Kim's game:** 15–20 objects on a tray, one minute to look, cover, list from memory.
  Variant: remove one item, identify it.
- **Scene observation:** in a café or street, spend two minutes, then write: people
  (number, clothing, actions), objects, colours, exits, text. Compare with a photo.
- **Observational drawing-style looking:** draw an object without looking at the page
  (blind contour). It forces slow, attentive looking at edges and proportions.

## Logging
Track per drill: score, time, lighting, fatigue. Gains are fastest in the first weeks.

## Gotchas
- **Eye strain:** use the 20-20-20 habit on screens (every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away
  for 20 seconds) and stop drills when eyes feel tired.
- **Expectation bias:** you see what you expect; describe before you interpret.
- **Afterimages and adaptation:** staring at one colour shifts perception of the next;
  rest on neutral grey between colour trials.

## Verify
- [ ] Recent eye test and a colour-vision screen.
- [ ] Colour drills done under daylight-balanced, consistent light.
- [ ] Hue-ordering and detail scores logged over time.
- [ ] Scene observations checked against a photo or reality.
