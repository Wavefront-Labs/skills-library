# Tense-and-release relaxation: from full protocol to cue-controlled

**Goal:** learn to recognise and release muscle tension on purpose — first in a
20-minute guided session, eventually in a few seconds with a cue word.

Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) was developed by Edmund Jacobson in the 1920s
and 30s; the shortened versions most people use come from Bernstein & Borkovec.
It has reasonable evidence for reducing stress and anxiety and for improving
sleep, and is a component of "applied relaxation" for anxiety. *(Generally modest
effects; benefits grow with regular practice over weeks.)*

## How each muscle group works
1. **Focus** on the group.
2. **Tense** it firmly — about 50–70% of maximum, not to the point of pain or
   cramp — for **5–7 seconds**. Notice what tension feels like.
3. **Release** suddenly, all at once, and let the muscle go loose for **15–30
   seconds**. Notice the contrast: warmth, heaviness, softness.
4. Breathe in as you tense, out as you release.
The contrast — learning what "tense" and "relaxed" feel like — is the point.

## 1. Full protocol (about 16 groups, 15–25 minutes)
Lie down or sit in a supported chair, somewhere quiet. Go in order:
1. Dominant hand and forearm (make a fist)
2. Dominant upper arm (press elbow down / bend arm)
3. Other hand and forearm
4. Other upper arm
5. Forehead (raise eyebrows)
6. Upper cheeks and nose (squint, wrinkle nose)
7. Lower cheeks and jaw (clench teeth gently, pull corners of mouth back)
8. Neck and throat (chin towards chest without touching it)
9. Chest, shoulders and upper back (pull shoulder blades together)
10. Abdomen (tighten the stomach)
11. Dominant thigh
12. Dominant calf (pull toes towards you)
13. Dominant foot (curl toes gently)
14. 15. 16. The same three on the other leg
Finish with a minute of noticing the whole body, then get up slowly.

Practise once or twice a day for about two weeks, or follow a recorded script
(many free audio versions exist).

## 2. Shorter versions (once the full one is familiar)
- **7 groups (~10 min)**: both arms together; face; neck; chest/shoulders/back/
  abdomen; both legs and feet.
- **4 groups (~5 min)**: arms; face and neck; torso; legs.

## 3. Release-only (no tensing)
Scan each area and simply release, recalling the feeling of letting go. Useful
when tensing isn't practical (in public) or is uncomfortable (injury).

## 4. Cue-controlled relaxation
1. During practice, pair each exhale of the release phase with a silent cue word
   — "relax", "calm", "let go".
2. After a couple of weeks of pairing, the cue word alone starts to bring a
   wave of relaxation.
3. Use it in daily life: take a breath, say the cue on the exhale, let the
   shoulders and jaw drop. Takes seconds.

## 5. Tension spotting in daily life
Several times a day (tie it to a cue — phone unlock, sitting down), scan the
usual suspects: jaw, shoulders, hands, forehead, belly. Release on the exhale
with your cue word.

## Gotchas
- **Don't over-tense.** Strain and cramping (especially in calves and feet)
  defeat the purpose. Gentle is enough.
- **Injuries, pain conditions, recent surgery, pregnancy, or high blood
  pressure**: skip or reduce tensing in affected areas, use release-only, and check
  with a doctor or physiotherapist if unsure.
- **Falling asleep** is fine at bedtime but not when you're learning; practise
  sitting during the day too.
- **Relaxation-induced anxiety**: a minority of people feel more anxious as they
  relax (a sense of losing control). Keep eyes open, shorten sessions, focus on
  hands and feet.
- **When it's not enough**: if stress, anxiety or insomnia persist for weeks and
  affect daily life, talk to a GP — CBT for insomnia and therapy for anxiety are
  effective, and PMR works well alongside them.

## Verify
- [ ] You've completed the full protocol at least five times.
- [ ] You can feel the difference between tension and release in each group.
- [ ] You've moved to a shorter version and tried release-only.
- [ ] You have a cue word that brings noticeable relaxation in seconds.
- [ ] You adapt or skip tensing for any injured or painful area.
