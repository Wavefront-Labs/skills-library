# Self-guided exercises for moving through loss

**Goal:** offer practical, gentle things you can do with grief — after a death,
and also after other losses (a pet, a relationship, a home, health) — to
express it, stay connected to what was lost, and gradually make room for life
going on.

Grief isn't a problem to fix or a set of stages to complete in order. These
exercises are optional supports; use what helps and leave what doesn't. This is
general information, not a replacement for bereavement counselling or therapy.

## A helpful frame: oscillation
The **dual process model** (Stroebe & Schut) describes healthy grieving as moving
back and forth between:
- **Loss-oriented** activity: crying, remembering, looking at photos, missing the
  person.
- **Restoration-oriented** activity: handling new tasks, rest, distraction, new
  routines and relationships.
Both are needed. Taking breaks from grief isn't betrayal, and leaning into it
isn't wallowing. If you're only ever doing one, gently try a little of the other.

## 1. Unsent letters
Write to the person (or the thing) you've lost:
- What you miss, what you're grateful for, what you wish you'd said, what's
  unresolved — including anger or regret.
- Update them on what's happened since.
- Optional: write a reply from them, as you imagine they'd answer.
Keep it, burn it, bury it, read it at the grave — whatever feels right. Many
people return to this at anniversaries.

## 2. Memory work
- **Memory box**: gather photos, letters, a scent, an object, ticket stubs,
  recordings. Choose a place for it so you can open it when you choose rather
  than being ambushed by items around the house.
- **Memory book or file**: ask friends and family to write down stories. Others
  often hold memories you didn't know about.
- **Write specific memories** — one a day for a week: a scene, a phrase they used,
  a meal. Specific memories tend to feel more alive than general ones.
- **Sorting belongings**: there's no deadline. Do it in small sessions, with
  someone if possible, and keep a few items with real meaning.

## 3. Continuing bonds
Research has shifted away from the idea that you must "let go". Many people keep
a changed, ongoing connection — the **continuing bonds** idea:
- Talking to them, at a grave or anywhere.
- Carrying on something they valued: cooking their recipe, supporting their
  cause, tending their garden.
- Asking "what would they say about this?" in decisions.
- Wearing or keeping something of theirs.
Bonds tend to be comforting when they help you engage with life, less so if they
keep you stuck in disbelief that the loss happened.

## 4. Anniversaries and hard days
Birthdays, the date of death, holidays and "firsts" are often hardest. Plan
rather than letting them ambush you:
1. Mark them in your calendar in advance.
2. Decide on a deliberate act: a visit, a meal, lighting a candle, a walk they
   loved, a donation, gathering with others who knew them.
3. Plan for support: tell someone it's coming, arrange company or time alone as
   you prefer.
4. Lower expectations for that day and the days around it — the run-up is often
   worse than the day itself.

## 5. Look after the basics
Grief affects sleep, appetite, concentration and the immune system. Eat
regularly, move a bit, keep some routine, limit alcohol, and accept practical
help when it's offered.

## When to seek professional support
Grief can be intense for a long time and still be normal. Consider talking to a
GP, bereavement service or grief counsellor if:
- intense yearning or preoccupation with the person stays severe and disabling
  long after the loss — ICD-11 recognises **prolonged grief disorder** after at
  least 6 months, DSM-5-TR after 12 months for adults;
- you can't function at work or home, or avoid all reminders entirely;
- you feel life has no meaning, or have thoughts of wanting to die or join the
  person — contact a crisis line or emergency services now;
- you're using alcohol or drugs to cope;
- the death was traumatic (sudden, violent, suicide), which raises the risk of
  complications.
Effective specialised treatments exist (e.g. prolonged grief therapy).

## Verify
- [ ] You've tried at least one expressive exercise (letter or memory writing).
- [ ] You notice both loss- and restoration-oriented time in your weeks.
- [ ] Upcoming hard dates are in the calendar with a plan and support.
- [ ] You're keeping basic self-care going.
- [ ] You know the signs that grief needs professional help and where to get it.
