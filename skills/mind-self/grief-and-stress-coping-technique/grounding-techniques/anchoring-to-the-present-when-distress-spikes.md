# Anchoring to the present when distress spikes

**Goal:** when your mind is racing, flooded with worry or memory, or you feel
detached and unreal, use the senses and the surroundings to bring attention back
to the present moment, where you can think and act.

Grounding doesn't solve the cause of distress; it creates enough steadiness to
choose what to do next. It's widely taught in trauma-informed and skills-based
therapies. *(Evidence is mostly practitioner consensus and component studies
rather than trials of grounding alone.)*

## 1. 5-4-3-2-1 senses
Slowly, out loud or silently, name:
- **5** things you can **see** (be specific: "a blue mug with a chip in the rim")
- **4** things you can **feel/touch** (feet on the floor, fabric on your legs)
- **3** things you can **hear**
- **2** things you can **smell** (or two smells you like, if none)
- **1** thing you can **taste** (or one good thing about yourself)
Detail matters more than speed. If you lose track, start again — that's fine.

## 2. Orienting to the room
Turn your head slowly and let your eyes land on things around you. Name where you
are, the date and time, and that you're safe right now if that's true: "I'm in my
kitchen. It's Tuesday afternoon, October. I'm safe." Let your eyes rest on
something pleasant or neutral for a few breaths. Especially helpful when
memories make the past feel present.

## 3. Physical grounding
- Press feet firmly into the floor; notice the ground holding you.
- Push palms together or against a wall for 5–10 seconds, then release.
- Hold something cold (an ice cube, a cold can) or run water over your hands.
- Stretch, stamp, walk slowly noticing each step.
- Clap or rub hands and notice the sensation.

## 4. Object anchoring
Carry a small **grounding object**: a smooth stone, a textured keyring, a ring.
When distressed, hold it and describe it in detail — weight, temperature,
texture, edges, colour. Over time it becomes a quick cue for steadiness.

## 5. Mental grounding
When the senses aren't enough, engage the thinking mind:
- Name categories: as many dog breeds, cities, or footballers as you can.
- Count backwards from 100 in sevens.
- Describe a routine task in detail (making a cup of tea, step by step).
- Read signs or text around you, letter by letter.

## 6. Build your personal grounding script
Write a short, numbered sequence that works for you and keep it on your phone or
a card. Example:
1. "I'm having a wave of distress. It will pass."
2. Feet on the floor, press down. Three slow breaths with long exhales.
3. Orient: where am I, what's the date, am I safe right now?
4. 5-4-3-2-1.
5. Hold my stone and describe it.
6. Rate distress 0–10. If still above 7, call or message [support person].
Rehearse it when calm, a few times, so it's familiar under stress.

## Gotchas
- **Fast is less effective.** Racing through the list keeps the mind racing. Go
  slow, with detail.
- **Some techniques backfire for some people** — focusing on the breath or body
  can increase distress. Prefer external focus (sight, sound, objects).
- **Avoidance trap**: grounding is for getting steady, not for permanently
  avoiding all difficult feelings or memories.
- **Ask permission when helping others**, explain what you're doing, and don't
  touch them without consent.
- **Professional support**: frequent flashbacks, dissociation (feeling unreal or
  detached, losing time), or panic attacks are reasons to see a GP or a
  trauma-informed therapist — they're treatable. In an emergency or if you feel
  unsafe, contact emergency services or a crisis line.

## Verify
- [ ] You can do 5-4-3-2-1 slowly with specific detail.
- [ ] You've tried orienting, physical and mental grounding and know which suits you.
- [ ] You have a grounding object (optional) and a written script.
- [ ] You've rehearsed the script while calm.
- [ ] You know when to seek professional help instead of grounding alone.
