# Building capacity before and after adversity

**Goal:** be better able to absorb setbacks and bounce back — by practising with
manageable stress beforehand, recovering deliberately afterwards, interpreting
adversity realistically, and keeping your support network alive.

## What resilience is (and isn't)
Bonanno's research found that most people exposed to loss or potentially
traumatic events show a **resilient trajectory**: some disruption, then a fairly
stable return to functioning. Resilience is common, not heroic, and it draws on
many things — relationships, health, finances, flexible coping — not just mindset.
It doesn't mean not feeling pain. *(Resilience-training programmes show modest,
variable effects; the elements below are each reasonably supported.)*

## 1. Stress inoculation: graded challenge
Adapted from Meichenbaum's stress inoculation training:
1. **Understand your stress response**: what happens in your body and thoughts,
   and what usually helps (see trigger mapping and breathing skills).
2. **Build coping skills** in calm conditions: breathing, grounding, helpful
   self-talk ("I've prepared; one step at a time").
3. **Practise under rising, manageable pressure**: a ladder of challenges, rated
   0–10 for difficulty. Start around 3–4. Examples:
   - Public speaking: speak up once in a meeting → present to a few colleagues →
     present to a larger group.
   - Physical: cold showers for 30 seconds, building to longer; harder hikes.
   - Uncertainty: try a new restaurant without checking reviews; take an
     unplanned route.
4. Move up a rung only when the current one feels manageable. Debrief after each:
   what worked, what to try next time.

## 2. Recovery rituals
Stress isn't the main problem; stress without recovery is.
- **Daily**: a clear end-of-work ritual (shutdown checklist, a walk, changing
  clothes) that separates effort from rest. Sleep, movement and time outdoors are
  core.
- **After a hard event**: plan a recovery block — rest, a low-demand evening,
  time with people you like, a debrief with someone you trust.
- **Weekly**: something restorative you'd protect like an appointment.
- **Watch the "after-dip"**: many people feel worst a few days after a stressful
  event, once adrenaline wears off. Plan for it.

## 3. Reframing adversity (realistically)
**Cognitive reappraisal** — changing how you interpret a situation — is one of the
best-supported emotion regulation strategies. Not "everything happens for a
reason", but questions like:
- What's in my control here, and what isn't? Act on the first, accept the second.
- What's the most realistic outcome — not the worst, not the best?
- How have I got through hard things before? What did I use then?
- Will this matter in five years? In five weeks?
- Is there anything I can learn, or any value I can act on, even now?
Benefit-finding often emerges **later**; don't force it on yourself or others in
the raw early stage.

## 4. Support mapping and upkeep
1. Draw three circles: **inner** (people you could call at 3am), **middle**
   (friends, family you see regularly), **outer** (colleagues, neighbours, groups).
2. For each person, note what kind of support they're good at: practical help,
   listening, advice, fun.
3. Spot gaps: no one for practical help? Everyone relies on one person? Too few
   in the inner circle?
4. **Maintain** before you need it: regular contact, small favours, showing up
   for others. A monthly reminder to message a few people helps.
5. Know your **formal supports** too: GP, employee assistance programme, helplines.

## Gotchas
- **"Toughing it out" isn't resilience.** Asking for help is a resilience skill.
- **Toxic positivity**: pushing yourself to be grateful or upbeat too soon can
  feel invalidating. Feel first, reframe later.
- **Over-challenge**: stress inoculation with challenges too far up the ladder
  just stresses you. Keep it graded.
- **Structural problems** (unsafe work, poverty, discrimination) can't be
  out-resilienced; address or escape them where possible.
- **When to get help**: if after adversity you have persistent nightmares,
  flashbacks, avoidance, low mood or anxiety for more than a few weeks, or you
  can't function, see a GP or therapist. If you feel unsafe, contact emergency
  services or a crisis line.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a graded challenge ladder and have done at least the first rung.
- [ ] You have a daily recovery ritual and a plan for after hard events.
- [ ] You can apply the reappraisal questions to a current difficulty.
- [ ] Your support map exists, with gaps noted and a contact reminder set.
- [ ] You know when adversity calls for professional help.
