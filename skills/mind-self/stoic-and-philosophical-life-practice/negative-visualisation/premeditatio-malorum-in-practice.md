# Premeditatio malorum in practice

**Goal:** briefly and deliberately imagine plausible setbacks and losses so that, if
they happen, they land less as shocks — and so that what you have now is noticed and
valued. Educational philosophy-of-life guidance, not a treatment for anxiety.

## 1. Morning premeditation (3–5 minutes)
Marcus Aurelius begins *Meditations* Book 2 with a version of this: *today I will meet
interfering, ungrateful, arrogant people…* A practical routine:
1. Look at today's plan.
2. For each significant item, picture one realistic thing that might go wrong:
   - the meeting runs badly, the train is cancelled, a colleague is rude, the code fails.
3. For each, rehearse **your response**: "If X happens, I'll do Y, calmly."
4. Finish with one line: "Whatever happens, my response is up to me." (Links to
   mind-self.stoic-and-philosophical-life-practice.dichotomy-of-control.sorting-what-is-up-to-you.)

## 2. Loss rehearsal (occasional, deeper)
Seneca and Epictetus suggest reflecting that what we love is on loan. Epictetus's
striking example: when kissing your child, remind yourself they are mortal.
- Pick one thing or person you value.
- Spend **one or two minutes** imagining life without it — briefly and concretely.
- Then return to the present: they're here now. Let appreciation register. Maybe act
  on it (call them, use the thing, say thanks).
The aim is appreciation and preparedness, not dread.

## 3. Dosage and stopping rules
| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| A few minutes, then stop | Long, open-ended dwelling |
| Realistic, specific events | Extreme catastrophes you can't act on |
| End with a response or appreciation | End on the bad image |
| Occasional for heavy losses (weekly at most) | Daily rehearsal of loved ones dying |
**Stopping rule:** if your mood is noticeably worse 10 minutes later, or images keep
replaying, you've overdosed. Shorten it, make it more practical, or drop the loss
rehearsal for a while.

## 4. Turning it into action
Negative visualisation pairs naturally with practical preparation:
- Backups, spare keys, a buffer in the schedule, an emergency fund.
- A "pre-mortem" for a project: "It's six months from now and it failed — why?"
  Then fix the top causes now.
The Stoic twist: after preparing, accept the residue you can't prevent.

## 5. Gratitude from imagined loss
Hedonic adaptation means we stop noticing good things. Briefly imagining their absence
re-sensitises you. A one-line evening note — "Something I'd miss if it were gone today"
— is a compact form.

## Gotchas
- **Not for people prone to anxiety or rumination without care.** If you already worry
  a lot, lean on the response-rehearsal and practical forms, keep it very short, and skip
  loss rehearsal. If imagining loss triggers panic, intrusive images or deepening low
  mood, stop and talk to a mental-health professional.
- After a real bereavement or trauma, imagining further loss can be harmful; grief has
  its own process.
- It is not "expect the worst" pessimism. You're preparing, then letting go.
- Uncertainty: the dosage numbers are practical conventions from modern Stoic writers,
  not ancient prescriptions.

## Verify
- [ ] Your morning premeditation takes under 5 minutes and ends with a planned response.
- [ ] Loss rehearsals end on appreciation or an action, not the bad image.
- [ ] Your mood is the same or better afterwards; if not, you've cut the dose.
- [ ] At least one visualised risk led to a concrete preparation.
- [ ] You can state who should not use the loss-rehearsal form and why.
