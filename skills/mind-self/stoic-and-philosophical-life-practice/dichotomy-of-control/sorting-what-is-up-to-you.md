# Sorting what is up to you (the dichotomy of control)

**Goal:** in any situation, separate what is genuinely up to you from what isn't, put
your effort and concern on the first, and meet the second with acceptance. This is the
opening move of Epictetus's *Enchiridion* and the backbone of Stoic practice.
Educational philosophy-of-life guidance.

## 1. The core sort
Epictetus: up to us are our judgements, intentions, desires and aversions — our own
choices and how we respond. Not up to us: body, reputation, property, other people,
outcomes, the past. Practically:
1. Write the situation in one line ("My project review is tomorrow and I think it'll go badly").
2. Two columns: **Up to me** / **Not up to me**.
3. Fill them honestly. Typical result:
   - Up to me: how well I prepare tonight, what I say, how honestly I present problems,
     how I respond to criticism.
   - Not up to me: the reviewer's mood, the final decision, what others think of me.
4. Circle one action from the left column and do it. Note one item on the right and
   say to yourself something like "this is not up to me; I'll meet it when it comes".

## 2. Internalise your goals
Rewrite outcome goals as goals about your own conduct:
- "Win the match" → "Play my best, with full effort and fairness."
- "Get the job" → "Prepare thoroughly and present myself honestly."
- "Make them like me" → "Be kind and straightforward with them."
You still want the outcome; you just stake your sense of success on the part you own.

## 3. The trichotomy (a modern variant)
William Irvine proposes a third bin: things over which we have **some but not complete**
control (most real goals: health, career, relationships). For those, apply step 2:
take the controllable part as the goal. This is a modern reading; Epictetus himself
draws the line within our judgements and choices, so "partial control" items really
split into a part that is ours and a part that isn't.

## 4. The reserve clause
Stoics acted "with reservation": *I'll sail to Rome — if nothing prevents it.* Add
"…if fate allows" (or "…if circumstances permit") to plans. It's not pessimism; it pre-loads
acceptance so a blocked plan is a change of route, not a catastrophe.

## 5. In a crisis (fast version)
When something bad is unfolding:
1. Pause and breathe once.
2. Ask: "What, right now, is up to me?" — usually a small, immediate action.
3. Do that action.
4. Ask again. Repeat.
Leave the analysis of the uncontrollable for later.

## Gotchas
- The dichotomy is not passivity or "don't care". Stoics were active in public life;
  the point is to direct effort well, not to stop caring about outcomes and people.
- People overestimate what's not up to them in their own conduct ("I couldn't help
  snapping") and overestimate control of others. Be strict on both sides.
- Using it to suppress grief or legitimate anger can backfire. Feelings arising is
  normal; the practice concerns the judgements you add and what you do next.
- It doesn't replace help for serious situations (abuse, crisis, clinical anxiety);
  "what's up to me" may well be *asking for professional or practical help*.
- Uncertainty: translations of *eph' hēmin* ("up to us") vary; interpretations here
  follow mainstream modern Stoic writers.
