# Classifying desires and the tetrapharmakos

**Goal:** use Epicurus's practical ethics to reduce anxiety and pursue a calm,
pleasant life (*ataraxia*): sort desires, satisfy the ones that matter cheaply, savour
simple pleasures, and answer recurring fears with the "four-part cure". Epicurean
"pleasure" mostly means absence of pain and disturbance, not indulgence. Educational
guidance.

## 1. The desire sort
Epicurus (Letter to Menoeceus; Principal Doctrine 29) distinguishes:
| Type | Examples | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural and necessary** | food, water, shelter, warmth, friendship, peace of mind | Secure these first; they're usually cheap |
| **Natural but unnecessary** | gourmet food, nice clothes, sex, variety | Enjoy when available; don't depend on them |
| **Empty (vain)** | fame, wealth beyond need, status, power, "more" | Recognise and let go — they have no natural limit |
Exercise:
1. List 10 things you currently want or are working towards.
2. Label each N-N, N-U or E.
3. For each E, ask: "What natural need is this standing in for?" (status → belonging;
   wealth → security). Can you meet that need more directly?
4. For N-U items, ask: "If I didn't get this, would I suffer real pain?" If not, hold
   it lightly.

## 2. Savouring simple pleasures
Epicurus claimed he could rival Zeus for happiness with bread and water, and asked a
friend to send a little pot of cheese so he could feast.
- Once a day, eat or drink something simple slowly and attend to the pleasure.
- Notice the pleasure of *absence of pain*: being warm, not hungry, rested.
- Share simple meals with friends — friendship was central to Epicurean life.

## 3. The pleasure-pain calculus for choices
Epicurus: not every pleasure is to be chosen, and some pains are worth it.
For a decision, write:
- Short-term pleasure / pain of option A vs B.
- **Long-term** consequences — disturbance, dependence, regret, health, relationships.
Choose the option with the best long-run balance of tranquillity. (The term "hedonic
calculus" is Bentham's, later; the Epicurean version is informal and focused on calm.)

## 4. The tetrapharmakos (four-part cure)
Philodemus's summary of Epicurean teaching:
1. **Don't fear god(s).**
2. **Don't fear death** — "where death is, I am not".
3. **What is good is easy to get.**
4. **What is terrible is easy to endure** (intense pain is usually brief; long pain is usually mild).
Daily use: when a fear arises, ask which line answers it. Lines 3 and 4 are the most
broadly usable for modern worries about money and hardship. Lines 1 and 2 rest on
Epicurean physics and theology; take or leave them according to your own beliefs.

## Gotchas
- Epicureanism isn't hedonism in the modern sense; "eat, drink and be merry" misreads it.
- Line 4 ("easy to endure") does not apply well to chronic pain or serious illness;
  don't use it to dismiss real suffering — seek proper medical care.
- Withdrawing from ambition can slide into avoidance; check that letting go of a desire
  isn't fear in disguise.
- Uncertainty: the exact wording of the tetrapharmakos varies by translation; the
  classification table simplifies scholarly debates about where specific desires belong.
