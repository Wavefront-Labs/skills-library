# Keeping mortality in view (memento mori)

**Goal:** keep the fact of your own death deliberately in mind so it sharpens
priorities, cuts procrastination and petty grievance, and deepens appreciation of
ordinary days. Epictetus: keep death before your eyes daily, and you'll never have a
base thought or excessive desire. Educational philosophy-of-life guidance.

## 1. Daily death reflection (1–2 minutes)
Marcus: *You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think.*
Each morning or evening:
1. Say or write: "I will die. I don't know when."
2. Ask: "If today were among my last, what would I do differently?"
3. Pick **one** small thing to act on today (a call, a postponed task, dropping a feud).
Keep it brief. The reflection is a lens for action, not a mood to sit in.

## 2. Counting remaining time
Making finitude concrete:
- **Remaining summers:** at 40, with a typical life expectancy, maybe ~40 summers left.
  How many will you spend with your parents? If you see them twice a year, that may be a
  few dozen visits in total.
- **Life in weeks:** a grid of ~4,000 squares (about 80 years × 52 weeks), filled up to
  today. Seeing the filled part is sobering.
- **Specific counts:** books you might still read (say 20 a year × remaining years),
  holidays, seasons of a sport you love.
Then ask: given these numbers, what am I spending them on?

## 3. The last-time meditation
Most "last times" go unrecognised: the last time you carry a child, the last visit to a
place, the last conversation with someone. Practice:
- During an ordinary activity with someone you love, briefly reflect: *this could be
  one of the last times.*
- Let it turn into attention and warmth in the moment, not sadness.
This closely resembles the loss rehearsal in

## 4. Memento objects
Traditionally a skull, hourglass or coin; today, a phone wallpaper, a coin in your
pocket, a "life in weeks" poster. Use the object as a **cue** for the one-minute
reflection. Rotate or move it occasionally — cues you stop noticing stop working.

## 5. Turning it into priorities
Once a month, ask:
- What am I postponing "until later" that matters?
- Which grudges am I spending finite time on?
- What would I regret not having said?
Write one action for each and schedule it.

## Gotchas
- **Not for everyone right now.** If you're grieving a recent death, have health anxiety,
  are seriously ill, or have experienced suicidal thoughts, mortality practices can be
  harmful rather than clarifying. Skip them, or do them only with a therapist's support.
  If thinking about death brings up thoughts of ending your life, contact a crisis line
  or emergency service in your country immediately.
- If reflections increase dread or paralysis rather than focus, cut the dose or stop.
- Life-expectancy numbers are rough averages; use them for perspective, not prediction.
- Uncertainty: "life in weeks" and "remaining summers" are modern popular framings, not
  ancient Stoic exercises, though they serve the same aim.

## Verify
- [ ] Your daily reflection takes under 2 minutes and ends with one concrete action.
- [ ] You've calculated at least one remaining-time count that matters to you.
- [ ] You've used a memento cue this week and noticed it.
- [ ] Your monthly review produced at least one scheduled action.
- [ ] The practice leaves you more focused, not more anxious; if not, you've adjusted.
