# Auditing character against chosen virtues

**Goal:** pick a small set of virtues, define them as observable behaviours, check
yourself against them regularly, and put deliberate practice into the weakest one.
Rooted in ancient virtue ethics and in Benjamin Franklin's well-known self-improvement
method. Educational guidance.

## 1. Choose the list (3–13 virtues)
Options:
- **Stoic cardinal virtues:** wisdom, justice, courage, temperance (self-control).
- **Franklin's 13:** temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry,
  sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquillity, chastity, humility.
- **Your own list**, drawn from people you admire or values you hold (e.g. patience,
  honesty, generosity).
Fewer is better to start: 4–6.

## 2. Define each concretely
Abstract virtues can't be audited. For each, write:
- **Short definition:** Franklin gave each a one-line precept (e.g. *Silence: speak not
  but what may benefit others or yourself; avoid trifling conversation*).
- **Two observable behaviours that count:** "Patience: I let people finish sentences;
  I don't sigh or check my phone when someone's slow."
- **One clear lapse:** "Interrupting; snapping at delays."
Aristotle's mean helps: courage sits between cowardice and recklessness, so name both
failure directions where relevant.

## 3. Franklin's chart method
Franklin kept a small book with a page per week, columns for days, rows for virtues.
- Each week, **focus on one virtue**; record any lapses in all rows, but concentrate
  effort on the focus row.
- Mark a dot for each lapse at the end of the day (an evening review — see
  mind-self.stoic-and-philosophical-life-practice.philosophical-journaling.morning-preparation-and-evening-review).
- Rotate to the next virtue each week; with 13 virtues, a full cycle takes 13 weeks,
  four cycles a year.
Franklin reported never reaching perfection but being "a better and a happier man" for the attempt.

## 4. Weekly audit (15 minutes)
1. For each virtue, rate the week 1–5 using your behavioural definitions.
2. Note one specific episode for the lowest score: situation, what you did, what the
   virtuous action would have looked like.
3. Pick next week's **target virtue** (usually the weakest, occasionally the one most
   relevant to upcoming events).
4. Write one implementation intention: "When [situation], I will [behaviour]."

## 5. Targeted drills for the weakest virtue
- **Rehearse:** visualise the trigger situation and the virtuous response.
- **Model:** ask "What would [an admired person] do here?"
- **Seek reps:** create low-stakes chances to practise (to build courage, speak first
  in one meeting; for temperance, one evening without a snack).
- **Get feedback:** ask a trusted friend how you're doing on that one virtue.

## Gotchas
- Perfectionism turns this into a stick to beat yourself with. Count lapses as data;
  the trend matters, not a clean sheet.
- Picking too many virtues, or vague ones, makes the audit meaningless.
- Self-rating is biased; occasional outside feedback calibrates it.
- If audits feed persistent shame, guilt or scrupulosity (obsessive moral checking),
  stop and consider talking to a mental-health professional.
- Uncertainty: Franklin's list and chart are described in his *Autobiography*; the
  1–5 weekly rating is a modern addition.

## Verify
- [ ] Your list has 4–6 virtues, each with a precept, two behaviours and one lapse.
- [ ] You've kept a chart for at least two weeks with a clear focus virtue each week.
- [ ] Your weekly audit names a specific low-score episode and the better response.
- [ ] You have one written "when…I will…" intention for the current target.
- [ ] You've asked one other person for feedback on a virtue.
