# Morning preparation and evening review

**Goal:** use writing as a philosophical exercise — preparing for the day by your
principles, reviewing your conduct against them at night, and keeping a notebook of
maxims you return to. Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* is essentially such a private
notebook. Educational guidance.

## 1. Set up
- One notebook or a single digital file, private. Short entries; this is not a diary
  of events.
- A written list of **3–6 principles** you're working with (e.g. "Act justly toward
  others", "Don't add judgements to facts", "Do the task in front of you properly").
  See mind-self.stoic-and-philosophical-life-practice.virtue-self-examination.auditing-character-against-chosen-virtues.

## 2. Morning preparation (5 minutes)
Write answers to:
1. What's ahead today? (two or three key items)
2. What could challenge me? (people, temptations, setbacks — see
   mind-self.stoic-and-philosophical-life-practice.negative-visualisation.premeditatio-malorum-in-practice)
3. Which principle will I practise, and how specifically?
   "When the client pushes back, I'll listen fully before replying."

## 3. Evening review (5–10 minutes)
Seneca describes reviewing his whole day at night, once the lamp was out, hiding
nothing from himself. Epictetus recommends three questions (of Pythagorean origin):
1. **What did I do wrong?**
2. **What did I do right?**
3. **What did I leave undone that I should have done?**
Then:
4. What will I do differently tomorrow? (one concrete change)
Tone: a fair coach, not a prosecutor. Seneca explicitly says he pardons himself for
today but resolves not to repeat. Record facts and judgements separately ("I raised my
voice at 3pm" vs "I'm a terrible person" — only the first belongs here).

## 4. Hypomnemata: the maxims notebook
The ancients kept notebooks of useful sayings to reread (*hypomnemata*). Practice:
- Keep a section for short quotations and your own one-line principles.
- Write them out by hand when you meet them; rewriting is part of absorbing them.
- Each week, pick one maxim as your theme and reread it daily.
- Keep it short — a bloated collection stops being reread.

## 5. Writing to yourself as counsel
Marcus writes in the second person ("You have power over your mind…"). Try addressing
yourself as you would advise a friend: "You're annoyed because you expected
gratitude. Did you act well? Then that's enough." Second-person writing tends to
create helpful distance.

## Gotchas
- Turning review into self-punishment defeats the purpose; if evening reviews leave
  you anxious or ashamed night after night, shorten them, add the "what did I do right?"
  item first, and consider talking to a professional if low mood persists.
- Journaling that just replays grievances is rumination, not review. End every entry
  with a forward action.
- Don't aim to sound like Marcus. Plain, specific, private.
- Uncertainty: attribution of the three-question review to the Pythagoreans comes via
  Epictetus quoting the Golden Verses; details of ancient practice are reconstructed by
  scholars such as Pierre Hadot.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a written list of 3–6 principles at the front of the journal.
- [ ] Morning entries name a specific principle and situation for the day.
- [ ] Evening entries answer all three questions plus one change for tomorrow.
- [ ] Your maxims section has a current weekly theme you reread.
- [ ] Reviews end on action and leave you steady, not ashamed.
