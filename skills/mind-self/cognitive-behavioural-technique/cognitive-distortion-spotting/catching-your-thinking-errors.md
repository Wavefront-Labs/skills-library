# Catching your thinking errors

**Goal:** recognise common patterns of distorted thinking ("cognitive distortions"),
label them as they happen in your own head, and use the label as a cue to step back.
The catalogue comes from Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy and was popularised by David
Burns. This is self-help psychoeducation, not therapy.

## 1. The core catalogue
| Distortion | Pattern | Example | Counter-question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **All-or-nothing** | Only extremes | "If it's not perfect, it's a failure." | Where on a 0–100 scale is it really? |
| **Catastrophising** | Jumping to the worst outcome | "This headache is a tumour." | What's most likely? Could I cope with the worst? |
| **Overgeneralisation** | One event → always/never | "I always mess up." | Is it really *always*? Counter-examples? |
| **Mental filter** | Dwelling on one negative detail | Fixating on one critical comment among ten good ones | What am I filtering out? |
| **Disqualifying the positive** | Good things "don't count" | "They're only being nice." | Would I discount this for a friend? |
| **Mind reading** | Assuming others' thoughts | "She thinks I'm boring." | What's the evidence? Could I ask? |
| **Fortune telling** | Predicting negative outcomes as fact | "I'll fail the interview." | What happened last time I predicted this? |
| **Emotional reasoning** | Feeling = fact | "I feel stupid, so I am." | Feelings are real, but are they evidence? |
| **Should statements** | Rigid rules | "I should never be tired." | Who says? What's a more flexible "I'd prefer…"? |
| **Labelling** | Global label from one action | "I'm an idiot." | Would I call a friend that for the same thing? |
| **Personalisation / blame** | Too much (or no) responsibility | "The party flopped because of me." | What other factors were involved? |
| **Magnification / minimisation** | Inflating bad, shrinking good | "My mistake was huge; my success was luck." | Same yardstick both ways? |
Names vary between books; the patterns matter more than the labels.

## 2. Labelling in the moment
1. Notice the emotional jolt (tight chest, sinking stomach).
2. Catch the thought in words.
3. Label silently: "Ah — mind reading."
4. Ask that distortion's counter-question once.
5. Move on; for a sticky thought, do a full thought record (see
   mind-self.cognitive-behavioural-technique.thought-records.completing-a-thought-record).

## 3. Build a personal distortion profile
Over two weeks, tally each caught thought by distortion type. Most people have
**two or three favourites**. Knowing yours ("I catastrophise about health and mind-read
at work") lets you pre-empt them in the situations where they show up.

## 4. Catch-it drills
- **Media drill:** while watching drama or reading comments online, label distortions
  in others' speech — practice with low emotional stakes.
- **Diary scan:** once a week, reread journal entries or messages and highlight distortions.
- **Trigger rehearsal:** before a known trigger (a performance review), list the
  distortions you expect and their counter-questions.

## Gotchas
- A label is not a verdict that the thought is false. Sometimes the feared thing is
  likely; then the job is problem-solving, not reframing.
- Don't weaponise labels on other people ("you're catastrophising") in arguments.
- Spotting distortions can turn into self-criticism ("I'm *always* distorting" — itself
  overgeneralisation). Keep it curious.
- **Professional help:** if distorted thinking comes with persistent low mood, anxiety,
  obsessive thoughts or thoughts of self-harm, see a GP or qualified CBT therapist; for
  clinical conditions this works best inside guided treatment.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name and give an example of at least eight distortions.
- [ ] You labelled at least one of your own live thoughts today.
- [ ] You have a two-week tally and know your top two or three distortions.
- [ ] You've done one catch-it drill with low-stakes material.
- [ ] You treat a label as a prompt to check, not as proof the thought is wrong.
