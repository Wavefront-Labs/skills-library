# Postponing and sorting worries

**Goal:** stop worry from leaking across the whole day by giving it a fixed time slot,
sorting each worry into "I can act on this" or "I can't", turning the first kind into
actions and letting the second kind go. Worry postponement comes from CBT research on
chronic worry; this is self-help guidance, not therapy.

## 1. Set a worry window
- **15–30 minutes, same time daily**, same place (a desk, not your bed).
- **Not** within about 2 hours of bedtime.
- Have a notepad or notes app ready.

## 2. Postpone during the day
When a worry shows up outside the window:
1. Notice it: "I'm worrying."
2. Write it down in **a few words** ("rent increase", "Mum's test results").
3. Tell yourself: "I'll think about this at 6pm."
4. Bring attention back to what you're doing — engage fully with the task or the
   senses (a brief breathing or grounding exercise helps).
Expect to do this many times a day at first. It gets easier with practice.

## 3. Use the window
1. Read the list. Some worries will no longer feel important — cross them out. (Notice
   how often this happens; it's evidence that worries pass.)
2. For each remaining worry, apply the **worry tree**:
   - *What exactly am I worried about?*
   - *Can I do anything about it?*
     - **No / hypothetical** ("What if I get ill someday?") → let it go; switch attention
       to something else. Acceptance and defusion help here (see
       mind-self.cognitive-behavioural-technique.acceptance-and-defusion.unhooking-from-thoughts-and-acting-on-values).
     - **Yes / practical** → go to step 3.
3. **Convert to action:** What can I do? When? Write: "Call landlord Thursday 10am to
   ask about the increase." If it can be done now, do it now; otherwise schedule it.
4. When the window ends, **stop**, even mid-worry. Unfinished items go to tomorrow's list.

## 4. Problem-solving for practical worries
For bigger practical worries, use a structured approach:
1. Define the problem specifically.
2. Brainstorm solutions without judging.
3. Pick one; list pros and cons if stuck.
4. Plan the steps; do the first.
5. Review.

## 5. Interrupting rumination
Rumination (repetitive dwelling on past events or "why" questions) differs from
future-focused worry but responds to similar tactics:
- Catch the loop: "Am I solving something, or circling?" If no progress in ~5 minutes, it's rumination.
- Switch from "why" to "how" questions: "How can I handle this next time?"
- Shift into an absorbing activity using hands and attention (a puzzle, cooking, a walk).

## Gotchas
- Postponing is not suppressing. You're not trying to banish the thought, just
  rescheduling it; forcing thoughts away tends to make them return.
- Worry windows at night or in bed teach the bed to be a worry place.
- "Worrying is useful because it prepares me" is a common belief; test it — does the
  worry produce actions, or just loops?
- **Professional help:** if worry is near-constant, hard to control for months, comes with
  physical symptoms (tension, poor sleep, restlessness), panic, or low mood, see a GP or
  CBT therapist; generalised anxiety disorder is treatable and this technique is one
  component of fuller treatment.
- Uncertainty: window length and timing are typical workbook recommendations rather than
  fixed research values.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a fixed daily worry window, not near bedtime.
- [ ] You wrote and postponed worries during the day at least several times.
- [ ] In the window, each worry was sorted practical vs hypothetical.
- [ ] Each practical worry has a written action with a time.
- [ ] You can tell worry from rumination and have one interrupt you use.
