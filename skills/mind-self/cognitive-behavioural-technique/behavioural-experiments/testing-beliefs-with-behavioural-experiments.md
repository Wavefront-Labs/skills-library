# Testing beliefs with behavioural experiments

**Goal:** instead of arguing with a belief in your head, test it in the real world:
make a specific prediction, run a planned experiment, observe what actually happens
and update the belief. Behavioural experiments are a core CBT method; this is the
self-help version for everyday beliefs (e.g. "If I say no, people will be angry"). It
is not a substitute for therapy. A clinician-facing version is at
health-medicine.clinical-psychology.cognitive-behavioral-therapy.exposure-and-behavioural-experiment-design.

## 1. Pick a belief to test
Good candidates are **beliefs that drive avoidance or over-effort**:
- "If I make a mistake in a meeting, people will think I'm incompetent."
- "If I don't reply to emails within an hour, something bad will happen."
- "If I ask for help, I'll be seen as weak."
Rate how strongly you believe it now: 0–100%.

## 2. Turn it into a testable prediction
Make it **specific and observable**: what will happen, how you'll know, by when.
- Vague: "People will think badly of me."
- Testable: "If I say 'I'm not sure, let me check' in Thursday's meeting, at least one
  person will visibly react negatively (frown, comment), and my manager will mention it
  afterwards."
Also write the **alternative prediction** — what would happen if the belief were wrong.

## 3. Plan the experiment
| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Belief (rating) | Admitting uncertainty = seen as incompetent (75%) |
| Prediction | Visible negative reaction + manager mentions it |
| Experiment | Say "I'm not sure, let me check" once in Thursday's meeting |
| Safety behaviours to drop | Over-apologising, rehearsing a perfect answer |
| Likely obstacles | Bottling it; plan: write the phrase on my notepad |
| How I'll record | Notes right after the meeting |

**Safety behaviours** — things you do to prevent the feared outcome (over-preparing,
avoiding eye contact, apologising) — must be dropped or they'll get the credit for
the outcome and the belief stays intact.

## 4. Run it and record
Immediately afterwards, write:
- What actually happened (facts).
- Did the prediction come true? Fully / partly / not at all.
- What else did you notice?

## 5. Update the belief
- Re-rate the original belief 0–100%.
- Write what you learned in one line: "Saying I'm unsure got a nod and no comment."
- If the prediction came true, that's useful too: what does it tell you? How did you cope?
  Was the consequence as bad as feared?
- Plan the next experiment — one result rarely shifts a long-held belief. Vary context
  and repeat.

## 6. Survey experiments
When a belief is about what *others* think, ask them (anonymously if needed):
"Do you think less of a colleague who asks a question in a meeting?" Send to 5–10 people
whose judgement you'd trust. Results often show far more tolerance than you predicted.

## Gotchas
- Predictions that can't fail ("they'll think it, but hide it") can't be tested; rewrite
  them around observable outcomes.
- Experiments should be safe and proportionate. Don't test beliefs by doing something
  genuinely risky (driving recklessly, quitting a job on a whim).
- Keeping a safety behaviour "just in case" quietly ruins the test.
- **Professional guidance:** for beliefs tied to diagnosed anxiety disorders, OCD,
  health anxiety, eating disorders or trauma, design experiments with a qualified CBT
  therapist; poorly designed experiments can reinforce fear.
- Uncertainty: the planning table is a common worksheet layout, not a single canonical form.

## Verify
- [ ] Your belief has a 0–100% rating before and after.
- [ ] The prediction is specific, observable and time-bound, with an alternative written.
- [ ] You named and dropped at least one safety behaviour.
- [ ] You recorded what happened right afterwards, as facts.
- [ ] You wrote the learning in one line and planned a follow-up experiment.
