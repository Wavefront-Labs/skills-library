# Completing a thought record

**Goal:** slow down an upsetting moment on paper — what happened, what you felt, what
you told yourself — and test that thought against the evidence, ending with a more
balanced view and a re-rated mood. This is the core written exercise of cognitive
behavioural therapy (CBT), used here as self-help in the style of workbooks like
*Mind Over Mood*. It is **not** a substitute for therapy.

## 1. When to fill one in
- Soon after a noticeable mood shift (within hours, while it's fresh).
- Moderate intensity is ideal for practice; when you're overwhelmed, use a grounding or
  calming strategy first and write later.
- Paper, notes app or a printed worksheet — the format matters less than doing it.

## 2. The seven columns
| # | Column | What to write | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Situation** | Who, what, where, when — facts only | Tue 4pm, manager said "Can we talk tomorrow?" |
| 2 | **Moods** | One-word emotions, each rated 0–100% | Anxious 80%, ashamed 40% |
| 3 | **Automatic thoughts** | What went through your mind; circle the **hot thought** (most linked to the strongest mood) | "I've messed up." **"I'm going to be let go."** |
| 4 | **Evidence for** the hot thought | Facts only, not interpretations | Missed a deadline last month |
| 5 | **Evidence against** | Facts that don't fit the thought | Good review in March; deadline slip was agreed; she often schedules chats |
| 6 | **Balanced / alternative thought** | A believable statement that accounts for both columns; rate belief 0–100% | "One slip doesn't mean I'm being let go; she may want to talk about the new project." 70% |
| 7 | **Re-rate moods** | Same moods, new ratings; note any new ones | Anxious 40%, ashamed 15% |

## 3. Finding the hot thought
Ask: *What was going through my mind just before I felt this? What does this say about
me / my future / others? What's the worst that could happen?* Images count as
thoughts too. Thoughts phrased as questions ("What if they fire me?") should be turned
into statements ("They'll fire me") so they can be tested.

## 4. Questions for evidence against
- Have I had experiences that show this isn't completely true?
- If a friend had this thought, what would I tell them?
- When I've felt like this before, what helped me see it differently?
- Am I jumping to conclusions or ignoring something? (See
  mind-self.cognitive-behavioural-technique.cognitive-distortion-spotting.catching-your-thinking-errors.)
- Five years from now, how would I see this?

## 5. Writing the balanced thought
- It's **not** positive thinking — it must be believable and include the true parts of
  "evidence for".
- Often joins both columns with "and" or "although": "I missed a deadline, *and* I've
  generally done good work."
- If belief in it is under ~40%, gather more evidence or design a test (see
  mind-self.cognitive-behavioural-technique.behavioural-experiments.testing-beliefs-with-behavioural-experiments).

## Gotchas
- Evidence columns fill with interpretations ("she sounded annoyed") — keep only facts.
- Expect small mood shifts (say 80 → 50), not 0. That's a success.
- Don't use thought records to argue yourself out of a real problem; sometimes the
  thought is accurate and the next step is problem-solving.
- **When to get professional help:** if low mood, anxiety, intrusive thoughts or
  trauma-related reactions persist for weeks, disrupt work or relationships, or include
  thoughts of self-harm or suicide, see a GP or qualified CBT therapist (contact a crisis
  line or emergency service if you're at risk). Thought records for clinical depression,
  OCD, PTSD and similar are best done with a therapist.

## Verify
- [ ] Situation column has only observable facts.
- [ ] Each mood is a single word with a 0–100% rating.
- [ ] One hot thought is circled and phrased as a statement.
- [ ] Both evidence columns contain facts, not interpretations.
- [ ] Balanced thought is believable (≥ ~40%) and mood was re-rated.
