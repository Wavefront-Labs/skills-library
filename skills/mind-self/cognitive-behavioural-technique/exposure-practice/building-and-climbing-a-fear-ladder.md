# Building and climbing a fear ladder

**Goal:** reduce an everyday fear of something **safe** (making phone calls, speaking
up, driving on motorways, moderate heights, dogs on leads) by facing it in planned,
graded steps instead of avoiding it. Exposure is one of the best-supported techniques
in CBT; this skill covers self-directed practice for everyday fears, **not** treatment
of diagnosed phobias, panic disorder, OCD or PTSD (see Gotchas).

## 1. Check the fear is a good fit
- The feared situation is **objectively safe** (or acceptably safe, like ordinary life).
- Avoidance is costing you something you care about.
- You're not in crisis and the fear isn't tied to trauma.

## 2. Build the ladder
1. List 10–15 situations related to the fear, from mildly to very uncomfortable.
2. Rate each with **SUDS** (Subjective Units of Distress, 0–100): how anxious you'd
   expect to feel doing it.
3. Arrange as a ladder. Fill gaps so no step jumps more than ~10–20 points.
Example — phone calls:
| SUDS | Step |
|---|---|
| 20 | Listen to a recorded voicemail greeting, then hang up |
| 30 | Call a shop to ask opening hours (script allowed) |
| 45 | Same, without a script |
| 55 | Call a friend unexpectedly |
| 70 | Call to book an appointment, answering questions |
| 85 | Call to complain about a bill |

## 3. Run each exposure
1. Start at a step around **SUDS 30–40** — uncomfortable but doable.
2. Before: write your feared prediction ("I'll stammer and they'll hang up").
3. Do it, **staying in** the situation long enough to learn something — until the
   prediction is tested and anxiety has at least started to ease, or for a set time.
4. **No escape, no rescue:** don't leave at peak anxiety if you can avoid it.
5. Afterwards: record peak SUDS, what happened vs your prediction, what you learned.

## 4. Drop safety behaviours
Safety behaviours are subtle avoidance: scripts, sitting by the exit, carrying
medication "just in case" (where not medically needed), bringing a companion, not
making eye contact. Plan to **drop them progressively**; otherwise you learn "I coped
because of the script", not "I can cope".

## 5. Repeat, then climb
- Repeat a step several times, ideally close together (e.g. daily) — repetition is
  where learning sticks.
- Move up when the step feels manageable (often peak SUDS has dropped substantially)
  or vary the context (different shop, different time).
- Current research (inhibitory learning) suggests that tolerating anxiety and
  disconfirming predictions matter more than waiting for anxiety to fall to zero;
  varying steps and sometimes taking them out of order can help learning last.

## Gotchas
- Too-big steps lead to escape, which strengthens fear. Too-small steps waste time;
  aim for "challenging but doable".
- One-off exposures do little; frequency matters.
- Anxiety on a bad day can spike on an easier step — that's normal, not failure.
- **Get a therapist** for exposure if the fear is severe, involves panic attacks, is
  part of OCD (exposure needs response prevention designed with a professional), relates
  to trauma or PTSD, concerns eating, or if you've had suicidal thoughts. A clinician-facing
  design guide is at health-medicine.clinical-psychology.cognitive-behavioral-therapy.exposure-and-behavioural-experiment-design.
- Never expose yourself to situations that are actually dangerous.
- Uncertainty: step-size and "move up" thresholds are rules of thumb from self-help
  workbooks; therapists tailor them individually.

## Verify
- [ ] The feared situation is safe and the fear isn't trauma-linked or clinical.
- [ ] Your ladder has 10–15 steps with SUDS ratings and no big jumps.
- [ ] Each exposure log has prediction, peak SUDS, outcome and learning.
- [ ] You've identified and dropped at least one safety behaviour.
- [ ] You repeated your current step at least three times before moving up.
