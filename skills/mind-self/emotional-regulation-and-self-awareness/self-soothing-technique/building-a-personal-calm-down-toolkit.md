# Building a personal calm-down toolkit

**Goal:** have three or four techniques you've practised in calm moments, so when
anger, panic or overwhelm spikes you can bring your body down enough to think —
without having to work out what to do on the spot.

These tools reduce *arousal*; they don't solve the problem that caused it. Once
you're back in a thinking state, deal with the problem.

## 1. Fast physiological calmers (seconds to a few minutes)
**Physiological sigh / cyclic sighing**
1. Breathe in through the nose fully.
2. Take a second, shorter sip of air on top to fill the lungs.
3. Exhale slowly through the mouth, longer than the inhale.
4. Repeat 2–5 times for a quick reset, or for about 5 minutes as a practice.
A 2023 study found daily cyclic sighing improved mood somewhat more than
mindfulness meditation over a month. *(One study; promising, not settled.)*

**Extended exhale**: breathe in for about 4, out for about 6–8. A longer exhale
tends to engage the calming (parasympathetic) side of the nervous system. See
mind-self.grief-and-stress-coping-technique.regulated-breathing for more patterns.

**Cold-water reset**: splash very cold water on your face, or hold a cold pack
or a bag of ice wrapped in a cloth over eyes and cheeks for 15–30 seconds while
holding the breath briefly. This draws on the dive reflex, which slows heart
rate. **Caution:** skip this if you have a heart condition, low heart rate,
or an eating disorder affecting heart health, and check with a doctor first.

**Intense brief movement**: a fast walk, stairs, 20–60 seconds of vigorous
exercise burns off the adrenaline surge that anger and panic produce. Avoid
anything risky while agitated (driving, heavy machinery).

## 2. Sensory soothing (minutes)
Choose what works for you through each sense:
- **Touch**: a warm drink, a soft blanket, a weighted blanket, a pet, a hot shower.
- **Sound**: a specific calming playlist, nature sounds, humming.
- **Smell**: a scent you associate with safety (lavender, coffee, a loved one's
  jumper).
- **Sight**: a photo, an open view, a tidy corner.
- **Taste**: a strong mint, sour sweet or herbal tea — strong tastes pull
  attention to the present.
**Five-senses grounding** (5 things you see, 4 hear, 3 touch, 2 smell, 1 taste)
is covered in mind-self.grief-and-stress-coping-technique.grounding-techniques.

## 3. Assemble your protocol
Write a short, numbered plan on a card or phone note. Example:
1. Notice: "I'm at 7+ and rising."
2. Say a pre-set exit line: "I need ten minutes, I'll come back."
3. Go somewhere private. Three physiological sighs.
4. Cold water on the face, or a brisk walk round the block.
5. Two sensory soothers from my list.
6. Re-rate 0–10. Below 5 → return and address the issue. Still high → repeat
   or call my support person.

Also consider a physical **calm-down kit** (a "soothe box"): mints, a scent,
headphones, a photo, a stress ball, the card above.

## 4. Practise when calm
Techniques you've never tried won't come to mind at 9/10. Run the sighs and the
protocol a few times a week when you're at 2–3, so they become automatic.

## Gotchas
- **Escape vs soothing**: alcohol, bingeing, doom-scrolling or picking a fight
  give quick relief with costs later. Notice if a "soother" is one of these.
- **Hyperventilation risk**: very fast or forceful breathing can cause dizziness
  and tingling. Keep breaths slow and the exhale long.
- **Tell people your exit line** in advance so leaving isn't taken as storming off.
- **Not a crisis plan.** If you feel unsafe or have thoughts of harming yourself
  or someone else, contact local emergency services or a crisis line straight
  away. If acute distress or panic attacks are frequent, a GP or therapist can
  help — panic disorder and anxiety disorders respond well to treatment.

## Verify
- [ ] You've tried at least three techniques from different sections while calm.
- [ ] Your protocol is written down, numbered, and kept where you'll see it.
- [ ] You have an exit line others know about.
- [ ] You checked the cold-water caution against your own health.
- [ ] You know the difference between this toolkit and a crisis plan, and where to get help.
