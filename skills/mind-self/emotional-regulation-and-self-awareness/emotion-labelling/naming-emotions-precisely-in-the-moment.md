# Naming emotions precisely in the moment

**Goal:** move from "I feel bad" to "I feel embarrassed and a bit resentful" —
quickly, while it's happening — because a precise label gives you something to
work with.

## Why it helps
- **Affect labelling**: lab studies (e.g. Lieberman and colleagues) found that
  putting feelings into words reduces the intensity of the emotional response
  somewhat, compared with just looking at the trigger. Popularised as "name it to
  tame it".
- **Granularity**: people who distinguish finely between emotions ("frustrated"
  vs "disappointed" vs "anxious") tend to regulate better, because each label
  points to a different need and response. *(Correlational research mostly;
  training studies are fewer, but the practice is low-risk.)*

## 1. Grow the vocabulary
Start from a **feelings wheel** (many free versions online; Plutchik's wheel and
Willcox's feelings wheel are common). Core families branch out to finer words:
- **Anger** → irritated, frustrated, resentful, indignant, betrayed
- **Fear** → uneasy, nervous, anxious, dreading, panicky
- **Sadness** → disappointed, lonely, hurt, grieving, hopeless
- **Shame family** → embarrassed, self-conscious, humiliated, guilty (guilt is
  about an act; shame is about the self)
- **Joy** → content, relieved, proud, excited, grateful
- **Surprise / disgust** → startled, confused, repulsed, contemptuous
Pick 5–10 new words a week and use them in your log.

## 2. Label in the moment (30 seconds)
When you notice a shift — tension, a hot face, a sinking feeling:
1. **Pause** and take one slow breath.
2. **Body first**: where is it? ("tight chest, clenched jaw")
3. **Family**: which broad family is it? (anger, fear, sadness…)
4. **Refine**: which finer word fits best? Try two or three silently — the right
   one often "clicks" with a small sense of relief.
5. **Intensity**: rate 0–10.
6. Say it in a neutral frame: *"I'm noticing frustration, about 6."* The
   "I'm noticing" frame creates a little distance compared with "I am furious".

## 3. Untangle mixed emotions
Most real situations produce blends: proud *and* sad at a child leaving home,
relieved *and* guilty after quitting a job. To untangle:
- List every feeling present, even contradictory ones, with intensities.
- Ask what each one is *about* (relief → the pressure ending; guilt → letting
  colleagues down).
- Notice **secondary emotions** — feelings about feelings ("angry that I'm
  anxious", "ashamed of being jealous"). Naming these separately often softens them.

## 4. Practice outside the moment
- **Evening review**: three emotional moments today, each with the most precise
  label you can find and its intensity.
- **Prompt check-ins**: a phone reminder twice a day — "What am I feeling right
  now?"
- **Labelling others** (in films, books) builds the same vocabulary with less heat.

## Gotchas
- **Labels aren't verdicts.** "I feel attacked" is often an interpretation
  disguised as a feeling; the feeling might be hurt or threatened.
- **Over-analysing** can become rumination. Label, then move to what you'll do.
- **Labelling isn't venting.** Repeatedly rehearsing anger out loud can amplify
  it; calm naming is different.
- **Very intense states** (panic, rage) may be too hot to label well. Calm the body
  first — see mind-self.emotional-regulation-and-self-awareness.self-soothing-technique
  — then name.
- **Some people find this persistently hard** (sometimes called alexithymia; also
  common in some autistic people). Starting with body sensations and a written
  list helps; a therapist can support it.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name at least three finer words in each core emotion family.
- [ ] In a recent moment you labelled body, family, finer word and intensity.
- [ ] You've separated at least one mixed emotion into its parts.
- [ ] You use the "I'm noticing…" frame rather than "I am…".
- [ ] You know to calm intense states before trying to label them.
