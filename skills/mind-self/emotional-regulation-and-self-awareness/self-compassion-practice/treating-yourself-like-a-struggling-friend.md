# Treating yourself like a struggling friend

**Goal:** when you fail, struggle or suffer, respond to yourself the way you'd
respond to a good friend in the same spot — warmly and honestly — instead of with
harsh self-attack.

Neff describes three components:
1. **Self-kindness** rather than self-judgement.
2. **Common humanity** — struggle is part of being human, not proof you're uniquely
   flawed.
3. **Mindfulness** — acknowledging the pain clearly without exaggerating or
   suppressing it.

Research links higher self-compassion with lower anxiety and depression and more
willingness to learn from mistakes. *(Mostly correlational; intervention studies
are positive but smaller.)*

## 1. The self-compassion break (1–3 minutes)
In a hard moment:
1. **Mindfulness**: "This is a moment of suffering." Or plainly: "This hurts."
   "This is stressful."
2. **Common humanity**: "Struggle is part of life." "Other people feel this way
   too." "I'm not alone in this."
3. **Kindness**: put a hand on your chest or another soothing touch, and say "May
   I be kind to myself" or ask "What do I need to hear right now?" — then say it.
Use your own words. If the phrases feel fake, make them more neutral and true:
"This is hard, and it makes sense that I'm upset."

## 2. The friend test
Write down what your inner voice is saying ("You idiot, you always mess this
up"). Then ask: *what would I say to a close friend who did exactly this?* Write
that. Compare the two. Then read the friend version to yourself. Most people
find they hold themselves to standards they'd never impose on others.

## 3. Working with the inner critic
- **Notice and name it**: "There's the critic." Some people give it a name or a
  character; distance makes it easier to answer.
- **Find its motive**: the critic is usually trying to protect you — from failure,
  rejection, embarrassment. Acknowledge that: "Thanks, I know you're worried I'll
  fail."
- **Reply with a compassionate coach**: same goal (doing well), different method:
  "That went badly. You were tired and underprepared. Let's prep earlier next
  time."
- **Spot its patterns**: always, never, should, labels ("I'm useless"). Swap for
  specifics ("this presentation went badly").

## 4. Common-humanity reframes
- "I'm the only one who…" → "Lots of people struggle with this, even if they hide it."
- "This shouldn't be happening to me" → "This is a hard thing that happens to people."
- Recall someone you respect who's been through something similar.

## 5. Compassionate letter writing (20 minutes)
Write a letter to yourself about something you feel bad about, from the point of
view of an unconditionally kind, wise friend who knows all your flaws and history.
Acknowledge the pain, mention the causes and conditions (stress, upbringing,
circumstances), and suggest gently what might help. Put it away; reread it later.

## Gotchas
- **Self-compassion isn't self-pity or excuse-making.** It includes honesty and
  accountability: "I got this wrong, I'll repair it" — said kindly.
- **Backdraft**: when people who are very self-critical first try kindness, pain
  can rise before it falls (old hurts surface). Go slowly, keep practices short,
  and use neutral phrases.
- **Feels fake or uncomfortable?** Common at first. Behaviour counts too: rest,
  eat, ask for help — kind acts even without kind words.
- **Not a fix for everything.** Deep-rooted shame, trauma or depression may need
  a therapist (compassion-focused therapy is one approach built for this).

## Verify
- [ ] You can recite the three parts of the self-compassion break in your own words.
- [ ] You've done the friend test on at least one real self-criticism.
- [ ] You can name what your inner critic is trying to protect you from.
- [ ] You've written a compassionate letter and reread it later.
- [ ] You keep accountability and kindness together rather than choosing one.
