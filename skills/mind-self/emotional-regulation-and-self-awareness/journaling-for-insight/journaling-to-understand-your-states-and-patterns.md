# Journaling to understand your states and patterns

**Goal:** use regular writing as a mirror — get thoughts out of your head, notice
how you actually feel over time, and spot patterns you can't see day to day.

## Pick a format (any will do; consistency matters more)
- **Paper notebook** — slower, fewer distractions, more private from devices.
- **A single digital file or app** — searchable, which makes the monthly review
  much easier.
Date every entry. Keep it in one place.

## 1. Freewriting (clearing and discovering)
1. Set a timer for 10 minutes, or fill a set amount (Cameron's "morning pages"
   are three longhand pages first thing).
2. Write continuously. Don't stop, edit, or reread. If stuck, write "I don't know
   what to write" until something comes.
3. Don't aim for insight; it tends to surface on its own in the middle of
   rambling.
Best for: mental clutter, low-grade anxiety, creative block.

## 2. Prompted reflection (directed insight)
Choose one or two prompts per session. Useful sets:
- **Daily check-in**: What did I feel most strongly today, and when? What drained
  me? What gave me energy? What am I avoiding?
- **After a hard moment**: What happened (facts)? What did I tell myself it meant?
  What did I feel and do? What would I do differently? What do I need now?
- **Values and direction**: When did I feel most like myself this week? What
  would I regret not doing this year?
- **Gratitude / positive**: three specific good things and why they happened.
  Some studies show small mood benefits, though effects are modest.
Answer in full sentences; vague prompts yield vague answers, so add "specifically…".

## 3. Mood journaling (data over time)
A quick structured line daily, even on days you don't write more:
`date | mood 1–10 | energy 1–10 | sleep hrs | main emotion | one-line note`
Optional: exercise, alcohol, social contact, menstrual cycle, work stress. A
spreadsheet makes trends visible as a simple chart.

## 4. Mining past entries (where insight lives)
Once a month, reread the month in one sitting:
1. Highlight recurring words, people, situations and worries.
2. Look at the mood line: what do low days have in common? High days?
3. Ask: what did I worry about that never happened? What problems keep
   reappearing unsolved?
4. Write a short "month in review": three patterns noticed, one change to try.
Quarterly, reread the monthly reviews — longer arcs appear.

## 5. Make it stick
- Anchor to an existing habit (with morning coffee, before bed).
- Minimum viable entry: the mood line plus one sentence. Missing days is normal;
  just resume.

## Gotchas
- **Rumination trap**: writing the same grievance over and over can deepen it.
  If an entry loops, end with "What's one thing I can do, or let go of?" and move
  on. Shift from "why do I feel this way?" to "what's going on and what helps?"
- **Venting only** without reflection tends to give relief at best. Add at least
  one reflective question.
- **Privacy**: assume a journal could be read. Store it securely (a lock,
  encrypted app, password), and think about what you'd be comfortable with. For
  very sensitive writing, you can write and then destroy the pages.
- **Performative writing** — writing as if for an audience — blocks honesty.
  It's for you only.
- **Warning signs**: if entries show weeks of persistently low mood, hopelessness,
  or thoughts of self-harm, that's a reason to talk to a doctor or counsellor,
  not just to keep writing. In immediate danger, contact emergency services or a
  crisis line.

## Verify
- [ ] Entries are dated and in one place.
- [ ] You've tried both freewriting and at least one prompt set.
- [ ] A daily mood line exists for at least two weeks.
- [ ] You've done a monthly reread and written three patterns and one change.
- [ ] Your journal is stored somewhere you're comfortable with privacy-wise.
