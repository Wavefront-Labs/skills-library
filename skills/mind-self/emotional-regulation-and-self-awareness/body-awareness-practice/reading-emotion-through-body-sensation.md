# Reading emotion through body sensation

**Goal:** notice emotions earlier by noticing the body — the tight throat before
the tears, the hot neck before the outburst — and treat those sensations as data
rather than as something to ignore or obey.

## Why the body
Emotions show up physically: heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, gut feelings,
temperature. Often the body changes before you have words for the feeling.
*Interoception* is the sense of these internal states. People vary widely in how
well they detect them, and the skill appears trainable to some degree. *(Evidence
that interoceptive training improves emotional regulation is growing but still
early; the practices are low-risk for most people.)*

## 1. Interoception drills (5 minutes, daily)
- **Heartbeat sensing**: sit still, without touching your pulse, and try to feel
  your heartbeat — chest, neck, fingertips. Count beats for 30 seconds, then check
  with your fingers on the wrist. The aim is noticing, not accuracy scores.
- **Breath tracking**: notice where breath is felt most — nostrils, chest, belly —
  and its temperature, depth and pace, without changing it.
- **Short body scan**: move attention slowly from feet to head, about 10–20
  seconds per area, noting any sensation: warmth, pressure, tingling, tightness,
  or nothing. "Nothing noticeable" is a valid observation.

## 2. Sensation mapping
Over a couple of weeks, build your personal map. When you notice an emotion, ask:
- **Where** is it? (throat, chest, stomach, shoulders, jaw, face, hands)
- **What quality?** (tight, heavy, hollow, buzzing, hot, cold, fluttering, pressing)
- **How big, and moving or still?**
Record it beside the emotion label. A research study asking people to colour
where they felt emotions found broadly shared patterns — anger and fear in the
upper body and arms, sadness as reduced activity in the limbs, happiness spread
across the whole body — but your own map is what matters.

Example personal map:
| Emotion | Sensation |
|---|---|
| Anxiety | Fluttering stomach, shallow chest breathing |
| Anger | Hot neck and face, clenched jaw and fists |
| Sadness | Heavy chest, lump in throat |
| Shame | Face hot, urge to look down, shrinking posture |

## 3. Tension inventory (60 seconds, several times a day)
Check the usual holding spots: **forehead, jaw, tongue, shoulders, hands, belly,
glutes**. Rate overall tension 0–10. Soften what you can on an out-breath. Tie
it to a regular cue — every time you sit down at your desk, every red light,
every time you wash your hands.

## 4. Use sensation as an early signal
Once you know your map, a sensation can prompt a response before the emotion
peaks:
- Jaw clenching in a meeting → "I'm getting irritated" → slow exhale, unclench,
  choose words.
- Stomach fluttering on Sunday evening → "anxiety about the week" → write the
  worries down and plan Monday's first task.
This links directly to trigger mapping
(mind-self.emotional-regulation-and-self-awareness.trigger-mapping) and emotion
labelling.

## 5. Approach, don't analyse
When an uncomfortable sensation is there, try simply staying with it for three
breaths with curiosity — size, edges, whether it shifts — instead of
immediately fixing or explaining it. Sensations often change or ease when
observed rather than fought.

## Gotchas
- **Not every sensation is an emotion.** Hunger, caffeine, illness, pain and
  hormones produce similar signals. Ask "anything physical going on?" first.
- **Health anxiety**: some people who already worry about their health find
  intense body-monitoring increases worry. Keep scans brief and neutral, and
  focus on muscles and breath rather than heart or symptoms.
- **Trauma**: turning towards the body can feel unsafe or bring up distress for
  some trauma survivors. Keep eyes open, focus on neutral areas (hands, feet),
  stop any time, and consider a trauma-informed therapist.
- **New or unexplained physical symptoms** (chest pain, breathlessness, numbness)
  need medical assessment, not interpretation as emotion.

## Verify
- [ ] You've done the interoception drills on most days for two weeks.
- [ ] You have a personal map of at least four emotions as sensations.
- [ ] You run a tension inventory tied to a daily cue.
- [ ] You've caught at least one emotion early from a body signal and responded.
- [ ] You check for physical causes before reading a sensation as emotion.
