# Anchoring and framing an offer

**Goal:** set the reference points a negotiation or proposal is judged against — and
stop other people's reference points from quietly setting yours.

## Anchoring: the first number pulls everything
People adjust insufficiently from a starting number, even an arbitrary one (Tversky &
Kahneman, 1974). In negotiations, the first offer correlates strongly with the final
price (Galinsky & Mussweiler, 2001).

### When to go first
- **Go first when you know the market well.** Your anchor then frames the range.
- **Let them go first when you're unsure of value** — their number gives you information,
  and you avoid anchoring yourself too low (or high).

### How to set the anchor
1. Research the realistic range (comparables, salary data, quotes).
2. Anchor at the **ambitious end of what you can justify** — aggressive but defensible.
   An absurd anchor can end the conversation or damage trust.
3. **Give a precise figure** ("£47,800" rather than "about £50k"); precise anchors are
   often seen as better-researched and draw smaller counter-moves (supported in several
   studies; effect size modest).
4. **Attach the reason** immediately: "Based on three comparable sales at …".
5. Leave room to concede — plan your concession steps, shrinking each time.

### When someone anchors you
- **Don't counter right next to their number** — that accepts their frame.
- **Name it and move on:** "That's well outside what the market supports. Let's look at
  comparables." Then put your own well-justified anchor on the table.
- **Focus on your own numbers:** your walk-away point and target, written down before the
  talk (Galinsky & Mussweiler found thinking about your own alternatives or the other
  side's limits reduced the anchor's pull).
- If it's wildly off, it's fine to pause: "We're far apart; is there flexibility, or
  should we stop here?"

## Framing: same facts, different choice
- **Loss vs gain:** people respond more strongly to avoiding losses than achieving
  equivalent gains (loss aversion). "You'll lose £300 a year by not insulating" tends to
  motivate more than "save £300 a year" — though the effect varies, and loss frames can
  feel manipulative if overplayed.
- **Itemise vs bundle:** bundle costs into one figure ("one payment of £400"), itemise
  benefits — each separately appreciated.
- **Concession framing:** present your move as a loss to you ("To make this work I'd give
  up X"), which makes it feel more valuable to them.
- **Shared-problem framing:** "How do we make this fit your budget?" invites joint
  problem-solving (see
  `mind-self.persuasion-negotiation-and-deception-detection.principled-negotiation`).

## Contrast and decoys
- **Contrast:** a request seems smaller after a larger one; show the premium option first.
- **Decoy (asymmetric dominance):** a third option clearly worse than one target nudges
  choice toward that target (Huber et al., 1982). Effect is real in lab settings but
  less reliable in the field.

## Reframing what you've been framed into
Ask: what's the reference point here, who chose it, and what would the deal look like
from a different one? ("Compared with *what*?") Re-state the proposal in your own terms
before deciding.

## Gotchas
- Anchoring yourself: browsing high or low listings before negotiating shifts *your* sense of value.
- Extreme anchors with no justification read as bad faith in relationship-dependent deals.
- Frames that mislead (hiding fees, fake "was" prices) can be illegal under consumer law.
