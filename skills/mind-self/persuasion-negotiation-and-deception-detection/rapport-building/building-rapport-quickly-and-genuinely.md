# Building rapport quickly and genuinely

**Goal:** within a few minutes, help another person feel comfortable, understood and
willing to talk — at a party, an interview, a sales call, a negotiation, a doctor's
appointment — without manipulation.

Tickle-Degnen & Rosenthal describe rapport as three things together: **mutual
attention**, **positivity** and **coordination** (being in sync). Work on all three.

## 1. Open warmly and simply
- Eye contact, a genuine smile, their name if you have it (and use it once more later, not
  constantly).
- Start with something situational and shared: "How do you know the host?", "What
  brought you to this course?", "That was a long queue — have you been waiting long?"
- Offer a small piece of yourself early; disclosure invites disclosure.

## 2. Show real interest: ask follow-up questions
Huang et al. (2017) found people who asked more questions — particularly **follow-up
questions** that build on what the other person just said — were liked more.
- Open questions: "What's that like?", "How did you get into it?", "What's the hardest part?"
- Follow-ups: pick a detail they mentioned and go one level deeper.
- Let them finish; resist topping their story with yours.

## 3. Listen visibly
- Paraphrase occasionally: "So it was the commute more than the job itself?"
- Name the feeling you hear, tentatively: "That sounds frustrating."
- Small acknowledgements and nods; phone away and out of sight.

## 4. Find genuine common ground
Shared experiences, places, interests, problems or values. Similarity raises liking — but
only if it's real. Inventing common ground is easily exposed and destroys trust.

## 5. Match lightly
People naturally mimic each other's posture and speech when in sync (the "chameleon
effect"; Chartrand & Bargh found being mimicked increased liking). Use it gently:
- **Pace and volume:** slow down for a slow talker, bring energy for an energetic one.
- **Formality:** mirror their register — first names vs titles, jargon vs plain words.
- **Posture:** roughly similar openness; never copy gestures instantly or exactly.
Deliberate, obvious mirroring reads as mockery or a sales trick and lowers trust;
some later studies also find the liking effect smaller and context-dependent.

## 6. Calibrate to context
- Professional settings: rapport through competence, reliability and respect for their time.
- Cultural norms differ on eye contact, touch, personal space and how fast you reach
  first names. When unsure, follow their lead and err formal.
- Some people (busy, introverted, distressed) want efficiency, not chat — giving them that
  *is* rapport.

## 7. Close and follow up
End warmly, refer back to something they said ("Good luck with the move"), and follow up
on it next time. Remembering details is a strong signal of genuine interest.

## Gotchas
- **Techniques without sincerity** are noticeable. If you're not interested, find
  something you are interested in about them.
- **Interrogation:** questions with no self-disclosure feel like an interview.
- **Over-agreement** to build rapport can commit you to things in a negotiation — rapport
  is about the person, not conceding the point.
- Using rapport to exploit someone is manipulation; this skill is for genuine connection
  and fair dealing.

## Verify
- [ ] You asked at least two follow-up questions built on their answers.
- [ ] They spoke more than you did.
- [ ] You can recall three specifics they told you.
- [ ] Any common ground you mentioned was true.
- [ ] Your pace and formality roughly matched theirs, and nobody noticed you doing it.
