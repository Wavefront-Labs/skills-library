# Using and resisting influence principles

**Goal:** make honest requests more persuasive, and recognise when someone is using the
same levers to rush you into a yes you'll regret.

Cialdini's principles are shortcuts people use to decide quickly. They usually work
because they're usually *reasonable* — which is why they can be exploited.

## The principles — ethical use and defence
| Principle | Ethical use | Exploitative version | Defence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** — we repay what we receive | Give real value first: help, information, a concession | The "free" gift that obliges a purchase | Accept gifts as gifts; if it was a sales tool, you owe nothing |
| **Commitment & consistency** — we act in line with what we've said/done | Ask for a small, voluntary, ideally written commitment | Foot-in-the-door escalation; "you agreed earlier, so…" | "Knowing what I know now, would I agree again?" |
| **Social proof** — we copy similar others | Share true numbers: "Most of your neighbours already…" | Fake reviews, staged queues, "everyone's buying" | Ask: are these people like me, and is this real? |
| **Liking** — we say yes to people we like | Be genuinely friendly; find real common ground | Flattery and engineered similarity | Separate the person from the deal: "Would I take this offer from a stranger?" |
| **Authority** — we defer to experts | State real credentials and evidence | Uniforms, titles and jargon with no substance | Is this person truly expert *on this*, and are they honest here? |
| **Scarcity** — we want what's rare or ending | Tell the truth about real limits and deadlines | "Only 2 left!", countdown timers that reset | Would I want it at the same price with no deadline? |
| **Unity** — we favour "one of us" | Highlight shared identity that's real | Fake tribal belonging ("as a fellow parent…") | Does the shared identity change the merits? |

## Making an honest request more persuasive
1. **Lead with reasons.** Even a brief "because …" raises compliance; give the real one.
2. **Start small:** ask for a small first step the person genuinely agrees with.
3. **Show relevant proof:** what similar people did, with real numbers.
4. **Establish competence early** — and admit a weakness; it increases credibility.
5. **Be clear about real costs of delay**, without inventing urgency.
6. **Pre-suasion:** what people are attending to just before the request shapes the answer
   (Cialdini, 2016) — e.g. open by discussing the shared goal before the ask. Some of the
   priming studies cited in that book have since failed to replicate; treat specific
   effects with caution.

## Resisting: a quick routine
When you feel sudden pressure to decide:
1. **Notice the feeling** — rushed, obliged, flattered, anxious to miss out.
2. **Name the lever** (scarcity? reciprocity?).
3. **Buy time:** "I don't decide on the spot; I'll get back to you tomorrow." Honest
   offers survive a day.
4. **Evaluate on merits alone**: price, need, alternatives.

## Gotchas
- **Overuse backfires.** Heavy-handed scarcity or flattery triggers suspicion
  ("persuasion knowledge").
- Effect sizes vary widely by context and culture; lab results don't always hold in the field.
- **Scams** stack several levers at once (authority + scarcity + secrecy). Stacked pressure
  to pay or share details *now* is a red flag regardless of how legitimate it looks.
- "Ethical" means the other person would still agree if they saw exactly what you did and why.

## Verify
- [ ] Every claim you use (numbers, deadlines, credentials) is true.
- [ ] Your request includes a genuine reason.
- [ ] You can name the principle at work in the last sales pitch you received.
- [ ] You have a stock phrase for buying time, and you use it.
- [ ] You'd be comfortable if the other person read your plan for persuading them.
