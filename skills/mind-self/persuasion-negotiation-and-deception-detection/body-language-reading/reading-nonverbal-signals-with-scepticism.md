# Reading nonverbal signals with scepticism

**Goal:** pick up useful cues about comfort, engagement and emotional shifts in a
conversation — while avoiding the overconfident "crossed arms means lying" readings that
popular books encourage.

## The ground rules
1. **No single gesture means one thing.** Crossed arms can mean cold, comfortable, tired
   or defensive. Treat any one cue as a question, not an answer.
2. **Baseline first.** Notice how this person normally sits, speaks, gestures, blinks
   and fidgets in relaxed moments before the topic that matters.
3. **Look for changes from baseline** that coincide with a specific topic or question.
4. **Look for clusters:** several signals shifting together (voice tightens, posture
   withdraws, hands go to the face) mean more than one.
5. **Context explains a lot:** cold room, uncomfortable chair, culture, neurodivergence,
   anxiety about the setting rather than the topic.
6. **Confirm verbally:** "I sense some hesitation — what's on your mind?"

## A practical read: comfort vs discomfort
Navarro's framing is more reliable in practice than trying to read specific emotions.
- **Comfort / engagement:** relaxed face, open torso orientation toward you, leaning in,
  feet pointing toward you, synchrony in posture and pace, natural gestures.
- **Discomfort / withdrawal:** turning or leaning away, feet pointed toward the exit,
  barriers (bag, cup held in front), self-touch (neck, face, rubbing hands — "pacifying"),
  tight lips, reduced gesturing, sudden stillness.
Discomfort tells you *something* about this moment is uncomfortable. It does not tell you
*why* — it might be the topic, you, the price, or something unrelated.

## Faces and emotion
Ekman's work on basic facial expressions is influential, but a major 2019 review
(Barrett et al.) found that people do not reliably make the same face for the same
emotion across contexts and cultures, and that inferring emotion from facial movement
alone is error-prone. Brief "micro-expressions" exist but there's little evidence that
training people to spot them improves real-world judgement. Use faces as one input among
many.

## Your own nonverbal output
- **Open, oriented posture,** hands visible, steady but not staring eye contact.
- **Match their pace and energy** lightly (see
  `mind-self.persuasion-negotiation-and-deception-detection.rapport-building`).
- **Watch your own tells** under pressure — touching the face, rapid speech, rising
  pitch. Slow down, breathe low, pause before answering.
- **"Power posing"** (standing expansively for two minutes) may make you *feel* more
  confident, but the claimed hormonal and risk-taking effects did not replicate (Jonas et
  al., 2017). Don't rely on it.

## Cultural and individual variation
Eye-contact norms, comfortable personal distance, touch, gesture meanings and emotional
expressiveness vary widely across cultures and individuals. Autistic people, people with
anxiety and people with some medical conditions may show "discomfort" signals at
baseline. Baselining matters even more here.

## Gotchas
- **Confirmation bias:** if you already suspect something, you'll see "evidence"
  everywhere. Write down what you observed, not your interpretation.
- **Overconfidence** grows faster than accuracy with this skill.
- Reading body language is a prompt to ask better questions, not a lie detector (see
  `mind-self.persuasion-negotiation-and-deception-detection.deception-detection`).

## Verify
- [ ] You noted the person's baseline before the important part of the conversation.
- [ ] Any reading you made was based on a cluster of changes tied to a moment, not one gesture.
- [ ] You checked your read with a question rather than acting on it.
- [ ] You can name one cultural or individual factor that could explain what you saw.
- [ ] You noticed — and managed — at least one of your own tells.
