# Judging truthfulness with strategic questioning

**Goal:** make a better-than-chance judgement about whether an account is truthful — in
hiring, a landlord or business dispute, a workplace investigation, everyday life — by
focusing on verifiable content and well-designed questions, not nervous body language.

## Start with the error rates
A meta-analysis of 206 studies (Bond & DePaulo, 2006) found people judge truth vs lie
correctly about **54%** of the time — barely above a coin toss — and professionals
(police, judges) do little better. We also show a **truth bias** (we assume honesty) and
are overconfident. Gaze aversion, fidgeting and nervousness are **not** reliable lie
cues; truthful people under suspicion show them too.

So: never conclude "lying" from demeanour. The methods below raise accuracy modestly in
research settings (often into the 60–70% range); they don't make you a lie detector.

## 1. Get a free narrative first
Ask an open question ("Tell me everything that happened from when you arrived") and don't
interrupt. Truthful accounts, on average, contain more spontaneous detail — sensory,
spatial, temporal — and more unprompted corrections. Liars tend to keep stories simple.
Evidence for content cues (CBCA, reality monitoring) is moderate and noisy.

## 2. Ask for verifiable details (Verifiability Approach)
Tell the person you may check details, then note how many **checkable** specifics they give:
named people who can confirm, receipts, timestamps, phone records, CCTV-covered places.
Truth-tellers tend to give more verifiable detail; liars tend to give vague or
unverifiable detail ("I was walking around"). Then **actually check** a few.

## 3. Strategic use of evidence (SUE)
If you hold evidence (a document, a witness, a record):
1. **Don't reveal it early.** Ask open, then increasingly specific questions about the
   topics the evidence covers.
2. Note whether their account **contradicts or avoids** what you know.
3. Reveal evidence late and gradually, and observe how they adapt.
Liars often avoid or deny facts they don't know you have; truthful people usually mention
them freely.

## 4. Raise cognitive load with unexpected questions
Lying is mentally demanding. Questions that weren't rehearsed help:
- Spatial: "Sketch the layout of the room and where everyone was."
- Process: "How did you plan the trip — who booked what?"
- Reverse order: "Tell it again starting from the end." (Use with care — it also
  stresses honest people with poor memory.)
Look for content that becomes vague, inconsistent with earlier answers, or contradicts
verifiable facts — not for nervousness.

## 5. Compare against a baseline — of content
Ask about neutral events in similar depth first. If their neutral account is rich and the
key account is thin, that's a difference worth probing (but not proof).

## Gotchas
- **Memory is fallible.** Inconsistency in minor details is normal in honest accounts;
  perfectly consistent rehearsed stories can be lies.
- **Stress, culture, language, neurodivergence and trauma** all alter how people tell
  stories. Second-language speakers and trauma survivors often give fragmented accounts.
- **Accusatory interrogation** raises false confessions and false judgements. Keep
  questions open and non-accusatory.
- **Polygraphs and "micro-expression" reading** have weak validity; don't rely on them.
- The goal is to decide what to *verify*, not to declare someone a liar.

## Verify
- [ ] You got an uninterrupted free narrative before specific questions.
- [ ] You listed which details are verifiable and checked at least some.
- [ ] Any evidence you held was introduced late, and you noted consistency with it.
- [ ] Your conclusion rests on content and checked facts, not nervous behaviour.
- [ ] You stated your judgement with appropriate uncertainty and what would change it.
