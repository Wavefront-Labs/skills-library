# Preparing and running an interest-based negotiation

**Goal:** reach an agreement that meets both sides' real needs better than haggling over
fixed demands — or know clearly when to walk away. Works for pay, contracts, family
arrangements, neighbour disputes, purchases.

## The four principles (Fisher & Ury)
1. **Separate the people from the problem.** Be soft on people, hard on the problem.
2. **Focus on interests, not positions.** A position is what they ask for; an interest is
   why they want it.
3. **Invent options for mutual gain** before deciding.
4. **Insist on objective criteria** — fair standards both can accept.
Plus: **know your BATNA** (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement).

## Preparation sheet (fill in before you talk)
| Item | You | Them (your best guess) |
|---|---|---|
| Position (what's asked) | | |
| Interests (why) — list several, ranked | | |
| BATNA (what happens if no deal) | | |
| Walk-away point (worst deal you'd accept) | | |
| Target | | |
| Objective criteria available | market rates, precedent, expert valuation, policy | |
| Options that could serve both | | |
Then: **improve your BATNA** before the meeting (get another quote, another offer) — it
is your real source of power. And estimate theirs: what happens to them without you?

The overlap between your walk-away and theirs is the **ZOPA** (zone of possible
agreement). No overlap → no deal is better than a bad one.

## Running it
1. **Open on the shared problem:** "We both want this settled by spring at a price that's
   fair. Can we start with what matters most to each of us?"
2. **Ask about interests:** "Help me understand why that date matters." "What would
   happen if…?" Listen and summarise until they agree you've got it.
3. **Share your interests** (not your walk-away).
4. **Brainstorm options without committing:** "What if…?" Separate inventing from
   deciding.
5. **Trade on differences** in priority: give on what's cheap for you and valuable to
   them; ask in return for what's valuable to you and cheap for them (logrolling). E.g.
   lower salary rise but extra leave and remote days; lower price but faster payment.
6. **Use criteria to settle numbers:** "Let's use the three most recent comparable sales."
   When they push, ask "What's the principle behind that figure?"
7. **Write it down** — terms, who does what, by when — before leaving.

## When they play hard
- **Positional pushing:** don't attack; restate their position as an interest and ask why.
- **Threats or ultimatums:** don't react in kind; name it calmly and return to criteria —
  or invoke your BATNA if needed.
- **Dirty tricks** (fake authority limits, last-minute demands): name the tactic and
  negotiate about how you'll negotiate.

## Gotchas
- **Purely distributive deals** (one-off price, nothing else to trade) still involve
  anchoring and concessions; principled methods help but don't remove them (see
  `mind-self.persuasion-negotiation-and-deception-detection.framing-and-anchoring`).
- **Revealing your walk-away** gives away the ZOPA. Share interests, not limits.
- **Splitting the difference** feels fair but rewards whoever opened most extremely.
- **Overestimating your BATNA** is common; check it's real and available.
- Critics note Getting to Yes underplays power and emotion; pair it with the emotional
  skills in `mind-self.persuasion-negotiation-and-deception-detection.tactical-empathy`.

## Verify
- [ ] Your prep sheet was complete before the meeting, with a realistic BATNA.
- [ ] You can state at least two of their interests in your own words — and they agreed.
- [ ] At least two options were generated before any was chosen.
- [ ] Final numbers trace to an objective criterion.
- [ ] The agreed terms are written down, with owners and dates.
- [ ] The deal beats your BATNA — or you walked away.
