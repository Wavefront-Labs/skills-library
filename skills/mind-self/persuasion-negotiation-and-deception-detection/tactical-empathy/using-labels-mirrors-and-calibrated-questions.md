# Using labels, mirrors and calibrated questions

**Goal:** lower the emotional temperature, get the other side talking and revealing
what really matters, and steer toward a solution without direct confrontation. Drawn
from crisis-negotiation practice as popularised by Chris Voss; useful in salary talks,
customer disputes, family conflicts and sales.

**Evidence note:** these techniques are grounded in active-listening practice and some
affect-labelling research (naming emotions reduces their intensity — Lieberman et al.,
2007), but most specific claims (exact phrasings, voice tones) come from practitioner
experience rather than controlled trials in business negotiation.

## 1. Labels — name their emotion or position
Format: **"It seems like…" / "It sounds like…" / "It looks like…"** then pause.
- "It sounds like the delay has caused real problems with your own customers."
- "It seems like you're worried this will set a precedent."
Use "it seems", not "I think you feel" — it's less presumptuous and easier for them to
correct. Then **stay silent** and let them respond. A wrong label still works: they
correct you and reveal more.

## 2. Mirrors — repeat their last few words
Repeat 1–3 key words they just said, with a slight upward inflection, then wait.
- Them: "We just can't go beyond this budget this quarter." You: "This quarter?"
- They usually elaborate, often revealing timing, constraints or the real interest.
Use sparingly; three mirrors in a row feels like a parrot.

## 3. Calibrated questions — "how" and "what"
Open questions that make them solve your problem:
- "How am I supposed to do that?" (a polite no that invites them to rethink)
- "What's the biggest challenge you face on this?"
- "What would need to be true for this to work for you?"
- "How would you like me to proceed?"
Avoid "why" — it often sounds accusatory. Avoid yes/no questions when you need information.

## 4. Accusation audit
Before a hard conversation, list every negative thing they might think about you or your
proposal, then say the worst ones first: "You're probably going to think this is
unreasonable, and that I haven't considered your side." Naming it tends to defuse it.
Don't overdo it into grovelling.

## 5. Silence and tone
- After a label, a question or an offer, **stop talking**. Count to five in your head.
- Calm, slow, downward-inflected voice for firm points; warm and lighter tone for most
  of the conversation.

## 6. Aim for "That's right"
Summarise their view — interests, feelings, constraints — until they say **"That's
right."** That signals they feel understood. "You're right" often just means "please stop
talking". Only after "that's right" move to solutions.

## Sample flow
1. Accusation audit.
2. Calibrated question to open ("What's driving the timeline on your side?").
3. Mirrors and labels as they talk.
4. Summary → "That's right."
5. Calibrated questions to shape the solution ("How can we make the phased option work?").
6. Confirm implementation: "What happens if we hit a problem in month two?"

## Gotchas
- **Empathy is not agreement.** Labelling their frustration doesn't concede the point.
- **Scripted delivery** is obvious; practise in low-stakes conversations first.
- **Cultural fit:** directness of labels and comfort with silence vary across cultures.
- These tools gather information; you still need to know your own alternatives (see
  `mind-self.persuasion-negotiation-and-deception-detection.principled-negotiation`).
- Using them to manipulate vulnerable people is exploitation; use them to understand.

## Verify
- [ ] You opened with a question or accusation audit, not your demand.
- [ ] You used at least two labels and paused after each.
- [ ] You heard "that's right" (or equivalent) before proposing solutions.
- [ ] You learned at least one interest or constraint you didn't know beforehand.
- [ ] The other side talked more than you.
