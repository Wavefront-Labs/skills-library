# Deciding fast with good-enough heuristics

**Goal:** spend decision effort where it matters and decide everything else quickly and
well enough — avoiding both rash big decisions and hours lost choosing a toaster.

## 1. Triage the decision
Ask three questions:
- **Reversible?** Can you undo it cheaply (return the item, change the plan next week)?
  Jeff Bezos's "two-way door" vs "one-way door" framing captures this.
- **Stakes?** What's the realistic cost of a mediocre choice vs the best one?
- **Frequency?** One-off, or something you'll decide again and again?
| Decision type | Approach |
|---|---|
| Reversible, low stakes | Decide in minutes; good enough is fine |
| Irreversible or high stakes | Slow down; use full analysis (see `mind-self.decision-making-and-problem-solving.decision-matrices`) |
| Frequent | Make a **rule** once, then stop deciding |

## 2. Satisfice — with a written bar
Herbert Simon's satisficing: set your criteria for "good enough", then take the **first
option that meets them**. Example: "A laptop under £800, 16 GB RAM, reviews ≥ 4.3 from
two sources, available this week." Stop at the first match.
Schwartz et al. (2002) found people who habitually "maximise" (seek the best) often get
objectively slightly better outcomes but report less satisfaction and more regret.

## 3. Fast-and-frugal heuristics that work in the right environment
Gigerenzer's research shows simple rules can match or beat complex models when data are
noisy and limited — but each fits particular conditions.
- **Recognition heuristic:** if you recognise one option and not the other, infer the
  recognised one scores higher — works only when recognition correlates with the
  quality you care about (e.g. city size), not when it reflects advertising.
- **Take-the-best / one-reason:** rank cues by how predictive they are; decide on the
  first cue that discriminates between options. Good when one factor dominates.
- **Tallying:** count pros for each option without weighting; surprisingly robust when
  you don't know the right weights.
- **Defaults and rules of thumb:** "I always take the mid-priced wine", "invest in a
  low-cost index fund", "if in doubt, sleep on it".
- **Optimal-stopping rule of thumb (the "37% rule"):** when options come in sequence and
  you can't go back, look at roughly the first third without committing, then take the
  next one better than all of those. It's a mathematical result under strict assumptions
  (known number of options, no recall) — treat it as a rough guide for real life.

## 4. Set decision deadlines and timeboxes
- Give each decision a deadline proportional to its stakes ("I'll choose the holiday by
  Sunday").
- Timebox research: "30 minutes, then decide".
- When stuck between two close options, they're close in value: pick one (flip a coin
  and notice whether you feel relief or disappointment — that reaction is information).

## 5. Make recurring decisions once
Meal plans, a capsule wardrobe, a standard gift, rules for which meetings you accept. Each
removes a whole category of small decisions.

## Gotchas
- **Heuristics fail outside their environment.** Recognition misleads where marketing
  drives familiarity; gut feelings are reliable only where you've had lots of practice
  with fast feedback (Kahneman & Klein's conditions for intuitive expertise).
- **Misclassifying a one-way door** as reversible — job moves, large debts, medical
  decisions and relationships often aren't as reversible as they look.
- **"Decision fatigue"** as a depleting resource is contested (ego-depletion studies
  replicate poorly); the practical point — fewer trivial decisions — still holds.

## Verify
- [ ] Each pending decision is tagged reversible/irreversible and high/low stakes.
- [ ] Low-stakes decisions have a written good-enough bar and a deadline.
- [ ] You've turned at least one recurring decision into a rule.
- [ ] The heuristic you used fits its environment (you can say why).
- [ ] High-stakes, irreversible decisions got slower analysis, not a shortcut.
