# Resting in choiceless awareness (open monitoring)

**Goal:** let attention rest on whatever arises — sounds, sensations, thoughts,
moods — without selecting an object, while staying aware that you are aware.
Research literature contrasts this "open monitoring" with "focused attention"
(e.g. breath meditation); in practice most people need some focused-attention
stability first. Educational guidance only.

## 1. Prerequisite
You should be able to stay with a breath anchor for a few minutes at a time with
gentle returns (see
mind-self.mindfulness-and-contemplative-practice.breath-focused-meditation.building-a-stable-breath-meditation-practice).
Without that, "open awareness" usually means "daydreaming with eyes closed".

## 2. A staged session (20–30 min)
1. **Settle (5–10 min):** breath anchor as usual.
2. **Widen to the body (3–5 min):** let the breath become one sensation among the
   whole body's sensations.
3. **Add sounds (3–5 min):** let sounds come and go at the edge of the field without
   naming their sources.
4. **Open the field (remainder):** allow anything — sensation, sound, thought, emotion,
   image — to be the object of the moment. Don't pursue, don't push away. Whatever is
   most vivid is simply known, then the next thing.
5. **Close:** return briefly to the breath before ending.

## 3. Meta-awareness: the real skill
The difference between open monitoring and mind-wandering is whether you *know*
what's happening as it happens. Useful checks:
- Every so often ask silently: "What's here now?" or "Is awareness present?"
- Notice the *kind* of experience rather than its content: "thinking", "hearing",
  "planning", "feeling restless".
- A thought known *as a thought* is part of the practice. A thought you are lost in
  for three minutes is absorption.

## 4. Handling absorption in thought
When you discover you've been lost:
1. Recognise it ("ah, lost in planning").
2. Notice the *flavour* of what pulled you in — that's information about your habits.
3. Re-open to the field rather than snapping back to the breath.
4. If you've been lost repeatedly in the last few minutes, go back to the breath anchor
   for a minute or two to restabilise, then open again. Treat the anchor as home base,
   not as failure.

## 5. Balancing openness and stability
| Symptom | Too… | Adjust |
|---|---|---|
| Constantly drifting, foggy | Open / loose | Shorten open phase; re-anchor more often; eyes open |
| Tense, scanning for "the next thing" | Tight / effortful | Relax effort; let objects come to you; widen gaze |
| Dull, blank, sleepy | Sinking | Sit straighter; include sounds and light; shorter sits |
| Jumping between objects rapidly | Grasping | Let each object complete before the next; slow down |

## Gotchas
- "Choiceless" doesn't mean passive; you're still choosing not to choose. Light, steady
  intention keeps it from becoming zoning out.
- Labelling every event can turn into noting practice; that's a different (also fine)
  technique — see mind-self.mindfulness-and-contemplative-practice.noting-practice.labelling-experience-with-noting.
- Opening the field can let difficult material surface more easily than focused practice.
  If you hit persistent distress, intrusive memories or a sense of unreality, return to a
  concrete anchor, shorten sessions and seek a qualified teacher or clinician.
- Uncertainty: session staging is a common teaching pattern; traditions (e.g. Zen
  shikantaza, Tibetan rigpa-oriented practice) frame this differently and some skip the
  staged approach entirely.

## Verify
- [ ] You can move from a breath anchor to an open field and back on purpose.
- [ ] During a sit you caught at least one thought *as a thought* without following it.
- [ ] When lost, you re-opened rather than berating yourself, and re-anchored if needed.
- [ ] You can name your most common pull (planning, replaying, fantasising) from this week.
- [ ] You can tell from the table whether your last sit was too loose or too tight.
