# Metta phrases and widening circles

**Goal:** deliberately cultivate goodwill — toward yourself, people you love, strangers
and people you find difficult — by repeating simple wishes with attention. Drawn from
the Buddhist *brahmavihara* practices and taught widely in secular form. Educational
self-help guidance.

## 1. Write your phrases (once, then keep them)
Three or four short wishes, in words that feel natural to you. Classic forms:
- *May you be safe.*
- *May you be healthy.*
- *May you be happy* (or *at peace*).
- *May you live with ease.*
Rules of thumb: short, general, sincere, wishes not demands ("may you be free from
suffering", not "stop being angry"). If "happy" feels false, use "may you be as
well as you can be". Once chosen, don't rewrite them every session — familiarity helps.

## 2. The circles (a 20–30 minute session)
Spend a few minutes on each, repeating the phrases slowly with the person in mind
(an image, their name, or a felt sense of them):
1. **Benefactor** — someone whose kindness to you is uncomplicated (a mentor, a
   grandparent). Easiest place to find the feeling; many teachers start here.
2. **Self** — traditional start point, but many people find it hardest; it's fine to
   place it second.
3. **Good friend** — avoid romantic partners early on (feelings get tangled).
4. **Neutral person** — someone you see but don't know (a shop assistant).
5. **Difficult person** — start with *mildly* irritating, not your worst conflict.
6. **All beings** — widen outward: everyone in this building, this city, everywhere.
Beginners can do just two or three circles per session.

## 3. What to expect, and what to do
- **Flatness / nothing:** the most common experience. The intention and repetition are
  the practice; warm feelings are a bonus. Keep going.
- **Resistance to self-metta** ("I don't deserve this"): notice it kindly, then
  continue, or imagine a benefactor wishing these things for you.
- **Grief or tears:** can arise when kindness touches old hurt. Pause, rest with the
  breath, carry on if it feels okay.
- **Difficult person brings anger:** step back to the neutral person or benefactor,
  return later. Wishing someone well isn't excusing their behaviour.
- **Mechanical recitation:** slow down; let each phrase land with a breath.

## 4. Variants (the other brahmaviharas)
| Practice | Toward | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| Compassion (karuna) | someone suffering | *May you be free from suffering.* |
| Appreciative joy (mudita) | someone doing well | *May your good fortune continue.* |
| Equanimity (upekkha) | anyone, esp. when you can't help | *Your happiness depends on your actions, not my wishes.* |
Equanimity practice balances compassion when caring turns into overwhelm.

## 5. In daily life
Silently offer a phrase to strangers you pass, the person in a slow queue, or a
colleague before a hard conversation. Thirty seconds counts.

## Gotchas
- Metta is not positive thinking or suppressing anger; you can feel irritation and
  still wish someone well.
- Don't start with someone who has abused or traumatised you. Practising toward a
  perpetrator is not required, and doing so before you're ready can be re-wounding;
  that work, if wanted, is better done with a therapist.
- Compassion toward others' suffering without equanimity can lead to empathic distress;
  rotate in equanimity phrases.
- Uncertainty: the order of circles varies by teacher; the one here is a common beginner
  adaptation, not the canonical sequence.

## Verify
- [ ] You have 3–4 fixed phrases you can say without notes.
- [ ] You completed at least three circles in one session, including the neutral person.
- [ ] You continued through at least one flat or resistant patch without stopping.
- [ ] You tried the equanimity variant at least once.
- [ ] You offered a phrase silently to someone in daily life today.
