# Practising the body scan

**Goal:** move attention systematically through the body, registering whatever
sensation is there (or isn't) without trying to change it. The body scan is a core
exercise in mindfulness-based programmes such as MBSR; this is self-help guidance,
not therapy.

## 1. Set up
- **Lying form (classic):** on your back on a mat or bed, arms by your sides, legs
  uncrossed, a pillow under the knees if the lower back complains. 20–45 minutes.
- **Seated form:** upright in a chair; shorter (5–15 min); better if you fall asleep lying down.
- Warm room or a blanket: attention to the body makes you notice cold.
- Beginners usually do best with a recorded guided scan for the first couple of weeks,
  then go unguided.

## 2. Scan order and pace
A common sequence (either direction works; pick one and keep it):
1. Two or three breaths feeling the whole body's contact with the floor or chair.
2. **Toes of left foot** → sole → heel → top of foot → ankle → lower leg → knee → thigh.
3. Same for the right leg.
4. Pelvis and hips → lower back → belly → upper back → chest.
5. Fingers of both hands → palms → wrists → forearms → upper arms → shoulders.
6. Neck → jaw → mouth → nose → eyes → forehead → scalp.
7. Finish with the whole body at once for a minute or two.

Pace: roughly 30–60 seconds per region in a 30-minute scan. Faster sweeps (top to bottom
in 1–2 minutes) are useful as a mini-practice during the day.

**Resolution:** start coarse ("the left foot"), and as stability grows, narrow to
finer areas ("the pad of the big toe"). Fine resolution trains precision; coarse
resolution is more restful. Both are legitimate.

## 3. What you're doing in each region
- Notice what is actually present: temperature, pressure, tingling, pulsing, itch,
  contact with fabric, tightness, ease.
- **Numbness or "nothing" is a valid finding.** Note "no clear sensation here" and move
  on — don't hunt or invent sensation.
- Don't try to relax the area. Relaxation often happens, but making it the goal turns
  the scan into a performance you can fail.
- When the mind wanders, notice where it went, return to the region you were on.

## 4. Working with discomfort
- Approach gently: notice the edges and texture of the sensation (sharp, dull, throbbing,
  spreading), rather than the story about it.
- You can "breathe into" an area — imagine the breath travelling there — as a way to
  stay with it without bracing.
- If discomfort is intense, move to a neutral region (hands, feet) and come back
  briefly, or change posture. You are never required to sit with pain.
- Persistent or new pain is a medical question, not a meditation one.

## 5. The sleep question
Many people use the body scan at bedtime and fall asleep. That's fine as a bedtime
routine, but it isn't the training itself. If you want the awareness benefits, do at
least some scans sitting up or earlier in the day, and let bedtime scans be a bonus.

## Gotchas
- **Trauma and body memories:** attending closely to the body can bring up distress,
  dissociation or panic for some people, particularly those with trauma histories. Keep
  eyes open, use shorter scans, skip areas that feel unsafe, and work with a
  trauma-informed clinician if this happens regularly.
- Body scans can heighten awareness of symptoms in health anxiety; if that spirals,
  pair the practice with professional support rather than pushing through.
- Uncertainty: timings per region are conventions from guided recordings, not fixed rules.

## Verify
- [ ] You completed one full pass in a consistent order and finished with a whole-body sense.
- [ ] You noted at least one area with no clear sensation without straining to find one.
- [ ] You met one uncomfortable sensation by describing its qualities, then moved on.
- [ ] You can do a 2-minute quick scan on demand during the day.
- [ ] At least some of your scans this week were done awake and upright.
