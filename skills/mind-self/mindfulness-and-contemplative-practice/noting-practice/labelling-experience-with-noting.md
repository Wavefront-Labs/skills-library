# Labelling experience with noting

**Goal:** sharpen recognition of experience as it happens, and loosen identification
with it, by giving each moment a short, soft mental label. Noting is associated with
the Burmese Mahasi tradition and with modern freeform systems (e.g. "see, hear, feel").
Educational guidance, not therapy.

## 1. The label rules
- **Short:** one word, ideally one syllable or two. "Hearing", "thinking", "itching".
- **Soft:** about 5% of your attention on the label, 95% on the experience. The label
  is a pointer, not a commentary.
- **Present:** note what is happening now, not what just happened or why.
- **Generic, not content:** "planning", not "planning tomorrow's meeting with Sam".
- **Silent:** said in the mind (aloud is possible in a private room as a training aid
  when you're very scattered).

## 2. Mahasi-derived structure
1. **Primary object:** the abdomen. Note "rising" as it expands, "falling" as it contracts.
2. **Secondary objects:** when something else becomes more prominent, note it —
   "hearing", "thinking", "pain", "tingling", "wanting", "doubting" — until it fades or
   loses its pull, then return to "rising, falling".
3. **Intentions:** before moving, note "intending"; then "lifting", "moving", "placing".
4. Sessions alternate sitting and walking (see
   mind-self.mindfulness-and-contemplative-practice.walking-meditation.walking-meditation-formal-and-natural-pace).

## 3. Freeform style (a common modern variant)
Use a small set of category labels for whatever is most noticeable:
- **See** — visual input or mental images.
- **Hear** — sounds or mental talk.
- **Feel** — body sensations or emotional body feelings.
Some systems split each into *outer* and *inner* (e.g. "hear-out" for traffic,
"hear-in" for inner talk). A small, fixed vocabulary is easier to use quickly.

## 4. Speed and granularity
- **Beginner pace:** about one note per second or two. Slow enough to be accurate.
- **Faster:** as recognition improves, noting can speed up to several per second; the
  label may shrink to a flick of attention. Don't force speed — accuracy first.
- **Granularity:** start with broad categories; later you may notice components
  ("pressure", "heat", "tight" instead of "pain"). Finer isn't automatically better;
  match it to what's clearly perceptible.

## 5. When to drop the labels
Labels are scaffolding. Drop them (and just know experience directly) when:
- The labels become mechanical and you're noting words, not experience.
- Mind is very settled and labelling feels like clutter.
Pick them back up when you notice you're drifting.

## 6. Noting in daily life
- Brief noting during routine tasks: "walking", "reaching", "tasting", "irritation".
- Note emotions as they appear ("annoyed", "anxious") — naming an emotion often reduces
  its grip somewhat (research on "affect labelling" supports a modest effect).
- One to three minutes of noting while waiting (queue, kettle) is plenty.

## Gotchas
- Labelling the *story* instead of the *process* ("he's so rude" vs "anger") keeps you
  inside the content.
- Over-effortful noting causes tension and headaches; reduce speed and volume.
- Intensive noting retreats can produce strong perceptual and emotional effects
  (vibrations, fear, disorientation, insomnia). This is documented in the contemplative
  literature; on a retreat, do it with an experienced teacher who checks in regularly, and
  seek clinical support if effects persist after practice.
- Uncertainty: vocabulary and speeds differ across teachers; the "see/hear/feel" scheme
  here is a general description, not one teacher's exact system.

## Verify
- [ ] Your labels are one or two words and name process, not content.
- [ ] Attention stays mostly on experience; the label is faint.
- [ ] You can run 10 minutes of rising/falling with secondary-object notes.
- [ ] You know when you dropped the labels and why.
- [ ] You noted at least one emotion by name during ordinary activity today.
