# Inducing trance in a consenting partner

**Goal:** guide another adult who has asked for it into a relaxed, absorbed state and
back out cleanly, with their consent and control intact throughout. This is the craft
of the induction, practised between peers — not therapy, not stage work, and not a
substitute for a trained hypnotherapist or clinician.

## Before anything: consent and screening
- **Ask, explain, agree.** Say what you'll do, how long, and that they can open their
  eyes and stop at any moment — hypnosis doesn't remove that ability. Agree the
  single, benign purpose (relaxation, a calm-rehearsal image) in advance and stick to it.
- **Don't proceed** with anyone intoxicated, under 18, with a history of psychosis,
  dissociation, severe trauma or epilepsy, or who seems pressured into it. Refer those
  to a professional.
- **No surprise content.** Never introduce suggestions they didn't agree to, and never
  explore memories or "regression" — that's clinical territory with real risk of
  distress and false memory.

## The sequence
1. **Pre-talk (3–5 min).** Correct myths: they won't be unconscious, won't do anything
   against their values, will remember most of it. Fear is the biggest blocker; a calm,
   accurate pre-talk does most of the work.
2. **Suggestibility check (optional).** Hands-clasped or "heavy-and-light arms"
   (one hand holding an imagined heavy bucket, the other tied to balloons). Response
   tells you how literal and how imaginative to be — not whether they "can" be hypnotised.
3. **Induction.**
   - *Eye fixation:* gaze at a point above eye level; you suggest heaviness in the lids,
     timing "heavier" to their natural blinks. When lids flutter, invite them to close.
   - *Progressive relaxation:* slow body scan, releasing each part on the out-breath.
     Kinder for anxious or first-time subjects.
   - *Confusion methods* (overloading with counting tasks or contradictory directions)
     work fast on analytical people but can unsettle novices; leave them until you're
     fluent with the first two.
4. **Pacing and leading.** Describe 2–3 things that are verifiably true ("you can feel
   the chair under you, hear my voice, notice your breathing"), then one lead ("and you
   may begin to feel more settled"). Match your speech to their out-breath. Use
   permissive words — *may, might, perhaps, you can notice* — which give the subject
   nothing to resist.
5. **Deepen.** Countdown 10→1 or staircase imagery, on the exhale.
6. **Utilise.** Deliver only the agreed content: a safe-place image, a calm rehearsal.
7. **Emerge.** Count 1→5 with clear energy rising in your voice; finish with "eyes open,
   fully alert". Remove any temporary suggestion explicitly ("arms are normal and light
   again"). Chat for a couple of minutes before they stand or drive.

## Reading depth (gently)
Signs of absorption: slowed breathing, facial muscles smoothing, reduced swallowing,
eyelid flutter, delayed response. Eye-catalepsy tests ("try to open them and find they
stay closed") are traditional but can make a subject feel tricked or anxious; prefer
just observing. Depth beyond light-to-medium isn't needed for anything agreed above.

## When something goes wrong
- **Strong emotion (abreaction):** stay calm, slow voice, reassure, offer the safe-place
  image, then emerge slowly. Stay with them afterwards. If distress persists, suggest
  they speak to a mental-health professional.
- **They don't emerge promptly:** usually deep relaxation or sleep. Repeat the count-up
  more firmly, say their name, touch the shoulder lightly (agreed beforehand). It resolves.
- **Headache or grogginess afterwards:** often from an incomplete emergence — re-induce
  lightly and emerge again, more slowly.

## Gotchas
- Monotone isn't the goal; slow, warm and rhythmic is.
- Don't test or show off. Stage-style demonstrations on friends erode trust fast.
- Legal and professional rules on practising hypnosis vary by country and state;
