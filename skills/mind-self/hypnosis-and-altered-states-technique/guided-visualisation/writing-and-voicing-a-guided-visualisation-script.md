# Writing and voicing a guided visualisation script

**Goal:** produce a 10–15 minute script — for yourself or a consenting listener — that
reliably carries attention into a vivid imagined scene and back, for calm, mental
rehearsal or reflection.

## The arc (time budget for 12 minutes)
1. **Settle (2 min):** posture, three slow breaths, permission to let the eyes close.
2. **Transition (1–2 min):** a threshold — a path, a door, steps down. Count down if
   you like; it signals "entering".
3. **Scene (6–7 min):** the body of the journey, built in layers (below).
4. **Purpose beat (1–2 min):** the one thing the script is for — resting in the safe place,
   rehearsing the presentation going well, meeting a "wise future self" for a question.
5. **Return (1–2 min):** retrace the threshold, count up, re-orient to the room — sounds,
   weight on the chair, wiggle fingers, open eyes.

Never skip the return. Scripts that end in the scene leave listeners foggy.

## Sensory layering
Add one sense at a time, giving each a pause to form: *see* (light, colour, distance),
*hear* (near and far sounds), *feel* (temperature, texture underfoot, air on skin),
*smell*, then *taste* if it fits. Lead with whichever sense is strongest for the listener;
some people barely "see" images and do better with sound or body sensation — say so in
the script: "you might see it, or simply know it's there".

## Language rules
- **Permissive:** "you may notice", "perhaps", "whatever comes is fine". Imposing details
  ("the sand is white") jars anyone who imagined it otherwise.
- **Open, then specific:** "a place where you feel safe… notice what's beneath your feet".
- **Present tense, second person** for others; first person for self-scripts.
- **Avoid triggering imagery for a general audience:** deep water, enclosed spaces,
  heights, people, and childhood homes all carry risk. Offer a choice instead.
- **Mental rehearsal scripts** should be first-person perspective, real-time speed, and
  include the actual setting, the sensations of nerves *and* handling them — rehearsing
  a fantasy-perfect version transfers less well. *(Draft: summarised from sport-imagery
  guidance, not checked against primary studies.)*

## Pacing and voice
- Mark pauses in the script: `[5s]`, `[15s]`. The commonest fault is rushing: ~100–120
  words per minute with pauses means a 12-minute script is only ~900–1100 words.
- Slow down and drop the pitch slightly through the transition; lift energy gently on the
  return count.
- Record in a soft-furnished room, mic 15–20 cm away slightly off-axis to avoid breath
  pops. Keep background music very low or absent — it fights the voice for attention.
- Listen back with eyes closed before using it. Any line you stumble on, rewrite.

## Gotchas
- **Falling asleep** is fine for a sleep script, a failure for rehearsal — use an upright
  posture and a shorter scene for rehearsal.
- **Unexpected distress:** a calm scene can surface grief or memories. Build an exit line
  in: "you can open your eyes at any point and return to the room." If listeners have
  trauma histories, a clinician-guided approach is more appropriate.
- **Too many details** crowd out the listener's own imagery; three or four anchors per
  sense is plenty.

## Verify
- [ ] Script follows settle → threshold → scene → purpose → return.
- [ ] Each sense gets its own beat with a marked pause.
- [ ] Language is permissive and avoids high-risk imagery or offers choice.
- [ ] Read-aloud time matches the target length at an unhurried pace.
- [ ] The return re-orients to the real room and ends with eyes open, alert.
