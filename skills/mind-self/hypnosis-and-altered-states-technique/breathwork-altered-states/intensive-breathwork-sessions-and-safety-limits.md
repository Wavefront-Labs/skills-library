# Intensive breathwork sessions and their safety limits

**Goal:** practise consciousness-shifting breathing (fast rounds with retentions, or
continuous connected breathing) knowing what it does to the body and where the hard
lines are. Everyday calming breath (slow exhale, box breathing) belongs in stress-coping
skills, not here.

## What's actually happening
Breathing faster and deeper than you need blows off carbon dioxide. Low CO2
(**hypocapnia**) makes blood more alkaline and narrows brain blood vessels. Typical
effects: light-headedness, tingling in hands and lips, a buzzing body, sometimes cramping
of the hands into "claws" (**carpopedal spasm / tetany**), emotion, and vivid imagery.
Those effects are the altered state — and also the reason for the rules below. Oxygen
saturation barely rises; the urge to breathe simply arrives later because CO2 is low.
That delay is why breath holds after hyperventilation can end in sudden, warning-free
loss of consciousness.

## The non-negotiable rules
1. **Never in or near water** — bath, pool, sea, shower. Hyperventilation before a breath
   hold is the classic cause of **shallow-water blackout** deaths.
2. **Never while driving, standing, or anywhere a faint means a fall.** Sit or lie down
   on the floor.
3. **Never forced retentions.** Breathe when the body asks.
4. **Stop** for chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat, severe headache, or panic that
   doesn't ease after a few normal breaths.

## Who shouldn't do it (without medical clearance)
Pregnancy; epilepsy or a history of seizures; heart disease, high blood pressure, or a
history of stroke or aneurysm; glaucoma or retinal detachment; recent surgery; severe
asthma; a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe trauma (sessions can surface
overwhelming material). When in doubt, ask a doctor first.

## Round structure (fast-breathing with retention)
A commonly described pattern, done lying down:
1. 30–40 full, relaxed breaths — in fully through nose or mouth, let go without forcing out.
2. After the last exhale, hold *empty* only as long as comfortable.
3. When the urge comes, take one full breath in and hold 10–15 seconds, then release.
4. Repeat for **3 rounds** to start; rest with normal breathing after.
Keep a timer and note retention times, but treat them as a curiosity, not a score.

## Connected-breathing session (holotropic-style)
Continuous breathing with no pause between in and out, often with music, for 30 minutes
to well over an hour. This format, especially the longer versions, should only be done
in a group with a **trained facilitator** and a **sober sitter** paired to each breather.
Sitters keep the person safe, offer water and tissues, and support but don't interpret.
Hand cramping eases by slowing the breath.

## Integration
Allow 15–30 minutes afterwards before driving. Drink water, eat something, journal.
Strong emotions or memories that keep returning over days are a cue to talk with a
mental-health professional rather than to breathe harder next time.

## Gotchas
- More rounds or harder breathing is not "better" — it's more hypocapnia.
- Tingling and cramps are expected physiology, not "energy blockages"; respond by
  slowing down.
- Claims (immune boosting, disease cure) go well beyond the evidence; some small studies
