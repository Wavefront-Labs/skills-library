# Breaking an unwanted habit

**Goal:** reduce or end an automatic behaviour you don't want — nail-biting, compulsive
phone checking, evening snacking, procrastination rituals — by working on its cues and
payoff rather than fighting it with willpower alone.

**Scope note:** for substance dependence, gambling, self-harm or compulsions that cause
serious distress, this is not a substitute for professional help or a mutual-aid
programme. Use it alongside that support.

## 1. Map the loop (1–2 weeks)
Each time the habit happens (or you notice the urge), log:
- **Cue:** time, place, emotional state, who was around, what happened just before.
- **Routine:** what you actually did, and for how long.
- **Reward:** what it gave you — relief from boredom, a break, comfort, stimulation,
  avoiding a hard task.
Patterns emerge quickly: "always 3–4 pm, at my desk, stuck on a task" is a very
different problem from "late at night, alone, stressed". See
`mind-self.habit-and-self-management.self-monitoring.tracking-your-own-behaviour`.

## 2. Keep the reward, change the routine
Habits persist because they pay off. Find a **replacement** that meets the same need:
- Boredom/break → stand, stretch, walk to fill a water glass.
- Stress relief → three slow breaths, a short walk, squeezing a stress ball.
- Avoidance of a hard task → break the task into a 2-minute first step.
Test a couple of candidates; keep the one that actually satisfies.

## 3. Competing response (habit reversal)
For body-focused habits (nail-biting, hair-pulling, skin-picking), habit reversal training
has good evidence:
1. **Awareness training** — notice the earliest warning (hand moving to face).
2. **Competing response** — an action physically incompatible with the habit, held about
   a minute: clench fists lightly, sit on hands, hold a pen.
3. **Social support** — someone who gently points it out and praises progress.

## 4. Change the environment
Remove or block the cue where possible (see
`mind-self.habit-and-self-management.environment-and-temptation-design.designing-your-environment-against-temptation`):
gloves or plasters for nail-biting, phone in another room, no snacks in the house.

## 5. Urge surfing
When the urge comes, don't fight or obey it — observe it. Notice where you feel it in the
body, rate it 0–10, breathe, and watch it rise, peak and fall like a wave. Urges are
time-limited; each one you ride out without acting weakens the link. This comes from
mindfulness-based relapse prevention; evidence is moderate.

## 6. Plan for lapses
- **A lapse** (one slip) is not **a relapse** (return to the old pattern). Decide in
  advance what you'll do after a slip: note what triggered it, restart immediately, no
  "making up" or punishment.
- The **abstinence-violation effect** — "I've ruined it, so why bother" — turns lapses
  into relapses. Name it when you feel it.
- Identify high-risk situations (holidays, stress peaks, certain company) and prepare
  specific plans for them.

## Gotchas
- **Suppression backfires:** trying not to think about it can increase the thoughts.
  Redirect attention to the replacement instead.
- **Cutting several habits at once** usually fails; pick one.
- **Habits rarely vanish**; old cues can reactivate them years later. Expect occasional
  urges and keep the replacement handy.
- Popular "21 days to break a habit" claims have no solid basis; plan for months.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a log showing the habit's top 2–3 cues and the reward it provides.
- [ ] A replacement routine that meets the same need is in use.
- [ ] At least one cue is blocked or removed.
- [ ] You have a written lapse plan.
- [ ] Logged frequency over 4–8 weeks is clearly down.
