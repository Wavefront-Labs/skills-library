# Writing if-then plans

**Goal:** close the gap between intending and doing by deciding in advance exactly
*when*, *where* and *how* you'll act, so the situation itself triggers the behaviour.

## The format
> **If** [specific situation], **then I will** [specific action].

A goal says *what* ("exercise more"). An implementation intention links it to a cue:
"If it is 7:00 on Monday, Wednesday or Friday, then I will put on my running shoes at
the front door." Gollwitzer & Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis (94 studies) found a
medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d ≈ 0.65) compared with goal intentions
alone. Effect sizes in more recent, larger studies are often smaller — expect a useful
nudge, not a guarantee.

## 1. Write a starting plan
- **Situation:** time, place, or a preceding event you'll reliably notice. "When I get
  back from lunch" beats "in the afternoon".
- **Action:** the first physical step, not the whole task. "Open the report file and write
  the first heading", not "work on the report".
- Say or write the plan once or twice, and picture yourself in the situation doing it.

## 2. Write obstacle plans
List the 2–3 things most likely to derail you, and give each an if-then:
- If I feel too tired to run, then I will walk for 10 minutes instead.
- If a colleague asks for "a quick chat" during my focus block, then I will say "Can we do
  3 pm?"
- If I'm offered dessert at a work lunch, then I will order a coffee.
Obstacle plans tend to be the most valuable part.

## 3. WOOP (mental contrasting first)
Oettingen's WOOP sequence pairs a wish with a reality check before planning:
1. **Wish** — a goal that is challenging but feasible.
2. **Outcome** — the best result; imagine it vividly for a moment.
3. **Obstacle** — the main *inner* obstacle (a habit, emotion, belief) that gets in the way.
4. **Plan** — "If [obstacle], then I will [action]."
Pure positive visualisation without the obstacle step can reduce effort; the contrast is
what energises.

## 4. Coping plans for lapses
Plan the response to failure in advance: "If I miss a day, then the next morning I do the
tiny version and don't try to make up the missed session." This prevents one slip
becoming "I've blown it" (the abstinence-violation effect).

## Gotchas
- **Vague cues don't fire.** "If I have time" or "when I feel ready" are not situations.
- **Too many plans** dilute each other; 1–3 per goal is plenty.
- Plans work best when you actually **want** the goal. They don't create motivation, they
  channel it.
- Rigid plans can make you miss better opportunities; review them weekly and rewrite.
- For deeply ingrained habits, pair if-then plans with environment changes (see
  `mind-self.habit-and-self-management.environment-and-temptation-design`).

## Worked example
Goal: reply to email less compulsively.
- Start: If I sit down at my desk in the morning, then I will open my task list, not my inbox.
- Obstacle: If I feel the urge to check email during a focus block, then I will write the
  thought on a sticky note and keep working.
- Lapse: If I find myself in my inbox mid-block, then I will close it and restart the
  timer, no self-criticism.

## Verify
- [ ] Each plan has a concrete, noticeable situation and a first physical action.
- [ ] You have at least one plan for your most likely obstacle.
- [ ] You have a lapse plan.
- [ ] You can recite the plans without looking.
- [ ] After two weeks, you can point to times the cue fired and you acted.
