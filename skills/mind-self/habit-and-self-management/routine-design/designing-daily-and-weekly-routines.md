# Designing daily and weekly routines

**Goal:** routines that put your best hours on what matters and make the rest happen with
little thought — and that you can repair when life knocks them over.

## 1. Map your energy (one week)
For 5–7 days, note every couple of hours: energy (1–5), focus (1–5), and what you were
doing. Look for your peak focus window (for many people mid-morning; for "night owls"
later), your slump (often early afternoon), and your wind-down. Chronotype is partly
biological (Roenneberg's work); work with it where your obligations allow.

## 2. Assign work to energy
- **Peak:** the hardest thinking work — writing, studying, design, problem solving.
  Protect it as a calendar block.
- **Medium:** meetings, calls, collaborative work.
- **Low:** admin, email, chores, errands, light reading.
Don't spend peak hours on email by default.

## 3. Build the morning sequence
Keep it short (15–45 minutes), ordered, and anchored to waking. Example:
1. Water, light (open curtains or step outside).
2. Movement — 5–10 minutes.
3. Look at today's plan; pick the top 1–3 tasks.
4. Start the first top task before checking messages.
Long celebrity routines (journaling + meditation + cold plunge + reading) collapse on
the first late night. Design a **minimum version** (e.g. water, plan, start) for bad days.

## 4. The shutdown ritual (end of work)
1. Capture loose ends into your task list.
2. Check tomorrow's calendar; pick tomorrow's top task.
3. Tidy the desk / close tabs.
4. A fixed closing phrase or action (Newport says "shutdown complete") so your brain
   stops rehearsing work. The value is the capture step; the phrase is optional.

## 5. The evening wind-down
Set a consistent bedtime alarm about 30–60 minutes before sleep: dim lights, screens down
or away from the bed, next-day prep (clothes, bag), something calming. A consistent wake
time is the strongest single lever for sleep regularity.

## 6. The weekly review (30–45 min, same slot each week)
- Clear inboxes and notes into the task list.
- Review last week: what got done, what slipped, why.
- Check the coming week's calendar; place focus blocks first, then everything else.
- Choose next week's 3 priorities.
- Ask: which part of my routine didn't happen, and what would make it happen? Adjust one thing.

## Repairing drift
Routines erode quietly. Signs: the minimum version has become the norm, focus blocks
fill with meetings, bedtime creeps later. Fix one link at a time, starting with the
anchor (wake time, first action of the day), not the whole system.

## Gotchas
- **Over-scheduling every minute** leaves no slack; keep 20–30% of the day unplanned.
- **Copying someone else's routine** ignores your energy, job and family realities.
- Routines must survive **travel, illness and weekends** — define a reduced version.
- Shift workers and carers can't use clock-based routines; anchor to events (after
  waking, after the shift) instead of times.
- Evidence for specific routine components varies; sleep regularity and protected focus
  time have the strongest support. Much of the rest is practitioner wisdom.

## Verify
- [ ] You know your peak and slump windows from tracking, not assumption.
- [ ] Your hardest task sits in your peak window at least 3 days a week.
- [ ] Morning and evening routines each have a written minimum version.
- [ ] You run a shutdown ritual and a weekly review on a fixed slot.
- [ ] Last month you noticed one drift and repaired it.
