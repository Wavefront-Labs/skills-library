# Tracking your own behaviour

**Goal:** use measurement to change behaviour — not to build a dashboard. Monitoring
progress reliably improves goal attainment (Harkin et al., 2016 meta-analysis), and
partly works simply because watching yourself changes what you do ("reactivity").

## 1. Choose what to track (fewer is better)
- **Track behaviours you control,** not outcomes: "walked 30 min" rather than "weight";
  "wrote 300 words" rather than "finished the book". Track outcomes too if useful, but
  less often (weekly weight, not daily).
- **One to three metrics.** Every extra metric lowers the odds you'll record any.
- **Binary is easiest** (did / didn't). Use counts or minutes only where the amount matters.
- Define it precisely in writing: "Exercise = ≥20 min, heart rate up. Walking the dog counts."

## 2. Record at the moment
Record immediately after the behaviour or at a fixed daily anchor (e.g. after dinner).
Recall-based tracking at the end of the week is much less accurate. Options:
- Paper grid or wall calendar (big Xs — visible, satisfying).
- A habit app or spreadsheet.
- A diary for complex behaviours (what, when, where, with whom, mood).
Make the tracker sit where the behaviour happens.

## 3. Use streaks — with a forgiveness rule
A streak ("don't break the chain") adds motivation. But a long streak broken once can
trigger giving up entirely. Rules that help:
- **Never miss twice:** one miss is fine; two in a row is the alarm.
- **Count the tiny version:** the minimum counts as a tick.
- Track **% of days this month** alongside the streak so one miss doesn't zero your progress.

## 4. Review weekly
Five minutes: look at the pattern, not the individual days. Which days fail? What do they
have in common (travel, late nights, certain people, weekends)? Write one change for next
week. Monitoring without review gives the reactivity effect but misses the learning.

## 5. Monitoring to break a habit
Record each occurrence plus its context (time, place, feeling, what happened just
before). After a week or two, the cues become obvious — that is the input for
`mind-self.habit-and-self-management.habit-breaking`.

## Gotchas
- **Goodhart's law:** once a measure becomes a target, people game it — steps from waving
  the phone, pages counted from skimming. Re-check that the metric still reflects the
  real goal.
- **Recording becomes the chore.** If tracking takes more than a minute a day, simplify.
- **Shame spirals.** For some people (notably around eating or weight) detailed tracking
  can worsen anxiety or disordered patterns; if tracking makes you feel worse week after
  week, stop or switch to a gentler metric and consider professional advice.
- Reactivity often fades as tracking becomes routine; the review step keeps it useful.
- Public reporting of progress tends to strengthen the effect (Harkin et al.), but
  social pressure can backfire for some — choose your audience.

## Verify
- [ ] You track no more than three behaviours, each with a written definition.
- [ ] Entries are made the same day, at a fixed anchor.
- [ ] Your streak rule tolerates a single miss.
- [ ] A weekly review has produced at least one concrete change.
- [ ] The tracked behaviour is trending the right way over 4+ weeks.
