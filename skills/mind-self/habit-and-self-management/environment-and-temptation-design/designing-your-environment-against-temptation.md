# Designing your environment against temptation

**Goal:** make the behaviour you want the easy default and the one you don't want awkward,
so you need less self-control in the moment. People who seem to have strong willpower
often just face fewer temptations (Wood, 2019, summarising self-control research).

## 1. Audit cues
Walk through your day and spaces and note what *prompts* the unwanted behaviour: the
biscuit tin on the counter, the phone on the pillow, the game icon on the home screen,
the route past the bakery. Note what's missing for the wanted behaviour: no fruit in sight,
guitar in its case in a cupboard.

## 2. Adjust friction (the "20-second" idea)
Small effort changes have outsized effects. Add steps to the bad habit; remove steps from
the good one. (The "20 seconds" figure comes from a self-experiment popularised by
Shawn Achor — the principle is well supported; the exact number isn't.)

| To do less | Add friction | To do more | Remove friction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snacking | Store out of sight, top shelf, don't buy | Eating fruit | Washed fruit in a bowl at eye level |
| Phone scrolling | Charge it in another room; log out; grayscale | Reading | Book on the pillow |
| Social media at work | Website blocker with schedule | Exercise | Clothes laid out; gym on the commute route |
| Online shopping | Delete saved cards | Practising guitar | Guitar on a stand, tuned |

## 3. Remove or replace cues
Out of sight really is more out of mind. Turn off non-essential notifications; move
tempting apps off the first screen or into a folder; unsubscribe from sale emails; change
the route. You can't always remove the cue — then pair it with an if-then plan (see
`mind-self.habit-and-self-management.implementation-intentions.writing-if-then-plans`).

## 4. Precommitment devices
Decide now, while calm, in a way that binds your future self (a "Ulysses contract"):
- **Deadlines** imposed in advance — Ariely & Wertenbroch found self-set spaced deadlines
  beat a single end deadline, though externally set ones did better still.
- **Money at stake:** commitment contracts that cost you if you fail (services such as
  stickK, or give a friend money to donate to a cause you dislike).
- **Locks:** time-locked phone boxes, app and site blockers that can't be disabled
  mid-session, a savings account with withdrawal limits.
- **Social:** tell someone who will ask.
Choose devices whose penalty is real but not ruinous.

## 5. Temptation bundling
Only allow a "want" while doing a "should": the gripping audiobook only at the gym,
the favourite coffee only during the admin hour. In Milkman et al. (2014) bundling raised
gym visits initially, though the effect faded over weeks — renew the bundle or rotate it.

## Gotchas
- **Shared spaces:** others' snacks and screens are cues too — negotiate a shared rule or a
  personal zone.
- **Friction migrates:** blocked on the laptop, you scroll on the phone. Block across devices.
- **Workarounds:** if disabling a blocker takes five seconds, it's a suggestion. Make
  removal costlier than the urge lasts (most urges peak and fade within minutes —
  a commonly cited figure, rough).
- Environment change works best for habits; deep emotional drivers (stress eating,
  loneliness scrolling) also need the underlying need addressed.

## Verify
- [ ] You've listed the three strongest cues for your unwanted behaviour and removed or blocked at least two.
- [ ] The wanted behaviour's first step is visible and needs no setup.
- [ ] At least one precommitment device is active, with a real cost.
- [ ] Blockers cover every device you use.
- [ ] After two weeks, the unwanted behaviour's frequency (tracked) has fallen.
