# Decluttering to a floor and holding it

**Goal:** reduce possessions to what you use and value, set an explicit "floor" for each
category, and adopt acquisition rules that keep you there. The discipline is mostly in
the second half — anyone can have one big clear-out.

## 1. Decide why, and the scope
Write a one-line reason (less time cleaning, a smaller home, less spending, less
distraction). It's what you'll reread when keeping something feels easier. Choose scope:
whole home over months, or one category at a time. Agree shared spaces with housemates
first — **other people's things are not yours to declutter.**

## 2. Declutter by category, not room
Gather everything in one category (all clothes, all books, all cables) in one place;
seeing the total is what makes the cuts obvious. A workable order, easiest to hardest:
clothes → books/media → papers → kitchen and tools → miscellaneous → sentimental items.

Keep tests (use one or two consistently):
- **Used in the last 12 months?** (seasonal and emergency items excepted.)
- **Would I buy it again today at full price?**
- **Is it the best one of its kind I own?** Duplicates go.
- **Joy/value test:** does it earn its space?
Make four piles: keep, donate/sell, recycle, bin. Get the outgoing piles out of the house
**within a week** — a donation bag in the hallway drifts back into cupboards.

**Uncertain items:** box them, date the box, and store it. If you haven't opened it in
3–6 months, donate it unopened. (Some people prefer a "packing party": box everything and
unpack only what you need over three weeks.)

## 3. Set floors
For each category, write the number or space limit you're holding: "one shelf of books",
"two coats", "a 33-item seasonal wardrobe" (the Project 333 rule, which counts clothes,
shoes, accessories and jewellery but not underwear or exercise wear). A visible container
limit — one drawer, one box — enforces itself better than a number.

## 4. Acquisition rules
- **One in, one out:** a new item means an old one leaves the same week.
- **Waiting period:** non-essential purchases go on a list for 30 days (or 24–72 hours
  for small ones); buy only if you still want it.
- **No-buy period:** a month or a year without buying in chosen categories, with written
  exceptions (replacements for broken essentials, gifts, consumables).
- **Gifts:** tell family your preferences (experiences, consumables); accept graciously,
  then pass on what you won't use without guilt.
- **Unsubscribe** from retail emails and remove saved card details — friction works.

## 5. Maintenance
A quarterly 30-minute "reset": walk the categories, check against the floors, bag the
excess. Track a no-buy in a simple log: date, item wanted, decision.

## Gotchas
- **Buying organisers before decluttering** — containers come last, if at all.
- **Discarding things you'll need to rebuy:** keep basic tools and emergency supplies.
- **Sentimental items first** stalls the whole project; leave them to the end, and keep a
  photo of what you let go.
- **Waste:** prefer resale, donation and recycling; landfill is the last resort.
- **Minimalism as performance or deprivation:** if letting go or keeping causes intense
  distress, or a home is unsafe from accumulation, that may be hoarding-related or
  anxiety-related — a GP or therapist can help, and forced clear-outs usually backfire.

## Verify
- [ ] Each category was gathered in one place and run through the same keep tests.
- [ ] Outgoing items left the house within a week.
- [ ] Floors are written per category, ideally as container limits.
- [ ] At least one acquisition rule is active and logged.
- [ ] A quarterly reset is in the calendar.
