# Wording, witnessing and repairing a personal vow

**Goal:** make a self-chosen vow (no alcohol for a year, no social media on Sundays, write
every morning) that you actually keep, and handle a break so the practice survives it.
Religious, marriage or legal vows have their own forms; this is the personal craft.

## 1. Word it as a bright line
A vow works when, at any moment, it's obvious whether you're inside or outside it.
- Vague: "I'll drink less." Bright: "I won't drink alcohol at home, Monday to Thursday."
- Vague: "I'll be kinder online." Bright: "I won't post a reply within an hour of reading
  something that angered me."
Check the wording against edge cases *before* vowing: Does alcohol-free beer count? A
work dinner on Wednesday? Write the answers as part of the vow. Most broken vows are
really loopholes discovered under pressure.

## 2. Set scope and duration
- **Fixed term** (40 days, a year, until a date) beats "forever" for a first vow. You can
  renew.
- State what it covers and what it doesn't. Keep **one vow** active at a time when starting.
- Prefer vows within your control: "I will submit three applications a week", not "I
  will get a job".

## 3. Choose witnesses and stakes
- **Witness:** one or two people who will ask about it kindly and won't police you. Say
  it aloud to them; spoken commitments are noticeably stickier than private ones.
- **Stakes (optional):** a pledge to a cause, a public post, a commitment-contract site.
  Make the stake meaningful but not ruinous — punishing stakes encourage hiding breaks.
- **A form of making it:** write it, sign and date it; some people mark it with a small
  ritual (lighting a candle, a walk to a meaningful place). Form helps it feel distinct
  from a passing resolution.

## 4. Keep it visible
Put the written vow where the temptation happens (inside the wallet, phone lock screen,
fridge). Track days on a calendar. Pre-decide responses to likely moments: "When offered a
drink, I say 'I'm doing a year off' and order soda water."

## 5. Renewal
At the end of the term, review: keep, adjust, or let it go deliberately. A renewal
ceremony — even just rewriting and re-signing with your witness present — re-commits
better than drifting on.

## 6. Repairing a broken vow
The danger isn't the break; it's the **abstinence-violation effect** — "I've blown it, so
it doesn't matter" — which turns one slip into a collapse.
1. **Name it promptly** to yourself and your witness. Hiding breeds a second break.
2. **Treat it as data:** what situation, feeling, time, person? Was the wording unclear?
3. **Repair concretely:** if the vow involved others (a promise to a partner), apologise
   and make amends; if a stake was agreed, pay it.
4. **Recommit** — same vow, or a reworded one closing the gap — from that day. Don't
   reset the term to punish yourself; extending by the lapsed period is a reasonable option.
5. **Two or more breaks in quick succession:** the vow may be mis-sized. Shrink it rather
   than abandon it.

If the vow is about alcohol, drugs, gambling or self-harm and you can't keep it, that's a
signal to seek support (a doctor, a recovery group, a counsellor), not a willpower
failing. Stopping heavy alcohol or some drug use abruptly can be medically dangerous —
get medical advice first.

## Verify
- [ ] The vow is a bright line with edge cases answered in writing.
- [ ] Fixed term, one active vow, within your control.
- [ ] A witness knows it; any stake is meaningful but not ruinous.
- [ ] You have a written repair plan for a break before you start.
