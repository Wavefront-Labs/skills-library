# Provocation and random entry — deliberately leaving the obvious track

**Goal:** when every idea you have looks like the last one, use a deliberate jolt to
reach a different starting point, then work back to something practical.

## Why it works
Thinking follows well-worn patterns: the first idea that "fits" blocks the others.
Lateral-thinking tools introduce an input that does *not* fit, forcing a new route.
The skill is in the second step — turning nonsense into a usable idea — not the jolt.

## 1. Provocation ("po")
State something deliberately unreasonable, marked with "po" (de Bono's word for
"this is a provocation, don't judge it"):
- **Escape:** pick something taken for granted and drop it. "Restaurants have menus."
  → *Po: the restaurant has no menu.*
- **Reversal:** flip the usual direction. *Po: customers pay the waiters, the restaurant
  pays the customers.*
- **Exaggeration:** push a quantity to an extreme. *Po: the meal takes three seconds.*
- **Distortion:** change the order or relationship. *Po: you pay before you know what you'll eat.*
- **Wishful thinking:** an impossible fantasy. *Po: the food cooks itself at the table.*

## 2. Movement: from provocation to idea
Do not ask "is this good?" Ask how to *move* from it:
- **Extract a principle:** "no menu" → the chef chooses → a fixed daily tasting option.
- **Focus on the difference:** what would be different, and is any difference useful?
- **Moment to moment:** imagine it happening step by step; note any useful moment.
- **Positive aspects:** what is good about it, even if the whole is absurd?
- **Circumstances:** under what conditions would this actually make sense?
Generate several ideas per provocation; most will be ordinary, a few new.

## 3. Random entry
1. Get a genuinely random word — open a dictionary at a random page, use a random-word
   generator, or pick from a fixed list with dice. Nouns work best. Don't choose; choosing
   lets your current pattern pick.
2. List 5–10 associations of the word (functions, properties, contexts).
3. Force a connection from each association to your problem.
Example: problem — getting teenagers to read more; random word — *traffic light*:
signals → a three-colour rating of how gripping each chapter is; queues → book "waiting
lists" that make titles scarce; timing → reading that unlocks at set times.

## 4. Concept fan (to escape from one-solution thinking)
Write the purpose on the right, then step back: what *concepts* serve it, and what broader
*directions* do those concepts serve? From each level, generate new specific ideas.
"Reduce traffic accidents" → directions (reduce speed, separate traffic) → concepts
(physical barriers, incentives) → ideas.

## 5. Challenge (why this way?)
Of any feature, ask "why is it done this way?" — not to criticise, but to see whether it
is still necessary. Often it is a leftover from old constraints.

## Gotchas
- **Judging the provocation** — the whole point is that it's bad. Judge only the ideas
  that come out of movement.
- **Cheating the random word** — rejecting "difficult" words defeats it. Hard words yield
  the most distant ideas.
- **Stopping at the first connection** — push for several per stimulus.
- **Using it when you need analysis**: lateral tools are for generating options, not for
  choosing between them.
- "Po" and some tool names are de Bono's terms; descriptions here are paraphrased.

## Verify
- [ ] Provocations were stated without judgement and explicitly marked.
- [ ] Each provocation produced at least three ideas through a named movement technique.
- [ ] The random stimulus was actually random, and you kept it.
- [ ] At least one resulting idea is something you would not have reached by direct thinking.
