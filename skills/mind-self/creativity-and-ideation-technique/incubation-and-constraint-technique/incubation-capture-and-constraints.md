# Incubation, capture and constraints — managing when ideas arrive

**Goal:** stop relying on luck for the "it came to me in the shower" idea. Prepare
deliberately, step away in the right way, make sure arriving ideas are not lost, and
use constraints when freedom is the problem.

## The four-stage model (Wallas, 1926)
**Preparation → Incubation → Illumination → Verification.** It's a simplification, but
the practical lesson holds: insight rarely comes without real preparation, and it still
has to be checked afterwards.

## 1. Prepare properly (load the problem)
Incubation only works on a problem you've genuinely struggled with.
- Work on it hard for a focused block (e.g. 30–90 minutes) until you're stuck, not just
  bored.
- Write the problem down in one sentence plus what you've tried and *why* each failed.
- Note the specific sticking point ("I can't make the ending both surprising and fair").

## 2. Incubate deliberately
- **Low-demand activity works better than a demanding one or doing nothing** — walking,
  showering, washing up, gardening, an easy drive. A meta-analysis of incubation studies
  (Sio and Ormerod, 2009) found an overall positive effect, larger for divergent tasks and
  with low-demand fillers; effect sizes were modest. Treat specifics as tentative.
- **Sleep on it** — return the next morning before re-reading your notes; see what
  you now think first.
- **Switch to a different problem** for a while (parallel projects).
- Avoid filling the break with the phone; heavy input competes with the background work.

## 3. Capture — the step most people skip
Ideas that arrive during incubation are fragile and vanish within minutes.
- Have a capture tool everywhere: pocket notebook, phone note or voice memo, a waterproof
  notepad in the shower (these exist), a pad by the bed.
- Capture the idea in enough words to reconstruct it later — not just "the bridge thing".
- Funnel everything into **one** inbox you review daily or weekly. Scattered notes are
  as good as lost.

## 4. Use constraints to force novelty
Too much freedom produces the usual idea. Add an arbitrary rule and the familiar route
is blocked:
- **Resource limits:** "Solve it with zero budget." "Only three colours."
- **Form limits:** "The pitch in six words." "No adjectives." "Story in 50 words."
- **Method limits:** "Write the ending first." "Use only materials in this room."
- **Oblique Strategies** (Brian Eno and Peter Schmidt's card deck of gnomic prompts,
  such as variations on "honour thy error as a hidden intention"): draw one at random when
  stuck and obey it for 20 minutes. Printed and online versions exist; check availability.
- The Oulipo writers' tradition (e.g. writing without a given letter) is the extreme form.

## 5. Verify
Morning-after ideas feel brilliant; many aren't. Test the idea against the original
problem statement and the reasons earlier attempts failed.

## Gotchas
- **Incubating an unloaded problem** — a vague worry doesn't incubate; a well-specified
  stuck point does.
- **Breaks that become procrastination**: set a return time.
- **Losing the idea** — the commonest failure; capture beats memory every time.
- **Constraint for its own sake** — drop the constraint once it has broken the pattern.

- [ ] The break was low-demand and had a set return time.
- [ ] A capture tool was within reach throughout.
- [ ] Captured ideas were transferred to one inbox and reviewed.
- [ ] At least one constraint was tried when free generation stalled.
- [ ] The resulting idea was checked against the original problem.
