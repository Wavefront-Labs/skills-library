# Explaining to understand

**Goal:** move from "I've read it" to "I can explain why it works", by forcing yourself
to generate explanations and finding exactly where they break.

## The Feynman loop
Named after the physicist's teaching style (the four-step recipe is a popular
reconstruction, not something he wrote down).
1. **Pick one concept** — narrow: "how a vaccine trains immunity", not "immunology".
2. **Explain it on paper or out loud to an imagined 12-year-old.** Plain words, no
   jargon you couldn't define, a concrete example.
3. **Mark where you stalled, hand-waved or reached for a term you can't unpack.**
   Those are the gaps — the whole point of the exercise.
4. **Go back to the source for just those gaps**, then explain again from the start.
5. Repeat until the explanation flows and includes *why*, not just *what*.

## Elaborative interrogation (why/how questions)
While reading, stop at each claim and ask: *Why is this true? How does it connect to what
I already know? What would happen if it weren't?* Answer before reading on. Works best
when you have some prior knowledge to connect to; with a totally new topic, answers can
be wild guesses — check them.

## Self-explaining worked examples
For maths, science, code or any procedure: cover the solution, then for each step say
*what was done and why this step and not another*. Students who self-explain learn more
from the same examples (Chi et al., 1989). Then do a near-identical problem unaided.

## Building analogies
- Map the structure, not the surface: "voltage is like water pressure" works because
  the relationships (pressure drives flow through resistance) line up.
- **State where the analogy breaks.** Every analogy fails somewhere; knowing where is
  part of understanding.
- Use two different analogies for hard concepts so neither misleads alone.

## Connect and contrast
- Draw a quick concept map: boxes for ideas, arrows labelled with the relationship.
- Contrast with the nearest neighbour: "How is mitosis different from meiosis — and why
  does that difference matter?"
- Generate your own example, especially one from your own life or work.

## Teach for real
Explaining to an actual listener adds pressure and questions you didn't anticipate.
Study partners, tutoring, writing a blog post or a short internal note all count. Even
explaining to a rubber duck surfaces gaps.

## Gotchas
- **Illusion of explanatory depth:** people rate their understanding of everyday things
  (a zip, a toilet flush) highly until asked to explain step by step. Assume your
  confidence is inflated until you've explained it.
- **Fluent ≠ correct.** A smooth explanation can still be wrong; check it against the
  source or an expert.
- Elaboration is slower than reading; budget for it on core concepts, not every detail.
- Dunlosky et al. rate elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as *moderate*
  utility — good evidence, but less general than retrieval practice and spacing. Pair
  them with those.

## Verify
- [ ] You can explain the concept without notes in plain language, with an example.
- [ ] Your explanation includes at least one *why*, not only a sequence of facts.
- [ ] You can name where your favourite analogy breaks.
- [ ] A listener's question didn't stop you — or you went back and fixed the gap.
- [ ] You solved a fresh problem using the concept, not just restated it.
