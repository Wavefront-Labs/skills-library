# Designing a deliberate practice session

**Goal:** get measurably better at a skill (an instrument, a sport, writing, coding,
public speaking) per hour invested, by practising specific weaknesses with feedback
rather than repeating what you can already do.

## What makes practice "deliberate"
From Ericsson's research on expert performers:
1. **A specific, defined goal** for the session ("clean shifts between 3rd and 5th
   position at 80 bpm"), not "practise violin".
2. **At the edge of current ability** — you fail a meaningful fraction of attempts.
3. **Immediate, informative feedback** — a teacher, a recording, a measurement.
4. **Full concentration** — usually sustainable for only 1–4 hours a day in total, in
   blocks of roughly an hour or less.
5. **Repetition with refinement** — adjust each attempt based on the last.

## 1. Find the weakness
- Record yourself performing the whole skill (video, audio, a timed run, a draft).
- Review it — ideally with a coach or someone better — and list specific breakdowns:
  "rushes the fill in bar 12", "drops the elbow on the backhand", "loses the thread
  when asked a question".
- Pick **one** to work on. Choose the one that costs you most or that limits others.

## 2. Design the drill
- **Isolate**: extract just the problem part (the two bars, the single shot, the opening
  paragraph).
- **Slow down or simplify** until you succeed roughly 60–80% of the time (a rule of
  thumb, not a measured optimum). Below that you're guessing; above it you're coasting.
- **Make feedback fast**: metronome, mirror, video replay, a checker, a timer, a scorer.
- **Define the success criterion** in advance: "5 clean in a row at 80 bpm, then go to 84".
- **Re-integrate**: after drilling, put the fixed part back into the whole performance.

## 3. Run the session
- Warm up (5–10 min).
- Drill block (20–40 min), resting briefly between sets.
- Integration (10 min) — use the fixed element in context.
- Note-taking (3 min): what improved, what still fails, next session's target.

## 4. Keep a practice log
Date, target, drill, settings (tempo, distance, difficulty), success rate, notes. The log
shows progress invisible day to day and stops you repeating last week's drill by habit.

## Breaking a plateau ("OK plateau")
When you stop improving, you've usually automated a "good enough" version. Push out of
automaticity: raise tempo or difficulty slightly beyond comfort, change conditions, get
outside feedback, or study a top performer's version and copy one feature.

## Gotchas
- **The 10,000-hour rule is a popularisation.** Ericsson objected to it; the hours varied
  widely and were an average, not a threshold. Meta-analysis (Macnamara et al., 2014)
  finds deliberate practice explains a substantial but far from total share of
  performance differences, varying by domain — more in games and music, less in
  professions. Practice matters; it is not the whole story.
- **Fatigue degrades quality.** Stop drilling when attempts get sloppy; tired reps can
  rehearse errors.
- **Mindless repetition of the whole piece** is the commonest waste of practice time.
- Without an external eye you can drill the wrong fix; get periodic expert feedback.
- Physical skills: respect injury limits — increase load gradually.

## Verify
- [ ] Each session has one written, specific target and success criterion.
- [ ] Your drill success rate sits roughly in the 60–80% band; adjust if not.
- [ ] You get feedback within seconds of each attempt.
- [ ] Your practice log shows measurable change on the target over 2–4 weeks.
- [ ] A fresh recording of the whole performance shows the fixed weakness held up in context.
