# Planning and monitoring your own learning

**Goal:** know at any point what you need to learn, what you actually know, and whether
your current method is working — then adjust. This is the plan → monitor → evaluate
loop of self-regulated learning.

## 1. Map the territory
- Collect the syllabus, exam specification, job requirements or course outline.
- Turn it into a **topic checklist** — one line per assessable item, as specific as
  "can derive X" or "can conjugate irregular past tense", not "Chapter 4".
- Rate each line 0–3 (0 = never seen, 3 = can do cold). This is your starting map.

## 2. Schedule backwards
1. Start from the deadline. Reserve the final 10–20% of the time for mixed practice
   tests only.
2. Divide the remaining time across topics weighted by importance × weakness.
3. Put each topic in the calendar **at least three times** spread out (first learn, review
   after a few days, review after a couple of weeks) — that builds in spacing.
4. Plan sessions of 25–50 min with a single stated outcome each ("can solve
   type-3 problems"), not a page range.
5. Leave ~20% slack. Plans without buffer collapse on the first bad week.

## 3. Monitor what you know — don't trust feelings
- **Judgements of learning are biased.** People overrate their knowledge immediately
  after studying, while it's fresh, and after re-reading. Make judgements **delayed**
  (a day later) and **from recall**, not after glancing at notes.
- **Cold test** each topic: answer questions without notes, then score yourself.
- **Confidence ratings:** before checking each answer, rate confidence (sure / unsure /
  guessing). Over time, compare: if your "sure" answers are wrong more than ~10% of
  the time, you're overconfident — study what you think you know too.
- Update the 0–3 topic map weekly from test results, not feelings.

## 4. Evaluate and switch strategy
At the end of each week ask:
- Which topics moved up the map? Which didn't despite time spent?
- For stuck topics: am I re-reading instead of testing? Do I lack a prerequisite? Do I
  need an explanation from a different source, or a person?
- Switch method on stuck topics rather than adding hours of the same method.
Common switches: re-reading → practice questions; notes → teach-back; blocked → mixed
problems; solo → study group or tutor.

## Weekly review template (15 min)
1. Hours planned vs done.
2. Topic map changes.
3. Worst miss of the week and its cause.
4. One strategy change for next week.
5. Next week's calendar, with buffer.

## Gotchas
- **Fluency illusion:** easy reading feels like learning; it isn't evidence of it.
- **Planning as procrastination:** a beautiful colour-coded timetable is not study.
  Cap planning at about 5% of study time.
- **Overweighting favourite topics:** the map will show you avoiding weak ones.
- "Dunning-Kruger" as popularly told (the ignorant are most confident) overstates the
  original finding and is partly a statistical artefact; the useful point is simply
  that self-assessment is noisy — so test.
- Time estimates are usually too optimistic (planning fallacy); multiply your first
  guess by about 1.5.

## Verify
- [ ] A written topic checklist with current 0–3 ratings exists.
- [ ] Each topic appears at least three times on the calendar, spaced out.
- [ ] Ratings change based on cold tests, not after re-reading.
- [ ] You can name one strategy you switched this month and why.
- [ ] Your confidence and your accuracy roughly agree on recent self-tests.
