# Studying by retrieval practice

**Goal:** learn more in the same time by pulling information *out* of your head instead
of pushing it in again. Retrieval practice ("the testing effect") is one of the two
techniques rated highest-utility in Dunlosky et al.'s 2013 review (the other is spacing).

## Why re-reading fools you
Re-reading and highlighting make material *feel* familiar. Familiarity is recognition,
not recall, and exams, conversations and jobs need recall. Retrieval is harder and feels
less productive — that difficulty is what strengthens the memory.

## Core methods
### 1. Free recall ("blurting") after a study block
1. Read or watch a section (10–30 min).
2. Close everything. On a blank page, write or say all you remember: ideas, terms,
   diagrams, how they connect. 3–10 minutes.
3. Open the source and compare. Mark in a different colour what you missed or got wrong.
4. Those marks are your next study targets — not the bits you already recalled.

### 2. Closed-book summary
At the end of a chapter, write a half-page summary from memory with the book shut. Then
check. A summary written with the book open is copying.

### 3. Practice questions
- Use end-of-chapter questions, past papers, or write your own while studying (turn each
  heading into a question).
- Attempt them **before** you feel ready. Errors with feedback still help learning.
- Mix old and new questions so earlier material keeps getting retrieved (see
  `mind-self.learning-how-to-learn.interleaving-and-varied-practice`).

### 4. Flashcard discipline
Answer out loud or on paper before flipping. Keep cards that test understanding
("why does X cause Y?") as well as facts. Schedule them with
`mind-self.learning-how-to-learn.spaced-repetition.designing-a-spaced-review-schedule`.

## The error log
Keep a running table: *question / my answer / correct answer / why I got it wrong /
fix*. Classify the cause:
- **Didn't know it** → learn and card it.
- **Knew it, couldn't retrieve** → more spaced retrieval.
- **Misread the question / careless** → slow-down routine for exams.
- **Confused with similar idea** → make a contrast card.
Re-attempt logged questions a few days later without looking at the answer.

## A sample study hour
- 5 min: free recall of last session's material.
- 30 min: new material, pausing every section for a 2-minute recall.
- 15 min: practice questions mixing new and old.
- 10 min: check answers, update the error log.

## Gotchas
- **Feedback matters.** Retrieval without checking can lock in errors; always compare
  against the source.
- **Too hard too early** gives near-zero recall and demotivates. If you recall almost
  nothing, re-study briefly and try again sooner.
- **Multiple-choice is weaker** than producing the answer, though still better than
  re-reading. Prefer short-answer formats when you write your own questions.
- The benefit is strongest for delayed tests; it may look worse on a test five minutes
  later. Don't judge it by same-day feel.
- Evidence is strongest for fact and concept learning in lab and classroom studies;
  transfer to complex skills is less well established.

## Verify
- [ ] Each study session includes at least one closed-book retrieval step.
- [ ] You check every retrieval against the source.
- [ ] Your error log exists and logged items are re-tested later.
- [ ] You can answer a question on last week's material without looking anything up.
- [ ] Re-reading time has dropped; testing time has grown.
