# Choosing and running a note-taking system

**Goal:** notes that you actually reuse — for exams, writing, projects — rather than an
archive you never open. Match the method to the job, then keep it light enough to maintain.

## Pick by job
| Job | Method |
|---|---|
| Live lectures, meetings, a single course | **Cornell** or a simple outline |
| Reading for long-term understanding and writing | **Atomic linked notes** (Zettelkasten-style) |
| Lots of saved material you skim later | **Progressive summarisation** |
| Projects and reference organisation | A folder scheme such as PARA (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archive) |
Tool choice (paper, Obsidian, Logseq, Notion, a plain folder of text files) matters far
less than the habit. Pick one you'll open daily; prefer formats you can export.

## Cornell notes (lectures, classes)
Divide the page: a narrow left **cue column**, a wide right **notes column**, a strip at
the bottom for a **summary**.
1. During: notes in the right column — key ideas, abbreviations, no transcription.
2. Within 24 hours: write questions or keywords in the cue column that the notes answer.
3. Write a 2–3 sentence summary at the bottom.
4. Review: cover the right column, answer the cues aloud (this is retrieval practice —
   see `mind-self.learning-how-to-learn.active-recall-and-self-testing.studying-by-retrieval-practice`).

## Atomic linked notes (Zettelkasten-style)
From Niklas Luhmann's slip-box, popularised by Ahrens.
1. **Fleeting notes:** quick captures, thrown away or processed within a day or two.
2. **Literature notes:** brief notes *in your own words* on a source, with the reference.
3. **Permanent notes:** one idea per note, written as a full sentence or short
   paragraph that makes sense on its own months later. Give it a title that states the claim.
4. **Link** each new permanent note to existing ones it supports, contradicts or extends,
   and say *why* in the link text. Links are what make the system generate ideas.
5. Keep a few **index / map notes** as entry points to clusters.

## Progressive summarisation
Each time you revisit a saved note, add one layer: bold the key passages; next time
highlight the best of the bold; eventually write a short summary at the top. Only
material you keep returning to gets distilled — effort follows usefulness.

## Maintenance routine
- **Daily (5 min):** process the inbox — file, convert to permanent notes, or delete.
- **Weekly (20 min):** review recent notes, add links, archive finished projects.
- Search beats perfect folders; consistent titles and a few tags beat elaborate hierarchies.

## Gotchas
- **Collector's fallacy:** saving and highlighting feels like learning; it isn't. If you
  never rewrote it in your own words, you probably haven't processed it.
- **System-tinkering** (templates, plugins, colour schemes) is a common procrastination.
  Freeze the setup for a month before changing anything.
- **Laptop transcription:** Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) found longhand note-takers did
  better on conceptual questions, apparently because they summarised rather than
  transcribed. Replications have been mixed — the safer lesson is *don't transcribe
  verbatim*, whatever the device.
- Claims that a Zettelkasten "thinks with you" are anecdotal; its measurable value is in
  forcing your-own-words rewrites and connections.

## Verify
- [ ] Lecture notes have cue questions and a summary written within a day.
- [ ] Permanent notes are in your own words, one idea each, readable out of context.
- [ ] New notes link to at least one existing note, with the reason stated.
- [ ] The inbox is near empty at the end of each week.
- [ ] In the last month you retrieved and used notes for a real output (essay, exam, decision).
