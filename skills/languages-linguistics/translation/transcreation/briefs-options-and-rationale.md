# Transcreation: briefs, options and rationale

**Goal:** copy that does in the target market what the original does in its own — same
intent, emotional effect and brand voice — even when the words share nothing. The
deliverable is not just text; it's options plus the reasoning a non-speaking client
needs to choose.

## 1. Get (or build) a proper brief
Ask before writing a word:
- **Objective**: awareness, sign-up, sale, reassurance? What should the reader feel/do?
- **Audience**: age, market, region (es-ES vs es-MX), formality (tu/vous, du/Sie).
- **Brand voice** guide and previous approved local copy.
- **Constraints**: character limits, visuals the copy sits on (puns that rely on an image),
  must-keep elements (product name, legal claims, hashtag), channels (OOH, social, TV with VO
  timing).
- **Mandatories and no-gos**: claims the local regulator restricts (health, comparative,
  "free"), words the brand avoids.
- **Freedom level**: may you change the concept, or only the line?
If no brief exists, write one yourself from the client's answers and get it approved —
it protects you when feedback arrives.

## 2. Understand what the source does
For each line write one sentence: what does it *do*? ("Rhymes, sounds confident, plays on
'charge' = battery + energy, implies effortlessness.") You will transfer this function, not
the words.

## 3. Write options
Deliver usually **2–3 genuinely different routes**, e.g.:
1. *Close*: as near the source as works naturally.
2. *Adapted*: keeps the concept, new wordplay/idiom.
3. *Bold*: new angle that fits the market better, within the brief.
For each option give:
- the target copy;
- a **literal back-translation** into the client's language;
- a **rationale** (2–4 lines): connotations, register, why it fits the audience, risks;
- your recommendation.

## 4. Check before launch
- **Connotation and slang** check with a second native speaker from the exact market (a pun
  can be vulgar in one country and innocent in another).
- **Name/claim screening**: product names and taglines for unwanted meanings; trademark
  clearance is a legal task for the client — flag, don't certify.
- **Visual fit**: does the line still match the image, gesture, colour symbolism?
- **Length/timing** in layout or against the video.

## 5. Handling feedback
Clients often judge by back-translation — remind them it's an aid, not the copy. When a
local reviewer and the brand team disagree, go back to the brief's objective as the
arbiter. Log decisions for consistency across the campaign.

## Pricing note
Transcreation is commonly billed by the hour or per project/line rather than per word, because
effort doesn't scale with word count. *Rates and norms vary by market — no figures given here.*

## Gotchas
- Delivering one option with no rationale invites "make it more like the English".
- Over-literal back-translations make good copy look bad; add a natural paraphrase too.
- Untouchable source wordplay tied to a visual may need the client to change the visual.
- Culture-specific references (sports, holidays, idioms) may not exist or carry different
  weight in the target market.
- Regulatory claims ("clinically proven", "best") are not creative territory — keep them exact.

## Verify
- [ ] Written brief approved by the client before drafting.
- [ ] Each source line has a stated function you preserved.
- [ ] 2–3 distinct options delivered, each with back-translation, rationale and a recommendation.
- [ ] A second native speaker from the target market checked connotations.
- [ ] Copy fits the layout/timing and keeps mandatory and legal elements intact.
