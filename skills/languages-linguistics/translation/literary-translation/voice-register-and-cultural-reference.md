# Voice, register and cultural reference in literary translation

**Goal:** a translation that produces in its reader something close to what the original
produces in its reader — the same voice, tone, pace and strangeness — rather than a
word-accurate text nobody wants to read.

## 1. Before translating: read for voice
Read the whole work (or at least a long section) first. Write a one-page **style sheet**:
- narrator's voice (formal/colloquial, ironic, child, unreliable…);
- sentence rhythm (long periodic sentences, fragments, repetition used deliberately);
- register shifts and who speaks how (class, age, region, era);
- recurrent images, motifs, key terms that must stay consistent;
- deliberate oddities (neologisms, broken grammar) — these are *not* errors to smooth out.
Decide the general strategy before line 1: how foreign should the result feel?

## 2. Foreignising vs domesticating — per item, not dogma
For each culture-bound item (food, institutions, honorifics, measures, money, idioms,
songs, allusions) choose a procedure:
- **retain** (borrow the word, maybe italicised — house style varies; many now avoid italics);
- **retain + gloss in-text** ("she ate *injera*, the sour flatbread…") — keeps flow;
- **generalise** ("pastry" for a specific regional cake) when the specificity doesn't matter;
- **substitute** a target-culture equivalent — risky: it relocates the story;
- **omit** — only when the item carries nothing.
Footnotes and translator's notes interrupt reading; use them sparingly in fiction (more
acceptable in poetry, classics and scholarly editions). Agree with the publisher.

## 3. Dialect and sociolect
Mapping a source dialect onto a specific target dialect (e.g. a rural Italian dialect as
Scottish English) imports the wrong place and politics. Common alternatives: a **non-specific
colloquial register** (contractions, informal grammar, vocabulary choices) that signals
"non-standard" without locating it; keep a few source words. Be consistent per character.

## 4. Wordplay, rhythm, poetry
- Puns: identify what the pun *does* (comic beat, double meaning crucial to plot) and rebuild
  that function, even with different words or in a nearby sentence (compensation).
- Poetry: decide the hierarchy — meaning, form (metre, rhyme), sound — you cannot keep
  everything. State the choice in a translator's note.
- Read every page **aloud** in revision; rhythm problems are audible before they're visible.

## 5. Working method
1. Fast first draft, flag problems in brackets rather than solving them on the spot.
2. Second pass against the source: omissions, mistranslations, consistency against the style sheet.
3. Third pass **without** the source, as a target-language text: remove translationese
   (source-language word order, false friends, over-literal idioms).
4. Query list for the author (if living) — batch questions, keep them precise.
5. Final read aloud.

## Gotchas
- Over-explaining: adding clarity the author withheld.
- Smoothing deliberate repetition or ambiguity into "better" prose.
- Anachronism: modern slang in a period text, or stilted archaism nobody ever spoke.
- Names: keep or adapt consistently; watch for meaningful names (decide per book).
- Rights: you generally need the publisher to hold translation rights before publishing;
  a sample translation for pitching is different. *Contract terms (royalties, credit) vary by
  country — consult translators' associations' model contracts.*

## Verify
- [ ] A style sheet exists and was followed for recurrent terms and voices.
- [ ] Every culture-bound item has a deliberate procedure, applied consistently.
- [ ] A reader who doesn't know the source finds no passage that "sounds translated".
- [ ] A bilingual reviewer finds no omissions or meaning shifts in a sampled chapter.
- [ ] The whole text has been read aloud at least once.
