# Dating attestations, tracing borrowings and spotting folk etymology

**Goal:** an account of where a word came from that rests on dated evidence and regular
linguistic correspondences — and says clearly where the evidence runs out.

## 1. Start with the standard references, then go beyond them
- Read the entries in the major historical dictionaries for the language (for English, the
  OED and the Middle English Dictionary; for other languages their national historical
  dictionaries and etymological dictionaries). Note their first dates and proposed sources.
- Treat popular etymology websites as **secondary**: useful leads, not evidence. Follow their
  citations back to sources.

## 2. Find and verify the earliest attestation
- Search **digitised corpora**: historical newspapers, book archives (Google Books, HathiTrust,
  EEBO-type collections for early print), legal and parliamentary records, letters.
- **Verify every hit against the page image.** Digitised metadata is often wrong — a 1950
  reprint catalogued as 1850, a periodical volume dated by its first issue, OCR misreading
  (*modern* for *modem*, long-s *ſ* read as *f*).
- Record: exact form, date (and how the date is known), source, the **sentence**, and the
  sense. An early hit in a *different sense* is a different finding.
- Antedatings are common for informal words: written records lag speech, so a first
  attestation is a **terminus ante quem**, not a birth date. Say "first recorded", not "coined".

## 3. Trace the route of a borrowing
- Work backwards one step at a time: English ← Anglo-Norman/Old French ← Latin ← Greek, for
  example. Each step needs a plausible **contact situation** (trade, conquest, scholarship,
  religion) and date overlap.
- Check the **form**: does the sound and spelling fit the donor language at that date and the
  regular changes since? Inherited words follow **regular sound correspondences** across
  related languages (e.g. Germanic *f* where Latin has *p*: *father*/*pater*); loanwords tend not
  to, which helps distinguish a borrowing from a cognate.
- Check the **sense path**: semantic shifts should be explainable (narrowing, broadening,
  metaphor), ideally with intermediate senses attested.
- Watch for **multiple sources** (a word reinforced by both French and Latin) and
  **re-borrowings**.

## 4. Spotting folk etymology
Red flags for a story that's probably false:
- **Acronym origins** for older words (*posh*, many taboo words) — acronym-formation was rare
  before the 20th century.
- A neat anecdote with **no contemporary evidence**, first appearing long after the word.
- The story fits modern spelling but not older recorded forms.
- The supposed source's date is later than the word's first attestation.
Folk etymology is also a real *process*: speakers reshape unfamiliar words to familiar
parts (*asparagus* → "sparrow-grass" in older English). Record such forms as part of the history.

## 5. Writing the entry
- Order: source language and form, its meaning, further sources, then cognates — with dates.
  "From Old French *x* 'meaning', from Latin *y*."
- Label confidence: **"of uncertain origin"**, **"perhaps from"**, **"origin unknown"** are
  legitimate conclusions. Mention popular stories only to state that evidence doesn't support them.
- Cite the earliest attestation with date and source.

## Gotchas
- Confusing similarity with descent: chance resemblances across unrelated languages abound.
- Over-reliance on one corpus: coverage gaps look like absences.
- Dating a word by its earliest dictionary entry rather than usage.

- [ ] Popular stories checked against first-attestation dates.
