# Writing dictionary definitions, examples and usage labels

**Goal:** a definition a user can understand, that picks out exactly the meaning in the
evidence — not more, not less — supported by an example that shows real use.

## 1. Choose the defining style (set by the style guide)
- **Genus + differentia (analytical):** name the class, then what distinguishes this member.
  *kettle:* "a container with a lid, handle and spout, used for boiling water". The genus is
  *container*; the rest are differentiae.
- **Full-sentence (COBUILD-style):** the definition shows the word in its typical context.
  "If you *hesitate*, you pause slightly before doing or saying something, often because you
  are uncertain." Good for learners: it encodes grammar and typical subjects.
- **Substitutable definitions** (traditional): the definition can replace the headword in a
  sentence — match part of speech (define a verb with a verb phrase, an adjective with an
  adjectival phrase).
- **Functional definitions** for function words: explain use, not meaning ("used to show…").

## 2. Discipline rules
- **Defining vocabulary:** learner dictionaries use a list of roughly 2,000–3,000 common words
  for all definitions; any word outside it is shown in small capitals or cross-referenced. Use
  the list even when not required — it forces clarity.
- **No circularity:** don't define *wet* with *moist* and *moist* with *slightly wet*. Check
  that each word in a definition is itself defined independently somewhere.
- **No harder words than the headword.**
- **Cover the evidence, exclude the rest:** test your definition against 20 concordance lines.
  If a line fits the sense but not your definition, it's too narrow; if your definition would
  also cover a different word ("a large animal" for *elephant*), it's too broad.
- **Neutrality:** avoid opinion, cultural assumptions and gendered defaults ("a person who…",
  not "a man who…" unless meaning requires it).
- **Grammar in the definition:** indicate typical subject/object ("of a dog: to bark loudly")
  and patterns ("+ *to* infinitive") either in the definition or in grammar codes.

## 3. Examples
- Take from corpus evidence; choose lines that show the **most typical pattern and
  collocates**, not the most colourful.
- Edit only within the style guide's policy (shorten, remove obscure names); never change
  the pattern being illustrated.
- One example per sense minimum for learners; show a second when the grammar varies.
- Avoid examples that restate the definition or depend on wider context.

## 4. Labels and usage notes
- **Register:** formal, informal, slang, offensive, literary, technical.
- **Region/variety:** *UK*, *US*, *Australian*, *Scottish*.
- **Time:** dated, old-fashioned, archaic. **Domain:** law, medicine, sport.
- **Attitude:** disapproving, approving, humorous.
Label from evidence (distribution across corpus genres and regions), not intuition. Use
**usage notes** for contested points (*literally*, *fewer/less*): describe what people do,
and what some readers object to, rather than pronouncing.

## Gotchas
- Defining a word through its etymology rather than current meaning.
- Lists of near-synonyms instead of a definition ("happy, glad, cheerful").
- Over-splitting: a separate sense for every collocate.
- Offensive terms need explicit labels and care in examples; consult the style guide's
  sensitive-content policy.

## Verify
- [ ] Definition matches the part of speech (or uses full-sentence style consistently).
- [ ] Every defining word is within the defining vocabulary or flagged.
- [ ] Tested against ~20 concordance lines: none excluded wrongly, no other word covered.
- [ ] No circular pair with a related headword.
- [ ] Example is corpus-derived and typical; labels are backed by distribution evidence.
