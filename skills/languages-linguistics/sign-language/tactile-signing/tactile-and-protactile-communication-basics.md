# Tactile and protactile communication — basics for signers

**Goal:** communicate respectfully and effectively with a DeafBlind person whose
preferred mode is touch-based, starting from an existing sign-language base.

> Draft. Protactile (PT) is a DeafBlind-led movement and language practice that is
> evolving; this page summarises widely taught principles. **The DeafBlind person's own
> preferences override everything here** — ask, then adapt. Not reviewed by a DeafBlind
> instructor.

## 1. Ask, don't assume
DeafBlind people differ hugely: some use close-vision signing, some tracking (holding
your wrists to follow), some full tactile signing, some protactile, some print-on-palm,
some speech with amplification. Ask (in their mode, or via their interpreter/support
worker): *Which mode? Which hand? Where should I sit or stand? How much environmental
information do you want?*

## 2. Modes in practice
- **Close-vision signing:** sign smaller, inside a limited visual field, often at chest
  height and closer; dark plain clothing, good lighting, no backlight from windows.
- **Tracking:** the person lightly holds your wrists/forearms to follow movement while
  using residual vision; keep signs compact and steady.
- **Tactile signing (hand-over-hand):** the receiver places their hand(s) on the back of
  the signer's hand(s). Sign in a smaller space, closer to the body, at a steady pace.
  Facial grammar doesn't transmit — **convert it**: e.g. a question needs an explicit
  question sign or agreed touch marker; negation must be signed, not headshaken.
- **Protactile:** treats touch as the primary channel for everything, not an adaptation
  of visual signing. Communication happens on the body (often hands, arms, upper legs/knees
  when seated), with **reciprocal** contact and continual tactile feedback.

## 3. Backchanneling by touch
Silent stillness is unreadable. The receiver can't see nods, so feedback must be
tactile, continuously:
- Agreed taps for "yes/I'm following" (e.g. repeated light taps on the signer's arm or
  leg), a different movement for "no/wait/I don't understand".
- In PT, the listener gives frequent tactile responses; the signer should **pause for
  them** and not plough on.
- Agree the signals at the start of the conversation; don't import ones you've read about
  without checking.

## 4. Environmental information
A DeafBlind person misses what a sighted person absorbs passively: who's in the room,
who just arrived or left, laughter, the mood, what's on the table. Offer it, briefly,
in the agreed amount: "Three people at the table, your friend just came in on your left."
Always **announce yourself on arrival** (name sign or agreed identifying touch) and
**announce leaving** — never just disappear.

## 5. Guiding basics
- Offer your arm; the person holds above your elbow and walks half a step behind.
- Narrate steps, kerbs, doors and narrow gaps before you reach them; pause at stairs.
- Don't push or pull them by the body; let them follow your movement.
- When seating, place their hand on the back of the chair; they'll do the rest.

## 6. Ergonomics
Tactile conversations are physically tiring for both people: sit knee-to-knee or at a
corner, at comfortable height, with breaks. Keep nails short and hands clean/warm;
avoid rings and bracelets that snag.

## Gotchas
- Touching without first identifying yourself.
- Signing at visual speed with full-size movements on tactile.
- Dropping facial grammar without replacing it.
- Talking *about* the person to their support worker instead of to them.
- Assuming that once they agreed a mode yesterday, conditions (lighting, fatigue) are the
  same today.

## Verify
- [ ] Mode, hand and backchannel signals were agreed at the start.
- [ ] Questions and negation were marked manually or with agreed touch.
- [ ] You paused for, and received, tactile feedback throughout.
- [ ] You announced arrivals, departures and relevant room changes.
- [ ] The person confirmed the pace and position were comfortable.
