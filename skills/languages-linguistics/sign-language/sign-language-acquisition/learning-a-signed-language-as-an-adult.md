# Learning a signed language as an adult — a practical plan

**Goal:** go from zero to conversational in a national sign language (ASL, BSL, LSF,
DGS, Auslan, Libras…) without the usual plateau of "knows lots of signs, can't follow
a conversation".

> Draft. Built from second-language and second-modality (M2) learning research as
> generally summarised, plus common teaching practice. Timelines are rough.

## Why it's harder than it looks
Hearing adults are learning a **new modality** (visual-spatial) as well as a new language.
The usual traps:
- Vocabulary lists feel like progress but don't teach grammar, which lives in **space,
  movement and the face**.
- Expressive skill outruns receptive skill: learners can sign at you but can't read
  natural-speed signing back.
- Signing in English (or your spoken language's) word order.

## The plan
### 1. Pick one language and one local community
Sign languages are not universal; ASL and BSL are unrelated. Learn the one used by the
Deaf community you'll actually meet. Find: a class taught by a **Deaf** teacher, a
local Deaf club or signing social, and an online dictionary/corpus for that language.

### 2. Receptive first, every day (≈ 2:1 watching to signing)
- 15–20 minutes daily of real signing: vlogs, news in sign, storytelling.
- Start with content that has captions, then turn captions **off** and watch again.
- Keep a list of signs you see repeatedly but don't know; look them up or ask.
- Practise reading **fingerspelling** in context — names and places — separately (see
  the fingerspelling skill).

### 3. Voice off
In class and practice, stop speaking and stop mouthing full sentences. Voice-off forces
you to use the face and space. Many Deaf-led classes require it; adopt it at home too.

### 4. Drill the grammar that isn't a sign
Short daily drills:
- **Facial grammar:** sign 5 yes/no and 5 wh-questions; check eyebrows in a mirror.
- **Space:** set up two people left and right, tell a 3-sentence story using pointing and
  directional verbs between them.
- **Classifiers:** describe your room's layout, a car parking, a person walking up stairs.
- **Role shift:** retell a short exchange between two people by shifting shoulders/eye gaze.

### 5. Record yourself, weekly
Film a 1-minute monologue on a fixed topic (my week, my job). Compare with a fluent
signer's version; look for frozen face, English order, unclear locations, inconsistent
handshapes. Re-record the same topic monthly to see progress.

### 6. Real conversation
Go to signing events as a learner: introduce yourself, say you're learning, watch more
than you sign at first. Ask for repeats ("again, slower please" in the language),
not for someone to write it down. Expect to understand little for months; it's normal.

## Rough timeline (part-time, with community contact)
- **3 months:** introductions, family, numbers, simple questions; follow slow, clear
  signing on familiar topics.
- **12 months:** everyday conversations with patience from partners; use space and
  directional verbs consciously.
- **2–3 years:** follow natural-speed group conversation on most everyday topics.
Classroom-only learners take much longer.

## Etiquette while learning
- You're a guest in a linguistic community; don't expect free lessons on demand.
- Don't invent signs or use your own name sign — name signs are given by Deaf people.
- Don't talk about Deaf people in speech in front of them.
- Credit Deaf creators whose videos you learn from.

## Gotchas
- Learning from apps alone → no grammar, no receptive skill.
- One-handed "simplified" signing from children's resources is a different register.
- Assuming signs are iconic and guessing meanings.

## Verify
- [ ] You watch more signing than you produce, every week.
- [ ] You can follow a 2-minute captionless vlog on a familiar topic and summarise it.
- [ ] Your recorded monologue uses face, space and directional verbs, voice off.
- [ ] You have regular contact with Deaf signers, not only classmates.
