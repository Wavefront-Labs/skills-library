# ASL grammar essentials — stop signing English with your hands

**Goal:** produce ASL that a Deaf signer reads as ASL rather than signed English word
order, by getting the five load-bearing pieces right: the face, space, verbs that move,
classifiers and time.

> Draft. Written from standard ASL teaching curricula and sign-linguistics descriptions,
> not checked line-by-line with a Deaf ASL instructor. Your class teacher and Deaf
> signers outrank this page wherever they disagree. Regional and generational variation
> is real.

## 1. The face is grammar, not emotion
Non-manual markers (NMMs) carry sentence type. Getting the sign right with the wrong
face produces the wrong sentence.
- **Yes/no question:** eyebrows **up**, head slightly forward, hold the last sign a beat.
  `YOU DEAF?` with brows up = "Are you Deaf?"
- **Wh-question** (WHO, WHAT, WHERE, WHY, HOW): eyebrows **down/squeezed**, head forward.
  Learners often raise their brows here because English questions "go up" — wrong marker.
- **Negation:** headshake across the predicate, often with a slight frown; the headshake
  alone can negate.
- **Topic marker:** brows up on the topic, small pause, then the comment with neutral brows.
- **Adverbial mouth morphemes** (e.g. MM = normally/at ease, CHA = big, OO = small,
  TH = carelessly) change meaning of the verb or size. Learn them with the verb, not as a
  list. Exact forms vary by region and teacher.

## 2. Word order: topic-comment, time first
ASL allows several orders; for learners, two defaults keep you understandable:
- **Time first:** set the time frame at the start, then don't mark tense on every verb.
  `YESTERDAY ME GO STORE` — not "me go-ed".
- **Topic-comment:** `STORE, ME GO` (topic marked with raised brows). Use it when the
  topic is new information worth establishing.
- Wh-signs commonly appear at the **end** (or both start and end): `YOUR NAME WHAT?`
- Drop English articles and "to be"; ASL has no signs for "a/the/is" in ordinary use.

## 3. Space and indexing
Place people and things at locations in your signing space by pointing (indexing) or
signing them there. Then **point back** to refer to them — this is ASL's pronoun system.
- Set up referents to the left and right, not both in the same spot.
- Keep locations **stable** within a conversation; moving them mid-story confuses the viewer.
- Absent third person: point to the side, not at a random person in the room.

## 4. Verbs that move (directional / agreement verbs)
Verbs like GIVE, HELP, ASK, SHOW, TELL (variation exists) move **from subject to object**.
`I-HELP-YOU` is one movement from you toward the addressee; reversing the direction
reverses who helps whom. Plain verbs (LIKE, KNOW, LOVE) don't move this way — you
must index or name the object. Signing every directional verb in citation form is a
hallmark of a beginner.

## 5. Classifiers (depicting verbs)
Classifier handshapes stand for categories of objects and show location, movement and
shape: a 3-handshape for vehicles, 1-handshape upright for a person, B-handshape flat
for a flat surface. Order: **name the thing first, then classify it** —
`CAR CL:3-park-in-front-of-house`. Without the noun, the viewer doesn't know what the
classifier stands for. Classifier systems vary in handshape conventions; learn the
set your teacher uses.

## 6. Time and aspect
- The **time line** runs through the body: forward = future, back over the shoulder = past.
- **Aspect by movement:** repeating or circling a verb shows "over and over" or
  "for a long time" (e.g. a slow, repeated STUDY for "studied and studied").
- Numbers incorporate into time signs (TWO-WEEKS, THREE-DAYS-AGO) — one sign, not two.

## Gotchas
- **Mouthing English** every word pulls you into English order; mouth sparingly.
- **Fingerspelling everything** you don't know: ask "SIGN?" and learn the sign.
- **Dictionaries/videos show citation forms** — real signing modifies them for space,
  direction and aspect.
- Gloss conventions (CAPS, hyphens) are a notation, not the language; don't read
  meaning only from glosses.
- SEE/Signed English and Pidgin Signed English contact signing exist — useful to
  recognise, not the target if you're learning ASL.

## Verify
- [ ] Record yourself: brows **up** on yes/no, **down** on wh-questions, headshake on negation.
- [ ] Each referent has one location and you point back to it consistently.
- [ ] Directional verbs move between the right locations.
- [ ] Every classifier is preceded by the noun it represents.
- [ ] Time is set once at the start; verbs are not "tensed".
- [ ] A Deaf signer or instructor can retell your story without asking who did what.
