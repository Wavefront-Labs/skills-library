# Communicating in International Sign — signing across borders

**Goal:** hold a useful conversation with a Deaf signer who uses a different national
sign language, and follow International Sign (IS) at an international event.

> Draft. IS is a contact phenomenon studied relatively recently; descriptions vary and
> conventions drift between events. Requires an existing fluent national sign language —
> this is not a beginner's route into signing.

## What IS is
- Not a full national language and not universal. It's a set of **conventions and
  strategies** used when signers of different sign languages meet: shared international
  vocabulary, heavy use of iconic depiction, and lots of checking.
- Used at the World Federation of the Deaf (WFD) congresses, Deaflympics, European bodies
  and international Deaf events; conference IS interpreting exists.
- "Gestuno" was an earlier attempt at a standardised vocabulary (1970s); modern IS grew
  more from use than from that list.
- Comprehension depends heavily on the viewer's experience with international contact;
  newcomers often understand much less than regulars.

## Strategies that work (cross-signing)
1. **Find shared ground first.** Ask which sign languages each of you knows; many
   signers know some ASL or have international experience. Use any shared vocabulary.
2. **Depict, don't cite.** Use classifiers/depicting signs, size-and-shape descriptions
   and enactment rather than arbitrary lexical signs from your own language.
3. **Role shift / constructed action.** Show what a person did rather than labelling it.
4. **Slow down, expand, repeat.** Give one idea at a time; restate with a different
   strategy if you see confusion.
5. **Establish a sign and reuse it.** Describe a concept once at length, agree a short
   sign together, then use the short form for the rest of the conversation.
6. **Check constantly.** Watch for backchannel (nods, confused faces); ask "understand?"
   and invite them to show it back.
7. **Mouthing and fingerspelling are weak tools:** spoken languages and manual alphabets
   differ. Fingerspelling helps only with shared alphabets (one-handed Latin-alphabet
   users often overlap; BSL two-handed signers and others may not).
8. **Writing/drawing** on a phone is legitimate backup, especially for names and numbers.

## Vocabulary worth knowing
- **Country signs:** the endonymic sign (the sign the country's Deaf community uses for
  itself) is generally preferred in international settings. Learn them for countries you
  meet; WFD materials and Deaf-led sources publish lists.
- Common IS signs for: deaf, hearing, sign language, interpreter, meeting, question,
  finish, understand, same/different, time words. Learn them from a current IS teacher or
  video set — forms drift.
- Numbers: systems differ; show quantities clearly or write them.

## Following conference IS
- Expect much more **context-dependence** than in a national language: read programme
  materials beforehand, learn presenters' name signs and key topic signs.
- IS interpreters often work from a spoken-language or national-sign source and rely on
  your shared background; sit where you can see both slides and interpreter.
- Accept partial comprehension; ask peers afterward.

## Gotchas
- Assuming ASL = IS. ASL influence is strong, but signing pure ASL is not IS and some
  signers find it exclusionary.
- Using your national sign for a country that its own Deaf community finds offensive.
- Over-reliance on gesture that is culturally rude elsewhere (e.g. some hand shapes).
- Hearing non-signers: "just use gestures" isn't IS; IS rests on fluency in a sign language.

## Verify
- [ ] You can explain a 3-step process (e.g. getting to a venue) to a signer of a
      different sign language with them correctly showing it back.
- [ ] You know the endonymic country signs for the countries you'll meet.
- [ ] In a conversation you agreed at least one ad-hoc sign and reused it.
- [ ] You've watched a full IS presentation and can summarise its main points.
