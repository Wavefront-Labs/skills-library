# Fingerspelling fluency — read the word, not the letters

**Goal:** understand fingerspelled names and terms at native speed, and fingerspell so
others read you first time. Applies to one-handed alphabets (ASL, LSF, many others) and,
with adjustments, two-handed ones (BSL/Auslan/NZSL).

> Draft. Practice method distilled from common interpreter-training and ASL-class
> advice; the speeds quoted are rough targets, not measured norms.

## Why letter-by-letter reading fails
Fluent fingerspelling is fast (often several letters per second) and **coarticulated**:
each handshape is shaped by its neighbours and some letters are barely formed. If you
try to catch every letter, you fall behind by the third one and lose the word. Fluent
readers recognise the **overall movement envelope** — the word shape — plus the first
letter, last letter and length, and fill the rest from context.

## Receptive method
1. **Context first.** Before the word arrives you usually know the category (a name, a
   street, a medication, a brand). Predict.
2. **Lock first and last letters** and the approximate length; the middle comes from
   word shape.
3. **Don't interrupt mid-word.** Let it finish, then guess the whole word. Ask for a
   repeat only after.
4. **Ask for repeats the right way:** "again?" — not "slower" every time. A second pass at
   the same speed is often enough once you know the category.
5. **Learn lexicalised fingerspellings as signs.** In ASL, forms like #BACK, #JOB, #BUSY,
   #OK, #EARLY are short fingerspelled words that have become signs with their own
   movement; learn them as whole units. (Exact list varies; your teacher's set wins.)

## Daily receptive drill (10 minutes)
- Use a fingerspelling-practice app or videos at **increasing speed**: start where you get
  ~80% right, raise speed when you hit ~90%.
- Category runs: 20 first names, then 20 cities, then 20 food words. Context makes
  prediction trainable.
- Watch real signers (vlogs, news) and note every fingerspelled item you missed; replay.
- Practise **3–4 letter words** heavily — they're the hardest because there's little
  word shape to use.

## Expressive method (one-handed alphabets)
- **Hand position:** at shoulder height, slightly to the dominant side, palm mostly
  facing the viewer. Not in front of your face.
- **No bouncing:** don't push the hand forward on each letter; flow smoothly with the
  hand mostly still. Slight drift sideways is fine for long words.
- **Double letters** (BOOK, BALL): a small slide or quick re-form of the same letter,
  not two separate punches.
- **Rhythm beats speed:** steady pace, slight hold at the end of the word. Clean and
  steady at moderate speed is more readable than fast and messy.
- **Mouth the word** (if your language's norms allow) — it helps readers a lot.
- **Two-handed alphabets:** keep the base hand still and visible; vowels on fingertips
  must be crisp because they carry much of the word.

## When to fingerspell (and when not)
- Yes: names, places, brands, technical terms with no sign, and when introducing a
  term before giving its sign.
- No: as a substitute for signs you could learn. Fingerspell once, then ask for/agree on a
  sign.

## Gotchas
- Confusable ASL pairs: A/S/T/M/N (thumb placement), E/O, K/V, G/H, R/U; drill them
  as minimal pairs.
- Proper nouns with unusual spellings: confirm by repeating them back.
- Reading from the side: handshapes look different from an angle; practise off-axis too.
- Fatigue makes expressive spelling sloppy late in a long conversation.

## Verify
- [ ] You can read 10 fingerspelled first names from a fluent signer with ≥8 correct,
      no slow-down requested.
- [ ] You recognise your teacher's list of lexicalised fingerspellings as signs.
- [ ] Filmed, your expressive spelling has no bounce and no restarts on a 10-letter word.
- [ ] Double letters are visibly different from single letters.
