# BSL — two-handed fingerspelling, core grammar and the learning ladder

**Goal:** get started in British Sign Language with clean fingerspelling, the grammar
habits that matter most, and a realistic plan through the UK qualifications.

> Draft. Qualification names and awarding bodies (Signature, iBSL) change; check the
> current specification before enrolling. Not reviewed by a Deaf BSL tutor.

## 1. Two-handed fingerspelling
BSL uses a **two-handed** manual alphabet (shared with Auslan and NZSL — together often
called BANZSL). ASL's one-handed alphabet is a different system; don't mix them.
- **Dominant hand** does the active pointing/shaping; the **non-dominant hand** is the
  base. Left-handers swap roles — consistently.
- **Vowels:** the dominant index finger touches the fingertips of the non-dominant hand:
  thumb = A, index = E, middle = I, ring = O, little = U. Learn these first; they make
  up a large share of every word.
- **Consonants** are shapes built against or beside the base hand. Practise the confusable
  pairs: M/N (three vs two fingers laid on the palm), D/P, G/Q (regional forms vary),
  V/L.
- **Rhythm over speed:** a steady beat with a slight pause between words reads better
  than a fast blur. Keep hands at chest height in front of you, not at your face.
- **Receptive tip:** read the **shape of the whole word**, not letter by letter. Practise
  with names and places you already know, then with unfamiliar ones.

Fingerspelling is used for names, places, technical terms and some English loans (e.g.
initialised signs). Native signers don't fingerspell words that have a sign — ask
"sign for...?" rather than spelling everything.

## 2. Grammar habits that make BSL look like BSL
- **Topic–comment order:** set what you're talking about, then say something about it:
  `MY SISTER — TEACHER` ("my sister is a teacher").
- **Time first:** `TOMORROW ME GO LONDON`.
- **Placement:** put people/objects at points in space and refer back by pointing; keep
  them where you placed them.
- **Directional verbs** (GIVE, ASK, TELL, among others) move between placed referents.
- **Non-manual features:** eyebrows raised for yes/no questions, furrowed for wh-questions,
  headshake for negation; mouth patterns are very common in BSL, both English-derived
  mouthings and BSL-specific mouth gestures. More English mouthing is normal in BSL than
  in ASL — but keep the sign order BSL.
- **Classifiers/depicting signs** for movement and shape, always after naming the thing.

## 3. Regional variation is the norm
BSL varies strongly by region — numbers, colours and days of the week especially. Learn
your local variants first (your Deaf club and tutor are the reference), and recognise the
common alternatives for receptive skill. A sign in an online dictionary is not "wrong"
just because your area uses another.

## 4. The qualification ladder (UK, as commonly structured)
Approximate, check current specs:
- **Level 1** — basic conversation about self, family, everyday topics. Typically one
  academic year part-time, evening class.
- **Level 2** — everyday conversation in more depth (work, travel, routines).
- **Level 3** — wider range, more complex topics; often a prerequisite for interpreting
  routes.
- **Level 4 / Level 6** — advanced and near-native fluency; Level 6 is widely required
  before or alongside interpreter training (interpreting itself belongs in the
  interpreting field).
Hours per level vary by provider; budget for Deaf-community practice on top of
class time — classroom-only learners plateau.

## 5. Practice that works
- Attend Deaf events/clubs as a learner, politely; watch more than you sign at first.
- Use BSL dictionaries and BSL-language TV content for receptive practice.
- Film yourself fingerspelling a list of 20 place names; compare to a fluent model.
- Recognise that the BSL Act 2022 recognised BSL as a language of England, Wales and
  Scotland — it doesn't make it English on the hands.

## Gotchas
- Mixing ASL one-handed letters into BSL spelling.
- Over-fingerspelling instead of learning signs.
- Treating Sign Supported English (SSE) as BSL — SSE follows English order.
- Ignoring regional variants and "correcting" Deaf signers.

## Verify
- [ ] Vowels A–E–I–O–U correct and instant on the non-dominant fingertips.
- [ ] You can spell your full name and town at a steady rhythm without restarting.
- [ ] You can read back a fluent signer's spelling of five unfamiliar place names.
- [ ] Your sentences use topic–comment and time-first order.
- [ ] You know which level you're at and the next one's entry requirement.
