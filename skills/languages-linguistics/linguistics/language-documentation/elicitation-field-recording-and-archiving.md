# Elicitation, field recording and archiving

**Goal:** produce a lasting, usable record of a language — recordings, transcriptions,
translations and analysis — made with and for the speaker community, and deposited
where it will survive.

## 1. Start with the community, not the grammar
- Find out who speaks the language, who has authority over its use, and what the
  community wants (dictionary, teaching materials, recordings of elders).
- Agree **roles, credit and payment** for consultants; pay at a fair local rate.
- **Informed consent**: in a language and format people understand (oral consent
  recorded on audio is common); cover recording, archiving, access levels, public
  use, and the right to withdraw. Get institutional ethics approval too.
- Respect access restrictions (ceremonial, gendered or sensitive knowledge); the
  CARE principles for Indigenous data are a useful frame.

## 2. Equipment and recording settings
- Solid-state recorder (Zoom/Tascam class) or good smartphone app as backup;
  external mic: **lavalier** for one speaker, a directional mic for groups.
- **WAV (PCM), 48 kHz / 24-bit** (or at least 44.1 kHz/16-bit). Never MP3 as master.
- Video: a tripod, stable framing; essential for sign languages and gesture.
- Room: away from fridges, generators, roosters, tin roofs in rain. Do a 30-second test
  and listen on headphones.
- Say a **spoken slate** at the start: date, place, speakers (or codes), topic.
- Back up **every day** to two separate devices.

## 3. Elicitation
- Begin with a **word list** (basic vocabulary) to learn the sound system; repeat key
  words 2–3 times in isolation and in a frame ("Say ___ again").
- Move to **paradigms** (person/number, tense) and **sentences** — but elicitation
  through a contact language risks calques; check by asking consultants to judge
  sentences, not just translate them.
- Use **stimulus kits** (e.g. picture tasks, Pear Story-type videos, MPI field
  manuals) for comparable, more natural data.
- **Texts are the core:** narratives, procedures, conversations, songs. Grammar
  elicited without texts tends to miss what speakers actually do.
- Keep a session log: date, people, recording file names, topics, problems.

## 4. Processing pipeline
1. File naming convention set before the trip (e.g. `LANG_YYYYMMDD_Speaker_Topic_01.wav`).
2. **Metadata** per session: speakers (with consent level), place, genre, equipment.
3. **Transcription** in ELAN (time-aligned tiers: transcription, translation,
   notes) — ideally with a community member doing or checking it.
4. **Interlinearisation** in FLEx (FieldWorks) or Toolbox: builds a lexicon and
   morpheme glosses as you go (see languages-linguistics.linguistics.morphology-syntax.interlinear-glossing-and-constituency-tests).
5. Export regularly; FLEx/ELAN round-tripping can lose data — test on a copy.

## 5. Archiving
- Deposit with a recognised archive: **PARADISEC**, **ELAR**, **AILLA**, **The
  Language Archive**, or national/community archives. Contact them early: they have
  format and metadata requirements (often OLAC/IMDI-compatible).
- Deposit masters (WAV, uncompressed video where feasible), annotation files (`.eaf`,
  FLEx export), and a description of conventions.
- Set access conditions per item as agreed with speakers.
- Return copies to the community in usable forms (MP3s, booklets, apps).

## 6. Common failures
Lost metadata, unlabelled files, recordings with no translation, consent that didn't
cover archiving, and data that never left a laptop.

## Verify
- [ ] Consent recorded, covering archiving and access levels.
- [ ] Masters are WAV, backed up in two places daily.
- [ ] Every file has metadata and follows the naming convention.
- [ ] Texts, not only elicited words, are transcribed and translated.
- [ ] Deposit made (or scheduled) with an archive; community copies returned.
