# The comparative method and proto-form reconstruction

**Goal:** demonstrate that languages are related and reconstruct parts of their
common ancestor, using regular sound correspondences rather than look-alike words.

## 1. Assemble the data
- Use **basic vocabulary** first (body parts, low numerals, kinship, pronouns, natural
  phenomena — a Swadesh-type list): it is borrowed less often than cultural vocabulary.
- Use good phonemic transcriptions and reliable sources; note dialect and date.
- Collect **morphology** too (pronoun paradigms, irregular verbs): shared
  irregularities are strong evidence of relationship.

## 2. Build cognate sets and correspondence sets
1. Line up candidate cognates (same or similar meaning, similar shape).
2. Align segment by segment. For each position, record the correspondence:
   e.g. Latin `p` : Gothic `f` : Sanskrit `p` (*pater : fadar : pitar-*).
3. A **correspondence set** is valid when it **recurs** across many cognate sets. One
   example is anecdote; ten is a pattern.
4. Correspondences, not similarity, are the evidence: English `t` regularly matching
   German `ts` (*ten : zehn*, *tongue : Zunge*) proves more than any look-alike.

## 3. Reconstruct proto-phonemes
For each correspondence set, propose a proto-sound, guided by:
- **Directionality / naturalness:** changes like `p > f`, `k > tʃ` before front vowels,
  intervocalic voicing are common; the reverse is rare.
- **Majority** — a tiebreaker only, never decisive.
- **Economy:** fewest independent changes across daughters.
- **Phonological plausibility** of the resulting proto-inventory (a system with no
  vowels or no stops is suspect).
- **Complementary correspondence sets** (sets that never occur in the same environment)
  may reflect one proto-phoneme split by conditioning — merge them.

## 4. Write sound changes per daughter
For each daughter, list the changes from proto to attested forms as ordered rules
(e.g. Grimm's Law `*p *t *k > f θ x`). Then **derive every cognate** from its
reconstruction. Exceptions need an explanation:
- **Conditioned change** you missed (Verner's Law explained Grimm's "exceptions").
- **Analogy** (paradigm levelling).
- **Borrowing** (often betrayed by not following the expected correspondences, or by
  cultural-domain meaning).
- **Sporadic** change (metathesis, dissimilation) — admit it as such.

## 5. Ruling out alternatives
- **Chance:** short words and onomatopoeia match by accident; with a large list some
  look-alikes are expected. Systematic correspondences are hard to get by chance.
- **Borrowing:** loans cluster in cultural vocabulary; may show the donor's later
  sound changes.
- **Sound symbolism / nursery words** (*mama, papa*) — exclude.
- **Gotcha:** "mass comparison" of resemblances without correspondences is not
  accepted as proof by most historical linguists.

## 6. Internal reconstruction
Within one language, alternations (e.g. German *Tag* `[taːk]` / *Tage* `[taːɡə]`)
point to an earlier non-alternating form plus conditioned change. Use it to reach
pre-stages and to check comparative results.

## 7. Subgrouping
- Group languages by **shared innovations** (changes away from the proto-language),
  not shared retentions.
- Prefer innovations that are unusual — common changes may happen independently.
- Draw the tree; note where wave-like diffusion (dialect continua) makes a tree an
  idealisation.

## Verify
- [ ] Every proto-phoneme rests on a recurring correspondence set with multiple examples.
- [ ] Every cognate is derivable by the stated, ordered sound changes.
- [ ] Exceptions are explained (conditioning, analogy, loan) or flagged as sporadic.
- [ ] Loans and chance resemblances screened out.
- [ ] Subgroups are defined by shared innovations.

*Uncertainty:* computational phylogenetics (Bayesian cognate-based trees) is an active,
contested area; treat it as a complement, not a replacement.
