# Sight translation — reading a document aloud in another language

**Goal:** turn a written text (a consent form, a police statement, a letter, a
discharge note) into fluent, accurate speech in the other language, on the spot, at a
pace a listener can follow.

## 1. Why it's harder than it looks
The text stays in front of you, so source-language word order constantly pulls you into
calques. Written sentences are also longer and denser than speech. Sight translation is
tested in many court-interpreter certification exams for this reason (US federal and
state exams include it; check your jurisdiction's current format).

## 2. Before you speak: the scan (30–90 seconds if allowed)
Ask for time to read it through — it is professional, not a weakness. While scanning:
- **Purpose and register:** legal, clinical, bureaucratic, personal?
- **Structure:** headings, numbered clauses, tables, signatures, stamps.
- **Traps:** circle unfamiliar terms, abbreviations, numbers, dates, negations
  (especially double negatives), "unless/except/provided that" clauses.
- **Long sentences:** mark with a slash where you will cut them.
- **Handwriting / illegible bits:** decide now how you will flag them.

If time isn't allowed (e.g. a document handed over mid-hearing), say so to the
person in charge, then scan at least the first paragraph ahead of your voice.

## 3. Chunking and reformulation
- Read **one unit of meaning ahead** of what you are saying — your eyes lead your voice.
- Split long sentences: "The patient, having been informed of the risks, which include…,
  consents…" → "You have been told about the risks. These include… You agree to…"
- Rebuild in natural target order; don't wait for the source's final verb.
- Keep **every element**: sight translation is not a summary. Conditions, exceptions
  and numbers carry the legal or clinical weight.

## 4. Register and fidelity
- Keep the register of the document. A formal court order stays formal; don't
  "explain it simply" unless your role and the setting expressly allow it.
- Medical settings: render it faithfully; if the patient doesn't understand, the
  *clinician* explains — you interpret the explanation.
- Render visible non-text elements briefly: "[There is a stamp that reads …]",
  "[Signature, illegible]", "[Handwritten note in the margin: …]".

## 5. Delivery
- Even pace, no long silences, no "um" while you hunt. A short neutral filler
  ("…that is, …") is better than stopping.
- First person where the document speaks in first person ("I, the undersigned…").
- Self-correct cleanly once: "…on Monday — correction, on Tuesday."
- If a term is unknown, say so to the parties rather than guessing; ask for a
  dictionary moment where the setting permits.

## 6. Limits and ethics
- Long or highly technical documents (contracts, full medical records) are
  **translation** work, not sight translation. Say so and suggest a written translation.
- Don't sight-translate a document you can't read (bad copy) — report it.
- In court, sight translation of a document into the record may be governed by local
  rules; check them. (Court-record rules belong to law-government skills.)

## 7. Practice
- Daily: one page of a newspaper, a form, a leaflet; record yourself.
- Escalate density: patient leaflets → consent forms → legal notices → judgments.
- Time yourself against reading the source aloud; aim for comparable length.
- Back-check against the text for omissions, especially negations and numbers.

## Verify
- [ ] You asked for (or took) scanning time and marked traps first.
- [ ] Every clause, number, date and negation is in the rendering.
- [ ] Sentences were reformulated, not calqued in source order.
- [ ] Register matches the document; no unsolicited explanation added.
- [ ] Non-text elements (stamps, handwriting, illegible parts) were flagged.

*Uncertainty:* exam formats and court rules vary by jurisdiction; confirm locally.
