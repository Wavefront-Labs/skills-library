# Medical interpreting — accuracy and role in the clinical triad

**Goal:** let a clinician and a patient who share no language communicate as if they
did — completely, accurately, confidentially — while you stay as invisible as the
situation safely allows.

## 1. Pre-session (15 seconds, every time)
To both parties, briefly: "I'm the interpreter. I will interpret everything said, in
the first person. Please speak directly to each other. Everything stays confidential."
Ask the clinician for any context (e.g. "breaking bad news", "consent for surgery").

## 2. Positioning
- Common default: interpreter beside or slightly behind the patient, so clinician and
  patient face each other. Adapt for exams (behind a curtain, facing away), bedside,
  and when the patient prefers otherwise.
- Remote (phone/video): ask the clinician to introduce everyone in the room.

## 3. Accuracy rules
- **First person:** "I have pain here", not "she says she has pain".
- **Everything:** include hedges, repetitions, swearing, side comments to family —
  if it was said in the encounter, it's interpreted. Tell both parties this up front.
- **Register:** keep it. If the clinician uses jargon the patient won't understand,
  interpret it as said, then (per the role ladder below) flag a possible gap.
- **Numbers and doses:** repeat back if unsure ("5 milligrams?"); a misheard dose is
  a patient-safety event.
- **Don't add, omit, soften or explain** on your own initiative. Softening bad news
  is the classic, well-meant error.

## 4. The role ladder (step up only as needed)
A widely taught model (California CHIA standards) lists roles of increasing intervention:
1. **Message converter (conduit)** — default.
2. **Message clarifier** — you need a repetition, or a term has no equivalent.
3. **Cultural clarifier** — a belief or practice is causing misunderstanding.
4. **Patient advocate** — a real risk to the patient's health, wellbeing or dignity
   that the system isn't addressing; usually *outside* the session, through proper
   channels.
Step back down as soon as the issue is resolved.

## 5. The transparency rule
Whenever you speak for yourself, say so — **to both parties, in both languages**:
"As the interpreter, I'd like to ask for a repetition." "As the interpreter, I notice
the term 'x' may not have been understood." Never have a private side-conversation with
one party.

## 6. Terminology
- Learn body systems, common conditions, procedures, medications, and the
  everyday/colloquial terms patients actually use (e.g. regional words for symptoms).
- Keep a personal glossary per specialty (oncology, obstetrics, mental health).
- If you don't know a term: ask for clarification transparently. Never guess.

## 7. Hard moments
- **Distress, anger, crying:** keep interpreting; match tone without dramatising.
- **Family member wants to "help interpret":** interpret their contributions too;
  explain your role to the clinician if a minor or relative is being used as interpreter
  (common guidance discourages this).
- **Patient asks your opinion:** interpret the question to the clinician.
- **Vicarious trauma:** debrief with a supervisor or peer; use EAP services. Repeated
  exposure to bad-news and trauma sessions is a known occupational risk.

## 8. Ethics and certification
- Confidentiality (HIPAA in the US, GDPR/health-data rules elsewhere), impartiality,
  respect, professional development.
- US national certifications: **CCHI** (CoreCHI / CHI) and **NBCMI** (CMI). Other
  countries use national accreditation (e.g. NAATI in Australia). *Check current
  credential names and requirements in your jurisdiction.*

## Verify
- [ ] Pre-session delivered; parties speak to each other, not to you.
- [ ] First person throughout; nothing added, omitted or softened.
- [ ] Every intervention was announced transparently to both parties.
- [ ] Doses, numbers and dates confirmed.
- [ ] You stepped back to conduit after any clarification.
