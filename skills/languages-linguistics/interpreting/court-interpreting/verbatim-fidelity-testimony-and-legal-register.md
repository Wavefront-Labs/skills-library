# Court and legal interpreting — fidelity for the record

**Goal:** make the non-English-speaking (or other-language) party legally present:
everything said by and to them reaches the record with the same meaning, register and
force it had in the original. (Rules on certifying the record and who may appoint you
belong to law-government; this is the interpreting technique.)

## 1. "Verbatim" means legal equivalence
Courts say "verbatim"; interpreters mean **complete and accurate, preserving every
element of meaning and style**. You do not translate word-for-word, but you also
**do not** clean up, summarise, explain or improve:
- Keep **hesitations, false starts, self-corrections and hedges** ("I think maybe it
  was, um, around ten"). These affect credibility judgements.
- Keep **register**: a witness who speaks crudely is rendered crudely; a lawyer's
  convoluted question stays convoluted. Simplifying a question changes it.
- Keep **politeness markers and tags** where they carry meaning ("didn't you?").
- **Non-responsive answers** stay non-responsive — it's counsel's job to object.

## 2. Modes by stage
- **Witness testimony:** consecutive (often short segments) into the record.
- **Proceedings the defendant only needs to hear** (other witnesses, argument):
  simultaneous, usually whispered (*chuchotage*) or via portable equipment.
- **Documents read into evidence:** sight translation (see
  languages-linguistics.interpreting.sight-translation.scanning-chunking-and-oral-rendering-of-documents).
- Always first person, speaking as the witness ("I saw him").

## 3. Handling the flow
- Ask the court for segment lengths you can hold; signal with a raised hand.
- **Objections:** stop interpreting the moment an objection is made; interpret the
  objection and ruling to your client (simultaneously); do not render the pending
  answer until the judge rules.
- **Overlapping speech:** interpret the person with the floor; tell the court if
  speech was lost.
- **Ambiguity you can't resolve:** don't choose silently. Address the court: "Your
  Honour, the interpreter requests clarification; the word 'x' may mean A or B."
- **Slang, dialect, unknown term:** request permission to clarify with the witness,
  and say on the record that you did so.

## 4. Correcting yourself
If you realise you made an error — even days later — you have a duty to correct it on
the record: "Your Honour, the interpreter wishes to correct an earlier rendering…".
Concealing an error is the gravest professional fault.

## 5. Ethics in brief (NAJIT-style)
- Impartiality: no advice, no explanation of legal terms or procedure to the party
  (you can interpret counsel's explanation).
- Disclose conflicts (knowing a party, prior involvement).
- Confidentiality of privileged attorney–client communications you interpret.
- Don't take on cases beyond your competence; request a team for long proceedings
  (multi-hour simultaneous work is commonly teamed with ~30-minute rotations).

## 6. Legal register: build it deliberately
- Learn the procedural vocabulary in both systems (charge, plea, bail, remand, verdict,
  sentencing) — and that systems differ: a common-law term may have no exact civil-law
  equivalent; render the function, and consider a brief neutral rendering rather than a
  false equivalent.
- Keep a glossary for criminal, family, immigration and civil matters.
- Practise legalese reformulation (long conditionals, double negatives).

## 7. Outside the courtroom
Police interviews, depositions, lawyer conferences and immigration hearings follow
similar fidelity norms; recordings may be reviewed later, so assume every word will be
checked.

## Verify
- [ ] Hedges, hesitations, register and non-responsiveness preserved.
- [ ] First person; no explanation or advice to any party.
- [ ] Objections handled by stopping and waiting for the ruling.
- [ ] Ambiguities and clarifications put on the record, not resolved privately.
- [ ] Any error you noticed was corrected on the record.

*Uncertainty:* procedure for objections, teaming and corrections differs between
jurisdictions and court types; follow the local bench book and certification body.
