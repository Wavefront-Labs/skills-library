# Conference interpreting — preparation, glossary and working as a booth team

**Goal:** arrive at a multilingual meeting already knowing its subject, people and
terms, and work with your boothmate and other booths so the delegates never notice
the machinery.

## 1. Know your combination
- **A** = native/active language(s); **B** = active non-native; **C** = passive
  (understood, interpreted *from* only). Accept work only into your A (and B if you
  offer retour). Accepting outside your combination is the most common ethical breach.

## 2. Preparation (starts days before)
Ask the organiser or the team leader (*chef d'équipe*) for:
- Agenda, speaker list, previous minutes, draft resolutions, slides, speeches.
- Organisation's own glossary/term base, website, annual report.
- Names and titles of chairs and key speakers, plus pronunciations.

Then:
1. **Read for structure:** who are the parties, what is being decided, what is contested.
2. **Extract terms** into a glossary: source term | target term | note/context.
   Keep it short enough to scan in the booth (one or two pages per meeting, sorted
   by topic or alphabetically). Spreadsheet or term tool (InterpretBank, Interpreters'
   Help — check current availability) both work; paper is still fine.
3. **Check acronyms and official names** — use the organisation's official
   translation, not your own.
4. **Listen** to the speakers if recordings exist (accent, speed, pet phrases).
5. Day before: refresh; update with anything new from the organiser.

**Gotcha:** speeches handed over at the last minute — skim for numbers, quotes and
names; mark them; the speaker will usually deviate from the text, so interpret what's
*said*, flagging "check against delivery" items.

## 3. Booth teamwork
- Typical team: two interpreters per booth for a full day (three for long or
  demanding meetings in some markets); turns of **~30 minutes**. AIIC standards set
  team strength and working hours — check the current text and the contract.
- **Passive partner duties:** write numbers and names, find terms, hand over the
  glossary, watch the speaker list and documents, mute their own mic always.
- **Handover:** at a sentence/speaker boundary; the incoming interpreter slides into
  position before the outgoing one stops; no gap, no double voice.
- Agree beforehand: who starts, signals for "help me", how to pass notes.
- Never criticise your colleague in the booth or to the client.

## 4. Relay (pivot)
When no booth covers a language directly, you take the output of another booth.
- Know which booth is your **pivot** for each speaker and switch channels promptly.
- If you are the pivot: speak clearly, finish sentences, keep numbers precise, avoid
  long lag — several booths depend on you.
- Taking relay: allow slightly more décalage; the pivot may self-correct.

## 5. Professional norms (AIIC-style)
- **Confidentiality** of everything heard, including off-mic.
- Accept only work you are qualified for; prepare; don't use the work for gain.
- Recording of interpretation usually requires consent and extra terms.
- Working conditions: ISO booth standards (ISO 2603 permanent, ISO 4043 mobile) and
  ISO 20109 equipment; ISO 20108 covers sound/image for remote input.
  *Uncertainty:* verify the current ISO edition numbers before quoting them to a client.

## 6. On the day
- Arrive early: test console, channel, relay button, volume, mute key.
- Get the latest speaker list and documents; put names in the glossary.
- Keep water (no ice rattle), pencils, silent notepad; phone off.
- Debrief with the team after; update the shared glossary for the next meeting.

## Verify
- [ ] Working into your A/B only, per your stated combination.
- [ ] Glossary built from client documents and official names, scannable in the booth.
- [ ] Turn length, team size and handover signals agreed before starting.
- [ ] Relay sources known for each language; console tested.
- [ ] Confidentiality and recording terms are clear in the contract.
