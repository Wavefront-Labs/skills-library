# Community interpreting — dialogue sessions in public services

**Goal:** enable a service provider (caseworker, teacher, housing officer, asylum
interviewer) and a service user to communicate fully, with you staying neutral even
when the stakes are personal and the setting informal.

## 1. What makes it different
- **Dialogue (liaison) interpreting**: short turns, both directions, face to face or
  by phone. Usually short consecutive, sometimes whispered.
- **Power imbalance**: one party controls the outcome (benefit, school place, asylum).
  The user may see you as an ally because you share their language — or as a threat
  from their community.
- Often low status, low pay, little preparation material. Prepare anyway: ask what
  the meeting is about.

## 2. Opening the session
Introduce yourself to both: "I'll interpret everything both of you say, in the first
person. I'm neutral and bound by confidentiality. Please speak in short segments."
Seat so the parties face each other (triangle). Check you speak the user's language
*and dialect* — a mismatch must be raised immediately, not managed silently.

## 3. Turn management
- Keep segments short; raise a hand to pause a speaker.
- Interpret everything, including asides between family members or between officials.
- If both talk at once: stop, say "As the interpreter, I can only interpret one person
  at a time," then let the provider decide who goes first.
- Never conduct a side conversation in the shared language, even in the corridor. If
  the user approaches you before/after, say you can't discuss the case without the
  provider present.

## 4. Asylum and immigration interviews
- Accuracy of **dates, places, names, sequences** is critical; inconsistencies are used
  to assess credibility. Confirm spellings; note numbers.
- Render exactly — including vagueness. Don't "help" an applicant by making an account
  sound more coherent.
- Disclose any conflict: ethnic, political, religious, or personal links to the
  applicant's community; an applicant may reasonably fear an interpreter from a
  persecuting group. Ask about gender preference where trauma (e.g. sexual violence) may
  be disclosed — UNHCR guidance recommends offering this.
- Check comprehension of the interviewer's questions *through the interviewer*, not by
  rephrasing on your own.

## 5. Cultural brokering — where the line is
- Allowed (and transparent): "As the interpreter, I'd point out that in the user's
  region, 'cousin' may include more relatives than in English — you may want to ask."
- Not allowed: explaining what the user "really means", advising either party, filling
  in forms for them, or giving opinions on the case.
- If a cultural misunderstanding is happening, flag the *possibility* to both parties;
  let the provider ask the question.

## 6. Self-protection
- Asylum, child-protection and domestic-violence sessions expose interpreters to
  traumatic content repeatedly. Signs of vicarious trauma: intrusive images, sleep
  problems, numbness, avoidance.
- Use debriefs (ask the provider for one), peer supervision, limits on the number of
  heavy sessions per day, and professional support.
- Keep personal contact details private; decline contact outside sessions.

## 7. Qualifications
UK: DPSI (CIOL) and the National Register of Public Service Interpreters; Australia:
NAATI certification; elsewhere varies widely. *Check the current scheme in your
country.* See also human-services.community-services-and-development.settlement-services.settlement-orientation-interpreters-and-cultural-bridging
for the provider's side of working with interpreters.

## Verify
- [ ] Language and dialect match confirmed at the start.
- [ ] Introduction given; parties address each other.
- [ ] No side conversations; asides interpreted.
- [ ] Dates, names, places rendered and confirmed exactly.
- [ ] Any cultural input was announced to both parties and limited to flagging.
- [ ] You debriefed after a traumatic session.
