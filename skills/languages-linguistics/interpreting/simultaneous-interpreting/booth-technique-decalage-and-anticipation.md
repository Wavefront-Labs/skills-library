# Simultaneous interpreting — décalage, chunking and anticipation in the booth

**Goal:** deliver a complete, idiomatic rendering roughly a few seconds behind the
speaker, without losing the thread when they speed up, read a script or rattle off numbers.

## 1. The model you are managing
Gile's *Effort Model* is the most useful mental frame: listening/analysis, production,
short-term memory and coordination all draw on one limited capacity. Every failure in
the booth is one effort starving another. Technique is about lowering the load of each.

## 2. Décalage (ear-voice span)
- Typical lag is **2–4 seconds or a few words to one clause** behind the speaker. This is
  a working range from the training literature, not a rule — it varies by language pair.
- **Too short** → you commit to a structure before you know where the sentence goes
  (word-for-word calques, dead ends). **Too long** → memory overload; you drop items.
- Wait for a *unit of meaning* (subject + verb, or the verb in German/Japanese-style
  verb-final sources), then start. Lengthen the lag for dense or verb-final input;
  shorten it for lists and numbers.

## 3. Chunking ("salami technique")
Cut long source sentences into short target sentences. Start with a neutral opener you
can finish any way: "As for the budget, …", "There are three points here. First…".
Never wait for a 60-word sentence to end.

## 4. Anticipation
- **Linguistic:** predictable collocations and formulae ("I would like to thank the
  Chair…", "on the one hand… on the other").
- **Extralinguistic:** the agenda, the speaker's known position, the previous speaker.
  Preparation (glossary, speeches, background) is what makes anticipation safe.
- **Gotcha:** if your anticipation proves wrong, correct briefly ("…or rather, …") —
  do not stay silent and do not over-apologise.

## 5. Numbers, names and lists
- Numbers are where capacity collapses. Have your **boothmate write numbers down**
  as they come; reduce lag sharply when you hear one.
- Render magnitude first if needed ("about 4.5 billion") rather than freeze.
- In verb-final sources, park names and figures on the notepad.

## 6. Production discipline
- Speak in **complete sentences** with natural intonation; listeners judge you on
  delivery as much as accuracy.
- **Monitor your own output** with one ear (most interpreters wear the headset
  half-off one ear or keep the volume of their own voice audible).
- Keep your volume and pace steady; don't mirror a panicking speaker.
- Simplify, generalise or omit *redundant* detail when overloaded — a deliberate
  compression strategy beats an unfinished sentence.

## 7. Booth etiquette and mic discipline
- Mic **off** before you cough, rustle or speak to your boothmate; use the cough/mute
  key. Assume the mic is live until the light says otherwise.
- Hand over at sentence boundaries, typically every **~30 minutes** (common practice in
  professional standards; check local contract terms).
- The passive colleague is not on a break: they note numbers, look up terms, watch the
  speaker list, and are ready to take over.
- When working from **relay**, keep your lag a little longer — the pivot interpreter
  is already behind and may self-correct.

## 8. Recovery when you lose it
1. Finish the current sentence grammatically, even if general.
2. Drop the lost detail; catch the next unit fresh.
3. Never fill silence with "um, sorry"; a short pause is acceptable.
4. If a whole passage is lost (bad sound), tell the audience: "The interpreter could
   not hear the speaker" — sound problems must be flagged, not covered up.

## 9. Practice drills
- **Shadowing** in the same language at a lag of 3–5 words.
- **Dual-task**: shadow while writing numbers 100 → 1 backwards.
- **Paraphrase simultaneous** (same language, different wording).
- Record yourself and compare against the transcript; mark omissions vs additions.

## Verify
- [ ] On a recorded speech, your lag stays in a stable range and you don't freeze on long sentences.
- [ ] Every sentence you start, you finish.
- [ ] Numbers and names are correct (check against transcript).
- [ ] Mic was muted for every cough, aside or handover.
- [ ] Omissions are deliberate compressions of redundancy, not lost content.

*Uncertainty:* lag ranges and turn length are norms from training literature and AIIC-style
practice; exact figures vary by pair, market and contract.
