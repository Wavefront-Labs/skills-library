# Conflict-zone and field interpreting — impartiality and survival

**Goal:** interpret accurately for military, humanitarian, journalistic or diplomatic
clients in a crisis setting, keep your role clear to everyone around you, and come home
safe — during the assignment and afterwards.

## 1. Who is exposed, and how
Many field interpreters are **locally recruited**. They face risks that expatriate
staff do not: being seen as collaborators, threats to family, and loss of protection
when the employer leaves. Be realistic about this before you accept work. The
AIIC/Red T/FIT *Conflict Zone Field Guide* is the reference both interpreters and users
should read (paraphrased here).

## 2. Before deployment: agree in writing
- Your **role**: interpreter only, or also fixer, guide, driver, cultural adviser?
  Mixed roles are common but increase risk and blur impartiality — name them.
- **Protective equipment** (body armour, helmet) equal to the team's.
- **Insurance**, medical evacuation, and what happens if you're injured.
- **Identity protection**: may you cover your face, use a pseudonym, be left out of
  photos and publications?
- **After the mission**: relocation or visa provisions if your work becomes known.
  *Uncertainty:* national schemes (e.g. for military interpreters) change often and
  vary widely — get specifics from the employer, not assumptions.

## 3. Impartiality under pressure
- Interpret what was said, both directions, completely — including threats, insults
  and lies. Softening a commander's or a militia leader's words can cause the very
  incident it was meant to prevent.
- Don't negotiate on anyone's behalf; don't add your own warnings. If you see a
  danger the client doesn't, tell the client **privately and as yourself**, clearly
  marked: "This is my own observation, not interpretation."
- Expect both sides to test you ("Tell him X but don't translate this"). Answer: "I
  interpret everything." Say it out loud in both languages.
- Dress and conduct: avoid symbols of either side where you can.

## 4. Technique in the field
- **Checkpoints:** short, calm, consecutive; keep your hands visible; let the team
  leader speak, you render. Don't argue with armed personnel in your own voice.
- **Negotiations:** position yourself between the principals, slightly back; interpret
  in first person so principals address each other.
- **Interviews with victims or detainees:** slow pace, simple sentences, confirm
  understanding; flag your own emotional overload to the team leader.
- **Noise and distance:** step closer rather than shout; repeat key numbers and
  place names back.
- Carry a pocket glossary for military, medical, legal and humanitarian terms.

## 5. Personal security habits
- Keep your phone clean of sensitive contacts and photos; know what's on social media
  about you and your family.
- Vary routes; don't advertise your employer; keep a personal grab bag.
- Know the evacuation plan and your team's comms schedule; check in.
- If threatened, report to the employer immediately and document it.

## 6. Afterwards
- Vicarious and direct trauma are expected, not weakness. Ask the employer for
  psychological support; peer networks of former field interpreters exist.
- Keep copies of contracts, references and ID — they matter for later protection or
  visa claims.

## Verify
- [ ] Role, protective equipment, insurance and identity protection agreed in writing.
- [ ] You've said "I interpret everything" to both sides and kept to it.
- [ ] Personal warnings to the client are clearly marked as your own.
- [ ] Phone, social media and routes reviewed for exposure.
- [ ] Post-mission support and documentation arranged.

*Uncertainty:* written from published guidance rather than field experience; security
practice depends heavily on the specific conflict and employer.
