# Sign language interpreting — processing time, voicing and teaming

**Goal:** interpret between a signed language (ASL, BSL, Auslan, LSF…) and a spoken
language so that Deaf and hearing participants both get the full message, in the
right register, at a usable lag.

## 1. Processing time (lag)
- Signed and spoken languages differ in grammar (spatial reference, classifiers,
  topic-comment structure, non-manual markers). You need enough lag to grasp the
  *meaning* before restructuring — often several seconds; Cokely's research linked very
  short lag to more miscues. *Exact ideal lag is debated; treat "a few seconds" as a
  working norm.*
- Shorten lag for turn-taking moments (questions, jokes timing); lengthen for dense
  content. Watch for losing the speaker's next point while you're still producing.

## 2. Voice-to-sign
- Use **space**: set up referents (people, places, ideas) at locations and point back.
- Non-manual markers (eyebrows for questions, mouth morphemes, head tilts) are grammar,
  not decoration — dropping them changes meaning.
- Fingerspell names once, then establish a **name sign** or point if the speaker/
  audience agrees. Ask the presenter for slides and names ahead.
- Don't sign in spoken-language word order ("signed English") unless the consumer
  requests it.

## 3. Sign-to-voice ("voicing")
- Many interpreters find this direction harder. Common faults: flat monotone, lag too
  long, hesitations that make the Deaf speaker sound unsure.
- Choose a **voice and register that match the signer** (age, gender presentation,
  formality, humour). The audience judges the Deaf person by your voice.
- Use first person. Don't narrate ("she is saying…").
- If you missed something: ask the signer for repetition openly, rather than guessing.
- Prep matters even more here — know the Deaf presenter's topic, slides and name signs.

## 4. Team interpreting
- Assignments over roughly an hour, or demanding content, are typically teamed, with
  rotations around **20–30 minutes** (a common professional norm; check local
  standards and contracts).
- **The off interpreter "feeds"**: sits where the working interpreter can see them,
  supplies missed numbers, names, fingerspelling, or a sign, discreetly; monitors
  accuracy. Agree feeding signals in advance.
- **Deaf interpreters (CDI/DI)** work with hearing interpreters when the consumer uses
  non-standard signing, a foreign sign language, has minimal language, is a child, or in
  legal/medical high-stakes settings. The hearing interpreter feeds the DI, who
  renders into the most accessible signing. Request a DI when in doubt.

## 5. Platform, stage and remote
- **Placement:** near the speaker, in the Deaf audience's sightline, not in front of a
  bright window or projector beam; plain dark top contrasting with skin tone.
- **Lighting:** front-lit face and hands; if the room darkens for slides, you need a spotlight.
- On **video (VRS/VRI)**: plain background, framing from waist to above the head with
  room for signing space, stable connection; ask the Deaf caller to adjust if needed.
- Livestreams: confirm the picture-in-picture is large enough to read handshapes.

## 6. Ethics and community
- Deaf community norms matter: the community's trust is earned; respect consumer
  preferences for interpreters.
- Confidentiality; impartiality; work within competence; decline if you know a party
  personally in high-stakes settings.
- Credentials: RID (US), NRCPD/RSLI (UK), NAATI (Australia). *Check the current body
  and level for your country.*
- The Demand Control Schema is a widely used framework for reasoning through ethical
  and situational decisions.

## Verify
- [ ] Prep obtained: slides, names, agenda, name signs agreed.
- [ ] Placement and lighting give every Deaf participant a clear sightline.
- [ ] Voicing matches the signer's register, first person, without long dead air.
- [ ] Feeding signals and rotation agreed with your team.
- [ ] A Deaf interpreter was requested where the consumer or setting needed one.
