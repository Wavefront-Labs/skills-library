# Consecutive interpreting — notes that serve memory, and clean delivery

**Goal:** listen to a segment (a sentence up to several minutes), then deliver it
completely and naturally in the other language, using notes as a *prompt for memory*,
not a transcript.

## 1. Listen first, write second
Notes fail when you write words. Analyse while listening: *who does what, why, and how
does this connect to the last point?* Write the **idea** and the **link**. Most of the
content should come back from memory triggered by a few marks.

## 2. Layout (Rozan's principles, still the standard)
- **Narrow pad, top-to-bottom** (a spiral-top stenographer's pad; flip pages, don't turn).
- **One idea per block**, a horizontal line between ideas.
- **Diagonal/shift ("décalage")**: subject top-left, verb a step right and down, object
  further right and down. Your eye reads structure at a glance.
- **Verticality**: list items stacked one under another; parallel ideas aligned.
- **Links on the left margin**: because, but, therefore, so, if — these are what
  listeners lose most and are cheapest to note.
- **Negation**: strike-through or "x". **Emphasis**: underline (twice = very).
- **Tense**: a mark after the verb (e.g. `'` for past, `ll` for future) — choose one
  system and keep it.

## 3. Symbols: few, stable, yours
- Start with ~20–30 and grow slowly. A symbol you hesitate over costs more than a word.
- Make them *concepts*, not words: one symbol for "country" serves nation/state/national.
- Common choices: `→` lead to/send, `↑` increase, `↓` decrease, `=` same/equal,
  `≠` different, `□` country, `○` person, `?` question/problem, `!` important.
  These are conventions many trainers teach, but no list is official — build your own.
- Use the **target-language** abbreviations if that speeds delivery; many interpreters
  mix freely.

## 4. Always note exactly
- **Numbers, dates, names, titles, lists** — memory can't reconstruct these.
- The **first and last sentence** of the segment (openings and conclusions are judged).
- Anything you didn't understand (ring it; ask the speaker before delivering, if the
  setting allows).

## 5. Delivery
1. Glance at the whole page sequence before you start; take one breath.
2. **Look up at the audience**; eyes go down to the pad only between ideas.
3. Speak in full, natural sentences — reconstruct the speaker's logic, using the links
   in your margin to make the structure audible.
4. Use the first person ("I propose…"), not "he says that…".
5. Match register and keep pace even; no reading-aloud monotone.
6. Delivery should take *no longer* than the original, often less.

## 6. Short vs long consecutive
- **Dialogue/short consecutive** (medical, legal, business): segments of a sentence or
  two, notes mostly numbers and names. Manage turn length — politely interrupt with a
  hand gesture before a speaker runs past what you can hold.
- **Long consecutive** (speeches, toasts): 3–5+ minutes; the full note system matters.

## 7. Common failures
- Writing so much you stop listening → content gaps exactly where you wrote most.
- Private symbols invented on the day → illegible at delivery.
- Losing the page order → number pages or flip strictly one direction.
- Delivering the notes' word order → calques; rebuild the sentence from the idea.

## 8. Practice
- Memory-only retell of a 2-minute speech in the same language before adding notes.
- Take notes, wait 24 hours, deliver from them: shows which marks truly trigger memory.
- Record and compare against the source; tally omissions, distortions, additions.

## Verify
- [ ] Notes show structure at a glance (blocks, shift, margin links).
- [ ] All numbers, names and lists rendered exactly.
- [ ] You looked at the audience for most of the delivery.
- [ ] Delivery time ≤ source time, first person, complete sentences.
- [ ] A recorded check finds no lost logical links (but/therefore/because).

*Uncertainty:* symbol examples are common teaching conventions, not a standard; segment
lengths vary by school and market.
