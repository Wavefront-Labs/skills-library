# Learning characters by components — hanzi and kanji that stick

**Goal:** learn thousands of Chinese characters (hanzi) or Japanese kanji efficiently by
decomposing them into reusable parts, write them correctly, and look up any unknown one.

> Draft. Counts and list sizes are approximate (e.g. Japan's jōyō list is 2,136 kanji
> since 2010; Chinese literacy is commonly put at ~2,500–3,500 characters). Method is
> standard learner practice, not formal research.

## 1. Characters are built, not memorised as pictures
- Only a small share of characters are pictographs (山, 日, 木). Most are compounds.
- **Phono-semantic compounds** are the majority in Chinese: a **semantic component**
  (hints at meaning, often the radical) + a **phonetic component** (hints at
  pronunciation). E.g. 妈 (mā, mother) = 女 (woman) + 马 (mǎ, horse → sound).
  Phonetic hints are often approximate after sound change — treat them as hints.
- Learning ~200–300 common components turns new characters into recombinations of
  known parts.

## 2. A learning order that works
1. **Core components first:** the ~100 most productive radicals/components (人/亻, 口, 女,
   木, 水/氵, 火/灬, 心/忄, 手/扌, 言/讠, 金/钅, 糸/纟, 艹, 宀, 辶…). Learn the variant
   forms when used as parts.
2. **Then characters in frequency order**, but group by shared components when that's
   natural (all the 氵 water characters in a batch).
3. **Then words**, not isolated characters: most meaning lives in two-character words
   (Chinese) or kanji compounds with their readings (Japanese).
4. Japanese: learn **readings in words** — on'yomi (Sino-Japanese) in compounds, kun'yomi
   (native) usually with okurigana. Don't memorise every reading in isolation.

## 3. Stroke order and handwriting
Stroke order matters for legible handwriting, for cursive forms, and for stroke-count
dictionary lookup. Core rules (with exceptions):
- Top before bottom; left before right.
- Horizontal before vertical when they cross (十).
- Outside before inside; close the frame last (国: frame, inside, bottom stroke).
- Centre before symmetric sides (小, 水).
- Left-falling before right-falling (人).
Check each new character against a stroke-order animation; national standards differ
slightly between mainland China, Taiwan and Japan.

## 4. Looking up a character you can't read
- **Handwriting input** on phone/OS keyboards or dictionary apps: draw it — stroke order
  helps recognition.
- **Camera/OCR lookup** in dictionary apps for printed text.
- **Radical + residual stroke count** in paper dictionaries (214 Kangxi radicals).
- **Component search** in apps that let you pick parts.

## 5. Retention system
- Spaced repetition (Anki or similar) with three card directions as needed:
  character → meaning/reading, meaning → **write** it (by hand, then check), word →
  reading.
- **Mnemonic stories** from components (the Heisig/"Remembering the Kanji" approach) help
  meaning recall fast; still connect to real readings and words.
- Daily new-card budget: ~10–20 characters keeps reviews manageable; review load grows
  for months, so cut new cards before you burn out.
- Extensive reading (graded readers) consolidates far more than cards alone.

## Gotchas
- **Simplified vs traditional** (and Japanese shinjitai) forms differ: 马/馬, 们/們,
  国/國. Pick the set for your target region; learn to recognise the other.
- Look-alikes: 未/末, 土/士, 己/已/巳, 人/入 — drill as minimal pairs.
- Recognition outpaces handwriting recall fast; if you need to write, review in the
  write direction.
- Character ≠ word, especially in Japanese; don't assume a kanji's "meaning" predicts the
  compound's.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name the semantic and phonetic components of 10 new compound characters.
- [ ] You write 20 random known characters with correct stroke order (checked).
- [ ] You can look up an unknown printed character in under a minute without pinyin/romaji.
- [ ] Your review backlog stays under one day's worth for four weeks running.
