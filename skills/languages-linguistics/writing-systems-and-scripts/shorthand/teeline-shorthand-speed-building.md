# Teeline shorthand — from theory to 100 wpm

**Goal:** take accurate handwritten notes of speech at journalistic speeds (UK trainee
reporters commonly target 100 wpm for the NCTJ exam) and transcribe them reliably.

> Draft. Teeline specifics summarised from the system as commonly taught; outline details
> differ between textbooks and tutors — follow your course book. Speed figures are
> typical targets, not guarantees.

## 1. Pick the system
- **Teeline** (1968, UK): based on simplified longhand letters with vowels mostly
  dropped. Fastest to learn to a working level; dominant in UK journalism training.
- **Pitman** (1837): phonetic, thick/thin strokes and positions; very high ceilings, longer
  to learn, pen pressure matters.
- **Gregg** (US): phonetic, cursive, vowel circles and hooks; historically dominant in
  US secretarial/court work.
The habits below (drilling outlines, graded dictation, reading back) apply to all three.

## 2. Teeline theory in brief
- **Alphabet:** each letter stripped to its distinctive stroke (e.g. T as just the
  crossbar-like stroke). Learn the alphabet until instant.
- **Drop vowels** inside words; keep initial and some final vowels. Context restores them.
- **Drop silent letters** and doubled letters; write what's heard where the book allows.
- **Blends:** common combinations get merged forms (e.g. -tion, -ing, -ment; letter
  pairs like CM/CN, PR/PL, TR/DR written as one movement). Exact blend list: your book.
- **Word groupings/phrases:** frequent phrases joined as one outline ("in the", "it is",
  "I would").
- **Special outlines** for the most frequent words (the, and, of, to, have, would,
  should…) — learn as sight-words; they're a large share of running speech.
- **Position and disjoining** indicate some prefixes/suffixes and help reading back.

## 3. Speed-building plan
1. **Theory to automatic:** 4–8 weeks, daily 30–45 min. Read and copy the textbook
   outlines until you can write any outline without hesitation.
2. **Start dictation at 40–50 wpm** using graded passages (timed audio is widely sold
   and some training bodies publish practice material).
3. **The 3-pass method:** take a passage at target speed; transcribe; correct against
   the text; re-take the same passage at +10 wpm.
4. **Raise speed by 10 wpm when you hit ~97–98% accuracy** at the current speed (exams
   typically allow very few errors).
5. **Short daily sessions beat weekly marathons.** 20–30 minutes of dictation, every day.
6. **Plateaus at 60–80 wpm are normal:** fix them by drilling special outlines and
   phrase groups, not by writing bigger and faster.

## 4. Reading back
- **Transcribe the same day.** Cold notes are much harder.
- Read the whole sentence for sense before fixing a doubtful outline; context restores
  vowels.
- Mark doubtful outlines immediately with a ring; check against audio if available.
- In journalism, **verbatim quotes must be exact**: if you can't read a quote back
  confidently, don't publish it as a quote.

## 5. Kit
- Spiral-top reporter's notebook (pages flip over without the wire getting in the way).
- Smooth fine pen or sharp pencil; Pitman favours a pen that shows thick/thin.
- Write on one side, left margin for time markers or note flags.

## Gotchas
- Writing outlines too large — small and consistent is faster.
- Inventing your own abbreviations mid-course: they clash with system outlines.
- Practising only with familiar passages (you memorise the content).
- Skipping transcription practice — exam marks come from the transcript.

## Verify
- [ ] Alphabet, blends and the special outlines list written perfectly from memory.
- [ ] A fresh, unseen passage at your current speed transcribed at ≥97% accuracy.
- [ ] Your speed log shows steady +10 wpm steps over weeks.
- [ ] Quotes transcribed from notes match the audio word-for-word.
