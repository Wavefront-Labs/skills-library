# Reactivating and transmitting a heritage language

**Goal:** (a) for an adult heritage speaker, turn a childhood home language — often
understood but barely spoken — into active use; (b) for a family, keep the language
alive in the next generation despite the pull of the majority language.

## Part A — reactivating your own heritage language

### 1. Diagnose the typical profile
Heritage speakers are usually **uneven**, unlike classroom learners:
- strong: listening, native-like pronunciation, everyday/home vocabulary, intuitions;
- weak: speaking confidence, formal register, literacy, abstract/adult vocabulary,
  some morphology (e.g. case, aspect, agreement) simplified or lost.
Record a 5-minute chat with a relative and a 1-page reading attempt; note where you
switch to the majority language — those are your gaps.

### 2. Methods that fit
- **Receptive → active**: you already understand; the bottleneck is retrieval. Daily low-stakes
  speaking: voice notes to relatives, calls with one patient family member who agrees not to
  switch languages.
- **Register expansion**: news, podcasts and documentaries for adult/formal vocabulary the
  family never used at home.
- **Literacy catch-up**: learn the script first if it differs; then read children's and
  graded material quickly (your oral vocabulary will race ahead of decoding), then move up.
- **Heritage-learner classes** (where they exist) are better than foreign-language classes,
  which drill what you already know and skip what you lack.

### 3. Emotional side
Shame about "bad" grammar and correction from relatives are major blocks. Agree with family
that correction happens later, not mid-sentence. Expect dialect differences from the
standard taught in books; both are legitimate — learn the standard as an extra register,
not a replacement.

## Part B — transmitting to children

### 4. Family language policy
- Choose a model and write it down: **OPOL** (one parent, one language), **minority language
  at home** (everyone uses the heritage language at home), or time/place-based.
- Input share matters: children who hear a language only a small fraction of their waking
  time often become receptive-only. *Thresholds cited in the literature vary (roughly
  20–30%+ is often mentioned); treat as a warning sign, not a rule.*
- The main danger point is **starting school**: the majority language floods in and children
  begin answering in it. Plan for it before it happens.

### 5. Sustaining it
- Create **need**: people who only speak the heritage language (grandparents by video,
  community events, trips).
- Fun content in the language: shows, songs, games, books read aloud nightly.
- Community or Saturday schools for literacy; start reading early in both scripts if needed.
- Respond in the heritage language even when the child answers in the majority one; gently
  rephrase rather than refuse.

## Gotchas
- Forcing or punishing language choice usually backfires in teenage years.
- Parents slipping into the majority language "just for homework" is how shift begins.
- Comparing children to monolingual norms leads to false worry; mixing languages is normal.
- Professionals sometimes wrongly advise dropping the home language; evidence does not
  support that bilingualism causes language delay.

## Verify
- [ ] Adult: you can hold a 15-minute conversation without switching and read a news article.
- [ ] Adult: your gap list (register, literacy, specific grammar) shrinks over 3 months.
- [ ] Family: the policy is written down and both parents/carers follow it.
- [ ] Family: the child uses the language actively (not only understands) with at least one person.
- [ ] Family: a plan exists for school entry and the teenage years.
