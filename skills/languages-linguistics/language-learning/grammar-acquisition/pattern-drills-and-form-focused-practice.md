# Pattern drills and form-focused practice

**Goal:** move a grammar point from "I can explain it" (explicit knowledge) to "I use it
correctly at speaking speed without thinking" (automatised use). Knowing a rule and using
it are separate skills; most stalled learners have the first without the second.

## 1. The cycle for one grammar point
1. **Brief rule (10–20 min).** One clear explanation and 5–10 model sentences. Skip the
   exception list for now.
2. **Noticing in input (days).** Find the structure in things you read and hear; highlight
   or collect 20–30 real examples. Input frequency matters: points you rarely meet will not
   stick from drills alone.
3. **Controlled practice.** Drills that force the form while keeping meaning in play:
   - *Substitution*: "I bought **bread**" → cue *milk* → "I bought milk" (fine for agreement,
     case endings, articles).
   - *Transformation*: present → past, statement → question, active → passive.
   - *Meaningful drills*: the answer must be true for you ("What did you do yesterday?"),
     which forces processing meaning as well as form.
   Say them aloud, at increasing speed. Writing alone builds a slower system.
4. **Semi-free practice.** Short tasks that naturally require the form: describe last
   weekend (past tense), give directions (imperatives), compare two cities (comparatives).
5. **Free output with targeted correction.** In conversation or writing, ask your tutor or
   partner to correct **only** this point for a week.

## 2. Conjugation and case systems
- Learn paradigms as **high-frequency verbs in sentences**, not as abstract tables:
  the 10–20 most common verbs carry most irregularity (*be, have, go, do, say* equivalents).
- Learn nouns with gender/class and the forms that predict declension (e.g. German
  plural, Russian genitive singular, Latin genitive).
- Use cloze cards for endings: `Ich gebe {{c1::dem}} Mann das Buch.`

## 3. Correction that helps
- **Recasts** (the partner repeats your sentence correctly) are often missed; ask for
  **prompts** instead ("again?", "past tense?") so you self-correct.
- Keep an **error log**: each recurring error, the correct form, two example sentences.
  Review weekly; retire entries after three error-free weeks.

## 4. Sequencing
Some structures are acquired in a fairly fixed developmental order regardless of teaching
order (e.g. question formation stages in English). If a point keeps failing in speech
despite drilling, it may be beyond your current stage — park it and return in a month.
*This "teachability" idea is well attested for some structures, contested for others.*

## Gotchas
- **Drill-only fluency**: perfect in drills, gone in conversation. Always finish with free output.
- **Rule paralysis**: monitoring every sentence kills fluency; monitor one point at a time.
- **Textbook register**: drills often use formal forms natives rarely say; check spoken usage.
- **Fossilisation**: an error tolerated in fluent speech for months becomes very hard to
  fix — catch frequent errors early.
- Grammar apps that only test recognition (multiple choice) build little productive skill.

## Verify
- [ ] You can explain the rule in one or two sentences with an example.
- [ ] You produce the form correctly in a timed spoken drill (e.g. 20 cues in under a minute).
- [ ] In a recorded 5-minute free conversation, the form appears where needed and is
      correct in most (aim ≥80%) obligatory contexts.
- [ ] The point has left your error log after three clean weeks.
