# Frequency lists and sentence mining — learn the words that pay

**Goal:** spend vocabulary effort where it returns the most comprehension: the high-frequency
core first, then words you actually meet, each learned in a real sentence and kept alive
by scheduled review.

## 1. Why frequency first
Word frequency follows a steep curve (Zipf): a small set of words covers most running text.
Rough, widely quoted figures for English (other languages differ, and inflection-heavy
languages need more *forms* for the same coverage):
- ~1,000 word families → roughly 80–85% of everyday speech and general text.
- ~3,000 families → enough to start reading graded/easier native material with help.
- Reading comfortably needs ~95% coverage (1 unknown word in 20); unassisted pleasure
  reading closer to 98% (1 in 50), which for English novels is in the region of 8,000–9,000
  families. *Treat these as orders of magnitude, not targets — counts depend on how you
  define a "word" (lemma vs family) and on the corpus.*

## 2. Getting a usable list
- Prefer lists built from **subtitles or spoken corpora** (e.g. SUBTLEX-style lists) for
  conversation, and from written corpora for reading goals.
- Use **lemmas** (dictionary forms), not raw tokens, or the list fills with *is/was/were*.
- Strip names and items you already know; batch the remainder by rank (1–500, 501–1,000…).
- Past ~3,000–5,000 the list stops being useful — words that rare are better chosen by
  *your* reading and listening than by a general corpus.

## 3. Sentence mining (the core routine)
1. While reading/listening, note a sentence with **exactly one** unknown item (the
   "i+1" sentence). Two or more unknowns make a poor card.
2. Copy the full sentence, the target word, its dictionary form, and a short gloss —
   ideally a target-language definition once you can read one.
3. Add audio where possible (from the source, or TTS clearly marked as such).
4. Card front: the sentence with the word **highlighted** (recognition). Add a
   production card (gloss → word) only for words you need to *say*.
5. Cap intake: 10–20 new cards a day is sustainable for most people; reviews grow to
   roughly 5–10× daily new cards after a few weeks.

## 4. Mnemonics — selectively
Use the **keyword method** (link the foreign word's sound to a vivid image of its meaning)
for words that refuse to stick after 3–4 lapses, not for everything. Concrete nouns take
mnemonics well; function words and abstract verbs are better learned from many sentences.

## 5. Learn chunks, not just words
Record the collocation you met it in (*make a decision*, not *decision*), its gender or
noun class, irregular forms, and the preposition/case it governs. A bare word you cannot
use in a phrase is half-learned.

## Gotchas
- **False friends**: flag cognates whose meanings diverge (Spanish *embarazada* ≠
  embarrassed). Put the warning on the card.
- **Polysemy**: one card per sense you have actually met; don't preload all dictionary senses.
- **Over-collection**: mining every unknown word creates a review debt that kills the habit.
  Skip anything you are unlikely to meet again.
- **Recognition ≠ recall**: passing a card does not mean you can produce the word in speech.
- Machine glosses for rare senses are often wrong; check against a monolingual dictionary.

## Verify
- [ ] Your list is lemmatised and ordered by a corpus that matches your goal (spoken vs written).
- [ ] Every mined card has one unknown item, a full real sentence, and its source.
- [ ] Daily new-card intake is fixed and reviews are done before new cards.
- [ ] Spot-check: read a fresh page at your level and count unknown words — the share
      should fall month on month (aim for <5% before calling a text "your level").
- [ ] Lapse-prone words got a mnemonic or were deleted, not just re-failed indefinitely.
