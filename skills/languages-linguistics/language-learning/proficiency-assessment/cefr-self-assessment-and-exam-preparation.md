# CEFR self-assessment and exam preparation

**Goal:** know your real level in each skill, and if you need a certificate, pass the
specific exam with the score you need — which is a partly separate skill from knowing
the language.

## 1. CEFR in one screen
Six levels: **A1–A2** basic user, **B1–B2** independent, **C1–C2** proficient. Levels are
defined by *can-do* descriptors per activity (listening, reading, spoken interaction,
spoken production, writing, mediation). Profiles are usually uneven — B2 reading with
B1 speaking is normal; assess skills separately.
Typical usage: B1 = handle travel and familiar topics; B2 = work/study in the language with
effort; C1 = effective professional and academic use.

## 2. Self-assessment that isn't wishful
1. Read the CEFR self-assessment grid (Companion Volume) for each skill; tick only what you
   can do *now, reliably*.
2. Back each claim with evidence: a recording, a written text, a timed reading.
3. Calibrate once with an outside check: a tutor rating against the CEFR, or a free official
   sample test. Self-ratings commonly overestimate speaking and writing, and underestimate reading.

## 3. Pick the exam for the purpose
- **English**: IELTS (Academic for study, General Training for some migration/work);
  TOEFL iBT; Cambridge exams. Band/score requirement comes from the institution — check the
  per-section minimum, not just overall.
- **Japanese**: JLPT N5 (easiest) → N1. Tests vocabulary/grammar, reading and listening only —
  **no speaking or writing**. Held a limited number of times a year with fixed registration windows.
- **Spanish**: DELE A1–C2 (Instituto Cervantes), one level per exam, pass/fail;
  SIELE is a multi-level scored alternative.
- **Chinese**: HSK. *A revised multi-level framework (HSK 3.0, nine levels) is being phased in
  alongside the older six-level test; confirm which version your test centre runs.*
- **French/German**: DELF/DALF; Goethe-Zertifikat, TestDaF for study.
Exam formats and fees change: always read the current official handbook.

## 4. Preparation plan (8–12 weeks out)
1. **Diagnostic mock** under real timing. Score each section against the target.
2. **Learn the format** cold: number of tasks, timing, answer-sheet rules, whether spelling
   counts, how writing is scored (rubric criteria and weightings).
3. **Weakest section first**, but keep all sections ticking.
4. **Rubric-driven writing/speaking**: get model answers at your target band; practise the task
   types; get scored feedback against the official criteria (e.g. IELTS criteria cover task
   response, coherence and cohesion, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy).
5. **Timed full mocks** weekly in the final month; review every wrong answer by cause
   (didn't know, misread, ran out of time).
6. **Logistics**: ID requirements, arrival time, permitted items, computer- vs paper-based.

## Gotchas
- Passing JLPT N2 doesn't mean you can hold a conversation; HSK levels don't map neatly to CEFR —
  official equivalence claims are disputed.
- Memorised template essays are often detected and penalised.
- Under-length writing is penalised in many exams; over-length can cost time elsewhere.
- Listening often plays only once — practise single-play.
- Certificates may have validity periods for admission/immigration (IELTS/TOEFL results are
  commonly accepted for two years); check the receiving body.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a per-skill CEFR estimate with evidence and one external calibration.
- [ ] You have the receiving body's exact score requirement in writing, including section minimums.
- [ ] Your last two timed mocks meet the target with a margin in every section.
- [ ] You've read the current official handbook and sample paper for your exam date.
- [ ] Registration, ID and test-day logistics are confirmed.
