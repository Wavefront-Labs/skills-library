# Graded input and dictation — from learner audio to native speed

**Goal:** understand natural speech at normal speed. Listening fails for two different
reasons — you don't know the words (vocabulary) or you know them but can't pick them out
of fast connected speech (decoding). Extensive listening fixes the first slowly; intensive
dictation fixes the second fast.

## 1. Choosing the level
- **Extensive listening**: material where you follow the story/argument without strain —
  roughly 90%+ of it understood. Learner podcasts, graded audiobooks, slow-news services,
  children's TV, then familiar-genre native content.
- **Intensive listening**: short clips (30 s–2 min) at or a little above your level, with an
  exact transcript available.
- If you are lost after two minutes, the material is too hard for extensive use; keep it
  for intensive work or set it aside.

## 2. Daily structure (example: 45 min)
- 30 min **extensive**: one longer piece, no stopping, no lookups. Repeat favourites —
  re-listening to the same episode 3–5 times is efficient, not lazy.
- 15 min **intensive**: one short clip with the dictation routine below.

## 3. Dictation routine
1. Play the clip once through for gist.
2. Play one phrase (3–8 words), pause, write exactly what you heard. Replay up to 3–4 times.
   Leave gaps rather than guessing from meaning.
3. Compare with the transcript. Classify every miss:
   - unknown word → vocabulary card;
   - known word not recognised → a **decoding** issue (reduction, liaison, elision,
     unexpected stress);
   - grammar ending missed → note it.
4. Re-listen to each decoding miss while reading, until you hear it, then without text.
5. Finish by listening to the whole clip once more; it should now sound clear.
Decoding misses are the valuable ones: they reveal the patterns of connected speech
(English *gonna*, *wanna*, /t/ flapping; French liaison; Spanish synalepha) that textbooks skip.

## 4. Subtitles and speed — use deliberately
- **Target-language subtitles** help link sound and spelling; native-language subtitles
  mostly turn listening into reading. Watch first with TL subs, then again without.
- **Slowing to 0.75×** helps decoding work; don't live there. Most players keep pitch
  natural at modest slowdowns but artefacts grow below ~0.7×.
- Speeding to 1.25× on familiar material can make normal speed feel easier later.
  *Evidence for this is anecdotal; treat it as optional.*

## 5. Stepping up
Move along: learner podcasts → slow news → interviews/podcasts with two native speakers →
drama → unscripted multi-speaker talk with overlap, slang and regional accents (hardest).
Add one new accent/variety at a time.

## Gotchas
- **Background-only listening** (while working) builds little unless the material is already
  easy. Attention matters.
- Endless intensive work on hard clips is exhausting; keep the extensive volume up.
- Phone speakers and noisy rooms make decoding harder — use headphones for intensive work.
- Auto-generated captions contain errors; prefer human transcripts for dictation checking.
- Comprehension questions you can answer from general knowledge aren't testing listening.

## Verify
- [ ] Your extensive material is understood well enough that you rarely want to pause.
- [ ] Dictation error rate on comparable new clips falls over weeks (track misses per minute).
- [ ] Your decoding-miss notes show patterns you now recognise (specific reductions, liaisons).
- [ ] You can summarise a 5-minute native-speed clip in the target language after one listen.
- [ ] Once a month, test on a genre/accent you haven't practised to check transfer.
