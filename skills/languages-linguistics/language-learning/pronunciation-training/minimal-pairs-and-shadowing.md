# Minimal pairs and shadowing — perception first, then production

**Goal:** become easily intelligible in the target language by fixing the few contrasts
and prosody habits that actually cause misunderstanding — not chase a "no accent" ideal.
Research separates *accentedness* from *intelligibility*; a noticeable accent is fine,
mishearable words are not.

## 1. Find the contrasts that matter
- List target-language phonemes that your first language merges or lacks. Typical
  examples: English /ɪ/–/iː/ (*ship/sheep*) for Spanish speakers; /l/–/r/ for Japanese
  speakers; French /y/–/u/ (*tu/tout*); Mandarin tones; Arabic pharyngeals.
- Record yourself reading a short passage; have a native speaker (or tutor) mark words
  they misheard. Rank problems by **functional load** — how many real word pairs the
  contrast distinguishes. Fix high-load contrasts first.

## 2. Perception before production
You rarely produce reliably a contrast you cannot hear.
1. Build or find **minimal-pair** lists (two words differing in one sound).
2. Do identification drills: hear one word, pick which it was; get immediate feedback.
3. Use **many voices** (high-variability phonetic training): recordings from several
   speakers generalise much better than one teacher's voice.
4. Target >90% accuracy on new speakers before moving on. Short daily sessions
   (10–15 min) beat long weekly ones.

## 3. Set the articulation
- Look up the sound's IPA symbol and description (place, manner, voicing, lip rounding,
  tongue height). Use a mirror or phone camera for lip shape.
- Use bridges: French /y/ = say /i/ ("ee") and round the lips without moving the tongue.
- For tone and intonation, a pitch-tracking tool (e.g. Praat, free) shows your contour
  against a native one. *Pitch tracks are noisy on creaky voice and voiceless sounds — look
  at the overall shape, not every wiggle.*
- Practise in isolation → syllable → word → phrase → sentence; fluency regresses at each step.

## 4. Shadowing for rhythm and intonation
1. Pick 30–90 s of clear native audio **with a transcript**, at or slightly below your level.
2. Listen through twice; read the transcript; mark stresses, pauses and pitch moves.
3. Read along *with* the audio (synchronised reading), then shadow without the text,
   speaking a fraction of a second behind.
4. Record your shadow and compare by ear: where you lag, rush or flatten.
5. Repeat the same clip over several days until it's effortless, then change clip.

## 5. Transfer to free speech
Choose 2–3 target features per week and monitor only those in conversation. Ask a
partner to flag just those. Trying to monitor everything at once stalls speech.

## Gotchas
- **Spelling interference**: learners pronounce the letters (English *-ed* as /ɛd/ in *walked*).
  Learn from audio, not text.
- **Hyper-correction**: once a new sound is learned it leaks into words that don't have it.
- **One-speaker trap**: imitating one YouTuber gives you their idiolect, quirks included.
- Prosody (stress, rhythm, intonation) often affects intelligibility more than individual
  vowels — don't spend months on one vowel while sentence stress is wrong.
- Tonal languages: drill tone *pairs* (tone sandhi, e.g. Mandarin 3+3 → 2+3) not isolated tones.
- Physical strain or hoarseness means you are forcing; stop and reset.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a written, ranked list of your 3–5 problem contrasts.
- [ ] Identification accuracy on unfamiliar speakers is >90% for each drilled contrast.
- [ ] Recordings a month apart show the contrast audibly (a native listener confirms).
- [ ] A shadowed clip can be produced at native speed without the transcript.
- [ ] In unscripted conversation, listeners stop asking you to repeat the target words.
