# Explaining clearly in plain language without losing accuracy

**Goal:** the reader can **find** what they need, **understand** it, and **use** it — the
three tests behind the ISO plain-language standard — without the explanation becoming wrong
through oversimplification. For organisation-wide plain-language programmes see the
media-communication plain-language skills; this is the everyday writing craft.

## 1. Pin the reader and the job
Before writing, finish these sentences:
- "My reader is… and already knows…" (be concrete: "a tenant who has never read a lease").
- "After reading, they should be able to…" (decide, do, avoid, explain to someone else).
- "The one thing they must not get wrong is…"
Everything that doesn't serve that job is a candidate for cutting or a link.

## 2. Structure for finding
- **Front-load:** the answer or most important instruction first, then reasons and detail.
- Use **headings phrased as the reader's questions** ("What if I miss the deadline?").
- Steps as **numbered lists** in the order performed; one action per step.
- Put conditions *before* the instruction: "If the light flashes red, unplug it."

## 3. Sentences and words
- Average **15–20 words per sentence**; vary length, but split anything over ~30.
- **Active voice with a clear actor:** "We will send your refund" not "Refunds will be issued".
- Use **"you"** for the reader and "we" for your organisation when appropriate.
- Prefer verbs to nouns: "decide" not "make a decision", "apply" not "submit an application".
- Everyday words: *use* not *utilise*, *start* not *commence*, *about* not *approximately*
  (unless precision matters).

## 4. Jargon without dumbing down
Not all technical terms should go. Decide per term:
- **Replace** it if a plain word means the same ("inflammation" → keep; "pruritus" → "itching").
- **Keep and define** it once if the reader will meet it again (on a form, from a doctor):
  "a *lien* — a legal claim on your property until a debt is paid".
- **Show, don't define:** a concrete example often explains faster than a definition.
- **Analogies** help but state their limit: "a fuse is like a weak link in a chain — though
  unlike a chain, it's designed to fail first."
- **Keep the caveats that change decisions** (exceptions, risks, deadlines) and drop the
  ones that don't. Accuracy failures in plain writing usually come from deleting a qualifier
  that mattered ("usually", "for adults", "in England only").

## 5. Readability scores: a smoke alarm, not a target
Formulas like Flesch-Kincaid measure sentence and word length only; they can't tell whether
text is accurate, ordered well or understood. Use them to spot dense passages, then test
with people.

## 6. Test with a real reader
- **Paraphrase test:** ask someone from the target audience to read it and explain it back,
  or do the task. Where they hesitate or misstate, rewrite.
- Ask an expert to check that simplified statements are still **true**.
- Two quick tests beat one perfect draft.

## Gotchas
- Writing for yourself or your boss instead of the reader.
- Over-simplifying into falsehood — e.g. "antibiotics kill germs" (not viruses).
- Mixing several terms for one thing ("account", "profile", "membership") — pick one and keep it.
- Long preambles about why the document exists.

## Verify
- [ ] Reader, task and must-not-get-wrong point are written down.
- [ ] The answer or first action is in the first paragraph.
- [ ] Each technical term is replaced, defined once, or shown by example — consistently named.
- [ ] Decision-changing caveats survive the edit.
- [ ] A target reader explained it back correctly, and an expert confirmed accuracy.
