# Faithful summaries at set lengths and paraphrase without distortion

**Goal:** shorten or restate someone else's text so a reader gets its real meaning — the
claim, its strength and its limits — and nobody could accuse you of misrepresenting or
copying it.

## 1. Understand before you shorten
- Read the whole text once without notes. Then answer: **What is the main claim? What
  supports it? What does the author concede or limit?**
- Mark the text's **structure** (problem → method → result; claim → reasons → objection).
  Summaries that follow the original paragraph order usually miss the argument's shape.
- Note **hedges** ("may", "in mice", "among respondents over 65"). These are the words
  summarisers lose and the ones that matter most.

## 2. Summaries at set lengths
Build from the shortest outward:
1. **One sentence:** who says what, and why it matters. "The report argues that X, mainly
   because Y, though it notes Z." If you can't write this, re-read.
2. **~100 words:** the one-sentence version plus the two or three main supports and the key
   limitation.
3. **~10% of the original:** add the method/evidence and any significant counterpoint.
Rules at every length:
- Attribute: "the author argues", "the study found", not as though it's your own view.
- Keep proportions: if the author spends half the piece on a caveat, so should you.
- No new interpretation or opinion inside the summary; put your view in a separate,
  labelled section.
- Use **your own words**; include numbers exactly.

## 3. Paraphrase: change the wording, keep the meaning
A good paraphrase is roughly the same length as the original but different in **words and
structure**.
1. Read the passage until you understand it; then **cover it**.
2. Write it from memory, as you'd explain it to a friend.
3. Uncover and compare: Is anything missing, added, stronger or weaker? Have original
   phrases crept back?
4. Cite the source — paraphrase still needs attribution.
**Patchwriting** — swapping synonyms into the original sentence skeleton — is treated as
plagiarism in most academic settings, even with a citation.

## 4. When to quote instead
Quote when the **exact words matter**: definitions, legal or contractual wording, striking
phrasing you will analyse, or a contested statement where paraphrase could be seen as
spin. Keep quotes short, mark omissions with an ellipsis (…) and additions in [square
brackets], and never cut words in a way that changes meaning.

## 5. Everyday uses
- **Meeting notes:** decisions, owners, deadlines first; discussion summary after.
- **Email TL;DR:** one line at the top of a long thread.
- **Reporting a source to a colleague:** give the claim plus its strength ("small pilot study").

## Gotchas
- Inflation: "suggests" becomes "proves", "some" becomes "most".
- Cherry-picking the part you agree with.
- Reusing distinctive phrases unconsciously — compare against the source.
- Summarising the abstract only: abstracts often overstate; check the results and limitations.
- AI-generated summaries can omit hedges or invent details; check every claim against the source.

## Verify
- [ ] One-sentence version states claim, main reason and key limitation.
- [ ] Every hedge and number in the source is preserved accurately.
- [ ] Paraphrase compared side by side: no borrowed phrases, no change in strength.
- [ ] All summarised or paraphrased material is attributed; quotes are exact and marked.
- [ ] Your own opinions are clearly separated from the summary.
