# Writing formal, complaint, request and condolence letters

**Goal:** a letter the reader understands in one read and can act on — or, for personal
letters, one that says the right thing without clichés.

## 1. Formal layout (paper or PDF)
- **Your address** (top right in UK convention, top left in US block style), then the
  **date** written out ("14 October 2026").
- **Recipient's name, title, organisation and address**, left-aligned.
- **Reference line** if any: account, order, case or policy number — in bold or after "Re:".
  This one line often decides how fast the letter is routed.
- **Salutation:** "Dear Ms Okafor," when you know the name. Unknown name: "Dear Sir or Madam"
  or, better, "Dear Customer Relations Team".
- **Sign-off:** in British usage, *Yours sincerely* with a named person, *Yours faithfully*
  with "Dear Sir or Madam". US usage: *Sincerely* for both. Then signature and printed name.
- One page wherever possible; enclosures listed at the bottom ("Enc: receipt, photos").

## 2. The shape of any working letter
1. **Purpose in the first sentence:** "I am writing to request a refund for…"
2. **Facts:** what, when, where, reference numbers, in date order; no adjectives the facts
   don't support.
3. **The ask:** exactly what you want, by when.
4. **Next step:** what you'll do if there's no reply (politely).

## 3. Complaint letters
- Address it to the person who can fix it; check for a published complaints procedure and
  follow its steps — escalation routes (ombudsman, regulator) usually require that you did.
- State the **remedy** you want: refund, repair, replacement, apology, compensation of £X.
- Give a **reasonable deadline** ("within 14 days of this letter").
- Cite rights only if you're sure of them (consumer law varies by country; don't
  overstate).
- Keep anger out: firm, factual and courteous gets escalated less often and reads better to
  an ombudsman later.
- Keep **copies** and send in a trackable way when money or deadlines matter.

## 4. Request and thank-you letters
- Requests: explain briefly why you're asking *this* person, make the ask easy (dates,
  attachments, a simple yes/no), and thank them in advance without presuming.
- Thank-you notes: be **specific** ("the reference you wrote got me the interview") and send
  within a week or two; three sentences beat a generic page.

## 5. Condolence letters
- Handwrite if you can; send soon — a late letter is still better than none.
- **Acknowledge the loss by name:** "I was so sorry to hear of Ana's death."
- **Share a specific memory or quality** of the person; this is what the bereaved keep.
- **Offer concrete help** only if you'll give it ("I'll bring dinner on Thursday" beats
  "let me know if you need anything").
- Avoid explaining the death, comparing losses, or religious phrases the family may not share.
- Close warmly: "With love", "Thinking of you all".

## Gotchas
- Burying the ask in paragraph four: the reader may never reach it.
- Mismatched salutation and sign-off (named person + "faithfully") looks careless to UK readers.
- Threats you won't carry out weaken every later letter.
- Emotional first drafts: write it, wait a day, then cut.

## Verify
- [ ] Purpose stated in the first sentence; reference number visible near the top.
- [ ] One specific ask with a date; reader knows exactly what to do.
- [ ] Salutation and sign-off match the convention you're using.
- [ ] For complaints: copy kept, proof of sending, follows the organisation's procedure.
- [ ] For condolences: names the person, includes a specific memory, no clichés about reasons.
