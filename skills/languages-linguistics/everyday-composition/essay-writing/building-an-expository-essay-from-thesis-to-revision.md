# Building an expository essay from thesis to revision

**Goal:** an essay that answers the question asked, with one clear line of argument the
reader can follow paragraph by paragraph — produced by a process, not by staring at a blank page.

## 1. Decode the question
- Underline the **command word** (*discuss, evaluate, compare, explain, to what extent*) — it
  sets the job. "To what extent" demands a judgement of degree, not a yes/no.
- Underline the **scope limits**: period, texts, place, word count.
- Rewrite the question in your own words. If you can't, ask before writing.

## 2. Make a thesis that can be argued
A thesis is your **answer in one or two sentences**, specific enough that someone could
disagree.
- Weak: "Social media has advantages and disadvantages."
- Better: "Social media has widened access to political information but, by rewarding
  outrage, has made local news less sustainable — so its net effect on democratic
  participation is negative at the local level."
A good thesis usually contains its own map: the two or three reasons that become sections.
Draft it early; expect to revise it after writing.

## 3. Outline before drafting
- List each **claim** that supports the thesis, one line each, in the order the reader needs.
- Under each claim, note the **evidence** (quote, data, example, source) and what it shows.
- Add the strongest **counterargument** and your response. Addressing it makes the argument
  more convincing, not weaker.
- Budget words: e.g. 1,500 words ≈ intro 150, three body sections of ~350–400, counterargument
  ~200, conclusion 150.

## 4. Paragraphs that work (point, evidence, analysis, link)
1. **Topic sentence:** the paragraph's claim, linked to the thesis.
2. **Evidence:** a specific example, figure or short quotation, attributed.
3. **Analysis:** *why* the evidence supports the claim — the part most essays skimp on. Aim
   for at least as much analysis as quoted material.
4. **Link:** how this moves the argument forward or connects to the next paragraph.
One main idea per paragraph; if it needs two topic sentences, split it.

## 5. Introductions and conclusions
- **Introduction:** context in one or two sentences, define key terms if contested, state the
  thesis, signal the route. Skip "Since the dawn of time…" openers.
- **Conclusion:** restate the thesis in light of the argument (not word for word), show
  what follows from it, add no new evidence.
- Write the introduction **last** or rewrite it at the end — you only know what the essay
  argues once it exists.

## 6. Revision: top down
1. **Reverse outline:** write one line per paragraph saying what it *actually* argues. Gaps,
   repeats and off-thesis paragraphs become obvious. Reorder, cut, merge.
2. **Argument pass:** does every paragraph serve the thesis? Is the counterargument answered?
3. **Paragraph pass:** topic sentence + evidence + analysis present in each.
4. **Sentence pass and proofreading** last (see the self-editing skill).
Time: plan to spend at least a third of the total time revising.

## Gotchas
- Answering a related question you'd prefer, not the one set.
- Description dressed as argument: summarising sources without saying what they prove.
- Quotations doing the arguing for you — quote less, explain more.
- Citation format: follow the required style; missing attribution is plagiarism even when accidental.

## Verify
- [ ] Thesis is one or two sentences, specific, and arguable.
- [ ] Reverse outline shows one claim per paragraph, all serving the thesis.
- [ ] Each body paragraph contains evidence *and* analysis of it.
- [ ] A counterargument is stated fairly and answered.
- [ ] Word count is within limits and every source is cited.
