# Writing specific, honest personal statements and application answers

**Goal:** give a busy decision-maker clear evidence that you meet *their* criteria —
in your own voice, true in every detail, inside the limit.

## 1. Find out what's being scored
- Collect the **criteria**: the course page, the person specification, the scholarship's
  stated aims, the marking guidance if published. Copy them into a list.
- Highlight the **essential** criteria; those must each have evidence.
- Note the **format limits**: UK university applications, job portals and scholarship forms
  often cap by **characters including spaces** or by lines, not words — check which, and
  whether the form strips formatting.
- Expect to be compared with many others: reviewers skim, so each paragraph must make a
  clear point quickly.

## 2. Mine your evidence
List experiences (work, study, volunteering, caring roles, projects, setbacks) and for each:
what you did, what changed because of it, what you learned. Prefer **recent, specific,
verifiable** items. Small, real examples beat impressive vague ones.

## 3. Show, don't assert
Every adjective needs a fact behind it, or it goes.
- Assertion: "I am passionate about medicine and a strong team player."
- Evidence: "Volunteering on a stroke ward each Saturday for a year, I learned to adjust how I
  spoke to patients with aphasia — slower, with picture cards — and the ward adopted the
  cards for weekday volunteers."
Use **STAR** for job and competency answers: **S**ituation, **T**ask, **A**ction (most of the
words, first person "I"), **R**esult (quantify if you can), plus one line on what you'd repeat.

## 4. Structure
- **Opening:** your reason or angle, specifically — skip quotations and "Ever since I was
  young…". The first two sentences should be ones only *you* could write.
- **Body:** paragraphs mapped to the criteria, each led by its point, then the evidence, then
  what it shows about your fit.
- **Close:** where you're heading and why this programme or role is the step — tie to
  something specific about them (a module, a project, a value) without flattery.
For academic statements, roughly three-quarters about the subject and your engagement with
it is a common recommendation (varies by country and course — check guidance).

## 5. Short bios
Write two: a **50-word** and a **150-word** version, third person unless asked otherwise.
Lead with current role and focus, one or two specific achievements, one human detail if the
context suits. Keep a master copy and update it each time you use it.

## 6. Honesty and voice
- Never invent, inflate or borrow. Plagiarism detection is used in admissions, and
  interviews probe statements; an exaggeration discovered later can cost the place or job.
- Explain gaps or setbacks briefly and factually, with what you did next.
- Get feedback from someone who knows you, then make it *yours* — heavily edited
  statements lose the voice reviewers look for. Check the rules on assistance and AI tools
  for the specific application.

## Gotchas
- Re-using one generic statement for different applications — reviewers notice.
- Listing activities without saying what they show.
- Running out of characters at the end: draft long, then cut adjectives and throat-clearing.
- Pasting from a word processor can break quotation marks and line breaks; preview the final form.

## Verify
- [ ] Every essential criterion has at least one specific piece of evidence.
- [ ] No unsupported adjectives remain ("passionate", "hard-working") without a fact beside them.
- [ ] Opening lines are specific to you; close is specific to them.
- [ ] Within limit as counted by the actual form; previewed after pasting.
- [ ] Everything stated is true and you can discuss it in an interview.
