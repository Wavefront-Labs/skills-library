# Teaching programmes and safeguarding plans for surrogate languages

**Goal:** a whistled or drummed language that new generations actually use — not only a
recorded heritage item or a tourist show. Surrogates are doubly fragile: they depend on the
spoken language surviving *and* on the practical need (long-distance contact, ritual roles)
that phones, roads and social change remove.

*General revitalisation practice applied to surrogate speech; tailor every step to the
community, which must lead it.*

## 1. Assess vitality honestly
Gather, with the community:
- number of competent users by age group (who can *send*, who can only *understand*);
- domains still in use (herding, calling across valleys, ceremonies, chieftaincy, festivals);
- the status of the underlying spoken language — if it is shifting too, the surrogate cannot
  outlive it;
- existing recordings and teachers.
Frameworks like UNESCO's language-vitality factors or EGIDS-type scales help structure this.
*Neither was designed specifically for surrogates — adapt the questions.*

## 2. Transmission models
- **School programmes**: the best-known case is Silbo Gomero, made a compulsory school subject
  on La Gomera in the late 1990s, taught by local whistlers. Strengths: reaches every child,
  creates prestige. Risks: becomes a classroom performance without real-life use.
- **Master–apprentice**: one fluent elder works intensively with one or a few learners in real
  activities (herding, festivals, drum ensembles). Slower, but builds full competence. Fits
  hereditary drum traditions where knowledge is lineage-held.
- **Festivals and competitions**: e.g. the annual whistled-language festival in the Kuşköy
  area of Turkey. Raise visibility and motivate learners; add prizes for young participants.
- **Practical-use niches**: find genuine modern uses — signalling in noisy or wide outdoor spaces,
  call-and-response games, local radio, social media challenges.
- **Adult classes** for the parents' generation who understand but don't produce.

## 3. Teaching content
Sequence: production (whistle/drum control) → core phrases and formulas → comprehension
games → spontaneous messages. Use the community's documentation recordings as teaching material,
with permission. Train **local teachers**, not outside experts, so the programme survives
funding cycles.

## 4. Writing a safeguarding plan
If seeking support (e.g. UNESCO nomination, especially the Urgent Safeguarding List, or a
national heritage programme), a plan typically covers:
1. Description of the element and its community, and threats to it.
2. **Free, prior and informed consent** of the community, documented.
3. Objectives and activities (transmission, documentation, awareness, research).
4. Responsible bodies and community role at each step.
5. Timeline and budget, with sustainable funding beyond the first grant.
6. **Indicators**: number of new competent users per year, age profile, active teachers,
   domains of use, events held.
Inscription on a UNESCO list doesn't fund or guarantee anything by itself; the plan does the work.
*Nomination procedures and forms change; follow the current Operational Directives.*

## Gotchas
- **Folklorisation**: performance for visitors replaces everyday use; track real-life use, not shows.
- **Outsider-led projects** collapse when researchers leave; build local ownership from day one.
- **Standardising** one village's variety can sideline others.
- **Restricted knowledge**: some drum texts are sacred or lineage property; not all content should
  be taught in schools.
- Ignoring the spoken language's own decline.

## Verify
- [ ] A vitality assessment with user counts by age and domain exists and is repeated periodically.
- [ ] At least one transmission model is run by community members, not only external partners.
- [ ] Young learners can send and understand messages, not only perform set pieces.
- [ ] The safeguarding plan has dated indicators and a funding path beyond the first grant.
- [ ] Community consent and access rules are documented and respected.
