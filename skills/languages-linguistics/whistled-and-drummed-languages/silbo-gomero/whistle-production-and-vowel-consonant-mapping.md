# Silbo Gomero: whistle production and vowel/consonant mapping

**Goal:** understand how Silbo Gomero turns Spanish into whistles and start practising
it — loud, controllable whistle first, then the pitch code, then comprehension. Silbo is
not a separate language or a code of fixed signals: it is **Spanish, whistled**, so any
Spanish sentence can in principle be transmitted. You need working Spanish to use it.

*This skill is written from the descriptive literature, not from personal fluency in Silbo.
Real acquisition needs a teacher (silbador) — on La Gomera it is taught in schools — and
lots of listening to native whistlers.*

## 1. Background in three facts
- Traditionally used by shepherds and farmers across La Gomera's deep ravines; carries much
  farther than shouting (often quoted at 2+ km in good conditions; *figures vary by source,
  terrain and wind*).
- Became a compulsory school subject on La Gomera in the late 1990s after near-loss.
- Inscribed by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage in 2009.

## 2. Producing the whistle
The traditional technique uses a finger (or two) in the mouth, with the other hand often
cupped to direct the sound:
1. Place the tip of the index (or index + middle) finger against the tongue tip, pushing it
   back; lips close firmly around the finger.
2. Blow steadily from the diaphragm; adjust finger angle and lip tension until you get a
   clear, loud tone rather than air noise.
3. **Pitch** is changed mainly by tongue/mouth shape and air pressure, not by moving the finger.
   Practise slow glides from your lowest to highest controllable pitch.
4. Practise sustained notes, rapid stops (air cut by the tongue) and smooth glides — the three
   building blocks of the code.
Build volume gradually; overblowing early leads to sore lips and an unstable pitch.

## 3. The vowel code
Spanish has five vowels; whistled they collapse to pitch levels (the whistle follows roughly
the vowel's second formant). Descriptions differ:
- older analyses (e.g. Trujillo, Classe) describe **two** contrasting whistled vowels — high
  (*i, e*) and low (*a, o, u*);
- later acoustic work (Meyer and others) reports **four** usable pitch zones, roughly
  *i* highest, *e* high-mid, *a* mid, *o/u* lowest.
Practical takeaway: learn a clear high pitch for *i*, slightly lower for *e*, a mid for *a*,
low for *o/u*, and rely on context for the rest.

## 4. Consonants
Consonants are not sounds of their own but **modulations of the vowel pitch**: rising or
falling transitions into and out of vowels, brief interruptions (stops such as *p, t, k*),
and continuous vs broken glides (approximants and nasals vs stops). Descriptions group
consonants into a small number of classes (by whether the pitch rises or falls and whether
the sound is interrupted). Many consonants become indistinguishable; listeners resolve them
from **context, sentence rhythm and shared expectations** — as with a noisy phone line.

## 5. Practice route
1. Whistle vowel sequences (*a-i-a-o*) until pitch levels are stable.
2. Whistle short known phrases with a partner who knows the script (names, greetings,
   "¿dónde estás?").
3. Comprehension: listen to recordings and guess from a small set of possible sentences, then
   enlarge the set. Comprehension is harder than production at first.
4. Distance: practise across a field, then valleys; note how wind and echo blur transitions.

## Gotchas
- Learning from text descriptions alone yields a code nobody understands; calibrate with
  native recordings.
- Silbo depends on Canarian Spanish pronunciation; standard textbook Spanish differs slightly.
- Hearing protection: very loud finger whistles at close range can be uncomfortable — keep
  distance from partners' ears.
- Don't confuse with whistled *signals* (fixed calls); Silbo transmits arbitrary sentences.

## Verify
- [ ] You can produce a clear, loud finger whistle and hold it for 3+ seconds.
- [ ] You can whistle at least three distinct, repeatable vowel pitch levels.
- [ ] A partner identifies a whistled phrase from a set of 10 options above chance.
- [ ] You can recognise interruptions (stop consonants) versus glides in recordings.
- [ ] You've compared your whistling against native silbador recordings.
