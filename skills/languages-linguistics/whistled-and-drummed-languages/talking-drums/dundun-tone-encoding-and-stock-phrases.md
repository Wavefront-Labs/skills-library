# Dùndún tone encoding and stock phrases

**Goal:** understand how a talking drum carries actual sentences of a tonal language, and
how to begin learning the technique responsibly. Talking-drum traditions are hereditary or
guild-based crafts in many communities; real competence comes from apprenticeship to a
master drummer, and from speaking the language.

*Written from ethnomusicological and linguistic literature, not from performance practice.
Details vary by community and lineage — treat specifics as indicative.*

## 1. Why it works: tone languages
In tonal languages (Yoruba, Akan/Twi, Ewe, Lokele and many others) pitch distinguishes
words. Yoruba has three level tones — high, mid, low — so *ọkọ́* (hoe), *ọkọ̀* (vehicle/canoe)
and *ọkọ* (husband) differ only in tone (marked/unmarked as written). A drum that reproduces
the **tone melody and syllable rhythm** reproduces much of what makes the words recognisable
to a native listener. Vowels and consonants are not drummed.

## 2. The instruments
- **Dùndún** (Yoruba): double-headed **hourglass** drum. Leather cords run between the two
  heads; the drummer holds it under the arm and **squeezes the cords** to raise the pitch,
  relaxes to lower it. Struck with a curved stick (*kọ̀ngọ̀*) on one head, sometimes with the
  free hand damping or striking. The lead drum (*ìyá ìlù*, "mother drum") speaks; smaller
  drums (e.g. *gángan*) and supporting drums accompany.
- **Atumpan** (Akan): a **pair** of drums, one tuned higher, one lower, played with two sticks
  — tones mapped to the two drums.
- **Slit drums** (e.g. Lokele, Congo basin): hollowed logs with two lips of different
  thickness giving two pitches; used for long-distance messages.

## 3. Encoding
1. Write the sentence with its tones and syllable count.
2. Map tones to pitches: high = cords squeezed (or higher drum/lip), low = relaxed, mid in between
   (dùndún can also glide for contour/gliding tones).
3. Each syllable is a stroke; vowel length and phrase rhythm are kept.
4. Practise at speech tempo — the rhythm of the spoken phrase is part of the code.

## 4. Stock phrases solve ambiguity
Many different words share the same tone pattern, so a single drummed word is ambiguous. Drum
languages solve this with **fixed, longer formulas**: each concept is drummed as a
conventional phrase whose combined tone-rhythm pattern is unique. Carrington reported Lokele
drummers rendering "moon" as a longer phrase along the lines of "the moon looks down at the
earth". Yoruba drumming draws on **oríkì** (praise poetry), proverbs and praise names, which
listeners know by heart — so a few strokes call up the whole text.
Learning a drum language therefore means learning its **repertoire of formulas**, not only the
encoding rule.

## 5. How to start learning
1. Learn the spoken language's tones accurately (this is the bottleneck for outsiders).
2. Find a teacher in the tradition; ask about protocols (some texts, rhythms and contexts are
   restricted, sacred or lineage property).
3. Learn grip and pitch control on the specific drum: steady squeeze for high, release for low,
   stick technique.
4. Learn standard greetings, calls and praise formulas first; record lessons with permission.
5. Practise call-and-response with the teacher; listen to many performances.

## Gotchas
- Drumming random "tone patterns" from a dictionary yields messages no one can decode —
  without the formulas, meaning collapses.
- Tone marks are often omitted in everyday writing; get tone-marked sources.
- Different traditions (Yoruba dùndún vs bàtá vs Akan atumpan) encode differently — don't mix.
- Respect cultural ownership: some content is not for public performance or recording.

## Verify
- [ ] You can mark the tones of a short sentence in the language and drum its tone-rhythm pattern.
- [ ] You can produce at least two (ideally three) stable pitches on the drum.
- [ ] A native listener or teacher recognises a standard greeting or praise phrase you drum.
- [ ] You can explain why stock phrases are needed and give an example from your tradition.
