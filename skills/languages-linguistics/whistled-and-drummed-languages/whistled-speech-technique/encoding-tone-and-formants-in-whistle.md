# Encoding tone and formants in whistle

**Goal:** understand and practise the two ways whistled speech carries a spoken language,
so you can analyse any tradition you meet and start learning one with a native teacher.
Whistled speech is a **surrogate**: it reproduces parts of the spoken language's sound
pattern, not a separate vocabulary.

*Synthesis from the descriptive and acoustic literature; tradition-specific details should
be checked with community speakers and recent fieldwork.*

## 1. The core split
A whistle is essentially one frequency changing over time — it can't carry the full
spectrum of speech. Each tradition chooses what to keep:
- **Tonal languages** (e.g. Mazatec and other Oto-Manguean languages in Mexico, Hmong,
  Akha, Gavião in Brazil, several West African languages): the whistle follows the
  **tone/pitch melody** (F0) of the spoken words. Vowel and most consonant quality is lost;
  tone + rhythm + context carry meaning.
- **Non-tonal languages** (whistled Turkish of Kuşköy region, Greek Sfyria of Antia,
  Silbo Gomero Spanish, Béarnais Occitan of Aas): the whistle follows the vowels' **timbre**,
  mainly a frequency near the second formant (F2): front vowels like *i* whistle high,
  back/rounded vowels like *o, u* low. Consonants appear as transitions and interruptions.
Some traditions mix strategies; *classifying a specific tradition needs acoustic evidence.*

## 2. Production techniques
- **Bilabial** (puckered lips) — the everyday whistle; flexible but not the loudest.
- **Finger whistles** — one or two fingers against the tongue; very loud, used for distance
  (Silbo Gomero, whistled Turkish).
- **Tongue-against-teeth/palatal** whistles without fingers.
- **Instruments**: leaves, flutes or reeds in some traditions (e.g. Hmong courtship speech
  on leaves or mouth harps).
Loudness matters because use is typically long-distance: across valleys, fields or forest,
where whistles carry farther than shouts and stand out from background noise.

## 3. Training the whistler (non-tonal)
1. Whistle your language's vowels slowly, matching pitch to what you hear as the vowel's
   "brightness": *i* highest, then *e*, *a*, *o*, *u* lowest (exact order depends on the language).
2. Record and view a spectrogram (Praat or similar) next to native whistles; align vowel
   pitch zones.
3. Add consonants as shapes: rise/fall into the vowel, short silences for stops, smooth glides
   for liquids and nasals.
4. Practise whole phrases at speech-like rhythm — syllable timing is a major cue.

## 4. Training the whistler (tonal)
1. Learn the tones of the spoken language precisely (contour and register).
2. Map each tone to whistle pitch: register tones to levels, contour tones to glides.
3. Practise tone sequences, then real words, then common phrases.
4. Note any tone sandhi (tone changes in context) — the whistle follows the *surface* tones.

## 5. Training the listener
Reception is the harder half. Start with closed sets (choose among 5–10 known phrases),
then predictable dialogues (greetings, calling someone, asking location), then open messages.
Native listeners rely heavily on context: who is whistling, where, at what time of day.

## Gotchas
- Applying the non-tonal "formant" logic to a tonal language (or vice versa) produces gibberish.
- Recordings at close range sound different from whistles heard across a valley; train on both.
- Many traditions are endangered and community-owned; ask permission before recording or
  publishing, and credit speakers.
- Avoid whistling at painful volumes near people's ears.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state whether a given tradition is tone-based or formant-based and why.
- [ ] Your whistled vowels (or tones) match native examples on a spectrogram.
- [ ] A partner identifies whistled phrases from a closed set above chance.
- [ ] You can recognise stops vs glides (non-tonal) or tone contours (tonal) in recordings.
