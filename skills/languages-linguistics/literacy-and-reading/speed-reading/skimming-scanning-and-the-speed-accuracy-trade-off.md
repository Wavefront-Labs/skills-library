# Skimming, scanning and the speed–accuracy trade-off

**Goal:** get through more reading by choosing the right speed for each purpose, not
by a trick that lets you read everything at 1,000 words per minute.

## 1. What the research says (read this first)
- Typical silent reading for adults in English is around **200–300 wpm** for
  non-fiction (Brysbaert's 2019 meta-analysis estimated ~238 wpm average).
- Reading is limited mostly by **word recognition and language processing**, not by
  eye movements. Reviews (Rayner et al. 2016) found no good evidence that speed-reading
  courses raise speed *without* losing comprehension.
- **Subvocalisation** (inner speech) appears to help comprehension; trying to suppress
  it entirely tends to hurt understanding.
- **RSVP apps** (one word flashed at a time) remove the ability to look back; regressions
  (~10–15% of eye movements) are often needed to fix misreadings.
- What *does* reliably increase effective speed: **vocabulary and background
  knowledge**, practice reading in the domain, and **skimming deliberately**.
So: speed up by deciding *how much* of a text to read, not by forcing your eyes.

## 2. Measure your baseline
Read a 1,000-word passage of typical material for 3 minutes; count words; answer
5–10 questions. WPM × % correct gives a rough "effective reading rate" to track.

## 3. Triage: decide the reading mode
| Purpose | Mode | Typical approach |
|---|---|---|
| Is this worth reading? | **Preview** | title, abstract/summary, headings, conclusion: 1–2 min |
| Get the gist | **Skim** | first and last paragraph; first sentence of each paragraph; figures |
| Find a specific fact | **Scan** | search for the key term, number, name; ignore the rest |
| Understand / learn / decide | **Full read** | normal speed, active strategies |
| Check every detail (contract, dose) | **Careful read** | slower than normal, maybe twice |

## 4. Skimming well
- Know the text structure: academic papers (abstract → figures → discussion), news
  (inverted pyramid: key facts first), reports (executive summary).
- Read **topic sentences** and **signposts** ("however", "the main reason", "in
  conclusion").
- Expect to capture roughly the main claims, not the evidence. Comprehension when
  skimming is lower — accept that consciously.

## 5. Scanning well
- Decide what the target looks like (a capitalised name, a number, a date) before you
  look.
- On screen, use `Ctrl+F` / `Cmd+F`; on paper, use headings and index first.

## 6. Modest gains you can actually get
- **Read more** in the domain; familiarity speeds recognition.
- Reduce **needless regressions** caused by drifting attention: reading in a quiet
  setting, short focused sessions, a pacer (finger or card) can help some readers stay
  on track. *Evidence for pacers is limited; treat it as optional.*
- Larger font/line spacing can help on screen; dyslexic readers may benefit from
  specific formatting but should be assessed, not just told to "read faster".

## 7. When not to speed up
Legal documents, medical instructions, financial terms, exam questions, unfamiliar
technical material, poetry. Slow is correct there.
