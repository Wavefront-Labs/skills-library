# Active reading, summarising and comprehension monitoring

**Goal:** finish a text and be able to say accurately what it said, why it matters
and what it implies — instead of reaching the end and realising nothing stuck.

## 1. Before reading: preview (2–5 minutes)
- Title, headings, introduction, conclusion, figures, bold terms, questions at the end.
- Ask: **what kind of text is this** (argument, explanation, story, instructions,
  report)? Structure tells you what to look for: claim + evidence, cause → effect,
  problem → solution, compare/contrast, sequence.
- Turn headings into **questions** ("Causes of inflation" → "What causes inflation?").
  This is the Q step in SQ3R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review).
- Activate what you know: jot two things you already know about the topic.
  **Background knowledge is the biggest single factor in comprehension**; if you know
  nothing, spend five minutes on an overview first.

## 2. During reading
- Read **one section at a time** with your question in mind.
- **Annotate lightly:** underline at most one main sentence per paragraph; write a
  3–6 word margin note in your own words. Highlighting everything is a sign you're
  not deciding what matters.
- **Unknown words:** guess from context; look up only words that block meaning or
  recur. Keep a short vocabulary list.
- **Infer:** ask "why did the author say that?", "what must be true for this to
  follow?", "what's not said?". Inferences should be supported by text evidence.

## 3. Monitoring: notice when you've lost it
Signals: you've read a paragraph and can't say what it was about; you can't connect it
to the previous one; a pronoun or term is unclear.
**Fix-up strategies** (in this order):
1. Reread the last sentence or two, slower.
2. Read ahead a little — the next sentence may explain.
3. Identify the specific obstacle: a word, a reference ("this", "they"), a long
   sentence, a missing concept.
4. Paraphrase sentence by sentence.
5. Look it up or get a simpler source on the concept, then return.

## 4. After each section: recite
Cover the text and say or write **the gist in one sentence**. If you can't, go back.
This retrieval step is what makes the content stick.

## 5. Summarising (the proof)
- Delete trivia and repetition; replace lists with a category; find or create the
  topic sentence.
- Length guide: ~10–20% of the original for an article; one line per section.
- In **your own words** — copying sentences isn't evidence of understanding.
- Separate what the author says from your opinion of it.

## 6. Reciprocal teaching (for groups or tutoring)
Four roles rotated paragraph by paragraph: **predict, question, clarify, summarise**.
Well-researched for building comprehension, especially with struggling readers.

## 7. Matching depth to purpose
- Reading for a test or decision: full routine above.
- Reading for general interest: preview + gist.
- Dense technical text: expect two passes — one for structure, one for detail.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state the text type and its main point in one sentence.
- [ ] Each section has a one-line recall written from memory.
- [ ] You noticed at least one comprehension breakdown and fixed it deliberately.
- [ ] Your summary is in your own words and ≤ ~20% of the original.
- [ ] Inferences you make point to supporting lines in the text.
