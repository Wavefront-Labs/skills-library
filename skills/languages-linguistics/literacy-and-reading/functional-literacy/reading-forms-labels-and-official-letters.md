# Reading forms, labels and official letters

**Goal:** pull the information that matters — what you must do, by when, how much and
what happens if you don't — out of everyday documents, including ones in a language
you're still learning.

## 1. A universal first pass (any document)
1. **Who sent it** and **is it real**? Check sender name, logo, reference number,
   official contact details — look them up independently; don't use a phone number
   from a suspicious letter or text.
2. **What is it?** Bill, decision, reminder, appointment, contract, form to return.
3. **Action and deadline:** look for *must, required, by [date], within X days,
   failure to…*. Write the deadline on your calendar immediately.
4. **Amounts:** what you owe or will receive, how and where to pay.
5. **Reference number** to quote when you contact them.
6. **Who to contact** if you disagree or don't understand.

## 2. Official letters
- The key sentence is often in a box, in bold, or near the top under "What you need to
  do". The legal detail is usually at the end: **appeal rights** and time limits
  ("you have 30 days to ask for a review") — never skip them.
- "Decision", "notice", "final reminder" signal escalation. Don't ignore a letter
  because it's hard to read: deadlines still run.
- Keep a copy (photo) of every letter and anything you send back.

## 3. Forms
- Read the **whole form before writing** — instructions at the end can change answers
  at the start.
- Note which fields are **required** (often marked `*`), the date format (DD/MM/YYYY vs
  MM/DD/YYYY — check the example), and "block capitals".
- "If yes, go to question 9" — follow skips carefully.
- Don't sign a declaration you haven't read; it usually says the information is true
  and gives permission to check it.
- Ask for help *before* submitting: libraries, advice centres and community
  organisations often help with forms for free.

## 4. Medication labels and leaflets
- Find: **medicine name and strength** (e.g. 500 mg), **dose** (how many), **how often**
  ("every 4–6 hours" ≠ "4–6 times a day"), **maximum in 24 hours**, with/without food,
  warnings (drowsiness, alcohol).
- Check the **active ingredient** to avoid doubling up (e.g. paracetamol/acetaminophen
  in several cold remedies).
- Use the measuring device provided, not a kitchen spoon.
- If anything is unclear, ask a pharmacist — they can also often provide large-print
  or translated information.

## 5. Bills, contracts and small print
- Bills: **billing period, amount due, due date, estimated vs actual reading**.
- Contracts/terms: look for **length/term, cancellation, automatic renewal, fees,
  price changes, data sharing**. Search the document (`Ctrl+F`) for *renew, cancel,
  fee, penalty, terminate*.

## 6. Timetables and schedules
Find the right **direction**, **day type** (weekday/weekend/holiday), and read the
footnotes (symbols for "school days only"). 24-hour times: 17:45 = 5:45 pm.

## 7. In a second language
- Translation apps (camera translation) are good for gist; **don't rely on them for
  legal, medical or money decisions** — they can mistranslate negatives and numbers.
- Ask whether the organisation provides the document in your language or a free
  interpreter (many public services must).
- Build a personal glossary of recurring words: *deadline, appeal, balance, dosage,
  renewal, landlord, benefit*.

## Verify
- [ ] For each document you can state: sender, action needed, deadline, amount, reference.
- [ ] Deadlines are in your calendar; copies kept.
- [ ] Medicine dose, frequency and 24-hour maximum confirmed.
- [ ] Contracts checked for renewal, cancellation and fees.
- [ ] Machine translations of important documents double-checked with a person.
