# Tutoring adult beginner readers one-to-one

**Goal:** help an adult who reads little or not at all reach their own reading and
writing goals, using methods that respect their experience and rebuild confidence.

## 1. Begin with the person
- Adults arrive with **specific goals**: read to their children, pass a driving theory
  test, read work instructions, text family, get a qualification. Write the goal down
  in their words; every session should connect to it.
- Many carry **shame** from school. Keep sessions private, never ask them to read aloud
  in front of others, and never use children's books or "baby" materials.
- Ask about history gently: schooling, languages spoken (an English learner needs a
  different plan — ESOL), vision and hearing checks, any diagnosed difficulties.

## 2. Assess informally
- Letters: names and sounds of each letter.
- Phonological awareness: can they blend `/k/ /a/ /t/` → *cat*, segment *ship* into sounds?
- Word reading: a list of common words and a few regular nonsense words (tests decoding,
  not memory). Explain why you use nonsense words — adults find them odd.
- Short passage: accuracy and what they understood.
- Writing: their name, address, a sentence about themselves.
Keep it short, frame it as "finding where to start", and share what you found.

## 3. Teach decoding systematically
Research on adult beginners supports **explicit, systematic phonics** much as with
children, delivered in adult contexts:
1. Letter–sound correspondences in a planned order (start with high-utility letters:
   s, a, t, p, i, n…), short vowels, then digraphs (sh, ch, th), blends, long-vowel
   patterns, multisyllable words.
2. Every session: **review**, **new pattern**, **read words**, **read a sentence or
   text using them**, **spell/write them**.
3. **High-frequency irregular words** (the, said, was, of) taught as sight words a few
   at a time.
4. Use adult-relevant words for practice (*bus*, *shift*, *rent*, *sick*), not *cat* and *hat* only.
**Gotcha:** many adults have learned to guess from first letters and context; this
plateaus. Teach them to look through the whole word.

## 4. Language Experience Approach (LEA)
1. The learner tells you something (about work, family, a plan).
2. You write it down exactly as said, in clear print.
3. Read it together; then they read it alone.
4. Pick words from it for phonics and spelling practice.
It produces meaningful text at exactly their language level and gives instant success.

## 5. Bring in real-life reading early
Forms, signs, bus timetables, text messages, recipes, work notices — matched to the
goal. See languages-linguistics.literacy-and-reading.functional-literacy.reading-forms-labels-and-official-letters.

## 6. Writing and spelling
Short, frequent writing: a shopping list, a text, a note to the school. Teach spelling
patterns alongside reading; accept invented spellings in drafts, correct a few target
words each time.

## 7. Pace and progress
- Little and often beats long sessions: e.g. 1–2 hours a week plus 10 minutes of daily
  practice. Adults with full lives may miss sessions; make each one self-contained.
- Keep a **progress record the learner can see**: words they can read, sounds learned,
  goals met ("read the letter from the council myself").
- Possible dyslexia: if progress is very slow despite good teaching, refer for
  assessment; keep teaching meanwhile with structured, multisensory methods.

## Verify
- [ ] The learner's own goal is written down and revisited.
- [ ] Assessment covered letter sounds, blending, word reading and writing.
- [ ] Each session follows review → new pattern → read → write.
- [ ] Materials are adult and goal-relevant.
- [ ] Progress is recorded and shown to the learner regularly.
