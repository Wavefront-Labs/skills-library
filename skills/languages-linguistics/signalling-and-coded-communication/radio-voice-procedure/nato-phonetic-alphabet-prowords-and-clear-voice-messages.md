# NATO phonetic alphabet, prowords and clear voice messages

**Goal:** get information across a noisy, half-duplex voice channel — two-way radio, a
crackly phone line, a crowded venue — first time, without ambiguity.

## 1. The spelling alphabet
Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike,
November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray,
Yankee, Zulu.
- The official spellings **Alfa** and **Juliett** exist so non-English speakers pronounce
  them correctly.
- Use it for anything that must be exact: names, call signs, postcodes, serial numbers.
  Say **"I spell"** before spelling a word you also pronounce: "Smyth, I spell Sierra Mike
  Yankee Tango Hotel".
- **Numbers** digit by digit ("one five zero", not "a hundred and fifty"); aviation and
  marine use "niner" for 9 and some services "tree"/"fife" for 3/5 to stop confusion.
  Say decimals as "decimal" or "point" per your service.

## 2. Prowords that matter
| Proword | Meaning |
|---|---|
| OVER | I've finished; your turn to reply |
| OUT | Conversation ended, no reply expected |
| ROGER | I've received your last transmission (not "yes") |
| WILCO | I understand and will comply (includes roger) |
| AFFIRMATIVE / NEGATIVE | Yes / no |
| SAY AGAIN | Repeat your last (or "say again all after…") |
| READ BACK | Repeat my message back to me |
| CORRECTION | I made an error; the correct version follows |
| WAIT (OUT) | Pause; I'll come back to you |
| BREAK | Separating parts of a message |
Traps:
- **Never "over and out"** — they contradict each other.
- **"Repeat"** in military/artillery use means *fire again*; use **SAY AGAIN**.
- "Roger" doesn't mean "agreed"; if you'll do it, say WILCO.

## 3. Message format
1. **Who you're calling, "this is", who you are:** "Base, this is Team Two, over."
2. Wait for the answer, then the **message**: short, in the order who / what / where / when,
   numbers spelled out digit by digit.
3. **Ask for a read back** of critical details (grid references, frequencies, names).
4. End: OVER if you expect a reply, OUT if not.
Think *before* pressing the button; write longer messages down first.

## 4. Technique
- Press push-to-talk, **wait half a second**, then speak — or the first syllable is clipped.
- Microphone a few centimetres from the mouth, at the side to avoid breath noise; speak at
  normal volume, slightly slower than conversation, with even pace.
- Keep transmissions under ~20 seconds; others may have urgent traffic.
- **Radio check:** "Base, this is Two, radio check, over" → "Two, this is Base, loud and
  clear, over". Readability is often given on a 1–5 scale (1 unreadable, 5 perfectly readable).

## 5. Urgency and distress
- **MAYDAY** ×3 — grave and imminent danger to life or vessel/aircraft.
- **PAN-PAN** ×3 — urgent, no immediate danger to life.
- **SECURITÉ** ×3 — safety information (navigation, weather).
Then identity, position, nature of the problem, help needed, people on board. On marine VHF,
distress is on channel 16; local rules apply on other services.

## Gotchas
- Local or legacy alphabets (e.g. police "Adam, Boy…" in some regions) collide with NATO —
  use what your network uses.
- Overlap: two people transmitting at once produce a squeal or silence; neither hears the other.
- Don't use real distress prowords in training or jest.
- Radios on licensed bands may require a licence; don't transmit where you're not entitled to.

## Verify
- [ ] You spell your full name and postcode in NATO alphabet without hesitation.
- [ ] A practice exchange uses call → message → read back → OUT correctly.
- [ ] You never say "over and out" or "repeat" in a recorded exercise.
- [ ] You can give a MAYDAY call in the correct order from memory.
