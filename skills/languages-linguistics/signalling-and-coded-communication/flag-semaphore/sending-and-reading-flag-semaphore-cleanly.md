# Sending and reading flag semaphore cleanly

**Goal:** send semaphore that a partner at a distance can read on the first look, and read
theirs without the left/right confusion that wrecks most beginners.

## 1. Learn the structure, not 26 random pictures
- Each arm points to one of **eight positions**, 45° apart, like the hands of a clock
  (down, low diagonal, horizontal, high diagonal, up).
- Letters come in **"circles"**: in the first circle (**A–G**) one arm stays at rest pointing
  down while the other steps round the positions in order. In each later circle one arm is
  held at a fixed position and the other steps round (H–N, O–S, T–Y and so on, with a few
  exceptions such as J and V that break the pattern).
- So learn: the first circle cold, then for each circle the "anchor" arm position and the
  start. Drill A–G until automatic before anything else.
- Use an up-to-date chart for the exact positions; drawing them from memory is how errors
  creep in. (This skill deliberately doesn't redraw the chart.)

## 2. Sending technique
- Flags: square, diagonally split — **red/yellow** is the traditional sea pattern,
  **white/blue** the land pattern for contrast against the background. Choose the pair that
  contrasts with what's *behind you* from the reader's view.
- **Arms straight**, flags as an extension of the arm; the flag staff in line with the
  forearm. Bent elbows make diagonal positions ambiguous.
- **Snap** into each position, **hold** about a second (longer for beginners or bad
  visibility), then move directly to the next letter — no flapping through other positions.
- Between words, drop to the **rest/space** position (both flags down, crossed in front).
- **Double letters:** briefly drop toward the rest/space position (or pause noticeably) between
  them so the reader sees two letters, not one held long.
- Face the receiver squarely; turning your body changes apparent angles.

## 3. Control signals
- **Attention:** wave both flags overhead until the receiver acknowledges.
- **Numerals follow** and **letters follow** signals switch modes; after the numerals sign,
  A–I stand for 1–9 and K for 0 (J itself serves as the letters sign in most charts). Forgetting to switch back is a classic error.
- **Error/annul:** a dedicated signal, then resend the last word. Receiver acknowledgement
  conventions (e.g. signalling a letter after each word) vary between manuals — agree them
  with your partner beforehand.

## 4. Reading
- You see the sender **mirrored**: their right arm is on your left. Learn charts drawn from
  the **receiver's viewpoint**, or deliberately practise both — this is the most common
  failure.
- Read the **whole shape** (angle between flags) rather than each arm separately; shapes are
  memorable, arm positions in isolation are not.
- Call out letters to a writer; a single person reading and writing loses letters.
- At long range use binoculars; mount them or brace on something.

## Gotchas
- Diagonal vs horizontal positions blur at distance if arms sag: overemphasise angles.
- Background clutter (trees, crowds) kills readability — move to a skyline if possible.
- Wind wraps flags around the staff; heavier fabric or shorter flags help.
- Speed comes from fewer wasted movements, not from rushing positions.

## Verify
- [ ] You can send A–Z from memory and a partner reads 95% correctly at 50 m.
- [ ] You read a partner's message correctly with no left/right inversions.
- [ ] You send a mixed message with correct numerals/letters switching.
- [ ] Double letters and word gaps are visibly distinct to the reader.
