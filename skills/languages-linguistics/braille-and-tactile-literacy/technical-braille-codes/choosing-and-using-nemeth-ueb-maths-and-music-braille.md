# Choosing and using Nemeth, UEB maths and music braille

**Goal:** put maths, science and music into braille a student or musician can actually
work with — the right code for their setting, unambiguous switching, and layouts that
mirror how the work is done on paper.

## 1. Pick the code first (the decision that matters most)
- **UEB technical** (the maths and science sections of UEB) is the default in the UK,
  Australia, New Zealand and several other UEB countries.
- **Nemeth Code** remains in wide use in the US and Canada for maths and science; BANA
  permits either, and the decision is made per student — usually by the IEP team, based on
  what the student already reads and what their teachers can support.
- Never mix codes without a clear rule: a student switching textbooks between codes mid-year
  loses months. Ask before transcribing; record the decision in the transcriber's notes.

## 2. How the two codes differ (what trips readers)
- **Digits:** UEB writes digits in the upper part of the cell (like a–j) after a numeric
  indicator; Nemeth uses **lower-cell digits**, which can't be confused with letters, so
  letters and digits mix with fewer indicators — one reason maths teachers like Nemeth.
- **Linearity:** UEB is written mostly linearly with grouping indicators; Nemeth has
  dedicated fraction, radical, superscript/subscript level indicators and strong
  conventions for **spatial** (two-dimensional) layout.
- **Contractions:** in Nemeth material, contractions are restricted around maths; in UEB the
  literary and technical text share one code.

## 3. Nemeth inside a UEB document
US practice embeds Nemeth passages in UEB literary text with **switch indicators**:
an opening Nemeth Code indicator before the maths and a Nemeth Code terminator after it,
plus a single-word switch indicator for a short return to UEB. (Exact cell assignments:
check the current BANA guidance before use — they are easy to misremember, and this skill
does not reproduce them to avoid a stale error.) Gotchas:
- Put the switch **outside** the punctuation that belongs to the sentence, per the guidance.
- Don't switch for a lone number in a sentence ("chapter 3") unless the guidance says so.
- A worksheet that alternates text and maths every line becomes a thicket of indicators —
  consider making the whole problem section Nemeth.

## 4. Spatial maths layout
Long division, column addition/subtraction and matrices are often clearer **spatially**:
- Align digits by place value in columns; separation lines use a dedicated line of cells.
- Keep each problem on one braille page; a problem split across a page break is nearly
  unusable by touch.
- Leave room for the student to braille their working (if they'll write on it).

## 5. Music braille: the cell logic
- One cell carries **pitch and rhythm**: the upper dots (1, 2, 4, 5) give the note
  letter (the shapes of braille d–j map to C–B); the lower dots 3 and 6 give the value
  (none, 6, 3, or 3-6 for the four value pairs, e.g. eighth/128th, quarter/64th,
  half/32nd, whole/16th — context decides which of each pair).
- **Octave marks** precede a note when the octave isn't clear from the previous note.
- Chords are written as **intervals** from one note, not as stacked notes.
- Formats: bar-over-bar (parts aligned measure by measure, good for keyboard) or
  section-by-section (easier to read one hand/part at a time). Ask the musician which they prefer.
- Musicians read music braille, memorise, then play — a transcription must be complete
  (fingerings, dynamics, repeats) because it can't be glanced at while playing.

## Gotchas
- Software converters handle literary text far better than maths or music; expect to fix
  by hand and proofread against print.
- Chemistry structures and graphs need **tactile graphics**, not code (see tactile-graphics skill).
- A transcriber fluent in literary UEB isn't automatically competent in Nemeth or music:
  these are separate certifications in the US.
