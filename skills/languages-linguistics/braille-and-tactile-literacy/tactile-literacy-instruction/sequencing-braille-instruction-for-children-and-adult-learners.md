# Sequencing braille instruction for children and adult learners

**Goal:** move a learner from "can't tell dots apart" to functional, enjoyable braille
reading and writing, with a sequence that matches their age, touch and purpose.

## 1. Assess before teaching
Record: age and onset of vision loss; any residual vision (print may still serve some
tasks); hand use and **tactile sensitivity** (diabetes and neuropathy reduce it); cognitive
and motor needs; and **what they want to read** — school texts, labels, a menu, music.
The goal shapes the sequence: a 9-year-old needs full contracted literacy, a 70-year-old may
need reliable labels and phone numbers in eight weeks.

## 2. Pre-braille (emergent learners)
Before letters, build:
- **Tactile discrimination:** same/different with objects, then textures, then raised lines
  and shapes, then braille cells (full cell vs empty, missing dot).
- **Tracking:** following a raised line left to right, finding the next line, keeping place.
- **Hand skills:** light touch, both hands working, finger strength for a Perkins brailler.
- **Concepts of print:** books have a start, pages turn, marks carry words. Sighted children
  absorb this from the environment; blind children need it deliberately — braille-label
  their world (toys, drawers, their name).
- **Tactile books** with real objects tied to the story beat pictures re-drawn in raised line.

## 3. Sequencing for young readers
- Many current programmes teach **contracted braille from the start**, introducing common
  whole-word contractions (*and, the, for, you*) early so children read real stories quickly.
- Introduce letters in an order that **separates confusable mirror shapes** (`d`/`f`, `e`/`i`,
  `h`/`j`) rather than alphabetical order; teach one of a pair until secure before the other.
- Teach **reading and writing together**: brailling a letter reinforces its dot pattern.
- Practise little and often (daily), with real texts, and keep reading aloud to the child.
- Track progress: letters and contractions mastered, accuracy, words per minute.

## 4. Adapting for adult late learners
- Start with **jumbo or large-cell braille** if sensitivity is reduced, then move to standard.
- Many adults do well beginning **uncontracted (grade 1)** for functional goals, adding
  contractions if they want to read books. (Programmes differ — this is a judgement call.)
- Make it functional fast: labelling medication, spices, clothes; writing phone numbers;
  reading lift buttons. Early wins sustain motivation.
- Teach writing options: **Perkins brailler**, **slate and stylus** (written right to left,
  mirror-image, so dots read correctly when flipped — explain this up front), and braille
  input on a phone or display.
- Address emotion: adults losing sight often grieve print; frame braille as one tool among
  several (speech, magnification), not a defeat.

## 5. Preparing materials
- Use **clean, well-spaced braille** on good paper; worn copies frustrate beginners.
- Double-space lines for early readers so tracking errors are easy to recover from.
- Control vocabulary: early texts use only taught letters and contractions.
- Pair tactile graphics with explanation (see tactile-graphics skill).

## Gotchas
- Scrubbing (rubbing over dots) sets in early and is hard to unlearn — correct it immediately.
- Pressing hard is a common beginner habit; it fatigues and reduces sensitivity.
- Sighted family members who learn some braille dramatically help children; give them a cheat sheet.
- Don't over-rely on speech technology during the learning phase; braille literacy needs daily reading time.

## Verify
- [ ] An assessment record states goals, tactile sensitivity and preferred writing tool.
- [ ] The letter/contraction sequence separates mirror pairs.
- [ ] The learner reads a controlled-vocabulary text with light touch and no scrubbing.
- [ ] Adult learners achieve at least one functional goal (e.g. labelled medications) early.
- [ ] Progress is measured (accuracy, wpm) at regular intervals.
