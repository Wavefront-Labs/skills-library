# Bid calling — chant, spotting and the hammer

**Goal:** sell each lot at the highest price the room will pay, at a pace that keeps
energy up, with every bidder sure who is bidding and who won.

## 1. Build a chant you can sustain
A chant is just: **the number you have**, a **filler**, and **the number you want**.
"Two hundred dollar bid, now two-fifty, will ya give two-fifty…"
- Pick 1–2 short fillers ("now", "will ya give", "bid it") and drill them until
  automatic. Numbers must stay crystal clear; fillers are rhythm, not content.
- Practise number runs daily: count up in 5s, 10s, 25s, 50s, 100s while driving or walking.
- Speed is genre-dependent: livestock and car auctions use fast chants; fine-art and
  charity rooms want a slower, conversational style ("I have five thousand, will you
  say five-five?"). Match the room, not the stereotype.
- Breath at the end of each "have/want" phrase; record yourself and listen for
  swallowed numbers.

## 2. Open and increment
- Ask for an opening near or a bit below the low estimate. If silence, drop quickly to
  a figure someone will take, then climb — a stalled lot loses the room.
- Use a published **increment table** so bidders aren't surprised. A common pattern
  (house-specific; publish your own): under 100 → 5–10; 100–500 → 25; 500–1,000 → 50;
  1,000–5,000 → 100–250; above that roughly 5–10% of the current bid.
- **Split increments** ("half bids") only at your discretion near the end, to squeeze
  a last bid. Don't let bidders dictate increments early.

## 3. Spot and acknowledge
- Scan the room in a set pattern (left-centre-right, front-to-back) between every call.
- **Acknowledge every bid** visibly: point, say the number, and where helpful the
  location ("at the back, two-fifty"). Bidders who feel unseen stop bidding.
- With **ringmen/spotters:** agree signals beforehand (shout "Yup!"/"Ho!", raised arm),
  each owns a section, they relay bids and coax hesitant bidders.
- Integrate absentee, phone and online bids in strict order; announce "online bid"
  or "on the phone" so the room trusts the competition is real.
- **Vendor/reserve bids:** where local law and house terms allow the auctioneer to bid
  on the vendor's behalf up to the reserve, it must be done within those rules and
  never above the reserve. Rules vary by jurisdiction and sector (property auctions are
  often tightly regulated) — confirm before doing it.

## 4. Pace and the hammer
- Typical saleroom pace is roughly 60–120 lots/hour for general goods, slower for
  high-value lots (figures vary widely by sale type).
- When bidding slows: repeat the current bid and bidder location, give a fair warning
  ("Fair warning… selling once… twice…"), pause a beat for a last bid, then **hammer
  decisively** and announce the price and paddle number. The fall of the hammer
  usually forms the contract.
- If a lot fails to reach reserve, say "pass" or "not sold" neutrally and move on.
- Disputes (two bidders claim the last bid): reopen at the last undisputed bid
  immediately, per the conditions of sale.

## Gotchas
- Taking "bids off the chandelier" (fictitious bids) is fraud in most jurisdictions.
- Rushing the hammer on a rising lot leaves money and goodwill on the table; hesitating
  on a dead lot drains the room. Read bidder body language.
- Losing track of the current figure is the most common rookie error; clerk confirms
  each hammer price aloud or on screen.
- Licensing and conduct rules for auctioneers differ by state/country — check yours.

## Verify
- [ ] You can count a clean chant in 5s, 25s and 100s for two minutes without slurring.
- [ ] Increment table is published and followed.
- [ ] Every bid acknowledged with number and location; spotters had agreed signals.
- [ ] Fair warning given before each hammer; clerk record matches every hammer price.
- [ ] Vendor-bidding practice checked against local law and conditions of sale.
