# Specialist auction formats

**Goal:** adapt the core bid-calling and sale-management skills (see
`sales-marketing.auctioneering.bid-calling.chant-bid-spotting-and-hammer-control`) to
formats with different buyers, units of sale and legal rules.

Laws for property and livestock auctions vary sharply between countries and states.
The structure here is general; confirm local regulation, licensing and standard
contracts before running any of these.

## 1. Livestock
- **Unit of sale:** per head, or per kilo/pound liveweight (weighed before or in the
  ring). Announce the basis and the lot's head count and average weight before calling.
- Fast chant, small increments (cents per kilo or small amounts per head); buyers are
  professionals who bid with tiny gestures — ringmen are essential.
- Know the paperwork: animal identification/traceability tags, health declarations,
  vendor declarations required in your jurisdiction.
- Animal welfare in yards and ring is your responsibility on sale day; pause the sale
  for a distressed animal.

## 2. Estate / on-site auctions
- Preview day(s) before the sale; register bidders with ID and issue numbers.
- Sell in a planned walking route; lots tagged and numbered on the item.
- Clear rules on "sold as is, where is", payment same day, removal deadlines and who
  supplies labour for heavy items.
- Small items "choice" lots: high bidder chooses one or more items from a group at the
  bid price per item; explain it before the first choice lot.

## 3. Real-estate auctions on site
1. Before: contract and disclosure documents available for inspection; bidder
   registration where required; reserve agreed in writing with the vendor.
2. Opening: read the conditions — deposit (often a percentage payable on the fall of
   the hammer), settlement date, any rules on vendor bids — as local law requires.
3. Calling: slower, conversational; increments set by the auctioneer and often reduced
   near the reserve. Announce clearly when the property is **"on the market"**
   (reserve met).
4. If passed in, the highest bidder commonly gets first right to negotiate.
Many jurisdictions remove cooling-off rights for auction purchases and regulate vendor
bidding and underquoting — check before you advertise a price guide.

## 4. Charity / benefit auctions
- Few lots (8–15 live lots is a typical evening), placed after dinner while energy is high.
- Pre-qualify likely bidders with the organiser; seat them where you can see them.
- **Paddle raise / fund-a-need:** no item — ask for donations at descending levels
  ("Who will give 10,000?… 5,000… 1,000…"), naming and thanking every paddle. Often
  raises more than the items.
- Keep it short, warm and funny; tell the story of the cause between lots.
- Spell out what's tax-deductible per local rules (often only the amount above fair value).

## 5. Dutch and sealed-bid
- **Dutch (descending):** price starts high and drops in steps until the first buyer
  accepts — used for perishables (flowers, fish) and multiple identical units. Set step
  size and timing in advance; first acceptance wins.
- **Sealed bid / tender:** written bids by a deadline, opened together; highest valid bid
  wins at its own price (first-price) or, in a Vickrey design, at the second-highest
  price. Publish the opening rules, tie-break and whether the seller may reject all bids.

## Gotchas
- Mixing per-head and per-kilo language mid-sale causes disputes; repeat the basis.
- Charity auction items with no clear terms (dates, expiry, exclusions) generate complaints.
- Sealed tenders without a clear deadline time and timezone invite challenges.

## Verify
- [ ] Format's legal rules (licensing, cooling-off, vendor bids, traceability) checked locally.
- [ ] Unit of sale, deposit and payment terms announced before the first lot.
- [ ] Bidder registration and ID process in place where required.
- [ ] Charity lots have written terms; paddle-raise levels planned.
- [ ] Tender/Dutch rules published, including tie-breaks.
