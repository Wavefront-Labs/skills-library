# Lot cataloguing, estimates and sale order

**Goal:** a catalogue that tells buyers exactly what each lot is, what it's likely to
fetch, and runs in an order that builds energy — without overstating anything you'd
have to refund later.

## 1. Intake and lotting
- Give every item a stock/consignment number on arrival, linked to the vendor record.
- **Lot** sensibly: group low-value items into lots worth bidding on (a box of mixed
  tools, a pair of chairs); keep anything that could sell alone, alone.
- Photograph immediately: main view, maker's marks/signatures, any damage, scale
  reference. Natural, even light; no filters that hide condition.

## 2. Write the description
Use a fixed order so buyers can scan:
1. **Lot number. Headline:** what it is in the terms buyers search ("A Victorian
   mahogany chest of drawers").
2. **Maker/artist/period**, using your catalogue's **glossary of attribution terms**
   (e.g. "by" = you believe it is by the maker; "attributed to" = probably; "circle of",
   "style of", "after" = progressively weaker). Publish the glossary in the catalogue
   terms; buyers rely on it.
3. **Materials, technique, marks, dimensions** (H × W × D, units stated).
4. **Provenance** and literature, if documented. Don't invent a story.
5. **Condition:** a short line in the catalogue; a fuller **condition report** on
   request. Describe what you see ("losses to veneer at lower left corner"), never
   "good for age" alone.
6. **Estimate.**
Describe facts you can support. When unsure, say so ("possibly", "unsigned") — a
confident wrong attribution is the classic route to a rescinded sale.

## 3. Set estimates
- Pull **comparables**: your own past hammer prices, plus subscription price databases
  where your sector has them (which ones depends on the specialism).
- Adjust for condition, size, provenance, freshness to market and current demand.
- Set **low–high** as a realistic range, typically with the high around 1.3–1.5× the
  low (house conventions vary). The low estimate should be a price you'd expect to
  achieve; many houses keep the reserve at or below the low estimate.
- **Attractive estimates** (conservatively low) draw bidders; inflated estimates to
  please vendors produce unsold lots and stale "burnt" items.

## 4. Sequence the sale
- Open with a few attractive, competitively-estimated lots to warm the room.
- Spread star lots through the sale rather than all at once; put them where the room
  is fullest (often roughly the first third — watch your own attendance pattern).
- Group by category so specialist buyers can plan, but avoid 40 identical lots in a
  row (prices sag as demand is met).
- Note planned **lots per hour** and publish an approximate timetable for big sales.

## 5. Produce the catalogue
Online listing (with zoomable photos) plus print/PDF if your buyers use it. Include
conditions of sale, buyer's premium and tax, glossary, viewing times, bidding methods,
payment and collection deadlines.

## Gotchas
- Dimensions in the wrong unit or transposed figures generate returns — double-check.
- Photos that hide damage breach trust and may breach consumer law for online sales.
- Some materials (ivory, certain timbers, firearms) have legal restrictions on sale and
  export; check before cataloguing.
- Never reuse another house's description text — write your own.

## Verify
- [ ] Every lot has number, headline, attribution per glossary, dimensions, condition, estimate.
- [ ] Each estimate is backed by at least one comparable or a recorded reason.
- [ ] Reserves sit at or below low estimates (per house policy).
- [ ] Sale order reviewed for openers, star-lot spacing and category grouping.
- [ ] Catalogue includes terms, premium, glossary and collection rules.
