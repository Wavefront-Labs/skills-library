# Objection handling in the field

**Goal:** keep a conversation open long enough to find out whether there is a real fit —
and when there isn't, leave on good terms with a referral or a future opening.
Respect a clear, repeated no; persistence past that is pressure, and on a doorstep
it's also a legal and reputational risk.

## 1. Reflex vs real objections
- **Reflex (brush-off):** an automatic shield before they've heard anything — "Not
  interested", "We're good", "I'm busy". Answer lightly and give one reason to listen.
- **Real objection:** a specific concern after they understand the offer — price, timing,
  trust, partner, fit. Slow down and work it.
- **Firm no** (said twice, or with clear annoyance, or "please leave"): thank them and go.

## 2. The core pattern: acknowledge → ask → answer → check
1. **Acknowledge:** "Totally fair." / "That makes sense." (Never argue or say "but".)
2. **Ask** to understand or isolate: "Is it the timing, or something about the price?"
   / "Other than that, is there anything else holding you back?"
3. **Answer** the actual concern briefly, with proof.
4. **Check:** "Does that help?" then move to the next step.
Feel–felt–found ("I understand how you feel; others felt the same; what they found
was…") is a common variant — use it only with a true example.

## 3. Common objections — starter responses (adapt; keep them honest)
**"Not interested."** (reflex)
"No problem — most people say that before they know what it is. I'm only here because
<true reason: crew on your street / rates changed>. Quick question: <qualifying
question>?" If they repeat the no: thank them and move on.

**"I don't have time."**
"Understood — I'll be 60 seconds, and if it's not relevant I'll be gone." Or book a
better time: "When are you both normally home — weekday evenings or Saturday?"

**"Just leave a flyer / send me information."**
"Happy to. So I send the right thing — <one qualifying question>?" Information requests
often hide disinterest; a question reveals which.

**"It's too expensive."**
Isolate: "Expensive compared with what you pay now, or compared with what you expected?"
Then show the comparison (cost per month, savings, cost of the problem) or the
smaller option. Don't discount by reflex — price drops on the doorstep invite distrust.

**"I need to talk to my partner."** (spouse stall)
"Of course — it's a joint decision. What do you think they'll ask?" Answer those, then
book a time when both are present (see
`sales-marketing.door-to-door-field-sales.appointment-setting.qualify-book-confirm-and-cut-no-shows`).

**"I want to think about it."**
"Makes sense. What part do you want to think over?" Usually one specific worry; address
it or book a callback with a date.

**"We already have someone / a provider."**
"Good — how happy are you with them, 1 to 10?" Anything under ~8: "What would make it a 10?"

## 4. Turn a no into a referral
When there's truly no fit: "No worries at all. Is there anyone on the street — or a
friend — who's been talking about <problem>?" Also ask "Mind if I check back in
<season/when your contract ends>?" and log it.

## 5. Practise
Write your top 6 objections and responses on a card; role-play daily with a teammate
(one plays a difficult homeowner). Record which responses keep conversations going.

## Gotchas
- Answering an objection before understanding it ("Price? We're cheapest!") — often not the real issue.
- Stacking three rebuttals in a row — that's pressure, not handling.
- Scripted lines delivered robotically; tone matters more than words.
- Ignoring a "please leave" or a no-soliciting sign has legal consequences in many places.

## Verify
- [ ] You can classify an objection as reflex, real, or firm no in the moment.
- [ ] Every response starts with acknowledgement and includes a question.
- [ ] Price objections isolated before any discount is considered.
- [ ] Firm no's ended politely with a referral ask; outcome logged.
- [ ] Top six objections practised in role-play this week.
