# Holding list and trading discount for commitment

**Goal:** when a customer asks "Can you do better on the price?", respond in a
way that protects margin, treats customers consistently, and — when a discount
is right — gets something real in return. The floor and discount rules are set
upstream (pricing strategy); this is the live conversation.

## 1. Before you're asked
- Know your **margin floor** and **approval matrix**: what you can give alone,
  what needs a manager or deal desk, and what's never available.
- Know your **tradeables** (see concession-ladder-planning): volume, term,
  upfront payment, timing (this month), collection instead of delivery, display
  model, case study/review, referral.
- Know the competitor's real price for the same thing, including what's not
  included.

## 2. First response: hold and explore
A discount request is often a question, not an ultimatum. Don't drop on the first ask.
1. **Acknowledge**: "Fair question."
2. **Ask why**: "Is it the total, the monthly cost, or are you comparing with
   something?" — the answer tells you which lever matters.
3. **Restate value** tied to their needs: what's included, what it saves or protects.
4. **Hold**: "This is our standard price for this, and it's the price everyone pays."
Many buyers ask by reflex; a calm, confident hold often ends it.

## 3. If there's a real gap: trade, never give
Use if–then: "If you can ___, I can ___."
- **Volume**: "If you take four instead of three, I can do 8% on all of them."
- **Term/commitment**: "On a 24-month agreement I can bring the monthly down to $X."
- **Timing**: "If we can confirm the order today, I can include installation" —
  only if a genuine reason exists (month-end target, stock clearing).
- **Payment**: upfront vs instalments.
- **Scope**: remove an item they don't need rather than discount the whole.
Prefer giving **value-add** (accessory, service, extended support) over cutting the
price: it costs you less than its retail value and doesn't reset the reference price.

## 4. Staying inside the floor
- Calculate before you offer: price after discount − cost ≥ floor margin.
  Include costs of free extras.
- If the ask exceeds your authority: "I'll need approval for that — what would
  you commit to if I can get it?" Get the commitment *before* asking the manager.
- If it's below the floor: say no clearly and kindly. "I can't go there; at that price
  we'd lose money on it. Here's what I can do." Offer the best tradeable alternative.

## 5. Consistency and integrity
- Same situation, same answer — inconsistent discounting spreads by word of mouth
  and trains customers to always ask.
- Record every discretionary discount with reason (for review and coaching).
- Don't fabricate urgency or fake "manager approval" theatre.
- Price-discrimination and fair-dealing rules may apply (e.g. regulated products,
  B2B competition rules); stay within company policy.

## Example
> Customer: "Another store has it for $1,050." (Yours: $1,149.)
> You: "Thanks — is that the same model with the 3-year warranty and delivery?
> ... It isn't? Then like-for-like we're about $40 apart. I can't move the price,
> but if you're happy to collect today I can include the wall bracket ($59)."

## Gotchas
- Discounting before discovering the reason for the objection.
- Giving discount to close, then giving extras on top (double concession).
- Quarter-end discounting habits teach customers to wait.
- **Uncertain:** whether value-adds are preferable depends on how your margin is measured; check with pricing.

## Verify
- [ ] Margin floor, authority limits and tradeables known before the conversation.
- [ ] First response explored the reason and restated value before any move.
- [ ] Every discount traded for a commitment in if–then form.
- [ ] Final price checked against floor including extras; approvals obtained with commitment in hand.
- [ ] Discount recorded with reason.
