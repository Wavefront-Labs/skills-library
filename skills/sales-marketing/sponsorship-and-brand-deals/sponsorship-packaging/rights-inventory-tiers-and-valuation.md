# Rights inventory, tiers and valuation

**Goal:** a team, event, venue, festival or creator (the "property") knows exactly what it
can sell, what each piece is worth, and packages it so sponsors can buy outcomes, not logos.

## 1. Audit the rights inventory
List every asset in a spreadsheet with: asset, quantity/period, audience reached, exclusivity
possible, cost to deliver, current owner (unsold/sold).
Typical categories:
- **Designations:** title/naming, presenting, official [category] partner.
- **Visibility:** logos on kit, signage, LED boards, website, tickets, broadcast.
- **Content & digital:** social posts, email mentions, video series, creator collabs.
- **Experiential:** on-site activation space, sampling rights, product demos.
- **Hospitality:** tickets, boxes, meet-and-greets, money-can't-buy experiences.
- **Data & access:** opt-in lead generation, research access (within privacy law).
- **Intangibles:** IP/mark usage, category exclusivity, B2B networking with other sponsors.
Unconventional assets (staff uniforms, Wi-Fi network name, a stadium's lift) are often unsold.

## 2. Value each asset
Use more than one method and triangulate:
1. **Cost per thousand (CPM) equivalent:** audience × comparable media CPM ÷ 1,000. Discount
   for clutter and quality (a logo among 30 is not an ad).
2. **Comparable deals:** what similar properties charge; industry reports and broker
   intelligence help.
3. **Cost-plus floor:** delivery cost + target margin — the minimum you'll accept.
4. **Intangibles premium:** exclusivity, prestige, fan affinity, B2B access — judgment, but
   argue it with sponsor-relevant numbers (e.g. % of fans who prefer brands that sponsor).
Write the method down; sponsors' procurement teams will ask.

## 3. Package into tiers
- 3–4 tiers, e.g. **Title / Premier partner / Official partner / Supplier**, each with
  clearly more value and exclusivity.
- Price each package at roughly the sum of its asset values (sometimes with a small
  bundle discount); make the top tier scarce (one title partner).
- **Category exclusivity** (only one bank, one beer) is often the most valuable right — define
  categories precisely ("alcoholic beverages" vs "beer").
- Leave modular add-ons (extra hospitality, a content series) for upsell.

## 4. Naming rights structures
Long term (often multi-year, longer for venues), priced on years of exposure across media
mentions, signage, maps and tickets. Include: name approval process, signage costs (who
pays), media obligation to use the name, early termination and rebrand clauses. Get legal advice.

## 5. The proposal
Tailor it to the sponsor's objectives (awareness, sales, B2B, employee engagement): their
audience overlap, an activation idea, the package, the price, measurement plan, term.
Customising one asset to their goal beats a generic deck of logos.

## Gotchas
- Selling the same asset twice (two sponsors promised the same LED slot) — keep one master
  inventory with sold status.
- Over-valuing via "media value" inflation damages credibility on renewal.
- Ambush risk: protect category exclusivity against other partners' and venue suppliers'
  conflicts (pouring rights, ticketing partner) before selling.
- CPM and valuation inputs vary by market; figures must be sourced locally.

## Verify
- [ ] Master inventory lists every asset with audience, quantity, cost and status.
- [ ] Each asset valued by at least two methods; floor price documented.
- [ ] Tiers escalate in value and exclusivity; categories precisely defined.
- [ ] Proposal maps assets to the sponsor's stated objectives with a measurement plan.
- [ ] No asset sold twice; conflicts with existing contracts checked.
