# Handling B2B objections and stalls

**Goal:** treat each objection as information, resolve the real concern behind it, and
keep the deal moving — without reflex discounting.

## 1. The core move: Listen → Isolate → Acknowledge → Respond → Confirm
1. **Listen** fully. Don't interrupt; write their words down.
2. **Isolate**: "Apart from price, is there anything else that would stop you going ahead?"
   If something else comes up, that's often the real objection.
3. **Acknowledge** without agreeing: "That's a fair concern — it's a meaningful spend."
4. **Clarify / respond**: ask a question first ("Too expensive compared with what?"),
   then answer the specific concern with evidence.
5. **Confirm**: "Does that address it?" If not, you haven't found the real issue.

## 2. Playbooks by objection type
**Price — "It's too expensive."**
- Find the comparison: budget? a competitor quote? doing nothing?
- Go back to the business case (sales-marketing.technical-b2b-sales.solution-selling.building-a-roi-business-case):
  "At the conservative figure it pays back in N months. Which assumption looks off to you?"
- If budget is the real limit: change scope (fewer seats, phased rollout), term (multi-year
  for lower annual price), or timing (start next quarter). Trade, don't drop: any discount
  gets something back — longer term, case study, earlier signature, upfront payment.

**Status quo — "We're fine with what we have."**
- Revisit cost of inaction with their numbers. Ask: "What happens if nothing changes for 12 months?"
- If they genuinely have no pain, disqualify politely; status-quo is the top competitor
  and you can't beat it without a problem to solve.

**Competitor — "Vendor X is cheaper / does Y."**
- Ask what they like about X. Don't trash the competitor.
- Reframe on their decision criteria where you differ; offer a reference customer who
  evaluated both.

**Risk — "What if implementation fails?" / security / vendor viability.**
- Offer proof: implementation plan with milestones, references at similar size, security
  documentation (SOC 2 report, ISO 27001 certificate, completed questionnaire — whatever
  you genuinely have), contractual protections (SLA, exit/data-return clause), a pilot.

**Timing — "Not now."**
- "What changes between now and then?" If nothing concrete, it's a soft no or a hidden objection.

## 3. Breaking late-stage stalls
Signs: champion goes quiet, close date slips twice, "legal is reviewing" with no detail.
- **Diagnose**: is the problem priority, budget, a new stakeholder, or a competing project?
- **Go to the mutual action plan**: "We agreed security review by the 10th to hit your go-live
  in March. Is March still the goal?" Tie the timeline to *their* event, not your quarter-end.
- **Multi-thread**: reach another stakeholder you've already met; ask your executive to
  contact theirs.
- **The honest check-in** (use once): "It feels like this has lost priority. Is it fair to
  close it for now?" Often surfaces the real issue.
- Avoid end-of-quarter "only if you sign by Friday" discounts as a habit — buyers learn
  to wait for them.

## 4. Log and learn
Record each objection in the CRM with the stage it came up at and whether it was resolved.
Monthly: top 5 objections → fix upstream (messaging, discovery questions, pricing pages,
security docs) so they come up less.

## Gotchas
- Answering the stated objection when the real one is hidden — always isolate first.
- Arguing; you can win the point and lose the deal.
- Conceding on price before the buyer has chosen you — you'll be asked again later.
- Using canned lines like "feel, felt, found" robotically; buyers recognise them.

- [ ] Every concession was traded for something and recorded.
