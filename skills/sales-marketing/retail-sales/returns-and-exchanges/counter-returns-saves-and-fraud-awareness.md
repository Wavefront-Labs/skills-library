# Counter returns, saves and fraud awareness

**Goal:** every return ends with a customer who'll shop again, a correct refund, and no
losses to fraud — in that order of what customers see, but all three matter.

## 1. Know the two rulebooks
- **Statutory rights** (the law): faulty, not as described or not fit for purpose goods
  usually entitle the customer to a repair, replacement or refund regardless of store
  policy. Details vary — e.g. the UK Consumer Rights Act gives a 30-day short-term right to
  reject faulty goods; EU distance-selling rules give a 14-day cooling-off period for online
  purchases. Learn your own jurisdiction's rules; don't quote these as universal.
- **Store policy** (goodwill): change-of-mind returns, time limits, receipt
  requirements, final-sale items. Policy can be more generous than the law, never less
  for faulty goods.
Getting these confused is the most common counter mistake, and refusing a legally
required refund can be an offence or a complaint to a regulator.

## 2. The counter routine
1. **Welcome, then listen.** "Sure, I can help with that — what's happened with it?"
   The reason decides the route: faulty, wrong size, didn't like it, gift.
2. **Check:** proof of purchase (receipt, card lookup, order number, loyalty account),
   condition, tags/packaging, date.
3. **Save attempt (once, and only if it fits):**
   - Wrong size/colour → "Would you like to swap it for the right size? I can check stock."
   - Didn't meet a need → "What was it not doing for you?" then suggest a better match.
   - Faulty → offer replacement first if they're happy, but don't pressure on a legal refund.
   If they want their money back, process it promptly and pleasantly.
4. **Process** in the POS against the original transaction where possible (refund to the
   original tender — card refunds go back to the same card; most POS systems enforce
   this). Never refund a card purchase in cash unless policy explicitly allows.
5. **Close well:** "All done — it'll show on your card in a few days (bank timing varies)."
6. **Route the stock:** resaleable → back to floor; faulty → damaged/RTV (return to
   vendor) bin with a note; hygiene items per policy.

## 3. No receipt
Follow policy exactly: often card lookup, store credit at lowest recent price, or ID
logged for tracking. Don't improvise exceptions alone — call a supervisor.

## 4. Fraud awareness (spot, don't accuse)
Patterns worth noticing:
- **Wardrobing:** worn items returned after an event (deodorant marks, removed tags reattached).
- **Receipt fraud:** a receipt from a bin or a previous purchase used to "return" a stolen item.
- **Price switching:** cheaper item returned in a dearer item's box.
- **Refund to a different card** or insistence on cash for a card sale.
- Same person, frequent no-receipt returns (many POS systems flag this).
Response: follow policy, involve a manager, decline politely citing policy. Never accuse a
customer of theft; log details in the incident system.

## Gotchas
- A rude return is a lost customer *and* a bad review — the refund costs the same either way.
- Gift returns: refund or credit should not reveal the price to the recipient; check policy.
- Promotional items: if a multi-buy is partially returned, the refund is usually the
  apportioned price, not the full single price — know how your POS calculates it.
- Unconfirmed: legal timeframes cited above are summaries; consult official guidance.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state the difference between your statutory obligations and store policy.
- [ ] Each return records a reason code and proof of purchase method.
- [ ] One exchange or save offered where it fits; refunds not obstructed.
- [ ] Refund went to the original tender; returned stock routed correctly.
- [ ] Suspicious returns escalated per policy with no accusation made.
