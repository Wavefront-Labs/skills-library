# Brand architecture — structure, naming rules and migration

**Goal:** a portfolio where every brand has a clear job and relationship to the master
brand, new offers get named by rule rather than politics, and brands are merged or
retired without losing the customers and equity they carry.

## 1. Audit the portfolio
For each brand/sub-brand/product name, record: revenue and growth, audience, positioning,
awareness and preference data, distinctiveness of its assets, legal/trademark status,
and overlap with other brands (same customers, same promise). A simple grid —
**equity (customer strength) vs strategic importance** — sorts brands into invest,
maintain, merge, or retire.

## 2. Choose the structure
The **brand relationship spectrum** (popularised by Aaker & Joachimsthaler) runs from
one master brand to fully separate brands:
| Model | What it looks like | Use when | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Branded house** | One master brand + descriptors ("Google Maps") | Offers share audience and promise; efficient marketing | One failure hurts all |
| **Sub-brands** | Master brand + distinct name ("Apple iPhone") | Need distinct offer with parent's credibility | Dilutes if overused |
| **Endorsed brands** | Own brand, parent as endorser ("Courtyard by Marriott") | Different positioning, still benefit from trust | Endorser effect can be weak |
| **House of brands** | Separate brands, parent invisible (P&G's brands) | Conflicting positions, different audiences/price tiers | Costly; no shared equity |
Most real companies are **hybrids**; the point is to make each relationship deliberate.

Decision questions for any brand or new offer:
1. Does the master brand add credibility here? (If yes → lean branded/endorsed.)
2. Would the master brand be harmed by association (price tier, risk, controversy)?
3. Does the offer need a distinct positioning the master brand can't hold?
4. Is there enough budget to build a separate brand? (Usually the deciding factor.)

## 3. Set naming rules
Write a one-page **naming system**: when to use a descriptor ("Brand + Analytics"),
when a sub-brand name is allowed (e.g. a distinct product line with its own roadmap
and budget), endorsement lock-ups and wording, and approval owner. New offers default
to descriptors; exceptions must pass the decision questions.

## 4. Migrate or retire brands (mergers, pivots)
Options, from fastest to most gradual:
- **Immediate switch:** rename everywhere on a launch date — clean, risky if the old
  brand had strong loyalty.
- **Endorsement bridge:** "Old Brand, now part of New Brand" or "Old by New" for a set
  period, then drop the old.
- **Co-brand / phased fade:** dual logos shrinking the old one over 12–36 months.
- **Keep as acquired brand** if its equity outweighs integration savings.
Before choosing, measure the old brand's awareness and preference, and tell customers
*what changes for them* (accounts, support, prices). Plan redirects for domains,
search listings, app store names, legal entity names and contracts.

## Gotchas
- Sub-brand proliferation: every team wants a name; customers can't track them.
- Architecture by org chart (each division its own brand) rather than customer need.
- Retiring a brand without migrating its search traffic and reviews.
- Overestimating endorsement: customers often don't notice "by Parent" lines; test it.

## Verify
- [ ] Every brand in the portfolio audited and placed on the equity/importance grid.
- [ ] Each brand has a defined relationship to the master brand.
- [ ] Written naming system with default rule and exception test.
- [ ] Migration plan covers customer communication, digital redirects and end date.
- [ ] Pre- and post-migration awareness/preference measured.
