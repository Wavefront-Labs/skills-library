# Brand positioning — statement and value proposition

**Goal:** a short, decided answer to "why should *this* customer choose us over the
alternative they'd otherwise pick?" — specific enough to guide product, pricing,
messaging and design decisions.

## 1. Gather evidence (don't position from a workshop alone)
- **Customer interviews:** 8–15 recent buyers and lost prospects. Ask what they were
  trying to get done, what else they considered, why they chose, what nearly stopped them.
  Record their exact words.
- **Competitor audit:** for 4–6 alternatives (including "do nothing" or DIY), capture
  their claims, pricing, audience and visual cues. Plot on a simple **perceptual map**
  using two attributes customers actually care about.
- **Capabilities audit:** what can you deliver that is hard to copy (cost, network,
  expertise, process, heritage)?

## 2. Decide the competitive frame
The **frame of reference** is the category you ask to be compared within — it sets the
competitors and the expectations. "A premium instant coffee" and "a café alternative
at home" invite very different comparisons. Choose the frame where your difference
matters most and is credible.

## 3. Points of parity and difference
- **Points of parity:** what you must match to be considered at all (e.g. safety,
  basic features). Being below parity disqualifies you.
- **Points of difference:** 1–3 benefits that are (a) *desirable* to the target,
  (b) *deliverable* by you, (c) *differentiating* from the frame's competitors.
  Score candidates on all three; drop any that fail one.

## 4. Write the positioning statement (internal tool)
A widely used template:
> For **[target customer]** who **[need/situation]**, **[brand]** is the **[frame of
> reference]** that **[key point of difference/benefit]** because **[reason to believe]**.
Example (fictional): "For small-practice vets who lose hours to paperwork, PawLedger is
the practice software that cuts admin to minutes a day, because it was built with
vets and automates insurance claims."
Rules: one target, one main benefit, a provable reason. If you write "and" twice, you
haven't chosen.

## 5. Value proposition (customer-facing)
Translate the statement into customer language: a headline (the benefit), a sub-line
(for whom/how), and 3 proof points. Use customers' own words from the interviews.

## 6. Test and commit
- Show the proposition (and 1–2 alternatives) to target customers: do they find it
  relevant, believable, different? Simple preference surveys or landing-page tests work.
- Pressure-test internally: could a competitor claim the same sentence? If yes, it's not
  a position.
- Document it, with the "we are / we are not" list, and make it the filter for decisions.

## Gotchas
- Positioning on attributes everyone claims ("quality", "innovation", "customer-first").
- Targeting "everyone" — the narrower target usually grows faster.
- Changing position every campaign; consistency builds memory structures over years.
- Positioning is a strategic choice; it doesn't replace distinctive brand assets or reach.

## Verify
- [ ] Interviews and competitor audit completed and quoted.
- [ ] Frame of reference chosen deliberately, with competitors listed.
- [ ] Each point of difference passes desirable/deliverable/differentiating.
- [ ] Statement has one target, one benefit, one reason to believe.
- [ ] Customer test done; competitor-swap test failed (i.e. they couldn't claim it).
