# Scouting, signing and pruning a roster

**Goal:** a roster you can actually serve, with a mix of earners now and earners next,
no unmanaged conflicts between clients, and a clear yearly decision about each one.

Commission splits and contract lengths below are typical figures, not standards;
regulated sectors (sports governing bodies, some talent-agency licensing regimes) set
their own rules — check them before signing anyone.

## 1. Know your capacity first
Rough rule: count the hours a client needs per month (pitching, deals, admin, hand-holding)
against your available hours. If the roster already consumes your week, signing one more
lowers service for everyone. Many agents work in a range of a few dozen active clients —
treat any figure as a starting point and measure your own.

## 2. Scouting funnel
Sources: referrals from clients and buyers (highest hit rate), queries/submissions,
showcases, competitions, festivals, junior leagues, social platforms, open calls.
Track in a sheet: name · source · date seen · stage (seen / meeting / trial / offer /
signed / passed) · notes · next action. Look at stage conversion monthly — it tells you
which sources are worth your time.

## 3. Signing criteria (score 1–5 each)
- **Sellability now**: could you pitch them to five named buyers this month?
- **Trajectory**: evidence of improvement, discipline, output rate.
- **Fit**: does your buyer network match their market?
- **Professionalism**: responsiveness, reliability, how they talk about past reps.
- **Conflict check**: do they compete directly with an existing client for the same
  roles/slots/buyers? (see §5)
Sign only if you'd pitch them with enthusiasm — buyers hear the difference.

## 4. The representation agreement
Agree in writing: scope (which territories, which work), commission rate per income type,
term and renewal, what happens to deals done during the term after it ends ("sunset" or
post-term commission), expenses policy, who receives payments, termination notice.
Encourage the client to take independent advice; it protects you both.

## 5. Conflicts between clients
- Write a conflict rule: e.g. disclose to both clients when two are up for the same
  slot; never steer a buyer from one to the other for your own gain.
- When a buyer asks "who do you have for X?", give an honest shortlist and tell each
  client they were put forward alongside others.
- If conflicts become constant between two clients, consider whether you can serve both.

## 6. Career-arc planning (yearly, per client)
One page: where they are, where they want to be in 3 years, the 2–3 deals this year that
move them there (not just the best-paid ones), and what they must do (output, training,
profile). Review mid-year. Sometimes the right deal is lower-paid but repositions them.

## 7. Roster review and pruning
Each year rank clients by: revenue (last 12 months), trajectory, hours consumed,
relationship health. Watch **concentration**: if one client is >30–40% of revenue,
the agency is fragile.
For clients you can no longer help: have an honest conversation, give notice per the
agreement, hand over files and open submissions in writing, and agree how in-flight
deals and post-term commission are handled. Part ways well — buyers and other clients notice.

## Gotchas
- Signing on potential alone and then not pitching — the client's time is wasted.
- Verbal arrangements about commission on "old" deals; put them in the agreement.
- Taking on a direct competitor of your biggest earner without telling them.
- Minors: parental consent, earnings-protection rules and permits — jurisdiction-specific.

## Verify
- [ ] Capacity estimate done before each new signing.
- [ ] Scouting tracker shows source and stage for every prospect.
- [ ] Every client has a signed agreement covering post-term commission and termination.
- [ ] Conflict rule written and disclosed to clients.
- [ ] Annual review completed with revenue concentration checked.
