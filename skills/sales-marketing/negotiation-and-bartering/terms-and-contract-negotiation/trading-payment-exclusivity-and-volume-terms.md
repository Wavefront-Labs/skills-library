# Trading payment, exclusivity, volume and renewal terms

**Goal:** treat terms as money. Two deals at the same headline price can differ
by 10–20% in real value once payment timing, commitment, exclusivity and exit
rights are counted. Settle these commercially, in a term sheet, before lawyers
draft — legal drafting is downstream and not covered here.

## 1. Put a value on each term
Build a quick calculator (spreadsheet) so you can say what a term is worth:
- **Payment terms**: value of cash timing ≈ deal value × your cost of capital ×
  days ÷ 365. $500k at 10% cost of capital: net-60 vs net-30 ≈ $500k × 0.10 ×
  30/365 ≈ **$4.1k**. Upfront annual vs monthly billing is worth much more.
- **Early-payment discount**: "2/10 net 30" (2% if paid in 10 days) is an
  annualised rate of ~37% to the buyer — generous; use carefully.
- **Volume commitment**: committed vs forecast volume changes your risk and
  capacity planning; price the difference.
- **Exclusivity**: what other sales you forgo (territory, category, period) — the
  opportunity cost is the floor for its price.
- **Term length and renewal**: longer committed terms reduce churn risk and
  acquisition cost.
- **Price escalators**: CPI or fixed % uplifts protect margin over multi-year terms.

## 2. Payment terms
- Ask for: upfront or annual-in-advance billing, short payment terms, deposits on
  custom work, milestone payments on projects, late-payment interest.
- Give in return: modest discount for upfront payment, longer term for higher volume.
- Watch large buyers' standard terms (net-60/90+) — negotiate, use supply-chain
  finance, or price in the cost. Some jurisdictions cap terms for small suppliers.

## 3. Exclusivity
- Only grant it for something concrete: minimum volume, marketing commitment,
  higher price, shorter period.
- Define narrowly: product, territory, channel, customer segment, period.
- Include **performance conditions**: exclusivity lapses if minimum volumes
  aren't met in a quarter.
- Ask for exclusivity yourself (preferred supplier) in exchange for a better price.
- Exclusivity can raise competition-law issues for dominant players — check with legal.

## 4. Volume commitments
- **Tiered pricing**: price steps at committed volume levels; true-up at period end.
- **Minimum purchase / take-or-pay**: buyer pays for the minimum even if not taken.
- **Rebates** on achieved volume instead of upfront discounts — they pay only if the
  volume happens.
- Commit pricing to committed volume, not forecast; forecasts tend to be optimistic.

## 5. Renewal, escalation and exit
- **Auto-renewal** with notice period (e.g. 60–90 days before term end); some
  jurisdictions regulate consumer auto-renewals — check.
- **Renewal pricing cap** requests: offer a cap (e.g. CPI or ≤ 5%) in exchange for a
  longer term.
- **Termination for convenience**: resist, or price it (early-termination fee
  covering unrecovered setup costs and discount given for term).
- Termination for cause, liability caps and indemnities belong to legal — but flag
  commercial impacts early.

## 6. Document in a term sheet
One page: parties, scope, price and tiers, payment terms, term and renewal,
exclusivity and conditions, volume commitments and true-up, escalators, exit
terms, key open items. Get sign-off before it goes to legal so redlines don't
reopen the commercial deal.

## Gotchas
- Conceding on payment terms "because it isn't price" — it is price.
- Exclusivity without performance conditions locks you into an underperforming partner.
- Auto-renewal you can't enforce in practice (buyer churns anyway) has little value.
- **Uncertain:** legal enforceability of these terms varies by jurisdiction; this is commercial guidance only.

## Verify
- [ ] Each non-price term has a calculated value.
- [ ] Payment, exclusivity, volume and renewal terms traded, not given.
- [ ] Exclusivity is narrow and performance-conditioned.
- [ ] Volume pricing tied to commitments with true-up mechanics.
- [ ] Signed-off term sheet before legal drafting.
