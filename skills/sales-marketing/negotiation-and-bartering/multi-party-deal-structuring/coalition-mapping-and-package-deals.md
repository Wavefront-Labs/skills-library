# Coalition mapping and package deals — multi-party negotiation

**Goal:** close a deal when several parties each hold part of the yes: a
reseller, an end customer and a financier; a consortium bid; a property sale
with several owners; a supplier, a retailer and a co-marketing partner. Two-party
tactics break down because parties form coalitions, and the order of
conversations changes the outcome.

## 1. Map the parties
Table per party: interests (what they need, not what they ask for), BATNA,
decision authority, influence on others, relationships/alliances, likely
position on each issue. Include internal parties — your own finance or legal
team can be a party.
Classify:
- **Must-haves** — without them no deal (signatory, financier, regulator approval).
- **Blockers** — can veto or delay.
- **Swing parties** — persuadable, undecided.
- **Natural allies** — interests aligned with yours.

## 2. Find coalitions
- Which subset of parties is enough to make the deal happen? That's the
  **winning coalition** to build.
- Which subset could block it? Work to split or satisfy that **blocking coalition**.
- Look for parties whose interests are complementary across issues (one cares
  about price, another about timing) — they can trade with each other.

## 3. Sequence the conversations
- Usually start with **allies** to build a base, then **swing parties**, then
  approach **blockers** with an already-strong coalition and a proposal that
  addresses their core concern.
- Get commitments from parties whose agreement others look to (the anchor customer,
  the lead investor) early — others follow.
- Keep a side-agreement log: what was promised to whom. Inconsistent promises
  discovered later collapse multi-party deals.
- Mind information flow: assume anything you tell one party may reach the others.

## 4. Build the package
- List all issues across all parties; for each, note who cares most.
- **Logroll**: give each party what matters most to them on the issues they value,
  in exchange for flexibility on issues they value less.
- Present **packages**, not issue-by-issue agreement: "nothing is agreed until
  everything is agreed" matters more with more parties.
- Use contingent terms to bridge differing forecasts ("if volume exceeds X, the
  distributor's margin steps up").

## 5. Converge with a single text
The **single negotiating text** method (used in diplomacy, e.g. Camp David):
one party (or a neutral facilitator) drafts a term sheet, circulates it, collects
comments from everyone, revises, and repeats. Parties critique one document
instead of defending separate positions. In commercial deals, the party holding
the pen has an advantage — volunteer to draft.

## 6. Use brokers and facilitators
A trusted intermediary (lead adviser, channel manager, mediator) can shuttle
between parties, test proposals without commitment and save face. Agree their
role and confidentiality rules up front.

## Gotchas
- A deal that excludes a party with veto power isn't a deal — check approvals
  (boards, regulators, lenders) early.
- Bilateral side deals can trigger fairness reactions and walk-outs if revealed.
- Long sequences lose momentum; set a timeline with checkpoints.
- Competition and anti-collusion rules apply when competitors negotiate together
  (consortia, buying groups) — involve legal.
- **Uncertain:** sequencing heuristics depend heavily on the deal; treat §3 as a default, not a rule.

## Verify
- [ ] Party map with interests, BATNA, authority and alliances.
- [ ] Winning and blocking coalitions identified.
- [ ] Conversation sequence planned; side-agreement log kept and consistent.
- [ ] Package built with logrolls across issues.
- [ ] Single draft text circulating, with you holding the pen where possible.
