# Opening offer and price framing

**Goal:** as the seller, make the number you want feel reasonable before
haggling starts. Negotiation research (anchoring studies, e.g. Galinsky &
Mussweiler) consistently finds that first offers pull final agreements toward
them — when the anchorer is well informed.

## 1. Prepare three numbers and one alternative
- **BATNA** — your best alternative if this deal fails (other buyer, keep the
  stock, sell later). It sets your strength.
- **Reservation price (walk-away)** — the worst deal better than your BATNA.
- **Target** — the good, realistic outcome.
- **Aspiration/anchor** — the most ambitious number you can still justify with a
  straight face.
Estimate the same for the buyer: their alternatives, budget, pressure (deadline,
pain of the status quo). The **ZOPA** is the overlap between your walk-away and theirs.

## 2. Should you go first?
- **Go first** when you know the value well and the buyer may not (most
  sellers in their own market). Your anchor sets the range.
- **Let them go first** when you genuinely don't know their value (unique asset,
  new market) — their number may be better than you hoped. Have a counter ready
  so their anchor doesn't stick.
- If they anchor absurdly, don't counter close to it — that legitimises it (see §4).

## 3. Choose the anchor
- Ambitious but defensible: you must be able to explain it with criteria —
  market comparables, cost, value to them, what others paid.
- A **precise** number ($48,750) signals calculation and invites smaller
  counter-moves than a round one ($50,000) — research supports this, though the
  effect is modest.
- Leave room: plan the concession path from anchor to target (see concession-trading).

## 4. Frame the price
- **Justify first, then name the number**: "For a three-year term with priority
  support and on-site training, which saved a similar client about 12 hours a
  week, the investment is $48,750." The rationale primes the value.
- **Frame against the right comparator**: cost of the problem, cost of doing nothing,
  premium alternative — not the cheapest competitor.
- **Gains vs losses**: people weigh losses more — "each month without this costs
  you about $6k in rework" can motivate more than "you'll save $6k".
- **Break it down** where honest: per user per month, per day, per unit produced.
- **Range offers** ("between $48k and $52k depending on term") anchor at the
  bottom of the range in the buyer's mind; if you use a range, make its bottom your target.
- Silence after naming the number. Don't justify further unless asked.

## 5. Neutralise their anchor
- Don't counter immediately; re-anchor on criteria: "That's well below what
  comparable work costs; let me show you how we price it."
- If it's extreme, say so and ask them to rethink rather than meeting halfway.
- Put a fresh, justified number on the table yourself.

## Gotchas
- An anchor so aggressive it's insulting can end the conversation — check against
  real comparables.
- Anchors work best with justification; a bare high number reads as greed.
- Don't anchor in writing on a price you can't later honour (quotes may bind).
- Repeated relationships (key accounts) punish tricks; framing must stay honest.

## Verify
- [ ] BATNA, walk-away, target and anchor written down before the meeting.
- [ ] Decision on going first is based on who knows the value better.
- [ ] Anchor justified by objective criteria and stated after the rationale.
- [ ] Comparator frame chosen deliberately.
- [ ] A plan exists to re-anchor if they open first or extreme.
