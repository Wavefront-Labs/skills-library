# Trial closes and nibble defence

**Goal:** get from "this looks good" to a signature, without giving away more at
the end than in the whole negotiation. Deals stall or leak most in the last 10%.

## 1. Trial closes throughout
A trial close tests readiness without asking for the order:
- "On a scale of 1–10, how well does this solve the problem you described?"
  (Anything under 8: "What would make it a 9?")
- "If we can sort out the delivery timing, is there anything else stopping us?"
- "Who else needs to be comfortable with this before it's signed?"
Each answer either surfaces the next objection or confirms you can close. Ask
them after presenting each major part of the deal.

## 2. Agree the path to signature
A **mutual action plan** (close plan): steps from now to signature with owners
and dates — legal review, security review, budget approval, signatory. Ask: "What
has to happen, and by when, for this to start on 1 March?" Deals without a
named signatory and date aren't close.

## 3. Use deadlines and scarcity honestly
- Real reasons only: price increase on a date, limited stock or production slot,
  implementation team availability, end of a promotion.
- State it once, with evidence; repeating it reads as pressure.
- Fake deadlines destroy credibility when they pass — and in consumer selling
  can breach unfair-practice rules.

## 4. Closing questions
- **Summary close**: recap what's been agreed (the trade log) then "Shall I send
  the agreement for signature today?"
- **Alternative close**: "Do you want to start on the 1st or the 15th?" — only
  when both options are genuinely fine.
- **Assumptive steps**: send the order form/contract with their details filled
  in after verbal agreement.
Then stop talking.

## 5. Splitting the difference (well)
Only when the gap is small relative to the deal and both sides have moved.
Better: propose a split *conditioned* on something ("If we meet at $94k, can
you sign by Friday?"). Don't be the first to suggest splitting if you can
avoid it — let them propose, then counter-split.

## 6. The final offer
- Say it's final only if it is: "This is the best I can do. If it doesn't work, I
  understand." Then hold.
- Give a reason it's final (approval limit, margin) and a time limit.
- If they push after a genuine final offer, be prepared to walk — or you've taught
  them your finals aren't.

## 7. Defending against nibbles
A **nibble** is a small extra request after agreement ("You'll throw in
installation, right?"). Defences:
- **Pre-empt**: summarise the full package and ask "Is there anything else we need
  to cover before we finalise?" — closes the list.
- **Trade it**: "Installation is $1,800. If it's important, I can include it if we
  move to annual billing upfront."
- **Price the menu** in advance so extras have visible prices.
- **Higher authority** (only if true): "The price was approved on this exact scope;
  changes go back to approval."
- Stay friendly; a nibble is a test, not a crisis.

## Gotchas
- Closing before discovering the real decision maker wastes the close.
- Over-talking after a closing question invites renegotiation.
- Contract redlines can be nibbles in legal language — check commercial impact.
- Discounting to hit quarter-end trains buyers to wait for quarter-end.

## Verify
- [ ] Trial closes used after each main element; objections surfaced early.
- [ ] Mutual action plan with signatory and date exists.
- [ ] Any deadline or scarcity claim is true and evidenced.
- [ ] Final offer had a reason and a time limit, and was held.
- [ ] Late requests were traded or priced, not given free.
