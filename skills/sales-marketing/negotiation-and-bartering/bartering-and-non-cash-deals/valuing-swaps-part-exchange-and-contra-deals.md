# Valuing swaps, part-exchange and contra deals

**Goal:** trade value for value when money isn't the medium — a goods swap, a
trade-in against a purchase, a services-for-services contra between businesses —
without losing out to fuzzy valuations, and without forgetting that tax
authorities usually treat barter as if cash changed hands.

## 1. Value both sides in money first
Barter fails when each side values its own goods high and the other's low.
Before negotiating, put a defensible money value on both:
- **Goods**: recent sale prices of comparable items (marketplace sold listings,
  dealer guides, auction results) — *sold* prices, not asking prices. Adjust for
  condition, age, completeness, and how quickly it could be sold.
- **Services**: your normal rate card × hours, **and** your actual marginal cost
  (often far lower — spare capacity is cheap to give).
- **Inventory/media/space**: list price vs realistic price you'd achieve.
Know two numbers for each side: **market value** (what it would sell for) and
**cost to provide** (what giving it costs you). Good barter gives what's cheap
for you and gets what's expensive for you to buy.

## 2. Negotiate the swap
- Agree the **valuation basis** before the items: "Shall we both use last-90-days
  sold prices?" Then the item haggle is about evidence, not opinion.
- Balance with cash where values differ ("my van plus $2,000 for your trailer and
  tools") — cash top-ups make uneven swaps possible.
- Specify quantity, quality/condition, delivery and deadlines for each side —
  in-kind obligations are easier to under-deliver than cash.
- For services, define scope tightly (hours, deliverables, period).

## 3. Part-exchange / trade-in (selling side)
- Separate the two numbers: the trade-in value and the price of what they're
  buying. Customers focus on the net figure; you should check both against margin.
- Appraise in front of the customer with a checklist (condition, faults, age,
  service history); explain deductions.
- Know your **disposal route and cost** (retail it, wholesale/auction, scrap) —
  trade-in value = expected resale − reconditioning − selling cost − margin.
- Avoid inflating the trade-in to hide a discount; it distorts margin reporting and
  stock valuation.

## 4. Contra deals between businesses
Common: advertising/media for goods, services between suppliers, venue for
sponsorship exposure.
- Write a short contra agreement: each side's deliverables, agreed money value,
  period, what happens if one side under-delivers (cash settlement at agreed rate).
- Track the **contra balance** like an account; reconcile monthly.
- **Barter exchanges** (trade networks) use trade credits to allow indirect swaps;
  check fees, credit expiry and how easy it is to spend credits on things you need.

## 5. Tax and records
In many jurisdictions barter is taxable as if sold at fair market value:
- Income tax: the value received is income (e.g. US IRS treats barter income at
  fair market value; barter exchanges report on Form 1099-B).
- Sales tax/VAT/GST: usually due on both supplies; each side issues an invoice at
  market value.
- Keep invoices or a written record of values used. **Uncertain:** rules differ
  by country and for small private swaps; check with an accountant.

## Gotchas
- Accepting goods you can't use or sell — barter value is only what *you* can realise.
- Services contras drift: one side delivers, the other delays. Set deadlines.
- Inflated "list price" valuations on media or inventory.
- Trade credits can become illiquid if the exchange shrinks.

## Verify
- [ ] Both sides valued from sold comparables or rate cards, plus your cost to provide.
- [ ] Valuation basis agreed before item-by-item haggling; cash top-up used if uneven.
- [ ] Trade-in value derived from resale minus costs and margin.
- [ ] Contra agreement written with deliverables, values and under-delivery terms; balance tracked.
- [ ] Tax treatment checked and invoices/records kept at market value.
