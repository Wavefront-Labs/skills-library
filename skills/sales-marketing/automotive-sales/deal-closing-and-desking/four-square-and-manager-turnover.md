# Desking and closing — four-square, turnovers and the walk-out

**Goal:** reach a signed deal that the customer feels good about and the store can
live with, without the back-and-forth that burns hours and goodwill.

## 1. The worksheet (four-square)
A sheet divided into four boxes, used by many dealers to structure the conversation:
| Price of vehicle | Trade-in value |
|---|---|
| **Down payment** | **Monthly payment** |
The tactic its critics describe: moving numbers between boxes so the customer loses
track of the total. Used transparently, it's simply a way to show the four levers.
Always also show the **out-the-door price** (price + fees + taxes − trade + payoff)
and the loan term — customers increasingly negotiate on that figure, and in many
places itemised disclosure is required anyway.

## 2. The first pencil
- The desk manager writes the first offer from the deal: list or advertised price,
  trade ACV, a down-payment ask and payment ranges at given terms.
- Present it confidently, then **ask for commitment**: "If we can agree these figures,
  are you ready to take it home today?" Without commitment, every concession is wasted.

## 3. Negotiating
- Find out which number matters most: total price, payment, down payment, or trade.
- **Payment buyers:** adjust term or down payment rather than price, but always state
  the term and total cost. Stretching to 84 months to hit a payment is a disservice
  the customer will remember.
- **Price buyers:** negotiate in decreasing increments to signal you're near the floor.
- Trade concessions for commitment: "I'll take that to my manager if you'll sign
  the offer sheet at that number."
- Get a **written, signed offer** (with deposit if store policy) before each trip to the desk.

## 4. Manager turnover (T.O.)
When you're stuck, introduce a manager or senior closer — fresh face, new authority.
- Brief them privately first: the customer's needs, hot buttons, objections, numbers so far.
- Introduce warmly: "This is <manager>, they can help us with the numbers."
- Stay in the room, quietly. The customer is still yours.
- If the T.O. fails, a respectful exit beats pressure (see 5).

## 5. "I want to think about it" (the be-back)
1. Agree: "Absolutely, it's a big decision."
2. Isolate: "Just so I help you think it through — is it the car, the payment, or
   something else?" Usually it's one concrete thing; address it if you can.
3. If they still leave: confirm the exact figures in writing, set a specific follow-up
   time ("I'll call Thursday at 6"), and log everything in the CRM.
4. Follow up as promised. A large share of buyers who leave without buying return or
   buy elsewhere within days — the follow-up decides which.

## Gotchas
- Holding keys or a licence to stop someone leaving is a pressure tactic that breaches
  trust and, in some places, consumer law. Hand them back.
- Advertised prices must be honoured; "addendum" markups and mandatory add-ons are
  regulated in many jurisdictions.
- Multiple managers grinding the customer for hours is a top complaint in dealer reviews.
- Front-end gross obsession can lose F&I and service revenue from a happy customer.

## Verify
- [ ] Out-the-door price and term shown with every payment quote.
- [ ] Commitment asked for before the first concession.
- [ ] Signed offer obtained before each trip to the desk.
- [ ] Manager briefed privately before every turnover.
- [ ] Walk-outs left with written figures and a scheduled follow-up logged in CRM.
