# Complaint de-escalation

**Goal:** bring the emotional temperature down enough to solve the actual problem,
protect yourself from abuse, and escalate only when escalation genuinely helps.

Many companies teach a named model (HEARD, LATTE, LEAP and similar). They share the
same sequence; this is the common core.

## 1. Let them finish (don't interrupt)
- Let the customer get the story out. Interrupting restarts the anger.
- Make small listening signals ("mm", "okay", "I see") on the phone; on chat, a brief
  "I'm reading all of this now" if they send several messages.
- Take notes: what happened, what they want, any deadlines or consequences for them.
- Don't take it personally — they're angry at the situation, you're the representative.

## 2. Acknowledge (feelings, then facts)
- **Label the emotion** and the impact: "That sounds really frustrating — you took a day
  off for a delivery that never came."
- **Apologise for the experience**, sincerely and specifically: "I'm sorry you've had to
  chase this three times." You can apologise for the experience before you know whose
  fault it was.
- Avoid: "calm down", "as I said", "that's our policy", "you should have…", and robotic
  repeated "I understand".

## 3. Take control with questions
- Move from emotion to facts with focused questions: "Can I get your order number so I
  can see exactly what happened?" Questions shift them from venting to answering.
- **Summarise** back what you heard and what they want: "So the parcel shows delivered,
  you don't have it, and you need it before Friday. Have I got that right?"
- Lower your pace and volume slightly; people tend to mirror.

## 4. Commit to a next step
- Say what you **can** do, not what you can't: "Here's what I can do right now…"
- Give options where possible (choice restores a sense of control).
- Give a specific time and own it: "I'll call you back by 3 pm tomorrow with the
  carrier's answer." Then do it. Broken promises create the next, angrier call.
- See `sales-marketing.customer-service-and-support.service-recovery.recovery-offers-and-closing-the-loop`
  for sizing goodwill.

## 5. Abuse boundaries
Angry ≠ abusive. Swearing at the situation can be tolerated; personal insults, slurs,
threats or sexual comments are not. A typical policy (follow your employer's):
1. **Warning 1:** "I want to help you, and I can't continue if the language is directed at me."
2. **Warning 2:** "If this continues, I'll need to end the call."
3. **End the contact** politely, log it with exact words, notify your supervisor.
Threats of violence or self-harm: follow your safety procedure immediately.

## 6. Escalation judgement
Escalate when: the fix needs authority you lack (refund above limit, policy exception),
there's a legal/regulatory or safety angle, the customer has had repeated failed
contacts, or the relationship with you specifically has broken down. Do a **warm
transfer**: brief the supervisor first so the customer doesn't repeat themselves.
Don't escalate merely because they asked loudly — offer the fix you can give first;
many accept it.

## Gotchas
- Arguing facts before acknowledging feelings almost always escalates.
- Over-apologising without action sounds scripted.
- Putting someone on hold without warning — always ask and give a time.
- Written channels: re-read before sending; tone reads colder in text.

## Verify
- [ ] Customer finished their account without interruption.
- [ ] Emotion and impact acknowledged; specific apology given.
- [ ] Issue summarised and confirmed before proposing a fix.
- [ ] A specific next step with a time was committed and logged.
- [ ] Any abuse handled with warnings per policy; escalations were warm transfers.
