# Underwriter relationships and hard-to-place escalation

**Goal:** have insurers who pick up the phone, quote your risks first, bend on
terms when it matters, and pay you fairly — plus a reliable route for risks
your usual markets won't touch.

## 1. Know what an underwriter needs from you
Underwriters are measured on loss ratio and on writing enough good business.
You become valuable by sending:
- **Fit** — risks inside their appetite (keep an appetite grid; see
  market-submission-and-binding).
- **Quality submissions** — complete, accurate, honest about the bad bits.
- **Hit rate** — if you get quotes and never bind, they deprioritise you. Track
  your quote-to-bind ratio per insurer and share it.
- **A balanced book** — not just their worst risks. Offering good risks earns
  flexibility on difficult ones.

## 2. Build the relationship
- Meet your key underwriters at least twice a year outside renewal crunch:
  share your pipeline, ask about appetite changes and target classes.
- Ask for an annual **portfolio review**: premium placed, loss ratio, retention,
  growth. Come with your own numbers.
- Be straight when a risk deteriorates or a claim is coming — surprises destroy trust.
- Escalate through the line (underwriter → underwriting manager →
  state/regional manager) only when needed, and tell the underwriter first.

## 3. Negotiating terms and commission
- **Terms on a risk:** trade information and risk improvements for price/cover
  ("they've installed monitored sprinklers since last year — can we revisit the
  rate?"). Ask what would get it over the line.
- **Agency/commission agreement:** base commission by class, volume or growth
  bonuses, profit commission tied to loss ratio. Bring data: premium volume,
  growth, loss ratio, retention, cost to serve. Agree measurement periods and
  definitions (earned vs written premium, how large losses are capped) in writing.
- Know the disclosure rules: many regimes require commission and conflict
  disclosure to clients, and some restrict contingent commissions.

## 4. Escalating hard-to-place risks
When standard markets decline:
1. **Diagnose why** — class, claims, location (flood/bushfire), size, missing controls.
   Fix what you can (risk improvements, higher excess) and retry once.
2. **Specialist and MGA markets** — underwriting agencies with binding authority
   for niche classes.
3. **Wholesale / surplus lines** — in the US, excess-and-surplus (E&S) through a
   wholesale broker, with surplus-lines filing/tax and diligent-search rules that
   vary by state. In the UK/elsewhere, Lloyd's via a Lloyd's broker or coverholder.
4. **Structure creatively** — split limits across insurers (layering), co-insurance,
   higher self-insured retention, named-perils instead of all-risks.
5. Tell the client early that cover will be dearer and narrower, and why.
Track hard-to-place outcomes by class so you know the route next time.

## Gotchas
- Using a wholesaler adds a second commission layer — make sure the client
  understands total cost.
- Don't shop a declined risk to every market at once; an underwriter who sees it
  from three brokers will decline all three.
- Hard-market cycles change appetite fast; re-check your grid quarterly.
- **Uncertain:** surplus-lines regulation and Lloyd's access routes are summarised
  loosely here — check current local rules before relying on them.

## Verify
- [ ] Appetite grid current; quote-to-bind ratio tracked per insurer.
- [ ] Annual portfolio review held with each key insurer, with your own data.
- [ ] Commission terms and definitions in a signed agreement; disclosures compliant.
- [ ] Hard-to-place risks diagnosed, improved where possible, then escalated with client briefed.
- [ ] Hard-to-place route logged by class for next time.
