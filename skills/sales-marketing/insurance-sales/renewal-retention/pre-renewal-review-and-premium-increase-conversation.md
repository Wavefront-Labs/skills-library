# Pre-renewal review and the premium-increase conversation

**Goal:** renew the policies worth keeping, at terms the client accepts, without
surprises — because a client who first hears about a 25% increase from the
renewal notice is a client who shops around. Most agency/broker profit is in
retained renewals, not new business.

## 1. Run a renewal calendar
From your agency system, pull renewals 120 days out every week and work them on
a fixed cadence:
- **90 days** — review the account (claims, changes, cover gaps); contact the
  client for updates on values, activities, staff, vehicles.
- **60 days** — submit updated information to the current insurer; request
  renewal terms early. Decide whether to remarket (see §3).
- **30 days** — have terms; present renewal to the client personally.
- **14 days** — chase unsigned instructions; escalate.
- **Inception** — confirm renewed, invoice, file. Never let a policy lapse because
  nobody asked for payment.
Large or complex accounts start at 120 days.

## 2. The pre-renewal review call or meeting
- "What's changed in the last year?" — new locations, revenue, staff, assets,
  contracts, renovations, new drivers, family changes.
- Update sums insured (rebuild cost, contents, stock, equipment) — inflation erodes
  adequacy every year.
- Review claims and what was learned.
- Spot cross-sell exposures (see cross-selling-cover).
This is where you show value; renewals handled only by mail are the ones that lapse.

## 3. Remarket or hold?
Remarket when: increase is large and not explained by claims or exposure growth,
the insurer reduced cover, service has been poor, or the client asks. Hold when:
the account had claims (other markets will price them too), the relationship
with the underwriter is strong, or the market is hardening generally.
Remarketing costs time and can sour the incumbent; do it deliberately.

## 4. Explaining a premium increase
Structure the conversation:
1. **Lead with the number** — never bury it. "Your renewal is $8,400, up from $6,900."
2. **Break it down**: how much from rate (market-wide), from exposure (higher sums
   insured, revenue), from claims, from taxes/levies. Get the breakdown from the
   underwriter; most will give it if asked.
3. **What you did** — markets you tested or negotiated with, what was achieved.
4. **Options** — higher excess/deductible, removing extensions the client doesn't
   need, adjusting sums insured to accurate (not lower than accurate) values,
   paying by instalments. Show premium and risk effect of each.
5. **Recommendation** and a clear ask for instructions by a date.
Never cut cover silently to hit a price; record what the client chose to drop.

## 5. Competitor quote defence
When the client has a cheaper quote: ask for it, compare line by line (limits,
excesses, exclusions, insurer strength). Often the gap is explained by cover
differences — show them. If it's genuinely like-for-like and cheaper, take it to
your underwriter; if they won't move, tell the client honestly.

## 6. Win-back of lapsed clients
Call within a week of lapse; ask why and log the reason. Diarise a call 30 days
before the *new* policy's renewal — that's when they're open to switching back.

## Gotchas
- Automatic renewals still need updated information; stale underwriting data can
  undermine claims.
- Some regimes require renewal notices with prior-year premium comparisons and
  specific lead times (e.g. UK pricing-practice rules, Australian general insurance
  code) — **check yours**; this skill doesn't list them.
- Track retention rate by count *and* by premium; losing a few large accounts hides
  behind a good count.

## Verify
- [ ] Renewals pulled 120 days out weekly and worked on the 90/60/30/14 cadence.
- [ ] Every client had a pre-renewal review with updated values.
- [ ] Increases presented with breakdown, actions taken, and options.
- [ ] Remarket/hold decision recorded with reason.
- [ ] Lapses logged with reason and a win-back date set.
