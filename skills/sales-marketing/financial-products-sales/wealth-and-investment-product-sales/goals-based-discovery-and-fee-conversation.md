# Goals-based discovery and the fee conversation

**Goal:** sell investment, fund and pension products by anchoring every
recommendation to the client's own stated goals, so the product presentation
and the fee are judged against outcomes they care about — not against the
cheapest index fund on the internet. Personal advice is regulated almost
everywhere; this covers the sales conversation, not licensing, and you must
use your firm's approved risk-profiler and disclosure documents.

## 1. Discovery meeting (60–90 minutes)
Structure it as three layers and take notes in the client's words:
1. **Facts** — age, income, assets, debts, existing funds/pensions (get
   statements), dependants, tax position, health if relevant to time horizon.
2. **Goals** — make each one concrete: *what*, *when*, *how much*. "Retire
   comfortably" becomes "stop full-time work at 60 on $70k a year in today's money."
   Rank them; there are usually 2–4.
3. **Feelings about money** — past investing experiences ("What happened to you
   in 2008/2020?"), what keeps them up at night, who else influences decisions.
Useful prompts: "If we met in three years and you were delighted, what would
have happened?" and "What would make you sell everything?" The second answer
is your real risk tolerance, whatever the questionnaire says.

Close discovery by reading the goals back and getting a "yes, that's it".

## 2. Risk profile — reconcile, don't just score
Run the firm's questionnaire, then compare to (a) behaviour in past downturns
and (b) the return the goals actually require. When they conflict (a
"conservative" profile that needs 7% a year), say so and offer the trade-offs:
save more, retire later, accept more volatility, or lower the goal. Record the choice.

## 3. Presenting the product
- One page per goal: goal → strategy → product(s) → projected range (not a
  single number) → what could go wrong.
- Show volatility as money, not percentages: "In a bad year this could be down
  about $40k on paper; in 2020 a similar mix fell ~X% and recovered in Y months"
  — use your fund's real history, not invented figures.
- Explain *why this and not the alternative* they've probably heard of (index
  ETF, their employer default fund). If you can't articulate it, reconsider the product.

## 4. The fee conversation
- Raise fees yourself, early, in dollars: "All-in, you'll pay about $4,200 a year:
  $2,700 advice fee, $1,500 in fund and platform costs." Percentages hide the number;
  dollars build trust.
- Tie fee to value delivered: the goal plan, rebalancing, tax structuring,
  behavioural coaching ("stopping you selling at the bottom"), estate coordination.
- Handling "that's too much": ask "Compared to what?" — then compare like with
  like (advice + implementation vs. DIY). Offer scope options (lower-touch
  service tier) rather than discounting the same service.
- Never be vague about commissions or trail; most regimes require disclosure and
  vagueness reads as hiding something.

## 5. Review meetings as selling
Annual review agenda: progress against each goal (traffic-light), life changes,
contributions, rebalancing, fee recap ("this year you paid $X; here's what it
did"), and one forward question ("anything coming in the next 12 months?").
New money and referrals come from reviews far more than from cold prospects.

## Gotchas
- Past performance tables invite chasing; lead with the goal fit, not returns.
- Couples: get both partners' goals; the silent one often vetoes later.
- Don't let a questionnaire score override a clear behavioural red flag.
- Rules on inducements, fee disclosure and ongoing-fee renewal consents vary by
  jurisdiction (e.g. UK, Australia and the EU are stricter than many) — check yours.

## Verify
- [ ] Each goal is written as what / when / how much and the client confirmed it.
- [ ] Risk profile is reconciled with past behaviour and required return.
- [ ] Fees were stated in currency, all-in, before the client asked.
- [ ] Recommendation explains why it beats the obvious cheaper alternative.
- [ ] A review date is booked with a goal-tracking agenda.
