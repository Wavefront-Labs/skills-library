# Building a palette that works in real light

**Goal:** colours chosen in a studio that still look right on the client's walls at
8 am and 8 pm, in a palette that holds the whole space together.

## 1. Think in three dimensions of colour
- **Hue** (red, green…), **value** (light/dark) and **chroma** (intensity). Most
  failed schemes are value or chroma problems, not hue problems.
- Use a notation that describes all three: **NCS** (e.g. S 1502-Y50R: 15% blackness,
  02% chromaticness, hue Y50R), Munsell, or a paint maker's system. It makes
  "slightly greyer and darker" a measurable instruction.
- **LRV** (light reflectance value, 0–100) tells you how much light a colour returns:
  whites ~80–90+, mid-tones ~30–60. In dark rooms, low LRVs feel cave-like unless
  that's the intention. LRV contrast also matters for accessibility (UK guidance
  often cites ≥ 30 points between key surfaces like doors and walls for visually
  impaired users — check current guidance).

## 2. Undertone is the decider
"Neutral" colours carry undertones: warm (yellow/red), cool (blue/green), or grey/
violet. Compare candidates side by side on white paper — the undertone jumps out.
Keep undertones consistent across the main neutrals, floors and large furniture,
or mismatches read as "dirty".

## 3. Light changes everything
- **Orientation (northern hemisphere; reverse in the south):** north light is cool
  and flat — warm-undertone colours often counteract the grey cast; south light is warm
  and strong — cooler colours stay crisp; east/west change through the day.
- **Artificial light CCT:** 2700 K pushes colours warm; 4000 K cool. Test under the
  lighting actually specified (see
  `architecture-construction.interior-design.interior-lighting-design.layered-lighting-scheme-lux-cct-and-controls`).
- **Metamerism:** two samples that match under one light differ under another. Check
  fabric-to-paint matches under daylight *and* the room's lamps.
- **Large areas read lighter and more saturated** than the chip; step down in chroma
  for whole walls.

## 4. Testing properly
- Paint **A3 or larger drawdowns** (two coats on card) rather than patches on the
  existing wall (the old colour around a patch distorts it). Move them around the
  room: near windows, in corners, at floor and ceiling level.
- Look at morning, midday and evening; photograph with the same white balance.

## 5. Build and coordinate
- A simple structure: a dominant base (walls, large surfaces), a secondary (joinery,
  large furniture, floor), and accents — the old 60/30/10 rule is a fine start, not a law.
- Decide ceilings and trim deliberately: a ceiling tinted from the wall colour (e.g.
  a quarter strength) often looks more refined than brilliant white; same for trim.
- **Colour flow:** in connected spaces, keep value steps and undertones related; use
  stronger colour in contained rooms (WCs, studies) or "colour-drench" a room
  (walls, trim, ceiling in one colour) for impact.
- Record final colours with maker, name, code, sheen and location in the finishes
  schedule.

## Gotchas
- Choosing from a 2 cm chip under showroom lights.
- Cool grey in a north room → gloomy.
- Testing on the old colour.
- Forgetting the floor and big furniture are part of the palette.

## Verify
- [ ] Every colour recorded with system code, sheen and LRV.
- [ ] Undertones consistent across main neutrals and fixed elements.
- [ ] Large drawdowns viewed at three times of day and under specified lamps.
- [ ] Adjacent rooms checked from doorways for relationship.
- [ ] Contrast between key surfaces meets accessibility guidance where relevant.
