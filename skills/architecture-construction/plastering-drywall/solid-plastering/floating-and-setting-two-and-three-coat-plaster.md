# Floating and setting: two- and three-coat solid plaster on masonry

**Goal:** a flat (typically within ~3 mm under a 1.8–2 m straightedge — check your
specification), crack-free wall with straight corners, where each coat bonds to the one below.

## 1. Know your coats
- **Three-coat work:** scratch (render) coat ~8–10 mm, keyed with a scratcher → float
  (floating/browning) coat ~8–10 mm ruled flat → set/finish coat 2–3 mm. Used on very
  uneven or traditional backgrounds, and for lime.
- **Two-coat work:** a single undercoat (~11 mm) ruled flat, then a 2–3 mm finish. Most
  modern gypsum work on reasonably true blockwork.
- **Skim:** finish coat only, over board or a sound existing undercoat.

Never apply a strong coat over a weaker one (e.g. cement-rich over lime) — the stronger
coat shrinks and pulls the weak one off. Each coat should be equal or weaker than the
one beneath.

## 2. Background and suction
- Brush off dust, remove grease and loose material. Rake out soft joints.
- **Suction** is the whole game: a very absorbent background (aerated block, old brick)
  sucks water out of the plaster so it can't cure; a dense one (concrete, engineering
  brick) gives nothing to grip.
  - High suction: dampen with water, or seal/control with a dilute bonding agent per the
    plaster manufacturer.
  - Low suction / smooth: a bonding coat, a proprietary bonding agent, or a spatterdash
    (cement/sand slurry thrown on).
- Mixed backgrounds (block and concrete lintel) → bridge with expanded metal lath or the
  system's reinforcing mesh to stop a crack along the junction.

## 3. Undercoat ruled to screeds
1. Fix **angle beads** at external corners and **stop beads** at edges, plumb and to the
   finished plane (minus the finish thickness).
2. Form **screeds** (vertical bands, or dots and timber/aluminium screed rules) at ~1.5 m
   centres, plumbed and lined through.
3. Lay on the undercoat between screeds with a hawk and trowel, firm pressure for
   adhesion.
4. **Rule off** with a straightedge (feather edge/darby) bearing on the screeds; fill
   hollows and rule again.
5. When it starts to firm up, **devil float** or scratch lightly to key the surface for the
   finish. Gypsum undercoats: lightly scratch; cement/lime: wood-float to an open texture.

## 4. The set coat
1. Apply the finish in two thin passes (first tight, second building to ~2–3 mm).
2. **Trowel up** as it stiffens — first flattening pass, then again as it firms, using
   a clean trowel and a splash of water.
3. Final **polish** when it has nearly set: firm strokes with the trowel slightly
   tilted. Too early = drag marks and pitting; too late = it won't close up.
   Timing is the craft: test with a finger — it should take a print but not stick.

## 5. Lime specifics
Lime plasters cure by carbonation: they need slow drying (days–weeks per coat), protection
from frost, sun and draughts, and misting. Rushing lime gives shrinkage cracking.
(Uncertain: exact per-coat intervals depend on hydraulic class — follow the supplier.)

## Gotchas
- Plastering on a hot, dry, high-suction wall → crazing and poor bond.
- Finishing over an undercoat that has fully dried with no key → delamination.
- Beads fixed proud → the finish ends thin and the corner shows.
- Retempering gypsum that has started to set weakens it. Mix fresh.

## Verify
- [ ] 2 m straightedge in several directions: no gap beyond the spec tolerance.
- [ ] Tap test after drying: no hollow ring (delamination).
- [ ] Arrises straight and plumb (level on each corner).
- [ ] No crazing, trowel marks or pinholes under raking light.
- [ ] Lime work protected and dried slowly before decoration.
