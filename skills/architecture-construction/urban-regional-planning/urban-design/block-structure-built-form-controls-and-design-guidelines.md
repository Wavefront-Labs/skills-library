# Urban design — blocks, built-form controls and design guidelines

**Goal:** streets and spaces that are walkable, legible and comfortable, secured through
controls that individual developments must follow.

## 1. Block structure and permeability
- Small, regular blocks give route choice and walkability. Many well-loved walkable
  cities have block sides of roughly 70–150 m; long superblocks (300 m+) force detours.
  Where large blocks exist, require **mid-block links** (public, open 24h, overlooked).
- **Perimeter blocks:** buildings face streets on all sides, private/communal space
  inside. Clear fronts (public) and backs (private) make streets safe and active.
- Measure connectivity: intersection density (intersections per km²) or ped-shed ratio
  (see transport-land-use-integration skill).

## 2. Street and space proportion
- **Enclosure:** ratio of building height to street width. Very roughly, 1:1 to 1:3
  (H:W) reads as a defined street; below about 1:4 enclosure is weak; above 1:1 (taller
  than wide) can feel canyon-like unless upper levels step back. Treat these as
  starting points, tested in 3D.
- **Solar access:** test overshadowing of key public spaces at the winter solstice (or
  equinox, per local rules) — e.g. "no additional shadow on the plaza between 11:00 and
  14:00 at the winter solstice".
- Preserve **sightlines** to landmarks and topography through view corridors.

## 3. Built-form controls
- **Street-wall height:** the podium height on the street edge (e.g. 3–6 storeys)
  consistent along a street, with **upper-level setbacks** above it so towers don't
  dominate the street.
- **Front setback** zero (urban/main streets) or a consistent landscaped setback
  (residential streets).
- **Height transitions** to lower-scale neighbours: angular planes (e.g. 45° from the
  rear boundary) or stepped heights.
- **Tower separation and floorplate limits** for slender towers, daylight and privacy.
- **Building depth** (e.g. apartment depths that allow natural light and
  cross-ventilation).
Express controls as dimensions and diagrams, with the **objective** each control serves,
so assessors can judge variations (see development-assessment skill).

## 4. Active frontages
- Map the streets where ground floors must be active (shops, cafés, lobbies, workshops)
  and set requirements: minimum proportion of glazed, transparent frontage; entrances
  at regular intervals (e.g. every 10–15 m on main streets); ground-floor height enough
  for non-residential use (often ~4–4.5 m floor-to-floor) even if residential initially.
- Limit blank walls, car-park entries and service doors on primary streets; put them on lanes.
- Residential streets: front doors and habitable rooms facing the street, low front fences.

## 5. Design guidelines and codes
- Structure: objective → standard (the measurable rule) → diagram → acceptable alternative.
- Form-based codes regulate building type and form by street/transect zone rather than
  use; they suit places where form matters more than use mix.
- Test every guideline with 3D massing on real sites before adoption; check it doesn't
  conflict with zoning yield (see zoning-land-use-planning skill).

## Gotchas
- Controls that look good in a perspective but make lots unbuildable get varied away.
- Requiring retail where there's no demand gives empty shopfronts; allow flexible
  "active" uses.
- Vague words ("appropriate", "high quality") without measurable standards cannot be enforced.

## Verify
- [ ] Block sizes and mid-block links defined; connectivity measured.
- [ ] Enclosure and overshadowing tested in 3D for key streets and spaces.
- [ ] Each built-form control has an objective, dimension and diagram.
- [ ] Active-frontage map and measurable frontage standards included.
- [ ] Guidelines tested against real sites and zoning yield.

*Uncertainty:* ratios and dimensions are widely used rules of thumb, not universal
standards; calibrate to local context and climate.
