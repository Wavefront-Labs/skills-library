# Strategic and regional planning — projections, land supply and settlement strategy

**Goal:** a 15–30 year strategy that says how much growth to plan for, where it goes,
and what infrastructure must come first — with numbers someone can audit.

## 1. From population to demand
1. Start from **official projections** (national statistics agency or state
   demographer) and note their base year and assumptions (fertility, migration).
   Use a range (low/central/high), not a single number.
2. Convert population to **households** with household-size or headship-rate
   assumptions — falling average household size can drive dwelling need even with
   flat population.
3. Households → **dwelling need**, adding a vacancy/second-home allowance (often a few
   percent) and any backlog (overcrowding, homelessness) per your method/guidance.
4. Employment: projected jobs by sector → floorspace (using employment densities, m²
   per worker, by type) → land (using plot ratios). Logistics and industry consume much
   more land per job than offices.

Many jurisdictions mandate a method (e.g. England's standard method for housing need);
follow it where required, and still document the inputs.

## 2. Capacity — where could growth go?
- **Existing zoned capacity:** vacant land, underused land, and redevelopment potential
  under current controls (see zoning-land-use-planning skill). Apply realistic
  take-up/feasibility discounts — not every lot redevelops.
- **Infill and intensification** around centres and transit.
- **Greenfield** options at the edge, screened against constraints (flood, habitat,
  farmland, hazards).
Compare demand to capacity by sub-area and over time (5-year bands). The gap drives the
strategy.

## 3. Growth scenarios
Build 3–4 spatial options, e.g. *consolidation*, *transit corridors*, *new settlement*,
*dispersed edge growth*. Evaluate each on:
- infrastructure cost and timing (water, sewer, transport, schools);
- travel demand and emissions (car dependency);
- exposure to hazards and climate risk (see climate-adaptation-planning skill);
- environmental and agricultural land impact;
- deliverability (market strength, land ownership, developer capacity).
Present trade-offs transparently; the preferred option is a political choice informed
by evidence, not a technical output.

## 4. Settlement hierarchy and policy
- Define tiers (regional city, district centres, towns, villages) with roles and the
  share of growth each takes.
- Spatial policies: growth boundaries or priority growth areas, protected rural land,
  employment land safeguarding, centres-first retail policy.
- **Infrastructure sequencing:** list which projects unlock which growth areas and in
  what order; tie release of land to infrastructure commitments.

## 5. Monitoring and review
Set indicators (dwelling completions by area, land supply years, density achieved,
jobs, mode share) with targets and a review trigger. Strategies that aren't monitored
drift until they're irrelevant.

## Gotchas
- Treating projections as targets or forecasts as certainties; they're scenarios.
- Double-counting capacity (same site in two categories) or ignoring market feasibility.
- Planning homes without employment, or vice versa, creating long commutes.
- Infrastructure agencies not signed up: the strategy then fails at delivery.

## Verify
- [ ] Projection source, range and assumptions stated.
- [ ] Population → households → dwellings chain is shown step by step.
- [ ] Capacity estimates discounted for realistic take-up.
- [ ] Scenarios compared on transparent criteria including infrastructure and risk.
- [ ] Monitoring indicators and review triggers set.

*Uncertainty:* method details (vacancy allowance, headship rates, employment densities)
vary by country and guidance; values here are illustrative.
