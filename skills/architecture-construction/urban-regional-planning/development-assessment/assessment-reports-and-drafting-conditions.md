# Development assessment — reports and conditions

**Goal:** a recommendation that follows logically from the controls and the evidence,
and conditions (or refusal reasons) that will stand up on appeal.

## 1. Completeness and the clock
- On lodgement, check the application has what the law and your checklist require
  (plans at scale, site survey, statements, specialist reports, fees, owner's consent).
  Request missing information early and in one letter; statutory clocks often pause or
  restart on requests.
- Identify the **controls that apply**: zone, overlays, plan policies, design codes,
  and any statutory considerations you must legally have regard to.
- Send **referrals** (roads authority, heritage, environment, utilities) promptly.

## 2. Site inspection
Visit before forming a view. Photograph the site, neighbours, street and any
interfaces mentioned in submissions (windows that may be overlooked, trees, drainage).
Check plans match reality: levels, boundaries, existing buildings.

## 3. Compliance table
For every numeric/objective control, a row: control | required | proposed | complies? |
comment. Height, setbacks, FAR/density, coverage, parking, open space, solar access, etc.
Measure from the plans yourself; don't copy the applicant's table.

## 4. Merit assessment
For discretionary matters and any non-compliance:
- State the **objective** of the control, then assess whether the proposal meets the
  objective despite the numeric variation (where your system allows variations).
- Address each issue raised in **submissions** — group them by theme and respond to
  each theme. Not every concern is a valid planning consideration (e.g. property values
  or competition are usually not); say so politely and why.
- Weigh precedent carefully: variations granted on one site get cited on the next.

## 5. Writing the report
Structure: proposal summary → site and context → controls → referrals → submissions →
assessment by issue → conclusion → recommendation. Write for the decision-maker and a
future appeal tribunal: plain, specific, with plan references and measurements.
Every reason for refusal must link to a specific control or consideration and the
evidence that it is not met.

## 6. Drafting conditions
Common legal tests (expressed differently in different systems, e.g. the six tests in
English policy) — conditions should be:
- **Necessary** to make the development acceptable;
- **Relevant** to planning and to the development;
- **Enforceable** — a breach can be identified;
- **Precise** — no "to the satisfaction of" without saying what satisfies;
- **Reasonable** — not unduly onerous.
Good structure for each condition: what must be done, by when (prior to commencement /
occupation / ongoing), to whose approval, and a short reason.
Example pattern: "Before any building work above ground level, a landscape plan
showing species, sizes and locations of planting, consistent with [plan ref], must be
submitted to and approved by the responsible authority. Planting must be completed
before occupation and maintained thereafter."
- Reference **approved plans by number and revision**.
- Don't use conditions to fix a fundamentally unacceptable design — refuse or seek
  amended plans instead.

## Gotchas
- Conditions that contradict the approved plans: whichever prevails, someone will argue the other.
- "Pre-commencement" conditions delay starts; use them only where truly needed before work begins.
- Failing to address a relevant consideration is a common legal ground for challenge.

## Verify
- [ ] Completeness checked; referrals sent and responses addressed.
- [ ] Site visited; plans checked against site.
- [ ] Compliance table measured independently.
- [ ] Every submission theme answered; every refusal reason tied to a control.
- [ ] Each condition passes the necessary/relevant/enforceable/precise/reasonable test.

*Uncertainty:* statutory timeframes and condition tests vary by jurisdiction; the
pattern given is generic.
