# Community engagement — consultation design, charrettes and submissions

**Goal:** input that genuinely shapes the plan, from a representative range of
people, documented well enough to show decision-makers (and a tribunal) what was heard.

## 1. Decide what's actually negotiable
Before designing any event, write down which decisions are open, which are fixed (by
law, budget, a higher-level plan), and who decides. Match the participation level — the
**IAP2 spectrum**: inform → consult → involve → collaborate → empower. Promising
"collaborate" when the decision is already made destroys trust faster than anything.

## 2. Engagement plan
- **Stakeholder map:** residents, businesses, landowners, community groups, Indigenous/
  traditional owners (with specific protocols and often statutory obligations), young
  people, renters, people with disabilities, non-English speakers, commuters who use the
  place but don't live there.
- **Methods mix:** drop-in sessions at different times and places (markets, schools,
  transport hubs), online surveys and interactive maps, focus groups, workshops,
  door-knocking in under-represented areas. One evening town-hall meeting reaches a
  narrow, self-selected group.
- **Accessibility:** step-free venues, childcare, interpreters, translated summaries,
  plain-language materials, large print, captioned video.
- Statutory minimums (notice periods, required notifications) are a floor, not the plan.
- Timeline, budget, roles, and how input will feed which decision.

## 3. Materials
- Plain language, visuals (3D views, before/after, maps with landmarks people
  recognise). Avoid jargon (FAR, setbacks) or explain it once with a picture.
- Present real options with honest trade-offs, not one preferred option and a straw man.
- Survey questions: neutral wording, one idea per question, mix of closed and open
  questions; pilot it.

## 4. Running a charrette
A charrette is an intensive, multi-day collaborative design process:
1. **Prep:** base maps, data, site tours; recruit a balanced mix of participants.
2. **Opening session:** context, constraints, what's open for change.
3. **Working sessions:** small tables with facilitators and designers sketching live.
4. **Feedback loops:** the design team synthesises overnight; presents back; revises
   — usually at least two cycles.
5. **Closing presentation** of the emerging plan, with what changed and why.
Facilitators should draw out quiet voices, record everything, and not let one group dominate.

## 5. Analysing submissions
- Log every submission with ID, date, channel, and (with consent) location.
- Build a **coding frame** of themes (e.g. traffic, height, open space, heritage) and
  code each submission — multiple codes per submission. Pilot on a sample and refine.
- Two coders on a sample to check consistency.
- Report counts by theme **and** the substance of arguments. Quantity ≠ planning merit:
  a campaign of 500 identical letters is one argument; a single expert submission can
  raise a decisive issue.
- Note who **didn't** take part compared with the area's demographics.

## 6. Report back
Publish a consultation report: what was asked, who responded, key themes, and a
"**You said, we did**" table — each theme, the response, and whether/how the plan
changed (or why not). Then tell participants directly.

## Gotchas
- Consultation fatigue: coordinate with other agencies consulting the same community.
- Personal data in submissions: follow privacy law when publishing.
- Online-only engagement excludes people without devices or skills.

## Verify
- [ ] Negotiable decisions and participation level stated up front.
- [ ] Stakeholder map includes hard-to-reach groups with specific methods.
- [ ] Submissions coded with a documented frame and consistency check.
- [ ] Representation gaps identified.
- [ ] "You said, we did" report published and sent to participants.

*Uncertainty:* IAP2 and charrette formats are widely used frameworks; statutory
engagement requirements differ by jurisdiction.
