# Climate adaptation planning — flood overlays, heat and adaptation pathways

**Goal:** stop new development locking in climate risk, and give existing exposed
places a staged, decision-ready path forward.

## 1. Choose scenarios and timeframes
- Match the planning horizon to the asset life: housing 70–100 years, so a plan made now
  must consider conditions around 2100.
- Use official guidance on scenarios (IPCC SSPs as interpreted by your national or state
  agency) and sea-level-rise allowances. Typical practice: plan to a central scenario but
  stress-test against a high one for critical infrastructure.
- Record the source and version of every hazard dataset; they get updated.

## 2. Hazard and risk mapping
- **Riverine/pluvial flooding:** flood extents and depths for a range of events (e.g.
  annual exceedance probabilities of 5%, 1%, 0.2%) — current and with climate uplift
  on rainfall/flows.
- **Coastal:** storm-tide levels + sea-level rise + wave run-up; erosion/recession lines.
- **Heat:** land surface temperature (satellite), canopy cover, plus vulnerability
  (elderly, outdoor workers, low-income households without cooling).
- Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability. A deep but rare flood under a park is low
  risk; a shallow frequent flood at an aged-care home is high.

## 3. Writing flood/coastal overlay controls
- Graduate controls by hazard: e.g. no new vulnerable uses (housing, hospitals, schools)
  in high-hazard floodways; limited/flood-compatible uses in flood fringe with conditions.
- **Floor levels:** design flood level + **freeboard** (commonly 300–500 mm; local
  standards set it) for habitable floors.
- No net loss of floodplain storage (cut-and-fill balance) and no obstruction of flow paths.
- Safe evacuation access, critical services above flood level, flood-resilient
  materials below it.
- State that the overlay will be updated as hazard mapping changes, and define which
  map version applies to an application.

## 4. Heat mitigation
- **Canopy targets** (many cities adopt around 30%+ canopy goals) with street-tree
  planting programmes prioritised by heat vulnerability, not by where residents ask most.
- Cool and green roofs, reflective/permeable paving, shade structures at transit stops
  and play areas.
- Building controls for passive cooling: cross-ventilation, external shading, limits on
  dark roof materials.
- Public cooling centres and heat-health plans — planning links to emergency management.

## 5. Adaptation pathways for existing places
For exposed settlements where risk grows over time:
1. Identify adaptation options: protect (levees, seawalls), accommodate (raise floors,
   flood-proof), avoid/retreat (stop new development, buy-back, relocate).
2. Use **dynamic adaptive pathways (DAPP)**: map which options remain effective up to
   which sea level or flood frequency (their "tipping points").
3. Set **trigger points** — monitored signals (e.g. number of nuisance floods per year,
   beach width) — that start the next decision early enough to act, given lead time.
4. Engage the affected community from the start; managed retreat raises equity,
   compensation and cultural issues that technical analysis can't resolve.

## Gotchas
- Using historical flood maps alone understates future risk.
- Levees create "levee effect": development behind them increases consequence if overtopped.
- Planning controls without insurance, emergency management and infrastructure
  agencies at the table fail at implementation.

## Verify
- [ ] Scenarios, horizons and hazard data sources documented.
- [ ] Risk (not just hazard) mapped, including vulnerability.
- [ ] Overlay controls graduated by hazard with floor levels, storage and evacuation rules.
- [ ] Heat actions targeted by vulnerability.
- [ ] Pathways with monitored triggers defined for exposed existing areas.

*Uncertainty:* freeboard, canopy targets and scenario choices are typical examples;
apply your jurisdiction's current guidance.
