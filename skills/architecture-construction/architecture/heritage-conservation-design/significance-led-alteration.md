# Significance-led alteration of historic buildings

**Goal:** change a historic building so it has a viable use, while keeping what makes it
matter — and be able to justify every intervention to a conservation officer in writing.

## 1. Understand before designing
- **Research**: historic maps, building records/archives, listing description (which is
  a summary, not an exhaustive account of what is protected), previous consents.
- **Record and survey** the fabric: phases of construction, original vs later material,
  joinery, plaster, floor structures, roof timbers. Date phases by evidence (construction
  joints, tool marks, fixings, materials) and mark them on phased plans.
- Note condition and the *causes* of decay (usually water), not just the symptoms.

## 2. Statement of significance
Describe *what* is significant and *why*, graded (e.g. high / medium / low /
neutral / detrimental) element by element. Value types (Burra Charter / Historic
England framing): **evidential/archaeological, historic/historical, aesthetic/architectural,
communal/social**. Detrimental elements (a 1970s partition through a principal room) are
opportunities for enhancement.

## 3. Design principles
- **Minimum intervention:** do as much as necessary and as little as possible.
- **Retain fabric:** repair rather than replace; replace only what cannot be repaired,
  like for like in material and method.
- **Reversibility:** new work should be removable without damaging the old where
  practical (fixings into joints rather than masonry faces, free-standing pods
  inside significant rooms).
- **Legibility:** new work should be distinguishable on close inspection — honest, not
  a fake period copy — while sympathetic in scale, proportion and material.
- **Use follows plan form:** choose uses that suit the existing spaces (cellular rooms
  suit offices or hotels better than open-plan retail).
- **Locate change where significance is lowest:** services in later wings or modern
  fabric, new openings in secondary walls.

## 4. Heritage impact assessment
For each proposal, state: the element affected, its significance, the impact (positive,
neutral, minor/major harm), justification, and mitigation. Harm must be clearly
justified by public benefit in most regimes. This becomes the planning/consent
heritage statement.

## 5. Compatible technical detailing
- Traditional solid walls are **vapour-open** and manage moisture by evaporation.
  Cement renders, gypsum plasters, impermeable paints and closed-cell internal
  insulation can trap moisture and rot timbers or spall stone. Use lime mortars and
  plasters, breathable paints, and vapour-open (e.g. wood-fibre) insulation, with
  hygrothermal assessment where risk is high.
- Mortar should be weaker and more permeable than the masonry units so it, not the
  brick/stone, is sacrificial.
- Secondary glazing is often accepted where replacing historic windows is not.
- Fire and accessibility upgrades: seek alternative compliance (fire engineering,
  intumescent upgrades to historic doors, discreet platform lifts) rather than
  defaulting to standard solutions.

## Gotchas
- Works to protected buildings without consent can be a criminal offence in some
  jurisdictions (e.g. UK listed buildings) — including interior works. Check before
  any opening-up.
- Opening-up for investigation itself may need consent.
- "Restoration" to an imagined original state destroys evidence of later significant phases.
- Frameworks differ by country; *confirm the local legal and policy regime.*

## Verify
- [ ] Phased plans and fabric record completed before design.
- [ ] Graded statement of significance covers every affected element.
- [ ] Each intervention has an impact entry with justification and mitigation.
- [ ] Materials in contact with historic fabric are compatible (lime, vapour-open).
- [ ] Consent route confirmed for all works including investigation.
