# Reviewing shop drawings and answering RFIs

**Goal:** keep the job moving with fast, clear answers, while not taking on design
responsibility the contract gives the contractor, and not slipping cost changes through
in a casual reply. Contract administration is where architects most often get sued.

> Terms differ by contract form (AIA, JCT, NEC, FIDIC, local). Read *your* contract's
> clauses on submittals, instructions and changes before the first submittal arrives.

## 1. Set up logs on day one
- **Submittal register:** spec section, item, required/received dates, reviewer,
  status, resubmission count, link.
- **RFI log:** number, date received, question, who answers (architect/consultant),
  due date, response date, cost/time implication flag.
- Agree turnaround times (often 10–14 days for submittals, a few days for RFIs) and stick
  to them; late responses become delay claims.

## 2. Submittal review — what you are checking
You review for **general conformance with the design intent and contract documents**,
not for dimensions, quantities, fabrication methods or the contractor's coordination
between trades — those stay the contractor's responsibility. Your review stamp and the
contract say this; don't contradict it in your comments.
Check:
- Product/system matches the specified one (or an approved substitution).
- Performance data meets spec: fire rating, U-value, loads, finish, colour.
- Interfaces with adjacent work shown on your drawings (head/sill/jamb conditions).
- Visible design changes the contractor has made "for convenience".
Typical action stamps: *No exceptions taken* / *Make corrections noted* / *Revise and
resubmit* / *Rejected* (wording varies). Annotate clearly in a distinct colour, and
reference each comment to a drawing or spec clause.

## 3. Writing RFI responses
- Answer **only the question asked**, citing the drawing/spec reference.
- If the answer changes scope, cost or time, it is not an RFI response — it is a change.
  Issue it as an **architect's instruction / ASI / change directive** under the contract
  procedure, so the change is valued properly. Phrases like "contractor to provide…"
  in an RFI answer are how unpriced variations happen.
- Don't redesign the contractor's work (temporary works, means and methods).
- If the question reveals a genuine document conflict, fix the documents and issue a
  revision; don't leave the fix only in the RFI.
- Consultant questions: route to the consultant; coordinate their answer before issuing.

## 4. Substitutions
Require the contractor's formal request with comparison against the specified product
(performance, cost saving/credit, warranty, lead time). Approve only in writing; never
by conversation on site.

## 5. Site observation
Visit at stages that matter (before close-up of envelope, after first-of-type installs,
mock-ups). Issue a field report: date, attendees, weather, progress, items observed,
non-conformances, photos. Use "observed" language — you aren't the contractor's
quality control or a full-time inspector unless contracted as such.

## Gotchas
- "Approved" stamps on shop drawings are risky wording; offices use "reviewed" variants
  for a reason.
- A shop drawing that changes the design and gets stamped can be argued to have
  changed the contract — note explicitly "deviation not accepted".
- Verbal site instructions are still instructions — confirm everything in writing.

## Verify
- [ ] Every submittal and RFI is logged with dates and status.
- [ ] Review comments reference documents and use the contract's stamp wording.
- [ ] No RFI response adds scope without an instruction/change procedure.
- [ ] Document conflicts found via RFIs are fixed in a drawing revision.
- [ ] Site visits are recorded in issued field reports.
