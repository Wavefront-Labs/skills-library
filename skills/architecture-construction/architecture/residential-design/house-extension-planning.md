# Planning a house extension or remodel

**Goal:** a domestic design that fixes how the household actually lives, fits the budget,
doesn't make the existing house darker, and can be approved and built — the most common
architecture job there is, and the one most often got wrong on daylight and cost.

## 1. Brief: how they live, then what they want
Ask about routines rather than rooms: where they eat on a weekday, who cooks, where
coats, bags, shoes and the recycling go, where children do homework, who works from home,
plans for ageing parents or more children. Then get a **real budget** including fees,
VAT/sales tax, fittings and a **contingency of 10–20%** for existing-building surprises.
Compare with local cost-per-m² data early; extensions routinely cost more per m² than
new build because of demolition, temporary works and making good. *Use local benchmarks.*

## 2. Measured survey
Survey the existing house (or commission one): plans, sections through stairs and the
extension zone, window head/sill heights, floor-level changes, drain runs and
manholes, boundary positions, neighbouring windows facing the site, roof pitches,
services positions. Note what is load-bearing (joist direction, walls carrying floors).
Errors in the survey become errors in every drawing.

## 3. Test the plan with furniture
- Draw **real furniture at real size**: dining table with chairs pulled out (allow
  ~0.9–1.0 m behind a chair), sofa, bed with clearance on access sides, kitchen run with
  appliance door swings.
- Kitchen: classic sink–hob–fridge "work triangle" with no side much under 1.2 m or over
  2.7 m and no through-route across it (a rule of thumb, not a law). Islands need ~1.0–1.2 m
  clear around them.
- Circulation: don't route the main path diagonally through the living space; keep a
  clear route from front door to garden and kitchen.
- Storage is the most commonly forgotten space in houses — allocate it explicitly.

## 4. Don't darken the house
A deep rear extension often turns the old middle room into a dark corridor. Counter with:
rooflights or a glazed strip where new meets old, a lantern over the middle zone,
a side return courtyard, or keeping a tall window to the old room. Check in section.

## 5. Structure and services early
- Opening up the rear wall needs a beam (steel or engineered timber) and padstones;
  get an engineer's sizing before the plan is fixed — deep downstand beams change
  ceiling design.
- Locate drains: building over a public sewer may need the utility's consent (UK) or be
  disallowed; moving a manhole costs real money.
- Plan for soil stack, kitchen extract route, underfloor heating zones and consumer
  unit capacity.

## 6. Approvals
- Planning: many jurisdictions allow small extensions without full permission within
  limits (e.g. UK permitted development for depth and height, but not in conservation
  areas or where rights were removed). Confirm rather than assume; a lawful development
  certificate is cheap insurance.
- Building regulations/code approval is separate from planning and always applies to
  structure, fire escape (especially loft conversions), insulation and drainage.
- Shared walls or excavation near neighbours may trigger party-wall procedures
  (UK Party Wall Act) — serve notice early.

## Gotchas
- Floor level match: new slab build-up (insulation + screed + finish) must meet the old
  floor; level thresholds to the garden need drainage detailing.
- "Small" alterations to the existing house (rewiring, new windows) eat the budget.
- Specific rules above are UK-flavoured examples; *verify local equivalents.*

## Verify
- [ ] Brief records daily routines and a budget with contingency.
- [ ] Plans show real furniture with clearances and storage.
- [ ] A section confirms daylight still reaches the existing rooms.
- [ ] Engineer has sized the main opening; drains are located.
- [ ] Planning route and building-code route are both confirmed in writing.
