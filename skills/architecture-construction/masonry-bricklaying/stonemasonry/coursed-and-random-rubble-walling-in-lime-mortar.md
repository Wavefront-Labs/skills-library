# Coursed and random rubble walling in lime mortar

**Goal:** a stone wall that's strong because the stones are bonded, not because the
mortar is hard — weathering well and breathing, in the character of local work.

## 1. Know the stone
- **Natural bed:** sedimentary stones (sandstones, limestones) have bedding planes.
  Lay them **on their natural bed** (planes horizontal) in walling. Face-bedded stone
  (planes parallel to the face) delaminates and spalls in frost. Exceptions: some
  cornices and arch stones are edge-bedded deliberately — follow the detail.
- Granite and other igneous stones have no bed but have grain/rift for splitting.
- Sort delivered stone by size: **quoins/corner stones**, **through-stones**, facing
  stones, and **hearting** (small fill).

## 2. Dressing and pitching
- Square a bed and ends roughly with a **walling hammer**; **pitch** a rock face with a
  pitching tool: a wide chisel set back ~10–15 mm from the edge, struck firmly to take
  off a strip and leave a bold face. Work towards the face, not into it.
- Pointed and claw chisels for tooling ashlar faces (banker work is its own craft).
- Wear eye protection and dust controls (respirable silica from sandstones and granite).

## 3. Laying principles
- **One over two, two over one:** every stone breaks the joint below; avoid vertical
  running joints ("stacked" joints) over ~2 courses.
- **Through-stones** (or bonders) spanning the wall thickness — or two-thirds through
  from each face, alternating — at intervals (e.g. roughly one per square metre of
  face; follow tradition and spec).
- Lay stones **length into the wall**, not along the face, for bond.
- **Hearting:** pack the core tight with small stones and mortar; no voids, no loose
  rubble.
- Walls built with a slight inward **batter** in traditional rubble work; ashlar is plumb.
- **Random rubble:** irregular stones, no courses, but bonded; **coursed rubble:**
  stones brought to level at course heights (every 300–450 mm or so).
- Quoins first, as leads; lines between them as in brickwork.

## 4. Lime mortar
- Use lime mortar to match the stone's softness: **NHL 2 or 3.5** natural hydraulic
  lime, lime putty, or hot-mixed lime with well-graded sharp sand (often ~1:2.5–1:3
  binder:sand by volume). Choose with the conservator/specifier for heritage work.
  The mortar must be weaker and more permeable than the stone, so water leaves via joints.
- Never use cement mortar on historic soft stone — it traps moisture and the stone
  decays instead of the joint.
- Joints recessed slightly and finished by beating with a stiff brush once firm (see
  `architecture-construction.masonry-bricklaying.pointing-jointing.joint-profiles-mortar-matching-and-repointing`).

## 5. Curing and weather
- Lime sets slowly by carbonation. Protect from **frost** (don't work below ~5 °C,
  and protect for days after), from **rain** washout, and from **drying** too fast in
  sun and wind: cover with damp hessian, mist as needed.
- Stone cladding on modern frames is a different system: restraint and support
  fixings (stainless steel, engineered) with a cavity — follow the cladding design.

## Gotchas
- Face-bedded sandstone spalling within a few winters.
- Long stones laid along the face — no bond, wall bulges.
- Cement mortar on soft stone.
- Lime mortar frozen or dried out in the first week.

## Verify
- [ ] Bedded stones on their natural bed.
- [ ] No continuous vertical joints; through-stones at intervals.
- [ ] Core fully hearted; no voids when tapped.
- [ ] Mortar mix appropriate (weaker than stone); recorded.
- [ ] Curing protection in place for the weather.
