# Firebrick ovens and kilns: expansion and curing

**Goal:** a hot face that survives thousands of heat cycles without cracking apart —
built from the right refractory, with thin joints, room to expand, insulation behind,
and a slow first heat.

## 1. Choose the refractory
- **Dense firebrick** (e.g. medium-duty, ~30–40% alumina): high thermal mass, durable
  hot face — oven floors and domes, fireboxes. Heavy, conducts heat.
- **Insulating firebrick (IFB)** graded by max service temperature (e.g. 2300 °F /
  2600 °F / 2800 °F classes): light, insulating, fragile — kiln hot faces where you
  want fast firing and low mass. Not for abrasion or flame impingement, not for oven floors.
- **Castable refractory** for shapes and arches; follow the maker's water content
  exactly — extra water ruins strength.
- Ordinary clay brick, concrete and cement mortar fail at refractory temperatures;
  don't use them on the hot face. Check the product's rated service temperature
  against your maximum (pizza oven ~400–500 °C; stoneware kiln ~1300 °C).

## 2. Mortar and joints
- **Refractory mortar** (air-setting or heat-setting, per product) in **thin joints**
  — ideally ~1–3 mm, buttered or dipped. Mortar is the weak link at heat; the thinner
  the better. Many kiln builders lay IFB dry or with a thin slurry.
- Dense brick may need cutting with a wet diamond saw for good fits; dust control
  (silica) — wet cut, respirator.

## 3. Ovens (dome or barrel vault)
1. Structural base (block/concrete slab) → **insulation layer** (calcium silicate board
   or vermiculite/perlite concrete, often ~75–100 mm) → **oven floor** of dense
   firebricks laid tight on a thin sand/fireclay bed, not mortared, so they can be
   replaced.
2. Dome: courses of firebrick (soldier or rowlock) tapering in, using an inner form or a
   radius stick/trammel for geometry. Typical dome height ~ 0.6 × internal diameter for
   neapolitan-style; door height ~ 63% of dome height is a commonly quoted ratio for
   good draw (treat as a rule of thumb).
3. Over the dome: **ceramic fibre blanket** (several layers, ~100–150 mm total),
   then a weatherproof render or enclosure — insulation decides how long it holds heat.
4. Flue at the front, outside the door, so smoke exits without passing through the
   cooking chamber.

## 4. Kilns and furnace linings
- Layered wall: IFB hot face → backup insulation (lower-grade IFB or board) → steel
  case or masonry. Arch roofs from tapered (arch) bricks, with a steel frame and tie
  rods to contain the thrust.
- **Expansion:** leave small gaps (e.g. a few mm per metre or per the brick maker) at
  intervals, filled with ceramic fibre, and allow arches to rise slightly; tie rods
  are loosened slightly on first firings per kiln-builder practice.

## 5. Curing / dry-out
- Masonry and mortar hold water; heating fast turns it to steam that spalls brick and
  cracks domes. Follow the mortar/castable maker's dry-out schedule. A common oven
  approach: several days of small fires, rising gradually (e.g. ~100 °C, then ~150,
  200, 250 °C over successive days) before full temperature. Kilns: very slow early
  ramp with vents open.
- Hairline cracks in outer render are normal; structural cracks in the dome are not.

## Gotchas
- Thick mortar joints crumbling out after a season.
- IFB used for an oven floor — shatters under peel and tools.
- No insulation under the floor — oven never gets hot enough.
- First fire roaring hot the day after building.

## Verify
- [ ] Refractory rated above max operating temperature.
- [ ] Joints thin and consistent; floor bricks laid without mortar.
- [ ] Insulation layers below and over the hot face.
- [ ] Expansion gaps / frame provided for arches and kiln walls.
- [ ] Dry-out schedule followed and logged.
