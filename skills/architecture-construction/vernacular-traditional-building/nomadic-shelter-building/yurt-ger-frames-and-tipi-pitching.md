# Nomadic shelters — yurt/ger frames and tipi pitching

**Goal:** understand and erect the two best-known portable dwellings so they stand
square, shed weather and survive wind.

Both are living traditions with cultural significance (Mongolian and Central Asian peoples
for the ger/yurt; Plains Nations for the tipi). Learn from tradition-bearers where possible
and respect their cultural protocols.

## Part A — Yurt / ger

### How the structure works
- **Lattice wall (khana):** crossing wooden laths pinned at intersections, expanding
  into a circle. Its height and diameter depend on how far it's opened.
- **Door frame:** rigid frame tied into the lattice ends.
- **Crown (toono):** a compression ring at the top, often supported by two central posts (bagana
  in the Mongolian ger; many Central Asian yurts have no posts).
- **Roof poles (uni):** from the lattice top to the crown. They push outward on the
  lattice top.
- **Tension band:** ropes or a woven band around the lattice top resist that outward
  thrust. Without it the lattice spreads and the roof drops — the critical element.

### Erecting
1. Level platform or ground; mark the circle (a rope from a centre peg).
2. Stand the door frame (traditionally facing south in Mongolia), open the lattice
   sections around the circle and tie them to each other and to the door frame.
3. Tie the tension band(s) round the lattice top and tighten — the circle should be
   round and lattice evenly opened.
4. Raise the crown (on its posts, or held up by a person) and insert roof poles in
   alternating opposite pairs so the crown stays centred.
5. Covers: inner liner/canvas, insulation (traditional **felt** layers, number by
   season), outer waterproof canvas, tied down with ropes around the outside.
6. Crown cover flap for ventilation and stove pipe; adjust with a rope.

### Gotchas
- Uneven lattice opening = crooked roof and uneven poles. Measure the circle.
- Wet felt is heavy and rots; keep covers ventilated and dry in storage.
- Wind: tie the cover down tightly and anchor to ground stakes in exposed sites.
- Stoves: proper flue, heat-proof crown collar, spark arrester, CO alarm.

## Part B — Tipi

### Components
Long straight poles (many lodgepole pine; number and length suited to cover size —
often 12+ poles for a typical family lodge), a semicircular-ish cover, two smoke-flap
poles, lacing pins, pegs, and an inner **liner** (dew cloth) that creates a draught
for the fire and keeps rain dripping down the poles away from the living space.

### Pitching (outline of a three-pole foundation method; four-pole traditions differ)
1. Lay three (or four) foundation poles on the cover to measure height; tie them near
   the top where they'll cross.
2. Raise the tripod, spread legs to the planned diameter, door position facing the
   preferred direction (traditionally east in many Plains traditions).
3. Lay remaining poles into the crotch in the established order, leaving the gap for the
   lifting pole at the back.
4. Tie the cover to the lifting pole, raise it into the back gap, wrap the cover around
   both sides, and lace it closed at the front with lacing pins.
5. Adjust pole feet outward to tighten the cover evenly, peg the bottom.
6. Set smoke-flap poles; angle flaps away from the wind so smoke draws.
7. Hang the liner inside.
Many tipis are deliberately tilted (back steeper) so the smoke hole sits over the fire.

### Gotchas
- Poles not straight or debarked well → wrinkles, leaks at the top.
- Flaps set into the wind → smoke blows back in.
- A loose cover flaps and wears; tighten by moving pole butts out.
- Open fire inside: keep fuel small, never leave unattended, keep liner clear.

## Verify
- [ ] Yurt: circle measured, lattice evenly opened, tension band tight, poles paired.
- [ ] Covers tied down; ventilation and any stove flue installed safely.
- [ ] Tipi: tripod at correct height/diameter; cover tight and laced; flaps working with wind.
- [ ] Liner installed; fire safety arrangements in place.

*Uncertainty:* sequences vary by tradition and maker; follow the maker's instructions
for commercial yurts and tradition-bearers for cultural forms.
