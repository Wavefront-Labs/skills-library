# Straw bale building — bales, pinning, plaster skins and moisture

**Goal:** thick, highly insulated walls that stay dry. Straw rots if it stays wet
(roughly above ~20% moisture content for long periods); almost every rule here is about
keeping water out and letting vapour out.

**Codes:** in the US, IRC Appendix AS (strawbale construction) covers load-bearing and
non-structural bale walls; other countries have their own guidance or require
engineering. Check before you design.

## 1. Load-bearing vs infill
- **Load-bearing ("Nebraska style"):** bales carry the roof via a **roof plate/box
  beam**, pre-compressed with straps or threaded rods. Simple, but the roof can't go on
  until the walls are up — rain risk during construction.
- **Infill:** a timber (or other) frame carries the roof; bales are stacked within or
  around it after the roof is on, so they stay dry. Most common where codes are strict or
  climates wet.

## 2. Bales
- Dry (moisture under ~15% at delivery — measure with a bale moisture meter probe), tight
  (dense; often cited around 100–130 kg/m³ for structural use), consistent size, two- or
  three-string, straw not hay (hay has seed heads and leaves that rot and feed pests).
- Store off the ground, covered, ventilated.
- Wheat, rice, barley and rye straw are all used.

## 3. Foundation and toe-up
- Raise bales well clear of the ground and of splash: a plinth/stem wall plus a
  **toe-up** (timber sill plates with insulation between) giving height above the
  finished floor, so a plumbing leak or flood doesn't wick into the first course.
- A capillary/moisture break between foundation and toe-up; gravel or insulation fill
  inside the toe-up, never a trough that can hold water.

## 4. Laying bales
- Lay bales on edge or flat consistently (flat is more common for load-bearing), in
  running bond, strings not exposed on the outer face where they'd be cut or exposed.
- **Custom bales:** retie to make part-bales with a bale needle; never just cut strings.
- **Notch** around posts; **stuff** gaps with loose straw packed tight (flakes dipped in
  clay slip work well). Voids become cold spots and condensation risk.
- **Pinning:** internal pins (bamboo, hazel) or external pins/mesh to stabilise walls
  during stacking; modern practice often uses external pinning or the plaster itself as
  the structural skin.
- Load-bearing: compress the walls with straps over the roof plate before plastering,
  and let them settle.

## 5. Plaster skins
- **Lime or clay plasters**, applied directly to straw (no vapour barrier). They are
  vapour-open so moisture can leave the wall. Cement renders and vapour-tight paints
  trap moisture and have caused failures.
- Three coats typical: scratch coat worked into the straw, brown coat, finish coat.
  Plaster thickness often ~25–50 mm per side.
- Mesh/lath at junctions with timber and around openings to control cracking.
- Lime needs slow, damp curing and protection from frost and fast drying.

## 6. Detailing to stay dry
- Large roof overhangs; flashed, sloped sills with drip edges at windows — window sills
  are the most common leak point.
- No horizontal ledges where water collects on the straw.
- Install moisture probes in a few vulnerable spots and check in the first years.

## Gotchas
- Bales rained on during construction: let them dry and test before plastering.
- Electrical cable runs in the bale joints/notches per code; plumbing *not* in exterior bale walls.
- Rodents: seal gaps at base and top; plaster all faces fully.

## Verify
- [ ] Code path chosen (e.g. IRC Appendix AS) and structural system decided.
- [ ] Bale moisture measured on delivery and before plastering.
- [ ] Toe-up above floor with moisture break.
- [ ] All voids stuffed; walls straight and compressed (load-bearing).
- [ ] Vapour-open plasters only; sills flashed; overhangs adequate.

*Uncertainty:* density and moisture thresholds are commonly published guidance values;
confirm against your code and local practitioners.
