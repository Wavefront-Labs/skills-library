# Adobe and earthen building — bricks, cob and earth plasters

**Goal:** durable earth walls. Earth is strong in compression and cheap, but weak in
tension and destroyed by persistent water — every decision is about keeping it dry.

**Codes and seismic warning:** earthen walls are vulnerable in earthquakes. Many
jurisdictions have specific codes (e.g. New Mexico's earthen building code, NZS 4297–4299,
Peru's E.080) requiring reinforcement, bond beams and engineering. Follow local code and
get engineering input for any habitable building in a seismic area.

## 1. Soil testing
- **Jar (sedimentation) test:** soil + water + a pinch of salt in a straight jar, shake,
  let settle 24 h. Layers show sand (bottom), silt, clay (top). Adobe soils are often
  around 15–30% clay with the rest well-graded sand/silt — rough guide only.
- **Shrinkage box test:** pack moist soil into a long box (e.g. 600 mm); measure
  shrinkage after drying. Large shrinkage and cracking = too much expansive clay; add sand.
- **Test bricks:** make several mixes, dry them, then drop, scratch and wet-test. The
  best evidence is a test brick from your own soil.
- Avoid topsoil (organic matter) and soils high in salts.

## 2. Making adobe bricks
1. Mix soil, water and chopped **straw** (a few % by volume; reduces cracking and adds
   tensile strength) to a stiff, plastic consistency. Let it soak/sour overnight if possible.
2. Throw into wetted, open-bottom wooden moulds on flat sanded ground; strike off; lift
   the mould straight up.
3. Dry in shade/sun on edge after a few days, turning to dry evenly. Full cure takes
   weeks depending on climate. Protect from rain.
4. Reject cracked or crumbly bricks. Check a few cured bricks for strength (e.g. a brick
   bridged between two others should carry a person's weight, an old field test — not
   an engineering measure).

## 3. Cob
Cob is wet earth + straw built up monolithically in layers ("lifts") by hand, trodden
and pressed together. Lift heights are typically a few hundred mm; let each firm up
before the next. Trim faces with a spade or cob paring tool once leather-hard.

## 4. Walls: "good boots and a good hat"
- **Foundation and plinth (boots):** stone, fired brick or concrete stem wall raising the
  earth well above ground and splash height (often 300–450 mm+), with a capillary break.
  Never start earth at ground level.
- **Roof (hat):** generous overhangs (e.g. 600 mm+ in wet climates) and gutters. Lack of
  overhang is the commonest cause of erosion.
- Lay adobe in **mud mortar** of similar soil (not cement mortar: too rigid and traps
  moisture), running bond, mortar joints full.
- Bond beam/ring beam (timber or reinforced concrete) at wall top to tie walls and
  spread roof loads; lintels with long bearings over openings.
- Keep openings away from corners; corners carry the walls.

## 5. Plasters
- **Earth plaster:** clay + sand + fibre; very breathable, repairable, needs protection
  from driving rain.
- **Lime plaster:** more weather resistant, still vapour-open. Apply in thin coats to a
  dampened, keyed surface; cure slowly (keep damp, shade).
- **Avoid cement render** on earth: it cracks, traps moisture behind it, and the wall
  decays unseen.
- Expect maintenance: re-plaster or lime-wash periodically.

## Gotchas
- Rising damp, leaking gutters and splashback dissolve the wall base first — inspect there.
- Mixing soils between batches changes shrinkage; keep a consistent source.
- Bricks dried too fast in hot sun crack.

## Verify
- [ ] Soil tested (jar, shrinkage, test bricks) and mix recorded.
- [ ] Plinth with capillary break raises earth above splash zone.
- [ ] Roof overhangs and drainage protect walls.
- [ ] Mud mortar and vapour-open plasters only; no cement render.
- [ ] Code requirements and engineering for seismic/structural loads checked.

*Uncertainty:* clay percentages, overhang and plinth heights are traditional rules of
thumb varying by climate and code.
