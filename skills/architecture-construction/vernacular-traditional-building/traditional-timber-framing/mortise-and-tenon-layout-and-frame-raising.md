# Traditional timber framing — mortise-and-tenon, layout and raising

**Goal:** a frame of large timbers joined with wooden joinery that fits first time at
the raising and stays tight as the wood seasons.

**Structural note:** joint sizes, peg spacing, and timber grades should be checked by an
engineer familiar with timber frames (and standards such as the Timber Frame Engineering
Council's TFEC 1 in North America, or Eurocode 5 with appropriate guidance in Europe).

## 1. Timber
- **Green (unseasoned) oak** is traditional in Britain and parts of Europe; Douglas fir,
  white pine and others elsewhere. Green timber is easier to cut but **shrinks** —
  mostly across the grain (tangential > radial), very little along it.
- Design so shrinkage tightens joints rather than opening them, and expect checking
  (surface splits) as it dries — normally cosmetic.
- Note boxed-heart vs free-of-heart; boxed-heart timbers check more but resist twist.

## 2. Layout systems
- **Scribe rule:** each joint is scribed to the actual, irregular timber it meets; the
  frame is laid up on the floor, pieces are matched, and every piece is unique and
  marked (marriage marks, often Roman numerals cut with a chisel). Highly accurate fit;
  slower.
- **Square rule** (developed in New England): assumes a perfect smaller timber inside
  each rough one (e.g. a nominal 8×8 has a 7½×7½ "inner" timber). Joints are cut to this
  inner timber, so pieces are interchangeable.
- **Mill rule:** relies on accurately sawn/planed timber, simply measured from faces.
- Pick **reference faces** (usually two adjacent faces, marked) and lay out everything
  from them, consistently.

## 3. Mortise-and-tenon joints
- Typical proportions: tenon thickness around a quarter to a third of the timber width
  (often 1½–2 in / ~38–50 mm in common frames), length sufficient for pegs with
  adequate end distance.
- Bore the mortise out with an auger or chain mortiser, pare to the line with a chisel/slick.
- Cut tenon cheeks with a saw; check thickness with a gauge or square.
- **Draw-boring:** drill the peg hole through the mortise cheeks, then drill the tenon hole
  offset slightly (often ~2–3 mm) towards the shoulder. Driving the peg pulls the shoulder
  tight. Too much offset splits the tenon or breaks the peg.
- **Pegs (trenails):** riven (split along the grain), not sawn, from dry, straight-grained
  hardwood; slightly tapered; often ~1 in / 25 mm diameter.
- **Braces** stiffen the frame against racking — essential.

## 4. Test fitting
Trial-fit joints before raising, especially on scribe frames. Check bents (cross-frames)
for square by equal diagonals. Correct anything tight now; on the raising day, nothing
should need a sledgehammer beyond seating.

## 5. Raising
- Plan the sequence: sill → bents (assemble flat, then lift) → connecting girts/plates →
  rafters. Write it down and brief everyone.
- Methods: human crews with pike poles, gin poles/A-frames with tackle, or a mobile crane
  (common now). Crane lifts need a competent lift plan, rigging and banksman.
- Temporary bracing on every bent as it stands; never leave a bent free-standing.
- Hard hats, clear exclusion zone, one person in charge, stop-signal agreed.

## Gotchas
- Mixing reference faces partway through layout puts a joint on the wrong side.
- Short tenons with pegs too near the end: relish (end grain) shears out.
- Green oak frames move for years; detail infill and glazing to tolerate shrinkage.
- Sapwood decays and attracts beetles; specify heart grade where exposed.

## Verify
- [ ] Layout system chosen and reference faces marked on every timber.
- [ ] Joint proportions and peg positions engineered or checked against guidance.
- [ ] Draw-bore offsets tested on offcuts first.
- [ ] Bents trial-fitted and square.
- [ ] Raising plan written; lifts and temporary bracing arranged; safety brief given.

*Uncertainty:* joint dimensions and draw-bore offsets are common practice values, not
engineering values.
