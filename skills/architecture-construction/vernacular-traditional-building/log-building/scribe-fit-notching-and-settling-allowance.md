# Log building — scribe-fitting, notching and settling allowance

**Goal:** tight, weatherproof log walls whose openings and fixtures still work after the
walls have shrunk and settled for several years.

## 1. Log selection and preparation
- Straight, low-taper logs of durable or suitable species (e.g. spruce, pine, larch,
  cedar, fir regionally). Minimal spiral grain — spiral-grained logs twist as they dry.
- Peel bark promptly (drawknife or pressure washer) to deter insects and fungi; keep off
  the ground and stickered while waiting.
- Note diameter, taper and any sweep (bow); place sweep consistently (often up or in)
  and alternate butts and tops course by course so the wall stays level.

## 2. Two broad traditions
- **Full scribe (Scandinavian / "chinkless"):** each log is scribed to the log below
  and a long **lateral groove** is cut along its underside so it sits snugly on the
  log beneath along the whole length, with insulation (e.g. wool) laid in the groove.
- **Chinked walls:** logs (often hewn square or flattened) sit with gaps between
  courses, filled with **chinking** — historically mud/lime daub over wood or stone
  slats, now often flexible synthetic chinking that tolerates movement.

## 3. Scribing (full-scribe method)
1. Roll the new log onto the wall over the one below, positioned and held with dogs.
2. Set the **log scribe** (dividers with bubble levels) to the largest gap between logs.
3. Keeping the scribe's levels plumb, trace the lower log's profile onto the upper log
   along both sides and at the notches. Wet the log or use a scribing pencil so the line shows.
4. Roll the log over, cut the groove and notches to the lines with chainsaw, then clean
   up with gouge and axe. Groove edges slightly undercut (cupped) so the outer edges
   bear tight.
5. Roll back, check fit; re-scribe tight spots if needed.

## 4. Corner notches
- **Saddle notch / round notch:** cut in the upper log to fit over the log below —
  common in full-scribe work; sheds water when cut on the upper log (cup facing down).
- **Dovetail notch:** common on hewn logs in Appalachian and central European
  traditions; locks the corner without extending ends.
- Never cut notches that hold water facing up.

## 5. Settling allowance
Green logs shrink across the grain as they dry, so a wall loses height — often
quoted as several percent of wall height (e.g. figures of roughly 3–6% get cited for
green logs; less for dry logs). Detail for it:
- **Window and door bucks** in a keyed slot in the log ends (spline), with a gap above
  (settling space, packed with compressible insulation and covered by trim fixed to only
  one side).
- **Posts** carrying roof or floors: adjustable screw jacks at the base, lowered
  periodically as the walls settle.
- **Interior partitions, stairs, plumbing and electrical risers, chimneys**: slip joints
  or slotted connections — nothing rigid tying log wall to non-settling elements.
- Keep logs in the wall dry from the outside: overhangs, gutters, high plinth.

## Gotchas
- A rigid frame-wall partition nailed to logs gets torn or the logs hang up and gaps open.
- Drying radial checks facing out let water in; orient checks down/inside where possible
  (a saw kerf on the hidden side can control where a log checks).
- Chainsaw work at height on rolling logs is dangerous: chaps, helmet, face protection,
  secure logs before cutting.

## Verify
- [ ] Logs peeled, sorted, and butt/top alternated to keep courses level.
- [ ] Scribed grooves bear on both edges with insulation in the groove.
- [ ] Notches shed water.
- [ ] Settling space above every opening; jacks or slip joints at every rigid element.
- [ ] Roof overhang and plinth keep the bottom logs dry.

*Uncertainty:* settling percentages vary widely with species and moisture content;
measure moisture and follow your builder's or association's (e.g. ILBA log building
standards) guidance.
