# Dry stone walling — batter, hearting and through-stones

**Goal:** a free-standing or retaining wall of stone laid without mortar that stands for
a century, because friction, gravity and good bonding hold it — not luck.

Regional styles differ greatly (Cotswold, Galloway dyke, Irish, Pennine, Andean,
Mediterranean). Organisations such as the UK Dry Stone Walling Association publish
specifications and run certification; learn the local style.

## 1. Strip and sort
- Strip the old wall or stone heap and **sort**: foundation stones (largest, flattest),
  building stones by size, through-stones (long), copes, and hearting (small, angular
  pieces). Sorting saves enormous time later.
- Lay stones out on both sides of the line within easy reach.

## 2. Foundation
- Strip turf and topsoil down to firm subsoil (often 100–150 mm or more).
- Foundation width is typically wider than the wall base; lay the biggest stones
  **length into the wall** (not along it), level, solidly bedded, faces to the line.

## 3. Batter frames and lines
- Set an **A-frame (batter frame)** at each end giving the wall's profile: base width,
  top width and height. A common free-standing profile tapers so each face leans in
  (e.g. a base around 600–900 mm narrowing towards ~300–400 mm under the copes; regional
  specs vary).
- Run lines between the frames on each face and raise them as you build.

## 4. Building the faces
- Build **two faces** at the same time, stones length-into-the-wall (the "rule of
  length in"), each stone's top bed level or tilted slightly **down and out** so water
  drains away from the core rather than into it.
- **Cross the joints:** one stone over two, two over one. Running joints (vertical
  joints lined up) are the main structural fault.
- Each stone touches the line at its face, sits firmly on the stones below — no rocking.
  If it rocks, turn it, try another, or pin it from **behind** (never wedge on the face).
- Bigger stones lower, smaller higher.

## 5. Hearting
- Fill the core between faces **tightly** with angular hearting as you go, each course —
  packed by hand, placed not tipped. Loose or rounded fill lets the faces slump in.
- Never use soil or rubble with fines as hearting.

## 6. Through-stones
- Long stones spanning the full wall width (sometimes protruding), placed at intervals
  — commonly around half-height, roughly every metre along the wall — tying the two
  faces together.

## 7. Copes
Copes (often set on edge, tight together) weigh down and protect the top. Wedge them
tightly, and finish the wall end with a "cheek end" of long stones bonded alternately.

## 8. Corbelling (huts and roofs)
In corbelled structures (Irish clochán, Apulian trulli) each course overhangs the one
below slightly, stones angled downward-outward to shed water, with heavy hearting/
backing behind to counterbalance, until the courses close at the top. Overhang per
course must stay small enough that the centre of gravity remains supported — build
slowly, test-load mentally each course.

## Gotchas
- Running joints and face-wedging are the faults inspectors look for first.
- Retaining walls carry soil and water pressure: steeper batter, thicker base, drainage;
  beyond modest heights, engineering is required.
- Safety: steel-toe boots, gloves, eye protection when dressing stone; lift with legs.

## Verify
- [ ] Stones sorted; foundation on firm subsoil, length into the wall.
- [ ] Batter frames and lines used both faces.
- [ ] No running joints; no rocking stones; no face wedges.
- [ ] Hearting tight each course; through-stones at specified spacing.
- [ ] Copes tight, wall ends properly bonded.

*Uncertainty:* dimensions are typical of British field walls; follow the regional
specification for your area.
