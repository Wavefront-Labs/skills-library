# Thatched vernacular construction — lashed frames, palm and grass thatch

**Goal:** a durable building made almost entirely from plant materials — poles, leaves,
grass and fibre — as built across the tropics and many other regions.

There are hundreds of regional traditions (e.g. makuti palm-leaf roofs on the East
African coast, nipa/cogon in South-East Asia, grass rondavels in southern Africa,
Pacific fale). Learn the local one from local builders: material names, harvesting
seasons and techniques differ. This skill covers principles that recur.

## 1. Materials and harvesting
- **Poles:** straight, durable species, harvested at the season and moon/sap stage local
  practice recommends (often the dry season, when sap and starch are low, reducing borer
  attack). Debark and season; some traditions soak in water or smoke to deter insects.
- **Thatch:** palm leaves (whole fronds, or leaflets folded over a batten into "shingles"
  or panels), grasses (long, mature, dry stems), or reeds. Harvest when mature and dry.
- **Lashing:** rattan, split bamboo, bark fibre, coir (coconut) rope, or vines.
  Prepare fresh or soak to make pliable; many lashings tighten as they dry.

## 2. Pole frame
- Post holes: posts set into the ground (charred or treated at the base to slow rot, or on
  stones to keep dry). Post-in-ground timber rots fastest at the ground line — replace
  posts in rotation.
- Ring beam/wall plate lashed to post tops; rafters lashed to plate and ridge.
- **Lashings:** square lashing for crossing members, diagonal lashing to resist racking,
  with frapping turns to tighten. Bracing is needed — a pole frame racks easily in wind.
- Tie the roof down to the walls/posts: high winds lift roofs before they push walls.

## 3. Roof pitch and thatch
- **Steep pitch** sheds water before it soaks in. Thatch roofs are commonly steep
  (often 45° or more); shallower pitches rot faster and leak.
- Lay thatch **from eave to ridge** in overlapping courses, each course covering the
  fixings of the one below. Only the exposed tips should weather.
- **Palm panels/shingles:** leaflets folded over a batten and stitched, then tied to
  purlins with close spacing (closer spacing = thicker, longer-lasting roof).
- **Grass:** bundles laid butt-down, tied to battens, beaten up to a consistent slope
  and thickness.
- **Ridge:** the most vulnerable point — capped with folded bundles, palm fronds, or a
  woven ridge, tied down securely.
- Generous overhangs protect walls and posts.

## 4. Walls
Woven bamboo or palm matting, wattle (woven sticks, sometimes daubed with earth),
grass panels, or left open for ventilation in hot-humid climates. Open, raised floors
(stilts) aid cooling and protect from floods and pests.

## 5. Hazards and maintenance
- **Fire:** thatch is highly flammable. Keep cooking fires away or in a separate
  kitchen, spacing between buildings, no flying sparks; consider fire-retardant
  treatments where available.
- **Termites and borers:** treated or naturally resistant timber, raised posts, regular
  inspection.
- **Wind:** tie-downs at the ridge and eaves; low profile in cyclone areas.
- **Lifespan:** palm roofs may last only a few years; good grass thatch longer. Patch
  leaks quickly; replace sections in rotation.

## Gotchas
- Thatch laid too thin or at a low pitch lets water track down to the battens and rots them.
- Fresh green leaves shrink and loosen as they dry; retighten.
- Plastic sheeting under thatch traps moisture and can rot it.

## Verify
- [ ] Poles harvested, treated and seasoned per local practice.
- [ ] Frame braced and lashed; roof tied down at eaves and ridge.
- [ ] Pitch steep enough; courses overlap so fixings are covered.
- [ ] Fire separation and fire practices in place.
- [ ] Maintenance cycle planned with local builders.

*Uncertainty:* written from general documentation of tropical vernacular practice;
techniques are regional and best confirmed with local craftspeople.
