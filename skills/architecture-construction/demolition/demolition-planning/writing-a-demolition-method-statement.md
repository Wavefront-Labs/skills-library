# Writing a demolition method statement — plan the removal before anything is removed

**Goal:** turn a building into a written, engineer-checked sequence that tells every
person on site what comes down, in what order, by what method, with what protection —
so the structure stays stable at every stage, not only at the end.

## 1. Gather the facts first (desk study + survey)
- **Drawings and history:** original and as-built drawings, alterations, previous uses
  (chemicals, fuel tanks, cold stores). Assume drawings are wrong until checked on site.
- **Hazardous materials survey:** in the UK a refurbishment and demolition (R&D)
  asbestos survey; in the US an asbestos inspection under NESHAP; elsewhere the local
  equivalent. Planning is not finished until this report is in hand — see
  `architecture-construction.demolition.hazardous-material-removal.planning-an-asbestos-abatement-job`.
- **Structural survey:** structural form (frame, load-bearing masonry, post-tensioned,
  precast, cantilevers, transfer beams), condition, and anything that depends on this
  building — party walls, shared foundations, basements retaining the road.
- **Surroundings:** neighbours, public highway, rail, overhead lines, trees, sensitive
  receptors (hospitals, schools, labs with vibration-sensitive equipment).

## 2. Isolate services — in writing, not by word of mouth
Electricity, gas, water, drainage, telecoms, district heating, fire mains, and anything
crossing the site to serve others. Get written disconnection confirmation from each
utility or a competent person; "the client said it's off" is not isolation. Locate
and mark live services that must stay (e.g. a substation feeding the street).
Gotcha: abandoned-but-live cables and gas in "dead" pipes are a classic killer —
test before cutting, and treat every service as live until proven dead.

## 3. Choose the method per element
Match method to structure and surroundings: hand demolition, mechanical (high-reach,
standard excavator, remote-controlled robot), selective strip-out, deconstruction for
salvage, or explosive felling (licensed specialists only). One job often mixes methods —
hand-strip the bay against the party wall, machine the rest.

## 4. Write the sequence that keeps it standing
Rules of thumb:
- **Reverse of construction:** roof → upper floors → lower floors; non-structural
  before structural; secondary members before primary.
- **Never remove an element something else still relies on** without temporary works
  designed by a competent engineer (propping, restraint to free-standing walls,
  facade retention).
- **Free-standing walls:** once floors go they lose lateral restraint; the engineer sets
  the safe standing height and the wind speed at which work stops.
- **Special structures** (prestressed/post-tensioned, cantilevers, long-span trusses)
  need an engineer-specific procedure; cutting a tendon releases stored energy.
- **Debris on floors** can overload them — specify maximum pile sizes and clear-down
  frequency (floor capacity to be confirmed by the engineer).

## 5. Define exclusion zones and protection
Draw them on a plan: an **exclusion/drop zone** around each machine and working face
(a common starting point is a radius not less than the height being demolished, often
more — confirm against your code and engineer), pedestrian and vehicle routes,
hoarding, screens, debris netting and public protection. Name who controls entry
(usually the demolition supervisor) and how it is enforced.

## 6. Environmental controls and monitoring
Dust suppression, noise and vibration limits with monitoring points, permitted hours,
waste segregation and recycling targets, drain protection, and the trigger levels that
stop work. Limits vary by permit — take them from the planning consent or local
authority rather than a rule of thumb.

## 7. Roles, emergency and sign-off
List the competent supervisor, appointed temporary-works engineer, plant operators'
qualifications, first aid, the unplanned-collapse and emergency procedure, and the
"hold points" where the engineer inspects before the next stage. Brief the crew
(toolbox talk) and record the briefing. Any change to the sequence means a revised,
re-approved statement — not a verbal tweak.

## Gotchas
- A generic template is worse than none — inspectors and courts look for site detail.
- Party walls and basements often turn a "simple" job into a temporary-works job.
- Update the statement when conditions change: hidden structure found, weather,
  plant breakdown, a neighbour's complaint about vibration.

## Verify
- [ ] Hazardous materials survey complete and removal scheduled before structural work.
- [ ] Written isolation confirmation for every service; retained live services marked.
- [ ] Sequence checked by a competent structural/temporary-works engineer, with hold points.
- [ ] Exclusion zones drawn on a plan with a named person controlling entry.
- [ ] Neighbours, highway and party-wall interfaces addressed.
- [ ] Crew briefed and briefing recorded; change process defined.
- [ ] Meets local law (e.g. CDM 2015 / BS 6187 in the UK, OSHA Subpart T in the US,
      AS 2601 and state WHS rules in Australia).
