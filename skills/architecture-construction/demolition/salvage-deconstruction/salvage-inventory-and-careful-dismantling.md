# Salvage inventory and careful dismantling — take it apart so it can be used again

**Goal:** identify what in a building is worth reusing, remove it undamaged in a safe
order, and deliver it graded, documented and saleable — instead of crushing it into
mixed waste.

## 1. Pre-demolition salvage audit (before the programme is fixed)
Walk the building with the drawings and a camera and list, room by room:
- **Element, material, quantity, dimensions, condition** (good / repairable / scrap).
- **High-value items first:** hardwood floors, joists and beams (old-growth timber),
  doors, panelling, fireplaces, stairs, radiators, roof slates and tiles, stone
  copings, ironwork, sanitaryware, light fittings, structural steel sections.
- **Fixing method** — nailed, screwed, bolted, lime or cement mortar. This decides
  whether recovery is economic: bricks in soft lime mortar clean easily; in hard cement
  mortar they usually don't.
- **Contamination:** lead paint, asbestos, treated timber, pigeon guano, oil. Hazardous
  items get removed by the right contractor first
  (`architecture-construction.demolition.hazardous-material-removal.planning-an-asbestos-abatement-job`)
  and contaminated material is not sold as reclaim.
Record a rough value and a buyer or route (dealer, reuse hub, client re-use) for each
line — salvage without a buyer is just slow demolition.

## 2. Plan the programme and space
- Deconstruction takes longer and needs more labour than mechanical demolition; agree
  the extra time with the client up front.
- Reserve a dry, secure **sorting and storage area** with pallets, racks and cover;
  timber left in rain degrades fast.
- Structural removal order still needs the demolition engineer's sequence — salvage
  never overrides stability.

## 3. Dismantle in reverse order of construction
1. **Soft strip / fixtures:** doors, ironmongery, fittings, sanitaryware, radiators.
2. **Finishes:** flooring boards (lift with flooring bars, starting at the groove side),
   panelling, skirting, cornices where feasible.
3. **Roof coverings:** slates and tiles by hand from a safe platform or scaffold — never
   by walking unprotected on old roofs; fall protection per local rules.
4. **Roof structure and floors:** unbolt or cut trusses and joists, lowered by hand or
   crane; don't drop them.
5. **Walls:** masonry taken down by hand course by course where reclaim is planned.
6. **Frame:** unbolt steel connections where possible rather than torch-cutting
   through members — full-length sections are worth far more.

## 4. Process and grade
- **Timber:** de-nail fully (metal detector check), trim splits, sort by species,
  section and length, mark any structural grading needed for reuse (a reclaimed
  structural member generally needs regrading by a qualified grader — check locally).
- **Brick:** clean off mortar, reject spalled or frost-damaged units, stack by type and
  colour; reclaimed bricks for new work may need testing for frost resistance.
- **Steel:** record section size, length, condition, coatings; for structural reuse
  the engineer may need mill certificates or testing (unverified requirements vary).
- **Fittings:** test, label with origin, photograph, wrap.
Keep a **material passport** or inventory sheet: what, how much, condition, where from.

## Gotchas
- Lead paint on old joinery and radiators: test before stripping or heat-sanding.
- Nails left in timber wreck saws and injure buyers — de-nail everything.
- Reclaimed structural material without traceability is often refused by engineers.
- Theft from site: secure storage and quick dispatch for high-value items.

## Verify
- [ ] Salvage audit with quantity, condition, value and buyer for each item.
- [ ] Hazardous materials removed first; contaminated items excluded from resale.
- [ ] Dismantling follows the engineer's stability sequence.
- [ ] Recovered items stored dry, sorted, graded and labelled.
- [ ] Inventory/passport issued with each lot; diversion rate recorded for reporting.
