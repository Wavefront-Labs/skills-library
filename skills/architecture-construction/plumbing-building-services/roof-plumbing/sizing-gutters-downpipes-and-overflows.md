# Sizing gutters, downpipes and overflows

**Goal:** the roof's design storm goes to stormwater, and when a gutter or downpipe does
block, the overflow dumps water *outside* the building rather than into the ceiling.

## 1. Catchment and rainfall
- **Catchment area** of each gutter run = the plan area of roof it drains, increased for
  roof pitch (codes give a slope factor) and for any wall that sheds water onto the roof.
- **Design rainfall intensity** from local data for the code's return period (e.g., 5-min
  duration, 1-in-20-year for eaves gutters in AS/NZS 3500.3; 1-in-100 for box gutters;
  US codes use 100-year, 1-hour rates). Get the number for your location — it varies
  hugely (100 mm/h to 300+ mm/h).

## 2. Downpipe sizing and spacing
- Each downpipe has a maximum catchment it can serve for a given gutter and intensity
  (code tables or manufacturer charts). Typical residential: 90 mm round or 100×50
  rectangular downpipes, one per ~40–60 m² of catchment in moderate rainfall — **a rule
  of thumb only.**
- Space downpipes so no gutter run is too long (often max ~12 m to a downpipe), and put
  them near valleys and at the end of long runs.
- Valleys discharge a lot of water into a short length of gutter — a downpipe close
  to the valley outlet avoids overflow there.

## 3. Gutter size and fall
- Choose a gutter with cross-section adequate for the downpipe spacing and intensity
  (tables).
- **Fall:** common minimum ~1:500 for eaves gutters (some suggest 1:200–1:300 for
  self-cleaning) — falls that are too flat pond water and corrode metal gutters.
  Fall towards outlets; split long runs with high points.
- Brackets at ~1.2 m max (closer in snow or cyclonic regions); front of gutter lower than
  the back (or slotted) so overflow goes outside, not back into the fascia/eaves.
- Expansion joints in long metal runs (often every ~12–18 m, see manufacturer).

## 4. Overflows
- Eaves gutters: the front lower than the fascia, slotted gutter, or overflow devices
  at outlets.
- Box gutters and internal gutters need **rainwater heads or sumps with dedicated overflow
  outlets** sized for the full design flow — a blocked box gutter without overflow is a
  common cause of ceiling collapses.
- Rainwater heads: overflow slot set below the gutter sole.

## 5. Downpipe connections
- Pop/outlet sized to the downpipe; sealed and riveted (metal) or solvent-joined (PVC).
- Downpipes clipped at ≤ ~1.8–3 m centres; offset bends kept minimal.
- Connect to stormwater via an inspection point; not to sewer.

## 6. Rainwater tanks
- Leaf screens/guards and a **first-flush diverter** to keep roof dirt out.
- Tank overflow sized at least equal to the inflow, discharging to stormwater away
  from foundations.
- Mosquito-proof screens on inlets/overflows; backflow prevention where the tank
  connects to mains-supplied fixtures (toilets, laundries).
- Don't harvest for drinking from roofs with lead flashings or some coatings without
  checking.

## Gotchas
- Front of gutter higher than fascia → overflow back into the eaves.
- Dissimilar metals (copper on zinc-coated steel) → corrosion; also no copper water
  onto galvanised gutters.
- Downpipe miles from a valley → valley gutter overflowing in every storm.
- Tank overflow dumped next to the slab → foundation movement.

## Verify
- [ ] Catchment and intensity calculation recorded per downpipe.
- [ ] Hose test: water runs to outlets, no ponding over ~2–3 mm.
- [ ] Overflow path tested by blocking an outlet briefly.
- [ ] Downpipes connected to stormwater with inspection access.
- [ ] Tank has screens, first flush and overflow to a safe point.
