# Fitting leaded panels with saddle bars and protective glazing

**Goal:** leaded panels fixed into masonry or frames so they're supported against
wind and sagging, weathertight, removable for future conservation, and protected
without trapping condensation. (Making the panels themselves is a separate craft —
see `handcrafts.glassblowing-glasswork.stained-glass.copper-foil-panel-construction`
for the related bench work.)

## 1. Survey and size
- Make a **template** of each light in the opening (card or rigid sheet) — stone
  openings are never square or identical, and tracery lights differ every one.
- Panel size: allow for the **groove depth** in stone (commonly ~15–25 mm each side
  in historic work) so the panel can be "sprung" in: one side pushed deep into its
  groove, the other side swung in, then centred. Confirm actual groove depth by probing.
- Large windows are built as a stack of panels (often no more than ~0.6–0.8 m tall
  each) with horizontal lap or **T-bar** joints between them; the weight of lead
  makes larger panels bow and sag.

## 2. Saddle bars (ferramenta)
- Round or flat bars (traditionally wrought iron; now often stainless or bronze/
  non-ferrous to avoid rust-jacking that bursts stone) set into holes in the jambs
  at roughly 300 mm centres across the panel.
- **Copper tie wires** soldered to lead joints on the panel's inside face are
  twisted around the bar to hold the panel against wind suction and stop it bowing.
  Twist firmly but not so tight that the panel distorts.
- On conservation jobs, keep and reuse original bar positions; drilling new holes in
  historic stone usually needs consent.

## 3. Fixing and pointing
- Spring the panel into the grooves, packing to centre. Point the groove with **lime
  mortar** (often a hydraulic or non-hydraulic lime/sand mix matched to the stone —
  confirm with the conservator), never hard cement: it must be possible to rake out
  without damaging the stone or lead.
- Inside timber or metal frames, fix with beads or putty as the frame dictates.
- Panels should be **cemented** (leaded-light cement brushed under the leaves of the
  lead cames) before installation to make them rigid and weathertight; check this on
  site if the panel looks dry or rattles.

## 4. Protective glazing
- External protective glazing guards against impact and weather, but **unvented**
  sheets trap moisture and heat → condensation on the painted inner face and
  corrosion. Current conservation practice favours **ventilated (isothermal)
  protective glazing**: the new clear glazing goes in the original groove outside,
  and the historic panel is moved inward a few cm onto a new frame, with a ventilated
  gap so the panel sits at room conditions. Ventilation gaps at top and bottom
  (dimension per the conservator's design).
- Polycarbonate sheets yellow and scratch; laminated or toughened glass is the usual
  choice.

## Gotchas
- Panel cut to the sight size with no groove allowance — it falls out.
- Iron bars rusting and splitting mullions; use non-ferrous where you can.
- Cement mortar in a historic groove.
- Sealed protective glazing cooking the paint layer.

## Verify
- [ ] Panel templated; sized with groove engagement on all sides.
- [ ] Saddle bars at suitable centres; ties secure; panel flat (no bowing).
- [ ] Lime pointing complete and set back neatly; no cement.
- [ ] Protective glazing ventilated; gap and vents clear.
- [ ] Panel removable in future without damage (conservation principle).
