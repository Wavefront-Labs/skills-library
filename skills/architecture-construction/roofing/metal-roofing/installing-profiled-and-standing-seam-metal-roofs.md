# Installing profiled and standing-seam metal roofs

**Goal:** sheets square to the eaves, laps that shed the weather, fasteners that hold
without leaking, and room for the metal to expand and contract.

## 1. Check before lifting a sheet
- Minimum pitch for the profile (deep trapezoidal can go to ~1–2°; corrugated often
  ≥5°; standing seam varies by system). End laps usually need steeper pitch.
- Purlin/batten spacing within the profile's span table for the wind zone.
- Sheets as long as possible (no end laps if you can get full lengths). Note transport/
  crane limits.
- Material compatibility: no copper or lead runoff onto zinc/aluminium-zinc coated steel;
  no direct contact between dissimilar metals; keep coated steel off treated timber
  (some preservatives are corrosive) — use a barrier.

## 2. Start square
- Check the roof frame for square (diagonals). If out, adjust so the *sheets* run square
  to the eaves (a slight overhang taper at the verge is fine; a creeping sheet line isn't).
- **Lay away from the prevailing weather** so side laps face away from the driving
  wind/rain.
- Set the first sheet with a string line along the eaves overhang (typically 50 mm into
  the gutter) and check every few sheets with a tape to the ridge to catch creep.

## 3. Profiled sheet fixing
- Fastener type to the purlin (timber vs steel thickness) and coating class to the
  environment (coastal → higher class/stainless).
- Fix **through crests** on roofs (water flows in the pans) unless the manufacturer says
  otherwise; walls often fixed in pans.
- Pattern per wind zone (e.g., every second rib at intermediate purlins, every rib at
  end purlins and in high wind).
- Drive screws square, EPDM washer compressed but not squashed out (a slight bulge).
- Side laps: stitch at the manufacturer's spacing where required.

## 4. Standing seam
- Concealed clips: **one fixed zone** per sheet (usually at the ridge or mid-length per
  system) and **sliding/floating clips** elsewhere so sheets expand along their length.
  Fixing both ends rigidly causes buckling, noise and torn fixings.
- Thermal movement is large: steel ~1.2 mm per metre per 100 °C; aluminium ~2.3 mm. A 20
  m aluminium sheet in a 70 °C range moves ~30 mm.
- Seam with the system's seamer (hand crimp at ends, machine for runs).
- Oil-canning (visible waviness in flat pans) is reduced by stiffening ribs, correct
  substrate flatness and not over-tightening clips; it's cosmetic, often not a warranty
  defect.

## 5. Penetrations and trims
- Large penetrations get a **curb** or saddle flashing that diverts water around
  (cut sheet ends turned up — "stop ends" — at the ridge side to prevent wind-driven water).
- Ridge capping fixed per schedule with closure foam to the profile.
- Turn up pans at the high end (stop-ending) and turn down at the gutter (drip).

## 6. Swarf and cut edges
- **Sweep swarf off immediately** (filings rust into the coating within days).
- Cut with snips or nibblers, not abrasive discs (hot particles burn the coating).
- Touch up only per manufacturer (some forbid touch-up paint on cut edges).

## Gotchas
- Sheets laid into the weather → leaks at side laps.
- Over-tightened screws → dished washers that leak.
- Rigid fixing of long standing-seam sheets → buckling/fastener failure.
- Swarf left overnight → rust-spotted roof and a warranty claim.

## Verify
- [ ] Sheets square to eaves; laps facing away from weather.
- [ ] Fastener type, spacing and coating class per the manufacturer and wind zone.
- [ ] Standing seam has fixed and floating clips per system.
- [ ] Stop-ends at high ends; drips at gutters; curbs at penetrations.
- [ ] Roof swept clean of swarf at the end of each day.
