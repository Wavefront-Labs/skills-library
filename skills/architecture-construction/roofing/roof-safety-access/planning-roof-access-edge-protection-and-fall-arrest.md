# Planning roof access, edge protection and fall arrest

**Goal:** nobody falls — and if a fall-arrest system is ever used, the person is stopped
before hitting anything and rescued within minutes. Falls from roofs are among the top
killers in construction worldwide.

## 1. Hierarchy — work down only when the level above is impractical
1. **Avoid** working at height (pre-assemble on the ground, use MEWPs from below).
2. **Collective prevention:** scaffold with guardrails, perimeter edge protection,
   covers on openings. Protects everyone, needs no user action.
3. **Personal restraint:** harness and lanyard short enough that you *can't reach* the
   edge.
4. **Fall arrest:** harness, energy absorber and anchor — stops a fall that's already
   happened.
5. Nets/airbags (soft landing) to reduce consequences.
Regulations encode this order (UK Work at Height Regulations; OSHA 1926 Subpart M with
the 6 ft trigger in US construction; AU/NZ WHS codes). Local trigger heights differ.

## 2. Edge protection
- Top rail ~950 mm–1.1 m (check local figure), mid rail, toe board; strong enough to take
  a person falling against it. Extend above the eave on pitched roofs so a person sliding
  down is caught.
- At the edge of pitched roofs, a scaffold platform at eave height plus guardrails is the
  gold standard.

## 3. Access
- Ladders: secured at the top, extended ~1 m (about three rungs) above the landing, at
  roughly **1 out for every 4 up** (75°). Three points of contact; tools in a belt or hauled.
- Prefer stair towers or scaffold stairs for long jobs.
- Plan the transfer from ladder to roof — it's a common fall point.

## 4. Anchors and harness work
- Anchors rated for fall arrest (often ≥ 15 kN / ~5,000 lbf per person — check the
  standard), installed per manufacturer into structure, not into battens or sheeting.
- **Fall clearance**: lanyard length + energy absorber deployment (often ~1.75 m) +
  harness stretch + person's height to D-ring + safety margin. Typical total ~5–6 m
  for a 2 m lanyard on a fixed anchor — low roofs may need a self-retracting lifeline
  or restraint instead.
- Avoid swing falls: anchor above and close to the work line.
- Inspect harness and lanyards before each use; remove any that have arrested a fall.

## 5. Fragile surfaces
- Skylights, old fibre-cement and translucent sheets, and rusted metal sheets won't
  hold a person. **Treat every roof as fragile until proven otherwise.**
- Cover/guard roof lights, use staging/crawling boards, and stay on purlin lines only
  where the method statement allows.
- Asbestos-cement roofs: fragile *and* hazardous — specialist procedures.

## 6. Rescue and conditions
- A rescue plan before anyone clips on: who, what kit, how fast. Suspension in a harness
  can become dangerous within minutes (suspension trauma).
- Stop for high wind (sheet handling especially), rain, frost, ice and dew.
- Exclusion zone below with debris netting/chutes.

## 7. Paperwork
SWMS / method statement / work-at-height plan specifying the controls; toolbox talk;
permit where the client requires.

## Gotchas
- Clipping to a TV aerial bracket, vent pipe or batten.
- Too little fall clearance → the arrest happens at ground level.
- Roof lights painted over and invisible.
- Ladder not tied off → slides on the gutter.

## Verify
- [ ] Controls chosen top-down; written plan explains why lower controls are used.
- [ ] Edge protection complete before roof work begins.
- [ ] Anchors certified; fall clearance calculated.
- [ ] Fragile areas identified, covered and marked.
- [ ] Rescue plan briefed and kit on site.
