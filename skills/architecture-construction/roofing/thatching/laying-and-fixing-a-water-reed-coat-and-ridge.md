# Laying and fixing a water-reed coat and ridge

**Goal:** a thatch at a steep enough pitch, laid thick and tight so water runs over the
outer ends of the stems, fixed securely, with a ridge that sheds water and is renewed
before the main coat fails.

*Thatching is a traditional craft learned by apprenticeship; this outlines the method and
the decisions, not a substitute for training. Materials and techniques vary by region
(water reed, combed wheat reed, long straw, palm, grasses).*

## 1. Pitch, timber and fire
- Thatch needs a steep pitch — commonly **≥45°, ideally ~50°** for reed. Lower pitches
  hold water and rot fast.
- Rafters with battens/laths at the spacing the thatcher wants (often ~200–250 mm).
- Fire: modern practice often adds a **fire barrier** board or membrane over rafters
  and a fire-retardant approach for new builds, plus chimney checks (spark arrestors,
  chimney height above thatch, liners). Many thatch fires start at chimneys.
  Check local building rules and insurers.

## 2. Material
- Water reed: long, rigid stems; graded and bundled. Combed wheat reed and long straw are
  laid differently (straw "yealms").
- Keep bundles dry until use; quality and length determine life (a good reed coat can last
  decades; ridges far less, often 10–15 years — **figures vary by climate.**)

## 3. Eaves and gables
1. Set the **eaves course** first: bundles tilted so the butts form the eave line with a
   drip clear of the wall, often over a tilting fillet/board to give the right angle.
2. Gables (verges) laid with bundles slightly tilted to throw water back onto the roof.
3. Line and trim the eave with a leggett so it's straight and crisp.

## 4. Coursing up the roof
1. Lay bundles in **courses** working upwards, each course covering the fixings of the
   one below. A good coat is thick (often ~300 mm or more of reed).
2. **Fix** each course: a horizontal **sway** (hazel rod or steel bar) laid across the
   bundles, held down to the rafters by **iron crooks** driven in or by **screws and wire**.
   Fixings must be hidden by the next course.
3. **Dress** each course up with a leggett (a bat with a ridged face) to drive the reed
   butts into a tight, even surface at the right pitch. The surface should be smooth and
   dense — water runs from stem end to stem end.
4. Keep the coat even across valleys and around windows (eyebrows/dormers are shaped
   carefully; water concentrates there).

## 5. Ridge
- Reed roofs are usually ridged with a more flexible material (sedge or straw), laid
  over a roll at the apex.
- **Flush ridge** (level with the coat) or **block ridge** (raised with patterns cut
  in the edge); secured with spars (split hazel staples twisted and pushed in) and
  liggers (hazel rods) along the surface.
- Ridges are renewed periodically — ridge renewal before the coat wears through it
  is key to long life.

## 6. Maintenance
- Netting against birds/vermin where needed (can trap moss and debris — mixed opinions).
- Keep moss and debris off; ensure trees don't overhang and drip.
- Patch worn areas (recoating or "spar coating" a new layer over a sound base, where
  permitted — heritage rules may require stripping to a certain layer).

## Gotchas
- Too shallow a pitch → rot within years.
- Fixings visible at the surface → water tracks along them.
- Loose, under-dressed coat → wind and water get in; fast wear.
- Ignoring the chimney → fire risk.

## Verify
- [ ] Pitch ≥ ~45°; coat thickness consistent (probe with a rule).
- [ ] No fixings visible; surface tight, dressed and even.
- [ ] Eave and gable lines straight, with drip clear of walls.
- [ ] Ridge spar-fixed, with liggers secure; renewal interval recorded.
- [ ] Chimney/fire precautions checked against local rules.
