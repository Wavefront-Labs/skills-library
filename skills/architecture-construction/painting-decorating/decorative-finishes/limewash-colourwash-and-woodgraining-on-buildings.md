# Limewash, colourwash and woodgraining on buildings

**Goal:** finishes with depth and movement that look intentional, are compatible with
the substrate, and survive cleaning — tested on boards first. (Gilding technique is
covered in `handcrafts.stone-and-surface-decoration-crafts.gilding.water-and-oil-gilding-on-gesso-and-bole`.)

## 1. Limewash (on lime plaster, lime render, soft brick, stone)
- What it is: lime putty (or hydrated lime) diluted with water to a thin milk, with
  limefast pigments (earth oxides) — sometimes additions like a little tallow or linseed
  oil (traditional water-resistance, exterior) or casein. Recipes vary; follow a tested
  one or a reputable proprietary limewash.
- It cures by carbonation into the surface — it's breathable and suits buildings that
  must lose moisture. It doesn't stick to modern paints, plastics or gypsum well.
- Method:
  1. Remove incompatible coatings; dampen the surface (mist) so it isn't absorbent.
  2. Apply **thin**, brushed vigorously into the surface with a large brush; many thin
     coats (often 3–5+) beat one thick one (thick coats craze and powder).
  3. Work in shade, keep each coat damp-cured (mist) for a day or so; avoid frost and
     hot sun.
  4. Colour dries much paler than it looks wet — test patches and let them dry fully.
- Safety: lime is caustic — goggles, gloves, long sleeves; eye splashes need copious
  water and medical attention.

## 2. Colourwash and broken colour (glazes)
- Base: a sound eggshell/satin basecoat (non-absorbent so the glaze stays open).
- Glaze: **scumble** (transparent glaze medium) tinted with paint/colourant — oil
  glazes give longer open time; acrylic glazes dry faster (add the maker's retarder).
  Typical starting ratio ~1 part colour to 4–10 parts glaze; test.
- Techniques:
  - **Colourwash:** apply with a wide brush in random criss-cross strokes, soften with
    a dry brush/softener into a cloudy, translucent finish.
  - **Ragging/sponging:** apply then lift (or apply with the tool) for texture.
  - **Dragging:** a long-bristle brush pulled straight down through wet glaze — a fine
    linear texture; use a plumb line mark.
  - **Stippling:** a stippling brush pounded into wet glaze for a fine, even mottle.
- Work in manageable sections, keeping a wet edge, two people on large walls (one
  applies, one textures). Corners: mask one wall, do the other, then swap.
- Protect with a clear varnish/glaze coat in wear areas.

## 3. Woodgraining
- Groundcoat in the wood's lightest colour (e.g. buff for oak, cream for pine), eggshell
  sheen, dry and lightly keyed.
- Graining colour: tinted scumble in raw/burnt sienna and umber tones.
- Tools: **graining combs** (steel/rubber) for straight grain; **rocker/heart grainer**
  rocked while dragging for heartwood figure; **flogger** (long-haired brush slapped
  along the grain) for pores; overgraining and **softening** with a badger or softener.
- Follow real joinery logic: grain runs along the length of each member — stiles
  vertical, rails horizontal, as on a real door; mask joints to keep them crisp.
- Varnish when fully dry for protection.

## 4. Marbling (brief)
Glaze on a white/coloured ground, veins drawn with a feather or fine brush in
diagonal drifts, softened; study a real stone sample — fake marble that ignores how the
stone forms looks wrong instantly.

## Gotchas
- Limewash over modern acrylic → flakes off.
- Thick limewash coats → crazing and dusting.
- Glaze drying before texturing → hard edges and lap lines.
- Graining that runs the wrong way across a rail.

## Verify
- [ ] Substrate compatibility confirmed (lime on mineral surfaces, glazes on sound basecoat).
- [ ] Sample board/test area approved dry under the room's light.
- [ ] Limewash in thin coats, damp-cured; no powdering when rubbed.
- [ ] Glaze work free of laps; corners clean.
- [ ] Graining follows joinery logic; protective coat applied where needed.
