# Boundary re-establishment — research, monuments and evidence

**Goal:** put the boundary back where the original survey put it, using evidence in
the order the law gives it weight — not where today's coordinates or fences say.

**Jurisdiction warning:** boundary law differs sharply between legal systems (e.g.
Torrens title states, US PLSS and metes-and-bounds states, England's general-boundaries
rule). Boundary determination is usually reserved to a licensed/registered surveyor.
This skill describes the common-law retracement method in general terms; follow your
jurisdiction's statutes, regulations and case law.

## 1. Research before fieldwork
- Current title/deed for the subject parcel **and the adjoiners**. Note the order of
  creation (senior and junior rights matter where descriptions conflict).
- Prior survey plans, plats and field notes — the **original** subdivision survey above
  all, plus later retracements by other surveyors.
- Road/right-of-way plans, easement instruments, covenants.
- Previous surveyors' marks and their descriptions (iron pin, drill hole, blaze, stone).

## 2. Recovering evidence in the field
1. Establish control and compute where record marks *should* be from the plan
   dimensions — but treat those as **search areas**, not answers.
2. Search for **original monuments** with metal detector, probe and careful digging.
   Photograph in situ with scale; record type, size, condition, and whether disturbed.
3. Measure between recovered marks and compare to record distances and bearings. Good
   agreement among several marks supports their originality; one wildly off is suspect.
4. Locate **occupations**: fences, walls, hedges, building lines. Note age and
   character (old established fence vs new post-and-wire).

## 3. Weighing evidence — the usual hierarchy
In most common-law jurisdictions, the general order of priority is:
1. **Natural monuments** (rivers, cliffs) called for in the title.
2. **Original artificial monuments** set by the creating survey, undisturbed.
3. **Adjoiners'** boundaries/monuments called for.
4. **Long-standing occupation** that can be shown to reflect the original survey.
5. **Measurements** — distances and bearings (and in some places bearings rank below distances).
6. **Area** — usually the weakest evidence.
The surveyor's job is to follow the footsteps of the original surveyor. Precise modern
coordinates do not override a recovered original mark.

## 4. Resolving conflicts
- Lost vs obliterated corners: an obliterated corner whose position can be shown by
  witness evidence is restored there; a truly lost corner is re-established by
  proportionate measurement or the method your jurisdiction prescribes.
- Excess/deficiency along a block is usually **distributed proportionately** between
  recovered original marks, not dumped on the last lot.
- Do not decide adverse possession or title disputes yourself — **report** the facts
  (encroachments, occupation discrepancies) and refer the legal question.

## 5. Marking and reporting
- Set new marks of a durable, identifiable type (cap with licence number where required).
- The plan/report should state: evidence found and relied on, evidence rejected and
  why, discrepancies with record, occupations and encroachments, easements.
- Lodge or file the survey where your jurisdiction requires it.

## Gotchas
- A fence is not the boundary until evidence ties it to the original survey.
- Plans from different eras use different bearing bases (magnetic, true, grid) — rotate
  before comparing.
- Imperial units (chains, links, rods) on old plans: 1 chain = 66 ft = 100 links ≈ 20.1168 m.
- Clients pressure for "where should it be"; your duty is to the evidence, not the client.

## Verify
- [ ] Title, adjoiners and original plans obtained and read.
- [ ] Every mark relied on is described, photographed and its originality argued.
- [ ] Bearing bases reconciled; excess/deficiency handled by the accepted method.
- [ ] Occupations and encroachments shown and reported, not adjudicated.
- [ ] Work is under (or reviewed by) a licensed surveyor where law requires.

*Uncertainty:* the evidence hierarchy is a generalisation of common-law practice;
exceptions are common and statute can reorder it.
