# Aqua fitness — water resistance, deck cueing and pool safety

**Goal:** run water-based classes that give a real workout with low joint
load, with movement adapted to how water behaves and instruction that
works from the pool deck.

## 1. How water changes exercise
- **Buoyancy:** water at chest depth supports much of body weight (often
  quoted around 70–75%; ~50% at waist depth) — great for joints, but
  weight-bearing load is low. **Approximate figures.**
- **Drag/resistance:** water resists movement in **every direction**; the
  faster you move and the larger the surface area, the more resistance.
  So intensity is controlled by **speed, range and surface area** (open
  hands, gloves, foam equipment), not by added weight.
- **Hydrostatic pressure** and cooling lower heart rate somewhat at a given
  effort — heart rates in water read lower than on land; use RPE.
- **Water temperature:** ~28–30 °C is commonly suggested for active
  classes; warmer suits therapy/gentle classes and can overheat vigorous ones.
  **Check your facility's guidance.**

## 2. Formats
- **Shallow water** (waist to chest deep): walking, jogging, jumping jacks,
  kicks; feet on the floor give stability.
- **Deep water** (feet off the floor): with **flotation belts**; deep-water
  running and cycling motions; zero impact. Participants must be comfortable
  in deep water.
- **Specialist:** arthritis/older adult programmes, pre/post-natal,
  rehab-oriented classes (often in warmer pools).

## 3. Movement adaptation
- Land moves need **water tempo**: often around **half** land tempo
  (music at ~125–150 BPM taken at half time). Moving at full land tempo
  shortens range and reduces resistance.
- Use **levels**: rebound (bouncing off the floor), neutral (shoulders at
  water level, knees bent — more resistance, less impact), suspended
  (feet off the floor briefly).
- Travel across the pool to increase intensity (frontal drag).
- Equipment: **noodles** (support or resistance), **foam dumbbells**
  (buoyant: pushing them **down** works the muscles that would lift on
  land the opposite way — e.g. pressing down works triceps/lats),
  **gloves** for more surface area.

## 4. Deck instruction
- The instructor is usually **on deck** for visibility and safety, so
  participants can see the full movement.
- Demonstrate the **water version** on land: slower, wider, showing hand
  shapes; use a chair or bar for balance on deck.
- **Visual cues** matter — acoustics in pools are poor. Use a waterproof
  mic system if available; large arm signals for direction, counts and
  "stop".
- Protect yourself: land-based impact at water tempo stresses the
  instructor; use low-impact versions on deck and good footwear.
  Avoid barefoot jumping on hard tiled decks.
- Some instructors enter the water to assist specific participants;
  only with a second person watching the class or a lifeguard present.

## 5. Safety
- Follow the facility's **lifeguard/supervision** requirements.
- Screen participants: swimming ability (for deep water), heart conditions,
  incontinence, open wounds, recent surgery.
- Wet decks: slip hazards for participants entering/exiting; non-slip
  footwear for you.
- Watch for fatigue, chills, overheating, and anyone drifting out of their depth.
- Electrical equipment (speakers, mics) kept away from the pool edge;
  use pool-safe or battery kit.

## Gotchas
- Moving at land tempo.
- Using heavy weights instead of drag.
- Cueing by voice only in a noisy pool.
- **Buoyancy percentages and temperatures are commonly quoted approximations.**
