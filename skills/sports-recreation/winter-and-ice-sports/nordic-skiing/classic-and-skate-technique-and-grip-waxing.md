# Nordic skiing — classic and skate technique, and grip waxing

**Goal:** move efficiently on cross-country skis in both techniques and get the
skis to grip uphill and glide on the flat — which on classic skis is mostly a
waxing problem, not a fitness problem.

## 1. Two techniques, two kinds of ski
- **Classic:** skis run in parallel set tracks. Each ski has a **kick (grip) zone**
  under the foot that must bite the snow when weighted, and glide zones at tip
  and tail. Grip comes from wax, a fishscale pattern (waxless) or mohair skins.
- **Skate:** skis push off at an angle like ice skating, on groomed corduroy, no
  grip wax — glide wax full length. Skate skis are shorter and stiffer; skate
  poles are longer (roughly chin to mouth height vs. armpit to shoulder for classic,
  as a rule of thumb).
- **Camber/flex fitting:** the kick zone should only touch the snow when your full
  weight is on one ski. **Paper test:** stand on both skis on a hard floor, slide a
  sheet of paper under the foot — it should move with weight split, and pin when
  you shift all weight to one ski. Wrong stiffness cannot be fixed with wax.

## 2. Classic technique
- **Diagonal stride:** opposite arm and leg, like walking. Commit weight fully
  onto the gliding ski; the "kick" is a short, sharp downward press that sets the
  wax, then the hip drives forward. Fault: kicking backwards (slips) instead of down.
- **Double poling:** both poles together, hinge at the hips, use the core and
  body weight, not just arms. Dominant on fast flats; modern racers double pole
  most of the course.
- **Kick double pole:** one kick plus a double pole — transition gear.
- **Climbing:** shorten the stride, then **herringbone** (tips out) on steep pitches.

## 3. Skate gears (like bike gears)
| Gear | Use | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| V1 / offset | steep climbs | one double pole on the "strong side" every two skates |
| V2 / one-skate | moderate terrain, speed | double pole on every skate |
| V2 alternate / two-skate | fast flats | double pole every other skate |
| Free skate | downhills | no poles |
Glide on a flat ski, then edge and push to the side; nose, knee and toe over the
gliding ski. Common fault: never fully transferring weight, so you shuffle on both skis.

## 4. Grip waxing (classic)
1. Mark the kick zone (from the paper test; roughly from the heel to ~30–60 cm in
   front of it, ski-dependent).
2. Lightly sand/roughen, apply a **binder** base layer if the snow is abrasive.
3. Choose **hard wax** by snow temperature and type: colder = harder wax (green /
   blue), near zero = softer (violet / red). Old, icy, transformed or wet snow
   needs **klister** (sticky tube wax).
4. Apply thin layers, cork each in. Several thin layers beat one thick one.
5. Test on a short climb. Slipping → add layers or lengthen the zone forward.
   Dragging/icing → too soft or too long; scrape some off.
**Gotcha:** fresh snow falling near 0 °C is the hardest condition — wax ices up.
Skin skis are the pragmatic answer for recreational skiers then.

## 5. Glide waxing
Hot-wax glide zones (or the whole skate ski) with an iron at the wax maker's
temperature, scrape when cool, brush out. Fluorinated waxes are banned in
FIS and IBU competition (since 2023–24) and many national races; check the
event's rules — fluoro testing is done at races.

## 6. Biathlon pacing (overview)
Shooting follows hard skiing: ease off on the final ~100–200 m into the range to
drop heart rate, breathe out and settle before each shot. Exact range procedures
belong to the IBU rules and your club's coach.

## Verify
- [ ] Paper test done; skis matched to your weight.
- [ ] Classic: you can climb a moderate hill in diagonal stride without slipping.
- [ ] Skate: you can name and use V1 on climbs and V2 on flats.
- [ ] Wax chosen from the day's snow temperature, tested before the session.
- [ ] Race wax legal for the event (no fluoro where banned).
