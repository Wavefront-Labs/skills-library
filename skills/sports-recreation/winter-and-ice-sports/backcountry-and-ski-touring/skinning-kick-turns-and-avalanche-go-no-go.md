# Backcountry and ski touring — skinning, kick turns and avalanche go/no-go

**Goal:** climb efficiently on skis and come home — using a repeatable
decision routine, not intuition. **This is not avalanche training**: take a
recognised course (AIARE/Avalanche Canada AST/European equivalents) before
touring in avalanche terrain.

## 1. Kit
- Touring bindings (pin/tech or frame) that free the heel for climbing; boots
  with a walk mode; **climbing skins** cut to the ski; adjustable poles.
- **Rescue kit, every person, every tour:** avalanche transceiver (beacon),
  probe, metal-blade shovel. Optional airbag pack. Also: map/app, first aid,
  repair kit, warm layer, headlamp. Snowshoers need the same rescue kit.
- **Beacon check at the trailhead:** every beacon in send, check group-wide
  (range and receive test), then everyone switches to send and keeps it worn
  under a layer, not in the pack.

## 2. Skinning technique
- Slide, don't lift: keep skis on the snow, short glide each step.
- Use **heel risers** to match slope; too high wastes energy and loses grip.
- Weight over the whole foot; leaning forward makes skins slip.
- Skins balling up with snow: wipe and apply skin wax; keep skins warm inside
  your jacket on cold transitions.

## 3. Setting an uptrack
- Aim for a consistent, moderate grade (people often quote ~15–20°; steep tracks
  exhaust followers and slip).
- Use terrain: ridges and low-angle trees, avoid crossing under steep slopes.
- **Kick turns** to change direction on steep sections: plant uphill pole,
  weight downhill ski, lift uphill ski and rotate it 180° to point the new way,
  shift weight, then bring the old ski around (tail clears by lifting the knee
  and letting the ski hang). Practise on gentle ground until smooth.
- Space out on suspect slopes: one at a time from safe spot to safe spot.

## 4. Avalanche go/no-go routine
1. **Before:** read the regional avalanche forecast — the **danger rating**
   (1 Low → 5 Very High, on the North American/European scale) by elevation band,
   **avalanche problems** (e.g. wind slab, persistent slab, wet snow, storm slab),
   their **aspects and elevations**, and recent activity. Most fatalities happen
   at Moderate (2) and Considerable (3), not only at High.
2. **Plan terrain to match:** most slab avalanches release on slopes ~30–45°;
   a route that keeps you below ~30° and off runouts reduces risk sharply.
   Measure with an app (slope-shading) or inclinometer.
3. **Terrain traps** raise consequences: gullies, creek beds, cliffs below,
   trees — even a small slide is deadly there.
4. **In the field — red flags:** recent avalanches, cracking (shooting cracks),
   "whumpfing" (collapsing), heavy new snow or wind loading, rapid warming. One
   red flag = choose simpler terrain.
5. **Group:** agree a turnaround time and the "anyone can say no" rule. Expose
   one person at a time; regroup in safe spots.

## 5. Companion rescue (outline)
Shout and watch the victim's last seen point → all switch to **search** →
signal, coarse, fine search → probe (perpendicular to slope) → **shovel
strategically** (dig from downhill, conveyor-belt). Speed matters: survival
drops fast after the first ~15 minutes. Practise with your group each season.

## 6. Transitions
Uphill → downhill: skins off (fold glue to glue), heel locked, boots in ski mode,
layers on, helmet on. Do it in a safe spot, not on the slope or under cornices.

## Verify
- [ ] Forecast read: rating, problems, aspects/elevations noted for your route.
- [ ] Route avoids slopes >30° and runouts when the forecast warrants it.
- [ ] Beacon group check done at the trailhead; all carry probe and shovel.
- [ ] You can kick turn both directions on a steep track.
- [ ] Turnaround time agreed before starting.

a certified avalanche course and local forecasts.*
