# Sliding sports — push starts, steering and track lines

**Goal:** understand how the three ice-track sports differ, what a newcomer
actually does on their first runs, and the principles that make a fast line.
Sliding is only done on certified tracks under coaching — there are around a
dozen-plus artificial tracks worldwide, and the way in is a federation or club
"try-out", a driving school, or a tourist taxi-bob run.

## 1. The three sports at a glance
| | Bobsleigh | Luge | Skeleton |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position | sitting inside a steered sled | on back, feet first | on front, head first |
| Crew | 1 (monobob), 2 or 4 | 1 or 2 (doubles) | 1 |
| Start | running push, then load | seated pull on handles, paddle with spiked gloves | running push, dive onto sled |
| Steering | pilot pulls rings attached to front runners | calf pressure on the runners' bows, shoulder and head shifts | shoulder/knee pressure, head, toe drags |
| Governing body | IBSF | FIL | IBSF |

## 2. Push starts
- **Bobsleigh:** athletes wear spiked shoes; the push is a 50 m-ish sprint with
  the sled. Start low and drive the sled, then build to an upright sprint.
  **Loading:** pilot first, then crew in order, brakeman last, with no loss of
  speed — practise on dry push tracks. Start times are a huge share of the
  final margin, which is why sprinters and throwers get recruited.
- **Skeleton:** a crouched sprint pushing the sled with one hand, then a smooth
  dive onto it without bouncing (bouncing scrubs runner speed).
- **Luge:** rock back and forth on start handles, explosive pull, then 2–3
  paddles with spiked gloves before lying back.

## 3. Steering — small inputs
- Inputs are tiny and early. Oversteering causes **skids** (the runners slide
  sideways) which cost far more than a slightly wide line.
- **Bob:** the pilot pulls the left or right ring to turn the front runners.
- **Skeleton:** shift shoulder pressure or press a knee into the sled; a toe
  drag is a correction of last resort.
- **Luge:** press the runner bow with a calf, plus subtle shoulder pressure.
- Head position matters in luge and skeleton: lift it and you add drag; the
  neck carries big G-loads (up to roughly 4–5 g in some curves).

## 4. Track walk and line choice
1. **Walk the track** before sliding: note every curve's entry, apex and exit,
   the transitions (where the ice changes from straight to banked) and any
   **labyrinth** (quick left-right sequences), **omega** or **kreisel** (360°
   curve) sections.
2. **Principle:** enter curves at the right height so the sled rises naturally
   and **exits low and straight** — exiting high and dropping late bangs into
   the wall and slows you.
3. Line choice in one curve sets up the next. Coaches give "drive points" — where
   to make the input.
4. Video and timing splits between sections show where time is lost.

## 5. Sled setup (overview)
Runner choice and polishing, runner temperature (rules control how warm they
may be), weight limits with ballast, and steering tension are all regulated by
IBSF/FIL rules. A newcomer uses club sleds; setup is the coach's and mechanic's job.

## 6. Safety
- Helmets with chin guard (skeleton), visors, spiked shoes; padding for bob crew.
- Crashes are usually slides down the track rather than impacts, but injuries,
  burns from ice friction and concussion/head-impact load (an active research
  concern in skeleton and bob) are real. Follow federation rules on run counts.
- Never run alone or on an uncertified track.
