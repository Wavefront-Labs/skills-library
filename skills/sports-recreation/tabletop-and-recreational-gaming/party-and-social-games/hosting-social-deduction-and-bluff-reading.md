# Party and social games — hosting, social deduction and bluff reading

**Goal:** keep a room of mixed people laughing and engaged: pick games
that fit, moderate hidden-role games cleanly, and play the bluffing side
well without making it personal.

## 1. Choosing for the room
- **Count and mood first.** 4-6 people: Codenames, Wavelength, Just
  One-style games. 7-12+: hidden-role games (Werewolf/Mafia, The
  Resistance/Avalon), team charades or Pictionary.
- **Low rules overhead:** the teach should be under 5 minutes.
- **Avoid player elimination** for casual groups — a player out in minute
  5 of a 40-minute Werewolf game is bored. Prefer games where everyone
  plays to the end (The Resistance, One Night-style variants) or keep
  rounds short.
- Read the group: competitive friends vs strangers vs family — strangers
  do better with cooperative or team word games before bluffing games.
- Start with an easy, quick game as an icebreaker.

## 2. Moderating hidden-role games (Werewolf/Mafia style)
- **Prepare roles in advance:** count the deck, check balance (rough
  guide in classic Werewolf: about one wolf per 3-4 players; check the
  specific edition's suggested setups).
- **Night phase script:** say the same words in the same tone every time,
  and have everyone tap the table or hum during night so movement is
  masked.
- **Keep a written log** of night actions — memory fails by round 3.
- Set a **timer for day discussions** (e.g. 3-5 minutes) to keep pace.
- Eliminated players: give them a job (watch, help moderate in apps-free
  versions) or keep rounds short.
- Be neutral in voice and face; never hint.
- Apps can replace the moderator for some games (with the trade-off of
  less atmosphere).

## 3. Playing: bluffing well
- **Have a consistent story** and stick to it; changing claims under
  pressure is the biggest tell.
- **Behave the same as when you're innocent** — if you're usually loud,
  stay loud.
- Accuse occasionally and reasonably; total silence attracts suspicion.
- As the "evil" team, **support a plausible wrong accusation** rather than
  inventing your own.
- Use the game's information (votes, missions failed) more than gut reads.

## 4. Reading others
- **Track voting patterns** and who pushed for which elimination — the
  record is more reliable than "they seemed nervous".
- Look for **inconsistency** with their own past statements, not
  nervousness (nervous people are often innocent).
- Note who benefits from the current direction of discussion.
- Know your friends' baselines; a tell is a *change* from baseline.

## 5. Keeping the room fun
- **Pacing:** end a game while people still want more; switch types
  (word → bluff → drawing) every 30-45 minutes.
- **Inclusivity:** pair shy or new players on teams; in drawing/acting
  games, let people pass; keep in-jokes from excluding newcomers.
- **Emotional heat:** lying games can sting; say "great bluff" at the end,
  and drop a game if someone is clearly upset.
- Rotate who explains and hosts.

## Gotchas
- Moderator forgets who was saved/killed → keep the log.
- A first-timer gets eliminated first and sits out the whole game.
- Treating "acting nervous" as evidence.

## Verify
- [ ] The game fit the head count and the teach was under 5 minutes.
- [ ] Night phases were scripted, masked and logged.
- [ ] Nobody sat out more than a few minutes.
- [ ] Each game ended with a reveal and a friendly debrief.
