# Tabletop RPGs — game mastering, session prep and improvisation

**Goal:** run (or play in) a role-playing game where everyone gets to
matter, sessions have momentum, and you don't burn out on prep. System-
neutral; examples from D&D 5e, Pathfinder and Call of Cthulhu.

## 1. Session zero (before the campaign)
- Agree **tone and genre** (heroic, grim, comedic), frequency, length,
  attendance expectations, what happens when someone misses a session.
- **Safety tools:** *lines* (content that won't appear at all) and
  *veils* (content that happens off-screen); an **X-card** or equivalent
  signal to skip content mid-session without explanation. Check in
  privately with players who are quiet about it.
- Make characters together so they have a reason to be a group; give
  each a tie to the setting or another character.
- Table rules: phones, rules lookups (rule now, check later), dice
  rolled in the open or not, splitting the party.

## 2. Lean session prep (about an hour, not ten)
A popular checklist (popularised as "lazy DM" prep) that works:
1. **Review the characters** — what does each player want right now?
2. **A strong start** — open in the middle of something.
3. **Possible scenes** — 3-5 situations that could happen, not a script.
4. **Secrets and clues** — ~10 one-line facts the players could discover
   anywhere; drop them wherever they fit.
5. **Locations** — a few evocative details each.
6. **NPCs** — name, want, one memorable trait.
7. **Monsters/opposition** for likely fights.
8. **Rewards** relevant to the characters.
Prep situations, not plots: the players will not take the path you
imagine.

## 3. Improvisation at the table
- **"Yes, and" / "yes, but" / "no, but"** — rarely flat "no". Let the
  players' ideas work at a cost.
- **Ask questions back:** "What does your character know about this
  cult?" — players fill in world detail you don't need to invent.
- Keep an **NPC name list** handy; write down names you invent on the fly.
- **Fail forward:** a failed roll creates a complication, not a dead end
  (the lock picks open but the guard hears it).
- Call for a roll only when failure is interesting and success isn't
  certain.

## 4. Pacing and spotlight
- **Spotlight rotation:** after a scene, check who hasn't had a moment;
  frame the next scene around them.
- Cut scenes that have stopped producing decisions ("You spend the
  evening haggling — you get the rope at a fair price. The next morning…").
- **Combat:** keep turns short (players plan on others' turns), describe
  hits vividly, have monsters flee or surrender when the fight is decided.
- **Encounter balance** formulas (like 5e's XP budget) are rough; watch
  resources (hit points, spell slots) rather than trusting the maths.
- End on a hook or cliffhanger and a 2-minute recap request next time
  ("someone remind us what happened?").

## 5. Online play
Virtual tabletops (Roll20, Foundry VTT, Owlbear Rodeo etc.) plus voice:
use push-to-talk etiquette, prepared maps/tokens, and keep handouts
simple. Lag and talk-over make spotlight management more important.

## 6. Good player craft
- Give your character a want and a flaw that create drama.
- Build on other players' moments; don't hog the spotlight.
- Know your character's abilities so your turn is quick.

## Gotchas
- Over-prepping a plot the players ignore → frustration and railroading.
- A single dominant player — fix it with spotlight rotation and a quiet
  word, not in front of the group.
- Skipping session zero: most mid-campaign conflict comes from mismatched
  expectations.

## Verify
- [ ] Session zero covered tone, schedule and safety tools (lines/veils, X-card).
- [ ] Prep fits on one page of situations, secrets, NPCs and locations.
- [ ] Every player had at least one spotlight moment this session.
- [ ] Failed rolls produced complications, not dead ends.
