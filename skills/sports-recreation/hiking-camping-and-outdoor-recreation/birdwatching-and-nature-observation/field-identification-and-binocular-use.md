# Field identification and binocular use

**Goal:** get a usable view of a bird quickly, notice the features that actually separate
species, and record what you saw in a way that is useful to you and to others.

## 1. Binoculars: choose and set up
- **8x42** is the all-round choice: bright, wide field, steady. **10x42** shows more
  detail but shakes more and is harder to aim at moving birds. Compacts (8x25) are
  dimmer in poor light. The **exit pupil** = objective ÷ magnification (42 ÷ 8 ≈ 5 mm);
  bigger is brighter at dawn and dusk.
- **Set the dioptre once:** close the right eye (or cover the right lens), focus the
  centre wheel on a sharp target with the left eye; then close the left eye and turn
  only the **dioptre ring** (usually on the right eyepiece) until sharp. Note the setting.
- Glasses wearers: twist the eyecups **down**; others twist them up.
- Adjust the hinge so you see one round circle, not two.

## 2. Getting on the bird
Beginners' most common problem is not finding the bird in the binoculars.
**Keep your eyes on the bird**, then bring the binoculars up to your eyes without
looking away. Pre-focus on something at about the same distance. To tell others
where it is, use a clock face on a big feature: "lone pine, three o'clock, halfway up".

## 3. What to look at — in this order
Colour is the least reliable feature (light changes it). Go through:
1. **Size** — relative to a bird you know (sparrow, robin, pigeon, crow).
2. **Shape** — bill shape and length, tail length, legs, wing shape in flight.
3. **Behaviour** — creeping up trunks, flicking tail, hovering, wading, flock flight style.
4. **Habitat and season** — what's expected here now? A field guide range map rules
   out many candidates.
5. **Field marks** — wing bars, eye-ring, eyebrow stripe (supercilium), rump patch,
   tail pattern, leg colour.
This overall impression (size, shape, movement) is what birders call **GISS** or "jizz";
experienced birders identify most birds from it before seeing any detail.
**Write or voice-record notes before opening the book** — memory reshapes itself to
match the first picture you look at.

## 4. Birding by ear
More birds are heard than seen, especially in woodland and early morning. Learn your
20 commonest local species' songs first. Describe: rhythm, pitch (rising/falling),
repetition, quality (buzzy, whistled, harsh). Sound-ID apps (e.g. Merlin) are a big
help but **do make mistakes**, especially with mimics, distant birds and noisy sites;
treat a suggestion as a lead to confirm by sight or a recording, not a record.

## 5. Field guides, apps and lists
- Use a guide for your region (e.g. Sibley for North America, Collins for Europe);
  illustrated guides show diagnostic features more clearly than photos.
- Keep a **life list** and, more usefully, **complete checklists** of every species seen
  at a site and time. Submitting complete checklists to a citizen-science database such
  as eBird makes your records count for science.
- Count, don't guess "lots" — estimate in blocks of 10 for flocks.

## 6. Ethics
The bird's welfare comes first: keep back from nests and roosts, don't flush birds
for a photo, stay on paths. **Limit playback** of recorded calls, and don't use it for
rare or breeding birds or where it's banned (many reserves). Keep the locations of
sensitive rarities private as asked by local reporting rules.

## Gotchas
- Juveniles, females, moulting birds and odd light all mislead; size and shape don't.
- Rarities are rare: check the commonest look-alike first.
- Cold hands and fogged lenses: keep binoculars inside your jacket in winter.

## Verify
- [ ] Dioptre set; you see one sharp circle with both eyes.
- [ ] You can get a bird in view on first lift most of the time.
- [ ] Your notes list size, shape, behaviour and habitat, not only colours.
- [ ] App IDs were confirmed by sight or recording before listing.
- [ ] Checklist recorded with counts; no disturbance at nests or roosts.
