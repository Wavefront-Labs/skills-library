# Climbing, descending and vert training for trail running

**Goal:** move uphill economically, come down fast without trashing your quads or
falling, and build the specific fitness that road mileage doesn't give you.

## 1. Uphill: run or hike?
- **Power-hike when hiking is as fast as running** — on most runners that happens
  somewhere around 15–20% gradient, sooner late in a long race (uncertain: varies a lot
  by runner; test it on your local hills). Hiking at the same speed costs less energy.
- Running uphill: short quick steps, upright posture leaning from the ankles, eyes
  a few metres ahead, arms driving.
- Hiking steep ground: **hands-on-knees** (push down on the thigh each step) or poles;
  full foot contact to save the calves.
- Effort cap: in anything longer than ~2 hours, climb at an effort where you could
  still talk. The race is decided on the second half.

## 2. Downhill: where time and injuries happen
- **Quick light feet, high cadence.** Many small steps beat few long braking strides.
- Lean slightly **forward with the slope**, not back — leaning back puts weight on the
  heels and causes slips. Bend the knees, arms wide for balance.
- **Eyes 3–5 steps ahead**, choosing the line; your feet handle the ground you're
  already committed to.
- On technical ground, aim for flat rock tops and stable spots, avoid wet roots and
  sloping slabs, and accept "dancing" side-steps across the slope.
- Descending is a skill: practise it in training at moderate effort, not only in races.

## 3. Poles
Useful on long, steep climbs and in races over roughly 50 km with big vert; mostly
dead weight on runnable courses. Plant them **beside or slightly behind your feet**
and push, don't reach. Practise stowing and deploying them on the move. **Check race
rules** — some races ban poles, or ban picking them up mid-race.

## 4. Building vert
- Track **weekly vertical gain** alongside distance. Target courses are often described
  in metres of climb per kilometre: about **50 m/km+** is hilly; **100 m/km** is mountain.
- Increase weekly vert gradually (a common guide is around 10% per week, with a
  down week every 3–4 weeks).
- **Hill repeats:** e.g. 6–10 × 2–4 minutes uphill at hard effort, walk/jog down.
- **Long climbs** at easy effort, matched to race gradient. No mountains nearby:
  treadmill at 10–15% incline, stairs, or repeats on a short hill.
- **Train the descents too** — downhill running is eccentric loading on the quads, which
  is what makes them sore and weak late in a race. Regular moderate descending, plus
  squats and lunges, gives protection (the "repeated bout effect").

## 5. Race fuelling and kit
- Start eating early: roughly **40–60 g carbohydrate per hour** for most runners in
  races over ~2 hours; trained guts tolerate more (often 60–90 g). Practise it in long
  training runs. Drink to thirst, add electrolytes in heat.
- Read the **mandatory kit** list (waterproof jacket with taped seams, headlamp,
  survival blanket, phone are typical for mountain ultras); kit checks can be enforced
  at the start or mid-race.

## Gotchas
- Flat-ground pace and heart-rate zones don't translate to climbs; use effort or
  heart rate, not pace.
- Shoes: aggressive lugs grip in mud but feel unstable on rock; pick for the course.
- Most falls happen late, when you're tired and stop lifting your feet — slow down on
  technical descents in the last hour.

## Verify
- [ ] You know your run/hike switch-over gradient from testing on a real hill.
- [ ] You can descend a familiar technical trail at moderate effort with no hard braking.
- [ ] Weekly vert logged and increasing gradually toward the race's m/km.
- [ ] Race fuelling plan (g carb/hour) tested on at least two long runs.
- [ ] Mandatory kit and pole rules checked against the race's current regulations.
