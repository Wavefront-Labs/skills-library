# Base weight and resupply planning

**Goal:** carry a pack light enough that the walking stays enjoyable, and never be
more than one bad day from running out of food or water.

## 1. Measure, don't guess
Weigh every item on a kitchen scale and put it in a spreadsheet (or a gear-list site
such as LighterPack). Three numbers matter:
- **Base weight** — everything except consumables (food, water, fuel) and what you wear.
- **Consumables** — varies along the trail; peaks just after resupply.
- **Skin-out weight** — total, including worn clothing.
Rough bands used in the community: traditional > 9 kg (20 lb), lightweight under
~9 kg, ultralight under ~4.5 kg (10 lb). These are conventions, not rules.

## 2. Cut weight in the right order
1. **The big three** — pack, shelter, sleep system (bag/quilt + pad). They usually
   make up half the base weight; this is where kilograms, not grams, come off.
2. **Things you never used.** After each trip, sort gear into used / used for
   emergency / never used. Drop the third pile unless it is safety kit (first aid,
   rain shell, headlamp, navigation, insulation).
3. **Duplicates and luxuries** — one pot, one knife, one spare pair of socks.
Order matters: buy the light pack **last**, because frameless ultralight packs only
carry comfortably once the rest of the load is already light (often cited as under
~11–13 kg total; check the manufacturer's rated load).

## 3. Size the food carry
- Plan **0.7–1 kg of food per person per day** (about 1.5–2 lb); thru-hikers past the
  first few weeks often need the top of that range ("hiker hunger").
- Aim for **calorie density ≥ ~4.5 kcal/g (~125 kcal/oz)**: nuts, nut butter, olive
  oil, chocolate, dried meats, instant mashed potato, tortillas. Fresh fruit and
  canned food are poor value.
- Food carry = days to next resupply × daily weight, **+ one spare day**.
- Water: carry only to the next reliable source plus margin; check a current
  water report where one exists — seasonal sources dry up.

## 4. Choose a resupply style for each stop
| Style | Use when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Buy in town** | Town has a real grocery store | Small stores = gas-station food, high prices |
| **Mail drop** to post office / hostel | Remote stops, special diets | Post office hours (closed weekends), hold limits; you are locked into food you packed months ago |
| **Bounce box** (mailed ahead, re-mailed) | Rarely-needed items: chargers, spare shoes | Postage adds up; plan forwarding |
Most thru-hikers do mostly town buys with a handful of mail drops where the trail
guide says shopping is poor. Plan stops from a current trail guide or app; stores
and hostels close, so confirm close to the date (uncertain until checked each season).

## 5. Plan the schedule with slack
Build an itinerary of daily distances, then add **zero days** (no miles) and **neros**
(near-zero) every 1–2 weeks. Early days should be short: most thru-hike injuries are
overuse injuries from starting too fast. Permits, seasonal windows (snow, heat,
hurricane season) and border/quota rules often fix your start date — check them first.

## Gotchas
- Lighter gear is often less durable; carry a repair kit (tape, needle, patch).
- "Ultralight" is not a reason to drop warm layers or the rain shell above treeline.
- Food weight dominates the first day out of town; time hard climbs for later.
- Mail drops need a name and estimated arrival on the package; follow the receiving
  business's exact address format.
