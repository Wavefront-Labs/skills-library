# Campsite selection and leave no trace

**Goal:** a night that is safe, dry and reasonably warm, and a site that the next
person cannot tell you used.

## 1. Arrive with time to choose
Aim to stop **at least an hour before dark**. Picking a site by headlamp is how people
end up in a drainage line or under a dead branch. Check the rules first: many parks
require designated sites or permits; wild camping is legal in some countries and
regions and banned in others (uncertain for any given place — check the land manager).

## 2. Read the site: four checks
1. **Look up — widowmakers.** Dead trees and hanging dead branches fall without wind.
   Do not pitch within falling distance of them.
2. **Look down — water.** Avoid dips, dry stream beds, and flat spots at the foot of
   slopes: rain collects there overnight. In canyon or desert country, never camp in a
   wash — flash floods come from storms you can't see.
3. **Feel the air — wind and cold.** Valley floors and hollows collect cold air on
   clear nights (often several degrees colder than a bench 20–30 m higher). Exposed
   ridges and saddles take the full wind. A gently raised spot with some tree or
   boulder shelter on the windward side is the sweet spot.
4. **Lie on it — the ground.** Lie down on the spot before pitching: find the slope,
   roots and stones now, not at 2 a.m. Sleep head-uphill.

## 3. Pitch for weather
- Put the **narrow, low end of the tent into the wind**; stake the windward corners first.
- Tension guylines; a taut fly sheds rain and flaps less.
- **Condensation** is worst near water, in still air, and on cold clear nights. Pitching
  under tree cover and away from lakes, and opening the vents, helps more than any fabric.
  Wipe the inner with a cloth in the morning rather than packing it wet.

## 4. Leave No Trace — the working version
The seven LNT principles (plan ahead; travel and camp on durable surfaces; dispose of
waste properly; leave what you find; minimise campfire impacts; respect wildlife; be
considerate of others) in practice:
- **Durable surfaces:** established sites, rock, gravel, dry grass. In pristine areas
  spread out and move on after one night; in popular areas concentrate use on the
  existing bare site — don't make a new one.
- **Distance from water:** camp and wash **about 60 m (200 ft, ~70 adult steps)** from
  lakes and streams. Wash dishes with a little unscented soap and scatter the strained
  greywater on land, not in the stream.
- **Human waste:** a **cathole 15–20 cm (6–8 in) deep**, 60 m from water, trails and
  camp; fill and disguise afterwards. Pack out toilet paper where required (deserts,
  alpine zones and many popular parks) and **always pack out wipes and sanitary products**.
- **Pack it out:** all rubbish, including food scraps and fruit peel.
- **Fires:** use a stove where possible. If fires are allowed, use an existing ring,
  dead and down wood thinner than your wrist, burn to white ash, drown and stir until
  cold to the touch. Check fire bans every trip.

## 5. Food and wildlife
Never keep food, rubbish or toiletries in the tent. Use what the area requires: bear
canister, bear locker, or a hang (rules and recommended methods vary by region —
follow the local land manager's guidance). Cook and store food well downwind of where
you sleep in bear country.

## Gotchas
- A beautiful lakeshore site is usually the coldest, wettest and most damaging choice.
- Ground that is "flat enough" in daylight never is; always lie-test.
- Stakes don't hold in sand or snow — bury a stuff sack or stick as a deadman anchor.

## Verify
- [ ] Rules/permits checked; stopped with an hour of daylight left.
- [ ] No dead wood overhead, not in a drainage or wash, lie-tested.
- [ ] Tent ~60 m from water; door/vents set for the wind.
- [ ] Food and scented items stored per local rules, not in the tent.
- [ ] On leaving: no litter, no new fire ring, cathole covered, site looks untouched.
