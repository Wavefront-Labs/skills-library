# Firecraft: from tinder to sustained fire

**Goal:** get a reliable fire from one spark, in damp conditions, without accelerants —
and leave no scar when you go. Fires are banned in many places and seasons; check the
land manager's current rules before every trip. Knots and lashings are covered
separately in handcrafts.knots-lashing-cordage.lashing.square-diagonal-and-shear-lashings.

## 1. Collect everything before you strike
Most failed fires starve in the first two minutes because the next size of fuel isn't
ready. Gather **at least double** what you think, sorted into piles:
| Grade | Size | Amount | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tinder | hair-fine | two good handfuls | birch bark (burns even damp, thanks to its oils), dry grass, cattail fluff, pine resin wood (fatwood), feather-stick curls |
| Kindling 1 | matchstick | a large double handful | dead twigs that **snap** cleanly |
| Kindling 2 | pencil | same again | |
| Kindling 3 | thumb | same again | |
| Fuel | wrist and up | an armful | split dead wood |
**The snap test:** dead dry wood snaps with a crack; if it bends, it's wet or green.

## 2. Find dry wood in wet weather
- Take **dead standing** wood and dead lower branches still on trees — not wood lying
  on the ground, which soaks up water.
- Look under dense conifers and rock overhangs.
- **Split it.** The inside of a wet wrist-thick dead branch is usually dry. Splitting
  with a knife and a striking stick (batoning) exposes dry faces; use a full-tang
  knife and keep the wood upright on a solid base.
- **Feather sticks:** shave long thin curls down a dry split stick, leaving them
  attached in a bunch at the bottom. Thinner curls catch first; aim for a dozen.

## 3. Ferro rod technique
- Hold the striker still and **pull the rod back** toward you, with the rod tip resting
  in the tinder. Pushing the striker forward scatters the tinder and the lay.
- Use the **spine** of a knife (if it has a sharp 90° edge) or the supplied striker;
  firm pressure, slow-ish full-length stroke.
- New rods have a protective coating — scrape it off before your first trip, not in the rain.
- Fluff tinder until it's mostly air; a dense ball won't take a spark. A cotton pad
  smeared with petroleum jelly, pulled open, is a reliable backup to carry.

## 4. Build the lay and feed it
1. Platform: on wet ground or snow, lay a bed of wrist-thick sticks first.
2. Tinder bundle in the middle, a loose **teepee** of matchstick kindling over it,
   leaving a gap on the windward side for the spark and air.
3. Light, then **wait** until the flame climbs into the first kindling before adding
   pencil-size, then thumb-size. Adding too much too soon smothers it.
4. Once it's burning, a **log-cabin** around the teepee gives a stable bed of coals for cooking.
Fire needs heat, fuel **and air**: if it smokes heavily and dies, it's usually choked,
not under-fuelled. Lift a stick and blow gently at the base.

## 5. Put it out — cold
Let it burn down to ash, then **drown, stir, drown again** until you can hold your hand
on the ashes. Scatter cold ash, return unburnt wood, and dismantle any ring you built.
Never leave a fire unattended, and never build one on peat, roots or duff — fire can
creep underground and resurface later.

## Gotchas
- Birch bark: peel only from dead or fallen birches; stripping living trees damages them.
- Fatwood and resin smoke heavily — great starters, poor cooking fuel.
- Batoning with a folding knife can break the lock; don't.

## Verify
- [ ] Fire permitted today, site chosen away from overhanging branches and roots.
- [ ] All grades (tinder → wrist) collected and sorted before striking.
- [ ] Fire lit with one or two ferro rod strikes, no accelerant.
- [ ] Fed in order without smothering; sustained for 20+ minutes.
- [ ] Extinguished until cold to the touch, site restored.
