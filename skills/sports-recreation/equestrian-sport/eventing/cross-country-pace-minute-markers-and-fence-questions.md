# Eventing: cross-country pace, minute markers and fence questions

**Goal:** get round cross-country clear, inside the time, and safely. Eventing's three
phases (dressage, cross-country, show jumping) share one horse; cross-country is where
fitness, pace judgement and bravery meet.

## 1. Optimum time and pace
- Each level has a set speed in **metres per minute (mpm)** — e.g. lower national
  levels roughly 435–475 mpm, rising to ~570 mpm at top level (verify for your
  federation and class).
- **Optimum time = course length ÷ speed.** Example: 2,600 m at 520 mpm = 5 min 0 s.
- **Time faults** for exceeding optimum time (commonly 0.4 penalties per second over in
  FEI rules); some national levels also penalise going **too fast** (speed faults /
  minimum time) — check.

## 2. Minute markers
On the course walk, mark where you should be at each minute:
- Divide the course by the speed: at 520 mpm, minute 1 is ~520 m from the start, etc.
- Pick a visible landmark near each point (a fence number, a tree).
- Ride with a stopwatch counting up (or down); check at each marker — a few seconds
  up or down is fine; adjust gradually, not by galloping wildly.
- Time is saved by **efficient lines and a steady gallop** between fences, not sprinting:
  no wasted turns, landing and moving on, minimal set-up for straightforward fences.

## 3. Walking the course
Walk it at least twice; the second time ride it in your head: your line, where to
balance ("set up"), approach speed, and where the alternative routes are. Look at
ground conditions and footing, the light (jumping from bright into dark woods), and
where spectators may be.

## 4. Fence questions and how to ride them
- **Skinnies** (narrow fronts): straight approach, steady, hands and legs channelling;
  the run-out is the risk — look past the fence.
- **Corners:** jump across the narrow end on a line bisecting the angle, close to the
  flag; aim lower-level horses nearer the narrow end but not too close.
- **Water:** steady, positive trot or bouncy canter in; the water's drag slows the horse —
  sit up, slip the reins, let the horse balance. Fence in/out of water adds difficulty.
- **Ditches and trakehners:** ride forward, eyes up, don't look in the ditch.
- **Coffins** (fence–ditch–fence): short, bouncy, powerful canter, keep the leg on.
- **Drops and banks:** trot or short canter; lean back slightly, slip the reins for the
  horse to use its neck, keep the leg on.
- **Alternatives:** longer routes are slower but safer — have a plan B decided on the walk.

## 5. Penalties (summarised; check current rules)
Refusal/run-out/circle at a numbered fence: 20 penalties; repeated refusals lead to
elimination (thresholds vary); activation of a **frangible/deformable device**: 11
penalties under FEI rules (national rules vary); fall of rider = elimination.

## 6. Kit and safety
- **Body protector** to the current standard (mandatory), **hat** to a current
  approved standard with harness fastened, often an **air vest** (permitted/encouraged,
  check rules), **medical armband/card** with your medical info.
- Horse: boots, studs suited to the ground, breastplate, fitted tack.
- Fitness: the horse must be fit for the distance — interval training and hill work
  over weeks; a tired horse makes mistakes late in the course.
- Retire rather than push on with a tired or uncertain horse.

## Common faults
Chasing the time early, riding all fences at the same pace, looking down in water or
ditches, not knowing alternatives, galloping into skinnies.

## Verify
- [ ] Optimum time calculated and minute markers chosen from the course plan.
- [ ] Each fence has a planned line, approach pace and plan B.
- [ ] Body protector, hat and medical armband checked before the start.
- [ ] Post-round: stopwatch splits compared with markers to refine pace judgement.
