# Endurance riding: pacing, vet gates and heart-rate recovery

**Goal:** complete long-distance rides with a sound, well-hydrated horse — "to finish is to
win" — and lose minimal time at vet gates. Horse welfare outranks placing; the vets
can and will eliminate a horse that isn't fit to continue.

## 1. How rides work (summarised; check your organisation)
- Distances from novice ~20–40 km up to 160 km (100 miles) in one day, split into
  **loops** each ending at a **vet gate**.
- At the gate, the horse must reach the **pulse criterion** (commonly **64 bpm** under
  FEI and many national rules; some use 60 or 68 — check) within a time limit, then pass
  a vet inspection (heart rate, gut sounds, mucous membranes, capillary refill, skin tent,
  muscle tone, back/girth soreness, tack galls, and **lameness at the trot-out**).
- Time stops when you present (in "gate into hold" systems); then a mandatory **hold**
  (rest) period before the next loop.
- Final vet inspection after the finish; failure = no completion.

## 2. Pacing
- Use a **heart-rate monitor** on the horse. Work mostly at an aerobic level (many riders
  keep cruising HR roughly in the 120s–150s bpm depending on the horse — individual
  horses differ; learn your horse's numbers in training).
- Slow down on hills and difficult footing; trot the flats and good ground; walk the
  steep downhills (leading on foot is common and kind).
- Last 1–2 km into the gate: walk or slow-trot so the HR is already falling.
- The horse's fitness from months of **long slow distance** conditioning is the base;
  don't attempt a distance beyond what training supports.

## 3. Fast recovery at the vet gate
- **Cooling:** in warm weather, sponge or pour water on the large vessels (neck, chest,
  between hind legs) and scrape off immediately so it doesn't trap heat; keep moving
  gently in shade. In cold weather, rug the back and loins.
- **Untack** quickly (or loosen girth) as rules allow.
- **Check HR** with a stethoscope or monitor; present as soon as you're below criterion
  — every minute waiting counts.
- **Cardiac recovery index (CRI):** at the vet check the HR is taken, the horse trots out
  ~40 m and back, and HR re-taken one minute after the start; a rise suggests fatigue
  or a problem.
- **Trot-out practice** at home: the horse trots straight and willingly in hand on a loose
  lead — a bad trot-out can look like lameness.

## 4. Hydration and electrolytes
- Offer water at every opportunity on the trail and at the gate; train the horse to drink
  on the trail.
- **Electrolytes** (sodium, potassium, chloride) given in sweat-loss conditions, with
  water/feed — dosing per vet advice; never give large doses to a horse that isn't drinking.
- Feed hay and soaked feed at holds to keep the gut moving (gut sounds are checked).

## 5. Crewing
Crew prepares water, sponges, scrapers, feed, rug, spare tack, HR monitor and the rider's
food at each gate; knows the timing and the vet routine. A well-drilled crew saves
minutes at every gate.

## 6. Tack and kit
Well-fitting saddle (long hours — check for pressure and rubs), breastplate, boots or
shoes that suit the terrain, hoof boots spare, GPS track of the course, helmet.

## Common faults
Racing early loops; arriving hot at the gate; poor scraping; no drinking practice in
training; untested electrolytes; skipping the trot-out rehearsal.

## Verify
- [ ] Training logs show conditioning distances approaching the ride distance.
- [ ] Your horse's working HR range and recovery time known from training.
- [ ] Vet-gate routine rehearsed with crew: cool, HR check, present.
- [ ] Trot-out practised in hand.
- [ ] Pulse criterion and hold times for the specific ride confirmed.
