# Show jumping: course walking, related distances and the jump-off

**Goal:** know before you mount how many strides every line rides, keep the canter
that makes those distances work, and plan a jump-off that saves time safely.

## 1. The canter is everything
Most distances are built for a **12 ft (≈3.6–3.7 m) canter stride** for horses (shorter
for ponies). Good jumping comes from a consistent, active, balanced canter: if the
rhythm stays the same, the distances "come". Chasing a stride (sudden acceleration) or
picking (pulling to fit in a short one) causes most faults.

## 2. Walking the course
1. Walk the entire track you'll ride, including turns, from the start line to the finish.
2. At every fence: note type (vertical, oxer, triple bar, water tray, wall), height
   and spread, direction of the course (fillers, light vs dark, spooky items).
3. **Related distances:** stride the line in your own measured strides. A common
   rule of thumb: count in human strides of ~3 ft (~0.9 m) — 4 human strides ≈ 1 horse
   canter stride — and allow about 2 human strides (≈6 ft) for **landing** and 2 for
   **take-off**. E.g. a line of 48 ft (≈14.6 m) is 6 + 36 + 6 → 3 strides.
   Calibrate your walking stride against a tape measure at home.
4. **Combinations** (doubles, trebles) measured A to B: typical one-stride ~24–26 ft,
   two-stride ~35–36 ft at moderate heights (course designers vary them on purpose).
   Decide if it rides **short** or **long** for your horse and whether you'll need to
   ride forward or steady.
5. Walk turns as you'll ride them; find the straight approach line for each fence —
   ideally at least a few strides straight in front.
6. Decide where to land on the correct lead or change.

## 3. Meeting the fence
- **Two-point / light seat** on the approach for many riders, or sitting in for
  balance; eyes up to the next fence, not down.
- Follow with the hands over the fence (release), stay in balance, land and look
  for the next line.
- Don't "fiddle" the last few strides — keep the rhythm and let the horse jump.
- Gridwork (fences at set distances) at home trains the horse's technique and your eye.

## 4. Faults (summarised — check your rules table)
Under common FEI-style tables: knockdown **4 faults**; first refusal/run-out **4 faults**;
second refusal **elimination** (in recent FEI rules — national bodies vary);
**time faults** for exceeding the time allowed (often 1 fault per second or part, or
per 4 seconds depending on table); fall of rider or horse = elimination. Different
competition tables (A, C, speed, accumulator) score differently.

## 5. The jump-off
- Walk the jump-off track in the first walk (it's usually posted on the course plan).
- Time is saved in **turns and lines**, not by galloping: inside turns, jumping fences at
  an angle (if the horse is trained for it), landing on the correct lead, cutting
  strides out of lines only when the horse is balanced enough.
- Know the clear-round time to beat when you go; early in the order, a safe clear can
  put pressure on others.
- A rail costs more than a few hundredths: don't take turns your horse can't manage.

## 6. Safety
Approved hat (current standard) and body protector (often mandatory for juniors/some
classes); check studs and boots; walk the warm-up rules (flag direction —
red on the right, white on the left).

## Common faults
Changing pace in the last strides, looking down at the fence, not planning the
landing lead, walking the course loosely, overcooking jump-off turns.

## Verify
- [ ] Every related line has a planned stride count and short/long note.
- [ ] Combination distances checked and approach pace decided.
- [ ] Jump-off track walked with turns marked.
- [ ] Practice rounds at home show the same canter rhythm through each line.
