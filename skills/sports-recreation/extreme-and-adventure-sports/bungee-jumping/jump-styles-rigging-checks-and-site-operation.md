# Bungee jumping — jump styles, rigging checks and site operation

**Goal:** for jumpers, a well-executed, comfortable jump; for site staff, a
repeatable operation where every jump is weighed, rigged, double-checked
and recovered the same way. Commercial bungee is safe when the system is
followed and dangerous when a step is skipped. **Operator content is
general (draft); follow your site's code of practice and local standard —
e.g. Australia/New Zealand AS/NZS 5848 — and manufacturer instructions.**

## Part A — Jumping

### Before the day
- Common **contraindications**: heart conditions, high blood pressure,
  recent surgery, back/neck problems, pregnancy, epilepsy, eye
  conditions (e.g. detached retina history), and weight outside the
  site's limits. Be honest on the medical form.
- Remove loose items (glasses unless strapped, phone, jewellery).

### Styles
- **Swan dive (forward):** arms out, look at the horizon, dive away from
  the platform. Smoothest deceleration and best experience.
- **Backward / "elevator":** fall backward; more disorienting.
- Ankle attachment gives a classic dive; body (waist/chest) harness is
  used for some styles, tandem and lower-confidence jumpers.

### At the edge
- Listen to the jump master's count; jump **out**, not down. Hesitation
  causes awkward falls into the cord.
- During rebounds: relax, keep arms out; at the bottom, follow recovery
  instructions (reaching a pole, being lowered to a boat, or hauled up).

## Part B — Operating a jump

### 1. Weighing and cord selection
- **Weigh every jumper on the day** and write the weight visibly
  (often on the hand) — never accept a stated weight.
- Select the cord or setting rated for that weight band and the jump
  height/clearance. Track **cord usage counts**; cords are retired at the
  manufacturer's/site's limit.
- Water-touch jumps need precise cord length calculation and conditions.

### 2. Harness and attachment
- Ankle harnesses fitted snugly with padding; plus a **back-up body
  harness** connection on many systems (redundancy).
- Every attachment uses locking hardware; screw gates fully closed.
- **Double-check principle:** a second staff member physically checks
  every connection and calls it out before the jumper moves to the edge.
  Use a spoken checklist ("ankles tight, harness done, cord attached,
  locked, weight 72, blue cord").

### 3. Jumper management
- Clear briefing: how to jump, what happens on rebound, recovery steps.
- The jump master controls the count; nervous jumpers can step back, never push.
- Never allow a jump if anything is uncertain: weight, check, conditions.

### 4. Recovery
- Practised recovery system (lowering to a boat/landing mat, hauling up with a
  winch). Recovery staff trained in rescue if the jumper is unwell.
- Monitor jumpers for distress; inverted time should be minimised.

### 5. Daily and periodic checks
- Daily inspection of cords, harnesses, connectors and anchors; logbook entries.
- Environmental limits: wind, lightning, water level for water-touch.
- Incident and near-miss reporting; regular staff re-training.

## Gotchas
- Using the wrong cord because the weight band was misread.
- Staff complacency late in a busy day — the double-check is exactly for this.
- Jumpers going down instead of out and twisting in the cord.

## Verify
- [ ] Jumper weighed on site and cord matched to weight band.
- [ ] Second-person verbal check of all connections before edge.
- [ ] Cord use count logged and below retirement limit.
- [ ] Recovery procedure rehearsed and equipment checked today.
