# Slacklining and highlining — progression, rigging and safety

**Goal:** walk a slackline end to end, rig it without damaging trees or
yourself, and understand why highlining needs far stricter systems. **Draft:
highline rigging is summarised for orientation, not instruction; learn it
from experienced riggers and the International Slackline Association (ISA)
safety guidance.**

## 1. First line set-up (ratchet kit)
- Beginner kit: ~15 m, 25–50 mm webbing with a ratchet. Rig **short and
  low**: 8–10 m between anchors, at knee height or lower in the middle
  when standing on it.
- **Anchors:** healthy trees at least ~30 cm diameter (rule of thumb),
  bark protected with **tree pads** or carpet under the sling — bare
  webbing abrades bark and can kill trees, and some parks ban lines for this.
- Check the ground under the line is free of rocks and roots.
- Tension: snug but not bar-tight; a beginner line that's too slack
  swings, too tight is twitchy and over-loads the ratchet.
- **Ratchet safety:** keep fingers clear when releasing; release slowly
  (some kits use a release-line or the handle fully opened). Wrap the
  ratchet handle closed and pad it — it's a heavy steel lump near your shins.
- Never use climbing slings or ropes of unknown rating as slackline anchors
  without knowing loads; long lines generate several kN.

## 2. Learning to walk
1. **Mount** near an anchor (less sway): one foot on the line, hand on a
   friend's shoulder, stand up in one motion over the foot.
2. **Gaze** at a fixed point at the far anchor, not your feet.
3. **Arms high** (above shoulder height), knees soft, hips under shoulders.
4. Stand on one foot for 5–10 s each side before stepping.
5. Steps: place the foot along the line, heel-to-toe, slowly shift weight.
6. Turnaround and sit-start come next.
- Short sessions often; balance improves between sessions.
- Falling: just step off to the side. Don't straddle-fall onto the line.

## 3. Progression
- **Longlines:** longer, more tension, slower sway; need low-stretch webbing,
  pulley systems, and proper load calculation.
- **Tricklines:** short, bouncy, used for jumps and flips; rig over soft ground.
- **Waterlines:** over water — check depth and swimmer traffic.

## 4. Highlining (principles)
- A highline is rigged with **full redundancy**: main webbing plus a
  **backup** (second webbing or rope) taped/connected alongside, so a
  failure of any single component doesn't drop the walker.
- **Anchors:** multiple bolts or bomber natural anchors, equalised and
  redundant; no single point of failure anywhere in the system.
- Walker wears a climbing harness with a **leash** (dynamic rope leash
  with a steel **leash ring** around the webbing), tied with standard
  climbing knots and checked by a partner.
- **Leash falls** are normal: practise falling and climbing back to the
  line (the "chongo" or simply hand-over-hand back up) on a low line first.
- Exposure is the main difficulty — walkers often train on longlines of
  similar length first.
- Rescue plan: how you'll reach a stuck walker in the middle.
- Obtain land-manager permission; many areas regulate or ban highlines.

## Gotchas
- Rigging on thin/dead trees or without tree protection.
- Ratchet over-tensioning or uncontrolled release.
- Beginners rigging long lines high.
- Highlining with gear or knots no-one has inspected.
- **Tree size and length numbers are rules of thumb from community guidance.**

## Verify
- [ ] Line rigged short, low, with tree protection and padded ratchet.
- [ ] Can stand 10 s per foot and walk the full line.
- [ ] Can explain every redundant component of a highline before stepping onto one.
- [ ] Leash and knot partner-checked; leash falls practised low.
