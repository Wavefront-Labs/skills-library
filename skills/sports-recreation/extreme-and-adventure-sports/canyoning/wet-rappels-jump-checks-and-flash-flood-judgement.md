# Canyoning — wet rappels, jump checks and flash-flood judgement

**Goal:** descend a canyon and come out the bottom, which in canyoning
means managing water and retrievable rope systems in places with no easy
exit. **Draft:** this outlines practice taught on canyoning courses
(e.g. national canyoning associations, ACA in the US); learn the rope work
in person before using it.

## 1. Plan from beta and data
- **Beta:** guidebook/online description — number and height of rappels,
  longest drop (sets rope length: you need ≥ 2× the longest drop for a
  doubled rope, or a pull-cord system), escape points, typical time.
- **Ratings:** many systems rate technical difficulty, water flow and
  commitment separately (e.g. the American "3B III" style: technical class,
  water level, time grade). Check which scale your region uses.
- **Flow:** many canyons have known safe flow ranges from a stream gauge;
  in high flow, a fun canyon becomes deadly.
- Leave a **trip plan** with someone: canyon, party, expected exit time.

## 2. Flash-flood go/no-go
- Check the forecast for the **whole catchment**, not just where you stand —
  storms miles upstream can flood a dry slot canyon within minutes.
- No-go signs: thunderstorm risk in the catchment, rising or murky water,
  debris floating, sudden change in sound or wind down the canyon.
- Know the **escape routes** (high ground) and commitment sections with none.
- If water rises: get to high ground immediately; don't try to outrun it
  down a slot.

## 3. Anchors
- Assess every anchor: bolts (corrosion, spin, spacing), natural anchors
  (tree alive and rooted, chockstone wedged and solid), webbing (UV and
  abrasion damage — replace suspicious slings and carry spare).
- Rig so the rope runs **clear of water hazards** and doesn't jam on retrieval.

## 4. Releasable (contingency) rappels
- In water or for beginners, rig the rope so the top person can **lower**
  someone stuck under a waterfall: e.g. a block with a releasable
  munter-mule-overhand or figure-8 block system with spare rope in the bag.
- Cut the rope to **just touch the bottom** (or land in a pool) to prevent
  loops tangling around a rappeller in moving water.
- First person down checks the landing and signals (whistle code agreed:
  e.g. 1 = stop, 2 = off rope, 3 = help — confirm with your group).
- Keep hair, clothing and webbing away from the descender; carry a knife accessible.

## 5. Jumps and slides
- **Check every jump, every time:** someone goes down first (rappel) and
  probes the pool for depth and debris with a pole or by diving — logs and
  rocks move between floods.
- Jump feet first, legs together, arms close; slides feet first on your back.
- Avoid jumps into foaming water near the fall (aerated water is less
  buoyant) and into **hydraulics/keeper pots** that recirculate.
- **Siphons and undercuts** can trap swimmers — know where they are from beta and scouting.

## 6. Kit
- Wetsuit appropriate to water temperature (cold-water canyons need 5 mm+),
  helmet, harness with butt protector, descender suitable for wet rope
  and adjustable friction, canyon shoes with grip, canyon bag that drains,
  dry bag/first aid, spare webbing and rapid links.

## Gotchas
- Not testing rope retrieval before the last person descends.
- Assuming a pool is deep because it was last year.
- Starting late; canyons in the dark multiply risk.
- **Ratings, whistle codes and system details vary by region; follow your course training.**

## Verify
- [ ] Rope length ≥ needs of longest drop; pull test done before last descent.
- [ ] Catchment forecast and gauge checked; escape points known.
- [ ] Releasable rappel rigged where water or novices are involved.
- [ ] Every jump probed by a person below before anyone jumps.
