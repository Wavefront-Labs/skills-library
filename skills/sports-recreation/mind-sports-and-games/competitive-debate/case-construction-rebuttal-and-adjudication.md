# Competitive debate — case construction, rebuttal and adjudication

**Goal:** win rounds by building clear, well-explained arguments, engaging
directly with the other side and showing the judge why your side matters
more. Focus on parliamentary formats (British Parliamentary — BP — and
World Schools — WSDC); US policy, Lincoln-Douglas and Public Forum differ
in evidence and structure.

## 1. Formats at a glance
- **BP:** four teams of two (Opening/Closing Government and Opposition),
  7-minute speeches, **15 minutes prep**, no internet, motion announced
  per round. Teams are ranked 1st-4th.
- **WSDC:** two teams of three, 8-minute substantive speeches plus
  4-minute reply speeches; mix of prepared and impromptu (often 1 hour
  prep) motions.
- **Points of Information (POIs):** short interjections offered during
  the middle of the other side's speeches (not the first and last
  minute); the speaker may accept or decline.
Check the tournament's rules pack for exact timings.

## 2. Reading the motion and prep
- Identify **motion type**: "This House would…" (policy), "This House
  believes/regrets/supports…" (value/analysis), "…as X, would…" (actor).
- Define fairly: the definition must be debatable; squirrelling (an
  unreasonable definition) loses credibility and judges' favour.
- Spend prep time in this order: **clash points** (where will the debate
  actually be?), your **2-3 strongest arguments**, likely opposition
  arguments and your responses, then speech allocation.

## 3. Building an argument
For each argument:
1. **Claim** — one sentence.
2. **Mechanism/analysis** — *why* it is true; the causal chain, incentives
   of actors, why this happens in the real world. This is where most
   speakers are thin.
3. **Impact** — why it matters, to whom, how much.
4. **Weighing** — why this matters more than the other side's points
   (scale, probability, reversibility, who is affected, moral priority).
Examples help but don't replace mechanisms.

## 4. Rebuttal
- Respond to the opponents' **best** version of their argument, not their
  weakest line.
- Tools: it's **not true** (mechanism fails), **not important** (impact
  small), **not unique** (happens anyway / also on your side), **turn**
  (it actually helps your side).
- Lead later speeches with rebuttal, then rebuild your case.
- **Flow** the debate: note-taking in columns per speaker so you answer
  every relevant point.

## 5. Roles (BP)
- **Opening teams:** set up the debate (definition, model) and the key
  arguments.
- **Closing teams:** must bring an **extension** — new, distinct material
  or deeper analysis — while staying consistent with their opening team.
- **Whip speakers:** summarise the debate by **clashes**, weigh, no
  brand-new arguments (new rebuttal is fine). Reply speeches (WSDC)
  are a biased summary: no new arguments.

## 6. POIs
- Offer regularly but don't badger; a good POI is one sentence that
  exposes a gap.
- Accept one or two in your speech (expected in BP); answer briefly and
  return to your structure.

## 7. How adjudicators decide
- Judges are to act as an **informed, ordinary-intelligent person**:
  they weigh what was said, not what they personally know.
- They reward **engagement and comparison**: an argument left unanswered
  is usually conceded; well-weighed clashes decide rankings.
- Style matters through clarity, not flourishes.
- As a judge: flow the round, decide the key clashes, give an oral
  adjudication explaining the ranking and specific feedback.

## Gotchas
- Assertions without mechanisms.
- Closing team with no extension (often ranked low).
- New arguments in a whip/reply speech.
- Ignoring the actual clash to repeat prepared material.

## Verify
- [ ] Each argument has claim, mechanism, impact and weighing.
- [ ] Every major opposing argument got a response.
- [ ] (BP closing) Your extension was distinct and named as such.
- [ ] You can explain the round's key clashes the way a judge would.
