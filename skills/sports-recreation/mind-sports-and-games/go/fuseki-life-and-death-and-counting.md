# Go — fuseki, life and death, and counting

**Goal:** progress through the kyu ranks by mastering the fundamentals
that decide most amateur games: life and death, opening priorities,
taking sente, and counting accurately.

## 1. Fundamentals to be sure of
- **Liberties:** empty points adjacent to a group; a group with none is
  captured. **Atari** = one liberty left.
- **Ladder** and **net (geta):** basic capturing techniques; before
  running from a ladder, check for a **ladder breaker** stone along the
  diagonal path.
- **Ko:** a single-stone recapture can't be made immediately; you must
  play elsewhere (a **ko threat**) first.
- A group lives with **two real eyes** (separate enclosed points where
  the opponent cannot play); one eye or false eyes die.

## 2. Life and death (tsumego) — the biggest lever
- Do **daily tsumego**: many easy problems solved fully in your head
  (don't place stones until you've read to the end) beats few hard ones.
- Key shapes: straight three dies if the opponent plays the vital point
  first; straight four lives; "bent four in the corner" is dead under
  Japanese rules; L-group, rabbity six, etc.
- **Vital point:** usually the central point of the eye space.
- In games, check weak groups' status *before* tenuki (playing elsewhere).

## 3. Opening (fuseki)
- Priority order: **corners → sides → centre** — corners make territory
  most efficiently.
- Approach / enclose corners; then extend along the sides. Classic
  extension distances from a two-stone wall (e.g. three spaces) keep it
  safe.
- **Joseki** are balanced corner sequences — learn a few common ones but
  **choose by direction**: the right joseki makes the result work with
  your other stones (thickness facing your side, not toward an opponent's
  strong position).
- Memorising joseki without understanding hurts: when the opponent
  deviates, you must punish or adapt with reading.
- Since AI (AlphaGo, KataGo, Leela Zero) some classic joseki were revised;
  amateur play still benefits most from principles, not latest AI trends.

## 4. Middle game priorities
- **Sente:** a move the opponent must answer — keep the initiative;
  **gote** moves end your turn's initiative. Prefer big sente moves.
- **Urgent before big:** save a weak group or attack one before taking a
  large empty area.
- Use thickness to attack, not to make territory directly.
- Avoid **crawling along the 2nd line** yourself (tiny territory, and the
  opponent gets a wall); likewise, pushing the opponent along a high line
  (4th/5th) from below hands them a large area (proverb wording varies).

## 5. Endgame (yose) and counting
- Play **double-sente** and **sente** endgame moves before gote ones;
  estimate move value in points.
- **Counting:** under **territory scoring** (Japanese/Korean) you count
  empty points surrounded plus captures; under **area scoring**
  (Chinese, AGA-style) you count stones on the board plus surrounded
  territory. Results are almost always the same; know which your event uses.
- **Komi:** compensation to White for moving second — commonly 6.5
  (territory scoring) or 7.5 (area scoring); check your ruleset.
- **Handicap games:** Black places extra stones; a rank difference of N
  kyu is roughly N handicap stones.

## 6. Practice and review
- Play on OGS or KGS (or a club); review losses with a stronger player
  or KataGo, focusing on the largest point swings and dead groups.
- Replay professional games quickly to absorb shape.

## Gotchas
- Tenuki from a group with unsettled status → loss of the whole group.
- Playing joseki that faces your thickness into the opponent's strength.
- Miscounting at the end because dead stones weren't agreed.

## Verify
- [ ] You solved tsumego daily, reading fully before placing stones.
- [ ] In your game record, weak groups were checked before tenuki.
- [ ] You can count a finished game under both territory and area rules.
- [ ] You know your event's ruleset and komi.
