# Competitive quizzing — knowledge mapping, buzzer technique and team play

**Goal:** win more quizzes by knowing more of what gets asked, getting to
the buzzer first without throwing away points, and making the team
smarter than its best player. For casual sports trivia see
`sports-recreation.spectating-fandom-and-sports-appreciation.sports-trivia`.

## 1. Know the formats
- **Pub/quiz league:** written answers, team conferring, rounds by topic.
- **Buzzer formats:** quiz bowl (US school/college; **tossups** answered
  individually by buzzer, then **bonuses** conferred by the team), TV-style
  "starter for ten"/bonus formats, and individual championships.
- **Pyramidal questions** (quiz bowl): clues run from hardest to easiest,
  so deeper knowledge buzzes earlier. Early wrong buzzes ("negs")
  usually cost points (often −5 in quiz bowl; check the rules).
- Online leagues (e.g. LearnedLeague, invitation-based) use daily written
  questions with defence scoring.

## 2. Mapping the canon
- Quiz knowledge is **finite and repetitive**: certain facts come up
  again and again (capitals, flags, Nobel laureates, Booker winners,
  Shakespeare plots, periodic table, US presidents/UK prime ministers,
  famous paintings and painters, Olympic hosts, opera composers).
- Start a **topic map**: list categories, rate yourself 1-5, and study the
  weakest high-frequency topics first.
- Use **past question archives** (quiz bowl packet archives are public;
  many leagues publish past papers) to see what's asked — study answers
  that appear repeatedly ("frequency lists").
- Learn facts as **clue → answer** pairs the way they're asked, not as
  encyclopedia entries.

## 3. Study technique
- **Spaced-repetition flashcards** (e.g. Anki): one fact per card, both
  directions where useful. 15-30 minutes daily beats weekend cramming.
- Read widely around a card (a Wikipedia article's lead) to build
  connected knowledge — connections give you guessing power.
- **Write questions:** writing teaches what's distinctive about an answer.
- Track misses from every quiz in a notebook → cards.

## 4. Buzzer technique
- **Buzz when you know**, not when you're sure the question is over.
  The ideal buzz comes at the first clue that uniquely identifies the
  answer.
- Know the **neg penalty**: with a penalty, buzz at about the point you'd
  be right most of the time; without one, buzz more aggressively near the
  end.
- Physical: hand on the buzzer, light pressure, react to the clue not to
  the reader's pause. Practise on a buzzer app or against recordings.
- After buzzing, say your answer promptly (time limits apply). Give the
  **specific** answer the question asks for (full name if required);
  check prompting rules.
- If the other team negs, the reader usually finishes the question for
  you; since it's complete there's typically no further penalty, so
  listen to every clue and then answer (check your format's rules).

## 5. Team play
- **Coverage over stars:** recruit teammates whose strong topics don't
  overlap.
- **Captain** gives the final answer on conferred questions; others say
  answer + confidence quickly ("Picasso, 80%").
- On bonuses: the person who knows speaks first; don't talk over.
- Keep morale: misses happen; review afterwards, not during.

## 6. Guessing well
- Use the question's structure: "This composer…" + clues of era + nationality
  narrows fast.
- Always give an answer on conferred/no-penalty questions.
- Prefer the famous answer unless the question flags an exception.

## Gotchas
- Buzzing before hearing the answer-type ("this city" vs "this country").
- Studying obscure topics while missing high-frequency canon.
- Team discussion running out the clock.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a topic map with self-ratings and a study plan for weak, frequent topics.
- [ ] Daily spaced-repetition reviews are happening.
- [ ] You know the event's neg and prompting rules.
- [ ] The team has a captain and a confidence-call protocol.
