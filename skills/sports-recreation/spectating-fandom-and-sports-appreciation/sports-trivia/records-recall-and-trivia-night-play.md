# Sports trivia — records, recall and trivia-night play

**Goal:** answer more sports questions correctly at a pub quiz, a
dedicated sports-quiz night or with friends — by studying what actually
gets asked and by playing the round smartly. For quiz as a competitive
discipline (leagues, buzzers, knowledge mapping) see
`sports-recreation.mind-sports-and-games.competitive-quizzing` skills.

## 1. Know what setters ask
Sports questions cluster in predictable places:
- **Winners lists:** World Cup and continental championship winners and
  hosts, Olympic host cities and years, Grand Slam champions, major golf
  winners, Super Bowl / World Series / Stanley Cup / league champions,
  Tour de France winners, Ballon d'Or.
- **Records and firsts:** all-time top scorers, world records in headline
  athletics and swimming events, first person to do X, youngest/oldest.
- **Nicknames, venues and colours:** stadium names, club nicknames, kit
  colours, mascots.
- **Numbers in rules:** players per side, pitch/court dimensions, length
  of a marathon (42.195 km), points for a try, overs in a T20 innings (20).
- **The sport of the moment:** whatever major event just happened. Read
  the last month's headlines before a quiz.
Questions skew toward the host country's popular sports — adjust your
study to the venue.

## 2. Studying efficiently
- Pick one list at a time (e.g. World Cup winners and hosts by year) and
  learn it in **spaced-repetition** flashcards (Anki or similar): you see a
  card again just before you'd forget it, which beats re-reading.
- Learn lists **in both directions** — year → winner and winner → years.
- Hook facts to stories: a final you watched, a famous upset. Isolated
  facts fade fastest.
- Timed list quizzes (Sporcle-style "name all the …") are good recall
  drills; repeat the same quiz until you complete it, then move on.
- Ten minutes a day beats a two-hour cram the afternoon of the quiz.

## 3. Playing the round
- **Write every answer, never leave a blank** unless there is negative
  marking. A plausible guess scores sometimes; a blank never does.
- **Educated guessing:** narrow by era (who was dominant then?), by
  nationality of the venue or question-setter, and by "quiz-worthiness" —
  setters ask about surprising answers, so if the obvious answer seems too
  easy for the question's wording, consider the famous exception.
- **Team protocol:** one writer; whoever is most confident on a topic
  speaks first; quiet confidence levels ("certain / think so / guess")
  stop the loudest person overruling the right one.
- Don't change a first instinct without a concrete reason — remembered a
  fact, not a vague doubt.
- Spelling usually doesn't matter unless the question asks for it; check
  the quizmaster's rule.
- Use jokers or double-points rounds on your strongest topic, decided
  before the round is read.

## Gotchas
- Records change: confirm current holders before a quiz rather than
  trusting what you learned years ago (a quizmaster's answer sheet can
  also be out of date — dispute politely with a source).
- Similar-sounding competitions (e.g. European Cup vs Champions League
  era naming, or the old vs new name of a stadium) — read what exactly is
  asked.
- "Which country" vs "which city" vs "which club" — answer the question
  type asked.

## Verify
- [ ] You can recite at least one core list (e.g. last 10 World Cup winners and hosts) both directions.
- [ ] You read recent sports news in the week before the quiz.
- [ ] Your answer sheet had no blanks (or you knew it had negative marking).
- [ ] The team used confidence levels and one final decision-maker.
