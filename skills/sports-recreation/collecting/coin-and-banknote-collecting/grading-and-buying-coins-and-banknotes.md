# Grading and buying coins and banknotes

**Goal:** judge condition well enough to pay the right price, know when third-party
grading is worth it, and avoid the classic losses — cleaned coins, fakes, and
overpaying for common dates.

## 1. The grading scale
Coins and (via PMG and similar) banknotes use the **Sheldon scale, 1–70**:
- **1–58 circulated:** G-4 (Good), VG-8, F-12 (Fine), VF-20/30, EF/XF-40/45, AU-50–58
  (About Uncirculated — traces of wear on high points only).
- **60–70 Mint State (MS)** for business strikes, **PR/PF** for proofs. MS-60 is
  uncirculated but ugly (bagmarks, poor lustre); MS-63 is the typical "choice"
  collector grade; MS-65 "gem"; 67+ is condition-rarity territory where prices jump.
Assess in order: **wear** on the highest points (cheek, hair, feathers) → **lustre**
(cartwheel effect when tilted under a lamp) → **marks/contact** in prime focal areas →
**strike** sharpness → **eye appeal** (toning).
Banknotes: fold lines, corner rounding, pinholes, stains, trimmed margins, and
"embossing" (original paper texture). One hard centre fold typically caps a note at
around AU/"About Uncirculated" on the PMG scale — check the specific service's standards.

Practical kit: 5x–10x loupe, a single incandescent or LED point light (not
diffuse daylight — it hides hairlines), cotton gloves or hold by the edge.

## 2. Problem coins — the value killers
- **Cleaned:** hairline scratches all in one direction, unnaturally bright or dull
  "dead" surfaces, residue in the recesses. Graders net-grade or body-bag these
  ("Details" grade). Never clean a coin.
- **PVC damage:** green film from soft vinyl flips. Store in Mylar/inert flips or
  slabs.
- Also: rim dings, holes, tooling, artificial toning, whizzing (wire-brushed to fake lustre).

## 3. Slabbed vs raw
Third-party graders — **PCGS** and **NGC** (coins), **PMG** and PCGS Banknote (notes) —
encapsulate with a grade and certificate number. Rough rule: slab when the coin's
value comfortably exceeds the grading fee plus postage and insurance (fees vary by
tier and change — check current schedules), when it's a key date likely to be
counterfeited, or when you'll sell. Always **verify the cert number** on the grader's
website and compare the photo — fake slabs exist. Stickers such as CAC indicate a
second opinion that the coin is solid for its grade; they affect price in some US
markets (unconfirmed how much elsewhere).

Raw coins are cheaper per grade point but you carry the grading risk; buy raw only
from sellers with returns, or when you're confident.

## 4. Building a set
Pick a series with a clear endpoint (date-and-mint set, type set, one country's
notes by signature). Price the **key dates** first — they set the budget; common dates
are cheap at any time, keys rarely get cheaper. Buy the best grade you can afford for
keys; filler grades are hard to sell later.

## 5. Buying
- **Price guides** (Red Book, Krause/Standard Catalog, grader price guides) are
  starting points; real prices come from **recent auction archives** (Heritage, Stack's,
  regional houses) for the same grade and service.
- Auction totals = hammer + **buyer's premium** (often around 20% at major houses;
  confirm) + shipping/tax.
- Bullion-heavy coins track metal spot price; numismatic premium sits on top.
- Avoid marketplace listings of "rare" coins from new sellers with stock photos,
  especially key dates, trade dollars, and Chinese-made replicas.

## Gotchas
- Mintage alone does not make a coin rare in practice — survival and grade do.
- Photos flatter or hide; ask for angled-light images or a video.
- Grades drift between services and decades ("gradeflation"); compare like with like.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name the grade-limiting factor on each coin you own.
- [ ] Every slab's cert number checked online and photo matches.
- [ ] No PVC flips in the collection.
- [ ] Each purchase price compared with at least 3 recent auction results at that grade and service.
- [ ] Key dates priced and budgeted before filler.
