# Toys and models: editions, boxed vs loose, and display

**Goal:** know which version you're buying, pay the right premium for packaging, avoid
reproduction parts, and keep plastic and packaging from degrading.

## 1. Edition and variant identification
Value often hides in small details:
- **Copyright/date stamps and country of manufacture** on the toy (legs, feet,
  underside of diecast).
- **Packaging variants:** card back number (e.g. vintage Star Wars "12-back" vs later),
  price stickers, language versions, logo changes, promotional offers.
- **Production variants:** paint colour, tampo errors, wheel types (Hot Wheels
  "Redlines" vs blackwalls), mould changes, accessory colour.
- **Line references:** collector guides and online databases for your line
  (fan-run archives exist for most major lines). Cross-check two sources; fan wikis
  contain errors.

## 2. Packaging terminology
- **MOC** (mint on card), **MIB** (mint in box), **MISB/NRFB** (sealed / never removed
  from box), **loose** (out of packaging), **C-grades** (C10 mint → C1 poor, used by
  many dealers).
- Graded packaging: **AFA** (action figures) and **CAS** encapsulate and grade;
  premiums for high AFA grades on vintage figures can be large. Check whether your line's
  market actually values grading before paying for it.

## 3. Boxed vs loose — how to decide
- Vintage carded/boxed in high grade: packaging is often most of the value.
- Modern mass-market releases: huge numbers were kept sealed by collectors; loose and
  sealed prices can be close, and sealed rarely appreciates much.
- If you want to **play/pose/display**, buy loose complete — far cheaper. "Complete"
  means every original accessory; check the line's accessory list.
- Don't open a valuable sealed item to "check it" — opened is permanently loose.

## 4. Spotting reproductions and restorations
- **Repro accessories** (weapons, capes) are everywhere: compare plastic colour,
  sheen, mould detail and markings with known originals; repro parts often lack the
  maker's mark or have soft detail.
- **Resealed/recarded** items: bubble glue that's too neat, wrong card stock, a
  bubble that doesn't match the figure's era.
- Diecast: repainted bodies, replacement wheels/axles (look for drilled rivets).

## 5. Condition checks for loose items
Paint wear on high points, loose joints, broken tabs/pegs, missing stickers, sun-fade
(one side lighter), yellowing of white/clear plastic, stress marks (white lines),
battery corrosion in electronic toys (always remove batteries in storage).

## 6. Display and storage
- **Light** yellows white plastic and fades card art — no direct sun; LED lighting
  (low heat, low UV) in cabinets.
- **Heat** warps bubbles and diecast boxes; avoid lofts.
- **Plastic degradation:** some soft PVC figures go sticky as plasticiser migrates;
  store separately so they don't damage others; don't seal in airtight bags with
  rubber items. Clean sticky figures gently — methods vary by plastic, test first.
- Glass display cabinets (flat-pack glass units are a common choice) keep dust off.
  Use clear protector cases for carded and boxed items (acid-free, UV-resistant
  options exist).
- Store boxes upright or flat as designed; never stack heavy items on window boxes.

## 7. Grail hunting and buying
Set saved searches on marketplaces and auction aggregators; check sold prices;
join line-specific forums and collector groups, where many rare items sell first and
reputations are known.

## Gotchas
- "Complete" listings often include repro parts — ask directly.
- Tight markets swing with film/anniversary releases; don't buy at peak hype unless it's for keeps.
- Batteries left in vintage electronics destroy contacts.
