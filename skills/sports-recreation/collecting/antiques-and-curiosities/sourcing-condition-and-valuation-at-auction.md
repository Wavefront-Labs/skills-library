# Antiques: sourcing, condition and valuation at auction

**Goal:** find good pieces at fair prices, judge age and condition in person, and know
when restoration helps or ruins value.

## 1. Where to source
- **Regional auction houses** — the best prices for buyers; browse aggregators
  (the-saleroom, LiveAuctioneers, Invaluable) by keyword and distance.
- **Antiques fairs and centres** — dealers have done the finding; you pay retail but can
  negotiate ("what's your best on this?" — 10–15% off is common for antiques,
  not guaranteed).
- **Car boots / flea markets / estate sales** — early arrival, cash, knowledge wins.

## 2. Read the catalogue properly
- Lot descriptions use coded language. In many houses: **"Attributed to"** < the
  artist's name in full; **"circa"/"style of"/"in the manner of"** = probably not
  period; **"19th century style"** = not 19th century. Read each house's own
  glossary — usage varies.
- The **estimate** is a guide, often set low to attract bidders.
- Request a **condition report** for anything you'll bid on remotely; ask specific
  questions (any restoration? repairs under UV? original handles?). Absence of
  condition notes in a catalogue usually does not mean perfect condition.

## 3. Inspect at viewing
Bring a loupe, a small torch, a UV torch, a magnet, and a tape measure.
- **Ceramics:** run a fingertip around rims for chips; tap gently — a dull note can
  mean a crack; UV light shows over-painting and restoration as dark/different
  fluorescence; check marks (but marks are easily faked — judge body, glaze and decoration too).
- **Silver:** hallmarks (assay office, date letter, maker) — check against a hallmark
  guide; worn or transposed marks and "silver-plated" (EPNS) items; magnet should not stick.
- **Furniture:** look underneath and inside — hand-cut dovetails, saw marks, shrinkage,
  wear where hands and feet actually go, consistent oxidation on secondary woods.
  Replaced handles leave old holes. "Marriages" (top and base from different pieces)
  show mismatched timber and construction.
- **Glass:** chips ground down on rims, sick/cloudy glass that won't clean.
- **General:** wear should be logical; uniform "distressing" is a fake sign.

## 4. Bidding with fees
Total cost = hammer + **buyer's premium** (often 20–30% plus local VAT/sales tax on the
premium; online platforms can add a few percent more) + shipping/packing. Set your
maximum **all-in** and back-calculate the hammer you can bid. Don't chase in the room.
Leave commission bids if you can't attend. Shipping large furniture can exceed the
hammer price.

## 5. Valuation
Value = recent **realised** prices for comparable items (aggregator archives,
price databases) adjusted for condition, maker, provenance and current taste.
Taste moves: "brown furniture" and many Victorian ceramics fell sharply in recent
decades while mid-century design rose. Retail prices at fairs are not values.

## 6. Restore or leave?
- **Leave:** original patina/finish on furniture, original paint on folk art, old
  repairs that are part of history. Stripping and refinishing period furniture can
  cut value heavily.
- **Restore (professionally, reversibly):** structural instability, active
  deterioration, items bought to use. Disclose restoration when selling.
- Never "clean" bronze patina, silver gilt, or coins.

## Gotchas
- Reproductions from the 20th century are now themselves old and wear convincingly.
- Ivory, tortoiseshell and some rare woods carry legal trade restrictions — check
  local law before buying or exporting.
- Photos online hide repairs; insist on a condition report.

## Verify
- [ ] Catalogue terms checked against the house's glossary.
- [ ] Condition report or in-person inspection done for every lot bid on.
- [ ] Maximum bid calculated with premium, tax and shipping included.
- [ ] Value estimate cites comparable realised prices.
- [ ] Legal restrictions checked for restricted materials.
