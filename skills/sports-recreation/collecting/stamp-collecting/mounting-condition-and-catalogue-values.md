# Stamp collecting: mounting, condition and catalogue values

**Goal:** store stamps without damaging them, judge condition the way buyers do, identify
the variety you actually have, and translate catalogue values into real money.

## 1. Tools
- **Stamp tongs** (spade-tipped) — never fingers; skin oil stains gum and paper.
- **Perforation gauge**, **magnifier** (10x), **watermark detector** (black tray +
  watermark fluid — use the non-flammable kind and good ventilation), a UV lamp for
  phosphor bands and paper types (modern issues).
- A **stockbook** with glassine or clear strips for unsorted/working stock.

## 2. Mounting
- **Mint never hinged (MNH)** stamps: hingeless mounts (clear strips with a black or
  clear backing, cut to size, sealed edge). Never hinge an MNH stamp — a hinge mark
  drops it to "mounted/hinged mint" and can halve modern catalogue value.
- **Used stamps** and already-hinged mint: peelable hinges are fine.
- Albums: printed (one country, spaces per issue) or blank leaves for thematic/postal
  history layouts. Use acid-free leaves with interleaving.
- Keep covers (envelopes) **intact** — don't soak stamps off old covers before checking
  whether the postmark, route or rate makes the cover worth more than the stamp.

## 3. Soaking used stamps off paper
Lukewarm water, face down, separate coloured/red envelope paper (dyes run), slide
stamp off when free (don't peel), rinse gum residue, dry face down between
blotting paper under light weight. **Don't soak** self-adhesives in plain water without
checking — many need special solvents or should stay "on piece".

## 4. Condition — what buyers check
1. **Centering:** design relative to perforations. Well-centred older stamps carry big
   premiums.
2. **Perforations:** all teeth present, none short or pulled.
3. **Gum (mint):** MNH > lightly hinged > heavily hinged > no gum / regummed.
4. **Faults:** thins (check with fluid or by holding to light), creases, tears, stains,
   toning ("tropical" brown spots), fading.
5. **Cancellation (used):** light, clean, ideally a dated "socked-on-the-nose" CDS;
   heavy smudged cancels reduce value.

## 5. Identifying the variety
Many stamps look identical but differ by **perforation** (e.g. perf 14 vs 14½ × 14),
**watermark** (upright, inverted, sideways), **paper**, **shade** or **printing
method**. The catalogue number — and value — depends on this. Measure perfs across
2 cm with the gauge; check watermark face down in fluid.

## 6. Catalogue values
Major catalogues: **Stanley Gibbons** (UK/Commonwealth), **Scott** (US/world), **Michel**
(Germany/Europe), **Yvert** (France). Their values are retail prices for fine
examples, not what you'll be paid. For common modern material, actual sale prices are
often a small fraction of catalogue, and many catalogues price a minimum value that
merely reflects handling costs. Better and scarce stamps in top condition can exceed
catalogue. Check recent auction realisations for real prices.

## 7. Expertising
For valuable stamps (inverted centres, early classics, overprints that are commonly
forged), buy with an **expert certificate** from a recognised committee (e.g. the
Royal Philatelic Society London, the Philatelic Foundation, BPA Expertising) — or
make the sale conditional on obtaining one.

## Gotchas
- Regummed and reperforated stamps are frauds targeting MNH premiums.
- Watermark fluid can damage some modern photogravure inks — test cautiously.
- "Catalogue £1000" collections in lots often sell for a few percent of that.

## Verify
- [ ] All MNH stamps in hingeless mounts, handled with tongs.
- [ ] Each valued stamp identified to the exact catalogue number (perf + watermark checked).
- [ ] Condition notes cover centering, perfs, gum and faults.
- [ ] Valuable items have, or are bought subject to, an expert certificate.
