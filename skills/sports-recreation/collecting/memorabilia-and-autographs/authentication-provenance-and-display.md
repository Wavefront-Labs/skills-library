# Memorabilia and autographs: authentication, provenance and display

**Goal:** buy items whose authenticity you can defend when you sell, and keep the
signature visible for decades.

## 1. The premise: assume it's fake until shown otherwise
Forgeries dominate the low and mid market for famous names. A "certificate of
authenticity" printed by the seller is only the seller's own claim — worth what the
seller is worth. The questions to ask of every item: **who saw it signed, and what
evidence links that moment to this object?**

## 2. Evidence ladder (strongest → weakest)
1. **You obtained it in person** (note date, place; photograph the signing if allowed).
2. **Witnessed private or public signing** by a reputable company with a hologram
   and a database lookup (team/league authentication programmes; established
   private-signing firms).
3. **Third-party authenticator opinion** — PSA/DNA, JSA (James Spence), Beckett
   Authentication (BAS) are the names most buyers accept in the US market. They
   compare against exemplars; full letters (LOA) carry more weight than quick
   "sticker" opinions. Verify the cert number on their website.
4. **Seller COA** from a dealer with a long track record and a lifetime guarantee
   backed by a refund.
5. **Anything else** — "comes with COA", "from a family collection", no paperwork.

Authenticators disagree and make mistakes; an opinion is not proof. For big money,
get two.

## 3. Red flags on the signature itself
- Slow, shaky, uniform-pressure strokes (traced) vs fluid tapering of a natural signature.
- **Autopen:** identical copies overlaid exactly; blunt starts and stops; common on
  politicians' and some celebrities' mail responses.
- **Secretarial/clubhouse** signatures — signed by staff on the star's behalf, common
  on vintage team balls.
- Pre-printed (facsimile) signatures on photos and balls — check with a loupe for
  print dots and no indentation.
- Signature era mismatch: compare with dated exemplars; signatures change over a career.
- Signed in a pen type that didn't exist then (e.g. Sharpie on items "signed" before
  its availability).

## 4. Game-used / game-worn items
Value hinges on **photo-matching** — matching unique features (stitching, wear,
tagging, marks) to game photographs. Use specialist authenticators (e.g. MeiGray for
hockey/basketball jerseys, Resolution Photomatching) and team/league hologram
programmes. "Player issued" ≠ "game used".

## 5. Provenance file
Keep with each item: purchase receipt, seller details, certs/LOAs with numbers, any
signing photo, prior auction listing screenshots, and correspondence. Provenance that
travels with the item is what lets it sell at full price later.

## 6. Display and conservation
- **Light is the killer:** Sharpie and felt-tip ink fades fast in daylight and even
  strong indoor light. Frame with **UV-filtering glass or acrylic** (99% UV-block
  products are common), hang away from windows, and still rotate items.
- Acid-free mats and backing; signature must not touch glazing.
- Balls in UV-blocking cubes; jerseys in shadow boxes with stitched (not glued) mounting.
- Keep humidity moderate (see collection-management cataloguing skill for ranges).

## 7. Valuation
Price from **realised** auction prices (Heritage, Goldin, PWCC/Fanatics Collect,
Lelands, RR Auction for historical) for comparable items with comparable authentication.
Unauthenticated items of famous names usually sell far below authenticated ones.

## Gotchas
- Charity-auction and cruise-ship gallery items are not exempt from forgery.
- "Matching" seller COA plus a hologram sticker means nothing if the company is unknown.
- Inscriptions and personalisations usually reduce resale value.

## Verify
- [ ] Each item sits on the evidence ladder at level 3 or better, or the price reflects that it doesn't.
- [ ] Cert numbers verified on the issuer's site.
- [ ] Provenance file exists for every significant item.
- [ ] Displayed items behind UV-filtering glazing, out of direct light.
