# Football (soccer) — first touch, positional play and set pieces

**Goal:** keep the ball under pressure, move it to the right teammate, and
be organised at dead balls. These three cover most of what separates a
team that plays from one that chases.

## 1. Scan, then receive
- **Scan before the ball arrives:** quick head checks over each shoulder
  while the pass is travelling. Good midfielders scan several times in the
  seconds before receiving; beginners scan zero times and get caught.
- **Open body shape:** stand side-on so you can see the passer and the
  pitch ahead. Receive on the **back foot** (the foot furthest from the
  passer) to turn forward in one touch.
- **Directional first touch:** the first touch should move the ball away
  from pressure and toward space, not stop it dead at your feet.
- **Cushion:** relax the receiving surface (inside foot, thigh, chest) on
  contact; a stiff foot bounces the ball away.

## 2. Passing and support angles
- Pass to the teammate's **far foot** (away from their marker) unless
  you want them to play back first time.
- Weight matters more than accuracy within a metre: a ball that arrives
  too soft gets intercepted, too hard is a bad touch waiting to happen.
- **Triangles:** the player on the ball should always have at least two
  passing options at angles, never a teammate standing directly behind a
  defender (a "hidden" player).
- Width and depth: the team spreads wide and long in possession to stretch
  defenders, then compacts quickly when the ball is lost.

## 3. Positional play basics
- A **formation** (4-3-3, 4-4-2, 3-5-2…) is a starting shape; the real
  structure changes in and out of possession.
- **Rondos** (e.g. 5v2 in a 10 m square) train scanning, body shape and
  quick passing; play them every session.
- **Small-sided games** (4v4 to 7v7) give more touches and decisions than
  11v11 for learners.
- **Defending basics:** press as a unit with a trigger (a bad touch, a
  backward pass, a pass to the touchline). A lone presser just gets passed
  around. The nearest defender delays, the second covers, the rest compact.

## 4. Offside (Law 11) in plain terms
A player is in an offside *position* if any part they can legally score
with is nearer the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the
second-last opponent (usually the last outfield defender), in the
opponents' half. It is only an *offence* if they become involved in play
when the ball is played by a teammate. No offside from a goal kick,
throw-in or corner. Level counts as onside. (Check the current IFAB Laws
of the Game; wording and interpretations are revised yearly.)

## 5. Set pieces
**Attacking corners** — pick one or two routines and rehearse them:
- **Inswinger to the near post** for a flick-on, or to the six-yard area
  where the keeper can't easily come.
- **Outswinger to the penalty spot** for an attacker running onto it.
- A **screen** (a teammate blocking a marker's path) is legal only if it
  doesn't become holding or impeding — referees vary, so don't rely on it.
- Leave at least two players back to stop the counter.

**Defending corners:**
- **Zonal:** players own areas (near post, six-yard line, edge of the area).
  Good against runners; weak if no one attacks the ball.
- **Man-marking:** each defender follows an attacker. Good against one or
  two aerial threats; weak against blocks and screens.
- Most teams mix: a few zonal players in the six-yard area plus markers on
  the main threats.

**Free kicks:** the wall is set at 9.15 m (10 yd); attackers must be at
least 1 m from a wall of three or more defenders (current Law 13 — check
your competition). Throw-ins: both feet on or behind the line, ball from
behind and over the head.

## Gotchas
- Receiving square-on facing your own goal — you can only play backwards.
- Everyone chasing the ball in youth games; give each player a zone.
- Overloading the box at corners and conceding on the break.
- Practising set pieces once and never again: they need weekly reps.
