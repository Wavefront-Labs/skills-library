# Glacier rope-team travel and crevasse rescue

**Goal:** travel on a snow-covered glacier so a crevasse fall is held by the team, and
be able to get the fallen climber out with what you carry.

> Draft: an outline of commonly taught methods. Crevasse rescue must be **practised
> on real terrain with a guide or course** before relying on it; methods vary (guiding
> associations differ on spacing, knots and systems). Two-person teams are much harder
> to rescue from than three or more.

## Roping up
- **Spacing**: commonly ~8–12 m between climbers for 2–3 person teams (more on
  heavily crevassed ground). A two-person team often ties in further in and carries
  the remaining rope in coils (kiwi coil) for rescue.
- **Knots on the rope** (butterfly/overhand) between climbers increase friction in the
  snow-lip and can help arrest a fall — common for two-person teams, debated for
  larger teams.
- Each climber carries: **prusiks** (or a progress-capture device like a Micro
  Traxion/Tibloc), a sling, locking carabiners, a picket or snow stake and/or ice
  screws, and knows the plan.

## Travelling
- Rope **taut-ish** — no big loops of slack; a slack rope lets the faller drop far.
- Travel **perpendicular** to suspected crevasses where possible; probe suspicious
  sags with the axe or probe.
- **Snow bridges** are strongest when cold: alpine starts (early morning) for a
  reason.
- Leader watches for sag lines, colour changes and old tracks.

## When someone falls
1. **Arrest**: everyone else drops into self-arrest (ice-axe arrest) or a braced
   position, digging in feet. The rope bites into the lip and helps.
2. **Anchor**: with the load held, the nearest team member builds an anchor
   **uphill/away** from the hole: a buried picket or ice axe (T-slot/deadman), or
   screws if on ice. Back it up.
3. **Transfer the load**: connect a prusik on the loaded rope to the anchor, then
   ease the load across slowly; keep the backup.
4. **Communicate**: approach the lip **on a belay/protected** (prusik on the rope) and
   check the faller — conscious? injured? Can they climb out with prusiks?
5. **Pad the lip** (axe shaft, pack) where the rope digs in.

## Hauling (outline)
- **3:1 (Z-pulley)**: anchor → progress capture on the loaded rope at the anchor; a
  second prusik further down the loaded rope; the free end redirected through a
  pulley/carabiner on that prusik, pulled back toward the anchor.
- **6:1** for a single rescuer or heavier load (a 2:1 added on top of the 3:1).
- Pull smoothly; reset the traveling prusik as it approaches the anchor.

## Gotchas
- Building the anchor is slow while holding the load: practise both, timed.
- Rope cutting into the lip can trap the faller under the lip — pad it, or drop a
  second rope.
- Falls of solo climbers or unroped teams are frequently fatal — glaciers are not
  hiking trails.
- Acclimatise before high glaciers; altitude illness makes rescue slower.

## Verify
- [ ] Spacing, knots and coils set for team size before stepping on the glacier.
- [ ] Every member carries prusiks/progress capture and an anchor item.
- [ ] Team has practised arrest, anchor build and 3:1 haul on a training slope, timed.
- [ ] Route plan uses early start and avoids afternoon snow bridges.
