# Chlorine residual and bacteriological sampling

**Goal:** collect a sample whose result reflects the water in the main, not your hands, the
tap or the time it sat in a van — and know what to do when the number is wrong.

## 1. Kit
- Pocket colorimeter with DPD free and total reagents (powder pillows or tablets), clean
  sample cells, zero blank. Check it against a secondary standard at the start of the day.
- Sterile bacteriological bottles **pre-dosed with sodium thiosulphate** (neutralises
  chlorine so bacteria present at sampling survive to the lab). Do not rinse them.
- Cool box with ice packs (target ~ 2–8 °C, not frozen), thermometer, chain-of-custody forms,
  gloves, alcohol wipes or a sterilising spray/torch per your lab's procedure.

## 2. Choose the tap
Dedicated sample taps or the customer's cold kitchen tap fed directly from the main. Avoid
taps with filters, softeners, hoses, mixer outlets that blend hot water, leaking glands or
anything under a sink full of cleaning products. Remove aerators/swivel nozzles if allowed.

## 3. Sequence at the tap
1. Run the tap until water temperature stabilises (often 2–5 min) — you want main water,
   not water standing in the service pipe. For **lead/metals "first draw"** sampling the rule
   is the opposite: follow that programme's stagnation protocol instead.
2. Turn off, disinfect the outlet (wipe/spray; flame only metal taps and only if the
   procedure says so), then run again briefly at a moderate, steady flow without splashing.
3. **Chlorine first, on site:** rinse cell, fill to mark, zero, add DPD free reagent and read
   within ~1 minute (free chlorine reading drifts upwards as chloramine reacts). Total
   chlorine: separate cell, read after the stated contact time (typically 3–6 min).
4. **Bacti bottle:** don't touch the inside of cap or neck, hold the cap facing down, fill to
   the shoulder/line leaving an air gap, close at once. One hand on cap, one on bottle.
5. Label with sample point ID, date, time, sampler, chlorine result, and put on ice.

## 4. Get it to the lab
Bacteriological samples should usually be in the lab and started within 24 h, ideally
within 6–8 h; check your regulator's limit. Fill in chain of custody completely —
a missing time makes a compliance sample unusable.

## 5. Interpreting results
- Free chlorine near zero at the far end of a chlorinated network = stale water or demand:
  flush, check reservoir turnover, look at upstream residuals.
- Total much higher than free on a free-chlorine system → combined chlorine / ammonia
  present. On a chloraminated system you'll normally report total.
- **E. coli detected in drinking water is an immediate incident**: notify per the incident
  plan, resample at the tap plus upstream and downstream, check chlorine, consider
  boil-water advice with health authorities. Total coliforms without E. coli → resample and
  investigate (tap hygiene is a common cause, so retain the tap condition notes).
- Turbidity spikes and chlorine dips together suggest disturbed sediment or ingress.

## Gotchas
- High manganese or other oxidants give false-positive DPD readings; use the
  manganese-corrected method if your water has it.
- Readings above the meter range bleach DPD and can read *low* — dilute and repeat if pink
  fades or the meter flags over-range.
- Sunlight and warm vans destroy chlorine in minutes; read on the spot, never back at base.
- Rinsing the bacti bottle washes out the thiosulphate.

## Verify
- [ ] Meter checked against standard today; reagents in date.
- [ ] Tap suitable, flushed to stable temperature, disinfected.
- [ ] Free chlorine read within ~1 min; total after stated contact time.
- [ ] Bacti bottle filled without touching neck, chilled, chain of custody complete.
- [ ] Any E. coli result escalated immediately under the incident plan.

*Uncertain:* holding times, temperatures and action thresholds differ by country and
regulator; the figures above are common practice, confirm against your compliance rules.
