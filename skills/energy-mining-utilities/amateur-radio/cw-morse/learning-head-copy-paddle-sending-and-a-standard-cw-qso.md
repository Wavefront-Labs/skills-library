# Learning head copy, paddle sending and a standard CW QSO

**Goal:** go from zero to making relaxed CW contacts at around 15–20 words per minute,
hearing characters as sounds rather than counting dots and dashes. The main pitfall is
learning slowly-sent code and memorised charts — it builds a lookup habit that
hits a wall around 10 wpm.

## 1. Timing you must know
- Dit = 1 unit; dah = 3; gap inside a character = 1; **between characters = 3;
  between words = 7.**
- Speed in wpm is based on the word "PARIS" = 50 units: **dit length (s) ≈ 1.2 / wpm.**
  At 20 wpm a dit is 60 ms. The unit timings above are (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked); confirm in ITU-R M.1677-1.

## 2. Learn to receive: Koch + Farnsworth
- **Koch method:** start with just two characters sent at your **target character
  speed** (around 20 wpm or more). When you copy 90% correctly (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked), add one character.
  Repeat until you have the full set (letters, numbers, `/ ? . ,` and prosigns).
- **Farnsworth spacing:** keep characters fast but stretch the gaps between them
  so you have thinking time; shrink gaps as you improve.
- Free tools: LCWO.net (Koch course), Morse Code Ninja (practice audio), phone
  apps. **10–20 minutes daily beats an hour once a week.**
- Never learn from a dot-dash chart; learn each character as a rhythm ("dah-di-dah-dit"
  for C).

## 3. Head copy
Writing every letter limits you to handwriting speed. Once you know all
characters:
- Start copying **common words** (THE, AND, NAME, RIG, WX) as whole sounds.
- Copy in your head and only jot down the essentials: call, name, report, location.
- Listen to real QSOs at speed and just follow along without writing anything.
- If you miss a character, **let it go** and catch the next one. Getting stuck on
  one miss loses the next five.

## 4. Sending
- **Straight key:** relaxed wrist, firm consistent pressure; most beginners send
  dahs too short and letter gaps too short.
- **Iambic paddles + electronic keyer:** left paddle dit, right paddle dah
  (normally); squeezing alternates. Mode B adds an extra opposite element when you
  release a squeeze; Mode A doesn't. Pick one and stick with it.
- Set the keyer to the speed you can **receive**; never send faster than you can copy.
- Practise into a sidetone and record yourself, or decode your sending with software;
  if the decoder garbles it, your spacing is off.

## 5. Shape of a standard QSO
```
CQ CQ CQ DE W1XYZ W1XYZ K
W1XYZ DE G4ABC G4ABC K
G4ABC DE W1XYZ GM TNX FER CALL UR RST 579 579 = NAME JOHN JOHN = QTH BOSTON BOSTON = HW? G4ABC DE W1XYZ KN
...
TNX FER QSO 73 G4ABC DE W1XYZ SK
```
Common pieces: **RST** (readability 1–5, strength 1–9, tone 1–9); `TNX/TU` thanks;
`FB` fine business; `UR` your; `WX` weather; `RIG/ANT`; `HW?` how copy?; `73` best
regards; `=` (BT) separator; `K` over; `KN` over, only you; `SK` end of contact;
`AR` end of message. **QRS** = please send slower — always honoured on CW.

## 6. First contacts
- Answer a slow CQ, or call CQ yourself at your comfortable speed; others will slow
  down to match.
- Slow-speed CW frequencies and clubs (CWops CW Academy, national beginner nets,
  slow-speed straight-key events) are friendly places to start.
- A short "rubber-stamp" exchange like the one above is fine and common.

## Verify
- [ ] You learned at ≥ 18–20 wpm character speed, not slow code.
- [ ] You can copy common words as whole sounds and let misses go.
- [ ] Your sending decodes cleanly in software at your set speed.
- [ ] You can follow and complete the standard QSO above, including RST and SK.
