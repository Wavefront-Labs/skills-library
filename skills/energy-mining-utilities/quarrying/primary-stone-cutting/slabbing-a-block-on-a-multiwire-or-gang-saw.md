# Slabbing a block on a multiwire or gang saw

**Goal:** cut a quarried block of granite, marble or similar into slabs of even
thickness (commonly 2 cm and 3 cm finished, sawn slightly thicker for polishing),
oriented to show the stone the way the customer wants, with high yield and few
broken or wedged slabs. **Diamond multiwire** saws are now the dominant method;
traditional **gang (frame) saws** — steel blades with abrasive shot for granite or
diamond segments for marble — are still in use.

## 1. Inspect and orient the block
- Check the block's grade record (dimensions, defects, photo) from the quarry; wet it
  and look for cracks, vents and colour flaws.
- Decide **cut orientation** with sales/quality: marble and many decorative stones look
  very different **vein cut** (across the bedding — linear veins) vs **cross cut**
  (parallel — cloudy pattern). The wrong orientation can make an expensive block
  worthless for the order.
- Plan to put defects in the trim waste or in a sacrificial outer slab, not across the
  middle of the block.

## 2. Square and set the block
- Trim irregular faces with a block cutter or wire saw so the block sits stable and
  slabs come out rectangular; trimming also improves the cut yield.
- Set the block on the saw cart on a **bed** (timber or rubber shims, then often
  plaster or mortar under uneven bases) so it can't rock. Movement during cutting
  breaks slabs and wires/blades.
- Clamp or glue support so slabs don't fall as they're freed — many operators bond the
  block base to the cart with mortar.

## 3. Multiwire saw settings
- Wires (dozens to 80+ diamond-beaded wires) run around drums; spacing sets slab
  thickness. Check wire count and spacers match the order.
- **Tension:** each wire tensioned per maker's guidance and checked equal; uneven
  tension causes wavy slabs.
- **Wire speed and down-feed rate:** start at the maker's recommendation for the stone
  hardness; harder, more abrasive stone (quartzite, some granites) needs slower feed.
  Watch motor load: rising current = feed too fast or wire glazing.
- **Water:** full flow along all wires for cooling and flushing; a blocked nozzle
  burns wire beads.

## 4. Gang saw notes
- Granite gang saws feed steel shot with lime slurry; slurry density and shot size
  must be kept in range (check by sampling) — thin slurry slows cutting, dirty slurry
  wears blades.
- Marble gang saws with diamond segments run slower and need constant water.
- Blade tension even across the frame; check before each block.

## 5. Unloading slabs
- At the end of the cut, the slabs stand free and fragile. Lift with a slab clamp or
  vacuum lifter one at a time; support on A-frames.
- Rinse slurry off immediately — dried slurry stains porous stone.
- Mark each slab with block number and sequence (bookmatching depends on it).

## Gotchas
- Stopping mid-cut (power loss) on a multiwire: restart carefully at reduced feed; a
  slurry-filled kerf can grab the wires.
- Broken slabs at the end of the cut usually mean the block moved or a hidden crack
  opened — check the bedding before blaming the saw.
- Slurry contains fine crystalline silica in granite/quartzite — wet methods and
  housekeeping matter; don't let dried slurry become dust.
- Uncertain: tension, speed and feed values are machine- and stone-specific.

## Verify
- [ ] Block inspected, cut orientation agreed and defects placed in waste.
- [ ] Block trimmed, bedded and bonded so it can't move.
- [ ] Wire count/spacing, tension and water flow checked before starting.
- [ ] Motor load stayed in range throughout the cut.
- [ ] Slabs rinsed, numbered in sequence and stored on frames.
