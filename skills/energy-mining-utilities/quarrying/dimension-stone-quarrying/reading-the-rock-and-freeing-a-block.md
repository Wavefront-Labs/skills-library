# Reading the rock and freeing a block

**Goal:** get large, sound, saleable blocks of granite, marble, limestone or sandstone
out of the bench with as little waste and as few hidden flaws as possible. Unlike
aggregate quarrying, the product is the intact rock — so every cut is planned around
the natural fractures and the stone's grain, and blasting is gentle or absent.

## 1. Read the rock first
Before laying out cuts, walk the bench and map:
- **Joints:** natural fractures, usually in sets. Their spacing sets the maximum
  block size. Spray-mark them on the bench floor and face.
- **Bedding** (sedimentary stone) and **foliation/veining** (marble, slate): the
  appearance customers buy depends on cutting relative to these.
- **Splitting directions** in granite, often called **rift** (easiest), **grain**
  (second) and **hardway** (hardest) — quarrymen learn them from the local stone; they
  decide which way plug-and-feather lines will run cleanly.
- **Defects:** colour changes, open "vents", dry seams, mineral veins, weathered zones,
  stylolites. A hairline crack invisible when wet may open in a slab — wet the
  surface and look along it in low sun, tap for a dull sound.

## 2. Lay out the cut
- Fit the largest commercial block size (driven by the gang saw or customer order;
  often around 3 m × 1.5–2 m × 1.5–2 m for granite and marble) between joints and
  defects. A slightly smaller sound block beats a big flawed one.
- Mark the "primary" cut (freeing a large slice from the bench, sometimes thousands
  of tonnes) and the secondary cuts that square it into blocks.
- Record expected **recovery** (saleable block vs extracted volume); dimension stone
  recovery is often low — 20–50 % is not unusual — so layout matters.

## 3. Choose the method
- **Diamond wire saw:** most common for marble and increasingly granite. Drill two
  intersecting holes (vertical + horizontal) to thread the wire, run the loop, keep
  water flowing to cool and flush. Set wire tension and speed to the machine maker's
  guidance; watch for wire snaps — stand clear of the wire line.
- **Line drilling + splitting:** a row of holes along the planned cut, then wedges
  (**plug and feathers**), **expansive (non-explosive) mortar**, or hydraulic splitters.
  Hole spacing depends on stone and direction (closer on the hardway).
- **Low-energy blasting** (some granite quarries): light charges in closely spaced
  holes, detonating cord or black powder, designed to split rather than shatter.
  Only under a licensed shotfirer and an approved blast plan.
- **Chain/belt saws:** soft limestone, marble, and some underground stone mines.

## 4. Turn, cut down and grade
- A freed slice is toppled onto a cushion bed (crushed rock or soil berm) using
  airbags, hydraulic jacks or an excavator — keep everyone out of the fall zone.
- Square into blocks with wire or splitting; dress off loose edges.
- **Grade** each block: dimensions, defects marked with paint, colour/appearance
  class, number and photograph for the stock yard.

## Gotchas
- Wire saws store huge energy; a snapped wire whips along the cut line. Guards and
  exclusion zones are not optional.
- Toppling a slice onto a hard floor cracks it; build the cushion.
- Expansive mortar is slow (hours to a day) and temperature-dependent; follow the
  product's temperature range.
- Uncertain: block sizes, hole spacings and recovery figures vary greatly by stone
  type and market.

## Verify
- [ ] Joints, bedding and defects mapped and marked before layout.
- [ ] Cut layout fits commercial block sizes between defects.
- [ ] Method chosen to suit stone and grain; blast work (if any) under a licensed shotfirer.
- [ ] Exclusion zones set for wire saw and toppling.
- [ ] Each block graded, numbered, photographed and recorded.
