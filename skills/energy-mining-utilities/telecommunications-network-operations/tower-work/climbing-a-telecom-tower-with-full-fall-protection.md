# Climbing a telecom tower with full fall protection

**Goal:** get up and down a telecom structure (self-supporting lattice, guyed mast or
monopole) and work at height while being **attached 100 % of the time**, with a rescue
plan that can bring a suspended climber down quickly. This is a qualified, trained
activity: certified climber training (e.g. under ANSI A10.48 in the US, or national
schemes elsewhere), a competent supervisor, and your employer's procedure come first.
This skill is the field checklist, not a substitute for training.

## 1. Before the climb
- **Paperwork:** site access approval, work plan, structural limits if adding load,
  RF power-down request for the antennas you'll pass or work near (see RF below).
- **Weather:** no climbing in lightning (stop at first thunder, commonly wait 30
  minutes after the last), ice on the structure, or high wind — limits are set by
  your procedure (often around 40–50 km/h sustained for climbing, lower for hoisting
  large antennas).
- **Crew:** minimum two qualified people on site, one trained in rescue and on the
  ground or able to reach the climber. Never climb alone.
- **Structure check from the ground:** missing or bent members, loose bolts, damaged
  safety-climb cable, guy anchors in good condition, bird nests or wasps.

## 2. Equipment inspection (every climb)
- **Full-body harness:** webbing cuts, burns, stitching, D-rings, buckles, labels
  legible, in date.
- **Twin-leg (Y) energy-absorbing lanyard** for 100 % tie-off; check the absorber
  pack hasn't deployed.
- **Positioning lanyard** for hands-free work.
- **Safety-climb sleeve / cable grab** matched to the installed system (wire rope or
  rail) — mixing brands is usually not permitted.
- Remove anything that's been in a fall; tag it out.

## 3. The climb
- Attach to the **safety-climb system** if installed and certified; otherwise climb
  with the twin lanyard, moving one leg at a time so one is always connected.
- Anchor to members rated for fall arrest (structural members, designed anchor
  points) — never antenna mounts, cable ladders, or feeder clamps unless rated.
- Connect at or above the dorsal D-ring where possible to limit free-fall distance;
  check clearance below (energy absorbers extend ~1 m+).
- Rest regularly using the positioning lanyard; fatigue causes slips.
- Tools tethered; drop zone below cordoned off.

## 4. At the work position
- Two points of contact for work: positioning lanyard around a member plus
  the fall-arrest lanyard tied off above.
- **RF exposure:** antennas you're close to must be powered down or reduced as agreed
  with the operators; wear a personal RF monitor and stop if it alarms.
- Hoisting: tag lines, qualified rigging, communication by radio with the ground.

## 5. Rescue readiness
- A suspended climber can suffer **suspension trauma** (orthostatic intolerance) within
  minutes. The plan must be to reach and lower them fast — typically using a pre-rigged
  rescue kit (descender plus rope) that the crew has practised on this structure type.
- Calling emergency services is part of the plan, not the plan — fire services may
  take far longer than the time available.
- Climbers carry relief straps (trauma straps) and know how to use them.

## Gotchas
- The most common fatal sequence is unhooking for "just a second" to pass an
  obstruction. Use the twin lanyard to pass it.
- Old safety-climb systems may be damaged or uncertified — test or don't use.
- Uncertain: wind limits, rescue timings and certification requirements vary by
  jurisdiction and employer; confirm locally.

## Verify
- [ ] Two qualified people on site, rescue kit staged and plan briefed.
- [ ] Harness, lanyards and cable grab inspected and in date.
- [ ] Weather inside limits; RF power-down confirmed where needed.
- [ ] Attached 100 % from ground to work position and back.
- [ ] Drop zone controlled; tools tethered.
