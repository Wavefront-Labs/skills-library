# Windrow composting: temperature, moisture and turning

**Goal:** turn green and food waste into a stable, pathogen-reduced, saleable compost
in a predictable time, without odour complaints. A windrow is a long pile turned
mechanically; the same principles apply to aerated static piles and in-vessel systems
with different aeration. Home-scale composting is covered in
`agriculture-environment.gardening.home-composting.hot-and-cold-compost-making`.

## 1. Build the recipe
- **Carbon:nitrogen ratio** target roughly 25–30:1. Food waste and grass are
  nitrogen-rich; woody shreddings, leaves and straw are carbon-rich bulking agents.
  Use lab values or book values for each feedstock and calculate the blend by weight.
- **Moisture** about 50–60%. **Squeeze test:** a handful squeezed hard should
  release a drop or two, not a stream (too wet) and should hold together (too dry
  if it crumbles).
- **Structure:** enough coarse material for air to move. Too much fine food waste
  and the pile slumps, goes anaerobic and smells.
- Remove contaminants (plastic, glass, metal) before mixing — they only get harder to
  remove later and ruin the product.

## 2. Pile shape
Windrow height and width are set by the turner. A pile too large overheats in
the middle and goes anaerobic; too small and it loses heat in winter. Keep
size consistent so temperature readings mean the same thing across piles.

## 3. Temperature monitoring
- Probe daily at fixed points (e.g. every 15–30 m) at about two depths, including
  the core. Log date, pile ID, location, readings, turning events, moisture added.
- **Pathogen reduction:** in US rules, windrows must hold **≥55 °C for at least 15
  days, with at least 5 turnings** in that period; aerated static piles and in-vessel
  systems need ≥55 °C for 3 days. Other countries' standards (e.g. PAS 100 in the UK)
  set their own time-temperature regime — **check yours**.
- **Above about 65–70 °C** microbial activity falls and fire risk rises: turn, add
  water, or reduce pile size.
- Temperature dropping early while the material is still raw usually means too dry
  or not enough oxygen, not "finished".

## 4. Turning and water
- Turn when temperatures and oxygen say so, not just by calendar: frequent turning
  early (high activity), less later.
- Add water at turning so it mixes in; water on the top just runs off.
- Turn when the wind is blowing away from neighbours; odour and bioaerosols
  (including Aspergillus spores) are released on turning.

## 5. Odour and water management
- Anaerobic piles smell of rotten eggs or sour vinegar. Fix by adding structure and
  turning, not by masking.
- Site on a sealed pad sloped to a leachate collection system; leachate is high-
  strength and must not reach watercourses. Reuse it on early-stage piles where
  permitted.

## 6. Curing, maturity and screening
- After the active phase, cure for several weeks (often 1–3 months) in larger piles
  with less turning.
- Maturity checks: temperature near ambient after turning, earthy smell, and lab or
  kit tests (self-heating test, respirometry such as Solvita, germination test).
- **Screen** (trommel or star screen) to product grades; return oversize to new
  windrows as bulking agent.
- Sample product against the quality standard (pathogens, heavy metals, physical
  contaminants, stability) before sale.

## Verify
- [ ] Recipe calculated for C:N and moisture; squeeze test passed.
- [ ] Daily probe log with time-temperature compliance demonstrated.
- [ ] Overheating and anaerobic signs acted on promptly.
- [ ] Turning timed for wind; leachate contained.
- [ ] Product cured, maturity-tested, screened and sampled before sale.
