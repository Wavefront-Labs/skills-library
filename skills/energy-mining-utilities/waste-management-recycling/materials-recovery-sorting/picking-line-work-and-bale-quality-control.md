# Picking-line work and bale quality control

**Goal:** turn a mixed recycling stream into bales clean enough that buyers pay full
price and don't reject loads, while keeping the people on the line safe. Most of a
modern MRF is machinery (screens, magnets, eddy-current and optical sorters), but
people on picking stations still make or break quality.

## 1. Where you stand matters
- **Presort** (start of the line): pull anything that will damage machines or hurt
  people downstream — film and bags that wrap disc screens, hoses and cables
  ("tanglers"), large rigid items, gas bottles, propane cylinders, **lithium
  batteries and devices with them** (a crushed battery in a baler or on a belt starts
  fires), sharps.
- **Quality-control stations** (after machines): clean up what the machines
  missed in each product stream.

## 2. Positive vs negative sorting
- **Positive sort:** you pick the target material out of a stream (e.g. pulling
  HDPE bottles off a mixed container line).
- **Negative sort:** you pick the *contaminants* out of an already-concentrated
  stream (e.g. pulling plastics and food-soiled items out of the paper line), so what
  stays on the belt is the product.
Know which you're doing: they need different focus. Negative sorting relies on
recognising what *doesn't* belong.

## 3. Picking technique
- Work the zone in front of you; reach across the belt only as far as your
  station is designed for.
- Use both hands, pick the largest contaminant first, drop into the chute without
  looking away for long.
- Pace: belt speed and burden depth are set by the shift supervisor. If you can't keep
  up, call it — a line running too fast for its pickers just moves contamination into
  the bale. A thin, even burden sorts better than a heavy, layered one.
- Don't wear loose sleeves or jewellery; never reach into nip points; use the
  emergency pull-cord when something goes wrong.

## 4. Keeping machines effective
- **Disc/star screens:** wrapped film and tanglers reduce separation; they need
  scheduled cleaning under full lockout (isolate, lock, try-start, then enter).
- **Overband magnets and eddy-current separators:** check product hoppers for
  misdirected items and splitter positions after cleaning.
- **Optical (NIR) sorters:** dirty lenses and windows, uneven burden and items stacked
  on each other reduce accuracy. Look at both the eject and reject streams.
- Check purity at each machine's output by grab sampling, not just at the baler.

## 5. Bale quality control
1. **Sample bales regularly** — pick bales at random and audit: break a sample
   apart (or sort a defined weight) and weigh target vs contaminant fractions.
2. Compare with the buyer's spec. Contamination limits are specific to the buyer and
   grade (and international import rules for paper and plastics have tightened); the
   commercial team holds the numbers.
3. Check bale weight, density, wire count and condition. Loose, under-wired bales
   break in transport.
4. Label bales (grade, date, line) so rejected loads can be traced to a shift.
5. Feed results back to the line within the shift, not at month's end.

## 6. Fires
MRFs and balers have frequent fire incidents, very often battery-related. Know the
fire detection, the extinguishers on your section and the evacuation route; report
smoke immediately; don't open a smouldering bale yourself.

## Verify
- [ ] Presort removes tanglers, batteries, cylinders and sharps.
- [ ] You know whether your station is a positive or negative sort.
- [ ] Burden thin enough to pick; speed problems escalated.
- [ ] Screen cleaning and jam clearing only under lockout.
- [ ] Bales audited against the buyer spec, labelled, and results fed back.
