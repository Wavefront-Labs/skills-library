# Routine tailings dam surveillance and deposition

**Goal:** keep the tailings facility behaving as designed — pond away from the
embankment, freeboard maintained, seepage and pore pressures within limits — and
escalate the first signs of trouble while there is still time.

> Status: draft. Every facility has an Operation, Maintenance and Surveillance (OMS)
> manual and trigger-action-response plan (TARP) written by its engineer of record;
> those set the actual limits. GISTM (the Global Industry Standard on Tailings
> Management) frames governance for many operators.

## 1. Deposition: where the tailings go
- The aim on most conventional facilities is a **wide, gently sloping beach** from the
  embankment toward the pond, so the water sits **away from the wall**. Water against an
  embankment raises pore pressure and is the main precursor of many failures.
- Rotate active **spigots** (or open-ended discharge points) along the perimeter per the
  deposition plan; depositing too long in one place builds a mound and pushes the pond
  around.
- Watch for the pond creeping toward the wall, beaches eroding into channels, or
  coarse/fine segregation that differs from design.
- Keep pipelines inspected for wear and leaks; tailings slurry is abrasive, and burst
  lines on embankments cause scour.

## 2. Water: decant and freeboard
- Run the **decant** (pump barge, decant tower or spillway) to keep pond volume and
  level inside the operating range. Record pond level daily.
- **Freeboard** — vertical distance between pond level and the embankment crest (or
  spillway) — must stay above the minimum, which typically allows for a design storm
  plus wave run-up. Lose freeboard and you lose storm capacity.
- Track the water balance inputs (slurry water, rainfall) and outputs (decant return,
  evaporation, seepage). A balance that doesn't close often means unexpected seepage.

## 3. Routine inspections
Walk the crest, faces, toe and abutments on the set frequency (often daily or per
shift for an active facility). Look for:
- **Cracks** on the crest or face, especially longitudinal ones.
- **Bulges, slumps or scarps** on slopes.
- **Seepage** at the toe or on the downstream face: new wet spots, flowing water,
  and particularly **cloudy or turbid seepage** (it may be carrying embankment
  material — possible internal erosion).
- **Sinkholes or depressions** on the crest or beach.
- Erosion gullies, blocked drains, damaged instrumentation, animal burrows.
Photograph from the same points each time so changes are obvious.

## 4. Instruments
- **Piezometers** (vibrating-wire or standpipe) measure pore pressure inside the
  embankment and foundation. Read on schedule and compare each value with its
  **trigger levels** in the TARP (often green / amber / red).
- Also: survey prisms or InSAR for movement, flow weirs on seepage collection,
  inclinometers where specified.
- What matters is both the level and the **trend**: steady rise after a pond rise is
  expected; a rise without a cause is not.

## 5. Escalation
Any amber or red trigger, turbid seepage, new cracking or sudden movement →
follow the TARP immediately: notify the responsible person and engineer of record,
increase monitoring, and do what the plan says (stop deposition near the area, lower
the pond, evacuate the inundation zone if required). Don't wait for the next
routine reading to "confirm".

## Gotchas
- Heavy rain events are when freeboard, decant and seepage all change at once.
- A broken piezometer reading "normal" is worse than no reading — flag faulty
  instruments and get them replaced.
- Equipment parked on or near the crest adds load and can mask cracks.

## Verify
- [ ] Pond located away from the embankment; spigot rotation followed.
- [ ] Freeboard measured and above minimum.
- [ ] Inspection completed with photos at fixed points; observations recorded.
- [ ] Piezometers read and compared to triggers and previous trend.
- [ ] Any trigger exceedance escalated per the TARP the same shift.
