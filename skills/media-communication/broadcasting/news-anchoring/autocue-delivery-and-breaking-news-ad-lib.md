# Autocue delivery and breaking-news ad-lib

**Goal:** sound like you're telling the viewer the news, not reading it — and stay calm,
accurate and fluent when the script disappears.

## 1. Before the bulletin
- **Read every script aloud** before air. Mark emphasis, breaths, pronunciation (write
  phonetics into the script: "Nguyen (WIN)"). Check names against the pronunciation
  guide or the reporter.
- **Rewrite for your mouth.** Ask the producer to change sentences you trip on; long
  subordinate clauses and number clusters are the usual offenders.
- Know the **running order**: what's live, what's packaged, where the hard outs are, who
  your guests and correspondents are and what you'll ask them.
- Agree the **prompter operator's** pace style: most follow the presenter; tell them if
  you'll ad-lib so they hold.

## 2. Reading the autocue
- Eyes on the lens, not the text: read slightly ahead, deliver to one person.
- **Thought groups, not lines.** The prompt breaks lines arbitrarily; phrase by sense.
- Emphasise the **new information** in each sentence (usually a verb or number), not every word.
- Vary pace: slow for numbers and names, quicker for transitions.
- Keep a **paper script** in front of you, in order, with pages turned as you go. Prompters fail.

## 3. Talkback (IFB) discipline
- The gallery talks in your ear during live output: countdowns ("30 to the package",
  "wrap"), changes, timings. Train yourself to listen without reacting visibly —
  no glances to the side, no pauses mid-sentence.
- Respond with action, not words: "wrap" means finish your current thought and hand off.
- Agree with the director how you'll signal a problem (a hand signal to the floor manager).
- Know the standard calls in your gallery; the exact wording varies by broadcaster.

## 4. Breaking news ad-lib
- **Say only what's confirmed and say where it comes from:** "Police in X say…", "We're
  getting reports — not yet confirmed by us — that…". Many broadcasters prohibit even that
  for casualties; follow your editorial guidelines.
- **Structure loops:** what we know → what we don't know → where it's coming from → what
  happens next. Repeat for viewers joining; refresh with each new fact.
- **Never speculate on cause, perpetrators or casualty numbers.** Corrections on live TV
  travel further than the original error.
- Be explicit about what's unconfirmed and update when it changes: "Earlier we said…; we
  now understand…".
- Use the producer's notes in your ear and on a tablet/paper; ask for facts, not opinions.
- Fill with useful context (location, background), not repetition of the same line.

## 5. Two-ways and live interviews
- Brief the correspondent on your first question; let them lead with their best material.
- Ask short questions; one at a time. Follow up on the thing they didn't answer.
- Watch the clock in your ear; start wrapping 15-20 seconds before the hard out.
- Live guests: pre-agree what's legally off limits (e.g. active court cases); be ready to
  interject if they defame someone, then clarify on air.

## 6. When things break
- Package doesn't run: "We'll bring you that report shortly," then move to the next item
  the gallery calls.
- Prompter fails: go to paper script without comment.
- Line drops in a two-way: thank them, summarise what they said, move on.
- Rule: **don't apologise at length or explain the technical fault.** Keep the viewer
  oriented and move.

## Verify
- [ ] Every script read aloud beforehand; pronunciations marked.
- [ ] Paper script in order on the desk.
- [ ] In playback: eyes on lens, no visible reaction to talkback.
- [ ] Breaking-news ad-libs stated sources and flagged unconfirmed facts.
- [ ] Hard outs hit within seconds.
