# Continuity junctions and promo reads

**Goal:** the seconds between programmes feel like one channel with one voice: on time,
informative, on brand, and calm when things go wrong.

## 1. What a junction does
A **junction** is the gap between programme end and next programme start, usually
filled by some combination of: back-announce (what you just watched), promos/trailers,
menu ("Coming up tonight…"), ident, then the **forward-announce** into the next show.
The schedule gives a target junction length; your copy must fit it.

## 2. Writing continuity copy
- **Timing first.** Speech runs roughly 2.5-3 words per second for most continuity voices
  (approximate; time yourself). A 10-second forward-announce is ~25-30 words, not 50.
- **Lead with the hook, then the title and time:** "Two friends, one stolen painting — 
  *Title* is next." Titles often go last, landing on the ident or programme start.
- Back-announces: brief, warm, accurate; mention catch-up availability if house style does.
- **Menus**: three items maximum, in time order, each with a one-line hook.
- Write to the channel's **tone guide** (playful kids' channel vs. sober news channel).
  Keep a shared phrase book: approved ways to say "next", "later", "on demand".
- Check every title, time and spelling against the schedule/EPG. Wrong times are the most
  common continuity error.

## 3. Content warnings and compliance
- Pre-programme warnings ("contains strong language and scenes some viewers may find
  upsetting") follow the broadcaster's compliance guidelines and regulator rules
  (e.g. Ofcom in the UK, watershed timing) — *check your house policy and regulator;
  wording is often mandated*.
- Read warnings **before** the programme, clearly, never jokingly.
- Live apologies (for offensive content, wrong programme, technical fault) usually use
  approved wording; get it from the duty editor/compliance.

## 4. Live timing
- Programmes under- or over-run. Have **fill copy** ready (extra menu items, a longer
  forward-announce) and know which promos can be dropped.
- Watch the transmission clock and the next-event countdown on the playout system.
- Hard-timed events (news, live sport, ad breaks with contractual times) take priority;
  trim promos first.

## 5. Voicing promos and trailers
- **Read the brief:** target audience, tone, key message, call to action (title, day, time).
- Mark the script: emphasis on the title and the time; breath points; pace changes
  following the edit.
- Record to picture where possible; hit the music beats and cut points.
- **Tag/end-line** ("Sunday, 9pm") is the most important line — clear, unhurried.
- Supply alternative takes: straight, warmer, more energy; and a few alternate tags
  (different days/times) recorded in the same session for re-versioning.
- Mic technique: consistent distance (a hand-span is typical), pop filter, avoid
  over-compression in your own delivery.

## 6. Keeping one channel voice
- Announcers rotate; the channel shouldn't sound like it. Share recordings of
  reference reads, maintain the tone guide and phrase book, and review a sample of
  junctions each week.
- Pronunciation list for presenters, titles and place names, updated with each new show.

## Technical failures
Black screen or wrong programme: announce briefly and calmly ("Apologies — we're sorting a
technical fault and will be back with *Title* shortly"), let playout put up the
apology slide/filler, and update every couple of minutes. No speculation about cause.

## Verify
- [ ] Every junction script timed aloud to its slot.
- [ ] Titles, times and spellings checked against the schedule/EPG.
- [ ] Required warnings read in approved wording before the programme.
- [ ] Fill copy and droppable promos identified for every junction.
- [ ] Promo tag read clearly; alternates recorded.
