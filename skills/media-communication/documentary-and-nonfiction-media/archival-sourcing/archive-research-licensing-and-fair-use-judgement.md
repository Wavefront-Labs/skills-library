# Archive research, licensing and fair-use judgement

**Goal:** get the archive the film needs at a price the budget can bear, with rights that
match where the film will be shown — and documentation that will satisfy a broadcaster's
legal review and an errors-and-omissions (E&O) insurer. Not legal advice: rights law varies
by country, and final decisions belong with production counsel.

## 1. Research
- **Sources:** broadcaster and news archives, national film/sound archives, commercial stock
  libraries, newsreel collections, university and museum collections, government archives
  (US federal works are generally public domain; other governments differ), home movies from
  families, and contributors' own photos and videos.
- Request **viewing copies** (watermarked/low-res) to cut with; order masters only for
  what's in the locked cut.
- Log for every item: source, item ID, description, timecode in/out, who owns the rights
  (not always the archive holding it), any third-party content inside it (music, artwork,
  identifiable people, logos).

## 2. Licensing basics
- Fees are usually **per second or per minute used**, often with a minimum, and depend on
  **media** (TV, streaming, theatrical, festival, educational, all media), **territory**
  (one country → worldwide), and **term** (years → perpetuity). Ask for quotes on several
  combinations; "all media, worldwide, perpetuity" is the most expensive and what many
  distributors want.
- **Festival licences** are a cheaper interim option — but budget for upgrading if the film sells.
- Read for **embedded rights**: an archive may license the footage but not the music playing
  in it or a performer's appearance (e.g. union reuse fees for some broadcast material).
- Photos: license from the photographer or agency; "found on the internet" is not a source.

## 3. Music
Needs two licences: **sync** (the composition, from the publisher) and **master use** (the
recording, from the label) — unless using library/production music with a blanket or
per-use licence, or commissioning an original score. Popular tracks can cost more than the
rest of the archive budget; check early.

## 4. Fair use / fair dealing judgement
- **US fair use** weighs four factors (purpose — especially transformative use such as
  commentary or critique; nature of the work; amount used; market effect). Documentaries
  that use material to make a point *about* it, using no more than needed, often have a
  strong position. The documentary community's *Statement of Best Practices in Fair Use* sets
  out commonly accepted situations; many E&O insurers accept fair-use claims backed by a
  lawyer's opinion letter.
- **Fair dealing** (UK, Canada, Australia and others) is narrower: specific purposes such as
  criticism and review, quotation, or news reporting, with sufficient acknowledgement.
  "It's a documentary" isn't itself a defence.
- Decide fair use *with counsel*, document the reasoning per clip, and don't use it as
  a way to avoid paying for material you're simply using as illustration (wallpaper).
- **Public domain** status depends on country and date; verify, and note that a restored
  copy or a specific transfer may carry its own access terms.
- **Orphan works** (owner can't be found): document diligent searches; some countries have
  orphan-works schemes. Insurer comfort varies.

## 5. Clearance log and budget
- A spreadsheet per item: description, timecode in the film, duration, source, rights
  holder, licence terms (media/territory/term), fee, status (requested / quoted / licensed /
  fair use with memo), contract reference, credit wording.
- Budget archive as a line with contingency — first-time producers commonly underestimate
  it badly. Negotiate package deals with one archive for many clips.
- Deliver the log and contracts to the distributor; credits as contracts require.

## Gotchas
- Cutting with a clip for months and discovering it's unaffordable or uncleared.
- Assuming the archive owns what it holds.
- Licences that cover festival only when the film sells to streaming.
- Identifiable people in archive used in a defamatory or private context.

## Verify
- [ ] Every archival item in the cut appears in the clearance log with rights holder and status.
- [ ] Licence terms (media, territory, term) match the distribution plan.
- [ ] Music has both sync and master licences, or a library/original-score licence.
- [ ] Each fair-use item has a written rationale reviewed by counsel.
- [ ] Contracts and the log are ready for the E&O application.
