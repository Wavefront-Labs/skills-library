# Paper edits from transcripts and narration writing

**Goal:** find and fix the structure on paper before the editor spends weeks in the timeline,
and write narration (if any) that adds what pictures and interviews can't — briefly, and
accurately.

In nonfiction, the script is usually written *after* (or during) shooting, from what was
actually captured. You are not inventing dialogue; you are selecting and ordering reality.

## 1. Transcribe and log everything
- Transcribe all interviews and key vérité scenes with **source timecode** (or clip name +
  timecode). Automated transcription is good enough for logging; correct anything you'll
  quote.
- Log vérité and B-roll too: a line per usable moment ("02_A014 14:03:10 — Maria finds the
  eviction letter, long silence").
- **Highlight selects**: the strongest 5–10% of each transcript. Tag each by theme,
  character and story beat.

## 2. Build the paper edit
A paper edit is the film written as a list of clips in order.
- Common format: a table or two-column script — **left:** picture (vérité, archive, B-roll);
  **right:** sound (interview bite, sync, narration, music) — with source timecode on each line.
- Start with a **structure outline**: opening (hook + question), act one (world and
  characters), act two (complications), act three (climax and resolution), ending (image
  or line that answers or reframes the question).
- Drop selects into the outline. Then cut: most first paper edits are two to three times
  too long.
- Look for **bites that carry structure** — a character stating what they want, a
  turning-point admission. These anchor scenes.
- Mark gaps: places where information is missing that could be filled with a pickup
  interview, archive, a graphic, or narration.
Editing software can turn transcripts into rough timelines (text-based editing in several
NLEs); the thinking still happens on paper.

## 3. Honesty while selecting
- Don't splice parts of an answer so it means something the speaker didn't mean.
- Don't put an interview bite against footage of an event it doesn't refer to as if it did.
- If you change order of events for clarity, make sure the overall chronology and causality
  aren't misrepresented. Many broadcasters' guidelines treat misleading chronology as a
  serious breach.
- Keep a **source sheet** for every factual claim, whether spoken by a contributor or narration.

## 4. Writing narration
- **Write to picture**: narration should add what isn't on screen — context, time jumps,
  facts — not describe what viewers can see.
- **Less is more**: leave room for sync sound and silence. A common rule of thumb is about
  2.5–3 spoken words per second, so time each line against the gap it fills.
- Short sentences, spoken rhythm, present tense often works for vérité ("It's 5am. The
  first shift starts in an hour.").
- **Hand over cleanly** to interview bites: don't repeat what the next person says.
- Read it aloud against picture; rewrite anything you stumble on.
- Decide early whether the film has a narrator at all — many vérité films avoid it,
  using captions/title cards instead.

## 5. Fact-check before lock
Before picture lock, check every narration line and on-screen caption against the source
sheet; check names, titles, dates, numbers. Send contributors factual-accuracy questions if
needed (not editorial approval).

## Gotchas
- Logging without timecode, so selects can't be found again.
- Treating the paper edit as final — it's a hypothesis to test in the cut.
- Narration that explains the emotion ("a heartbreaking moment") instead of trusting the scene.
- Last-minute narration rewrites without fact-checking.

## Verify
- [ ] All interviews transcribed with timecode; selects tagged by beat.
- [ ] Paper edit has picture/sound columns and a clear act structure.
- [ ] No bite is used to mean something its speaker didn't.
- [ ] Narration lines are timed to their gaps and add information not on screen.
- [ ] Every factual claim traces to a source on the source sheet.
