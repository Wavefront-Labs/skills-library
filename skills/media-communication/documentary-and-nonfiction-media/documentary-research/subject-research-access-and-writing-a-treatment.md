# Subject research, access and writing a treatment

**Goal:** turn an idea into a fundable documentary: a story with stakes that will unfold or
can be reconstructed, contributors who've agreed to take part on fair terms, and a treatment
that makes a commissioner or funder see the film.

## 1. Is there a film here?
A topic is not a story. Test the idea:
- **Who** do we follow? (a character with something at stake)
- **What do they want**, and what stands in the way?
- **What will happen** during filming — or what happened that we can reconstruct with
  archive, testimony and evidence?
- **Why now**, and **why us** (special access, expertise, relationship)?
- **What's the film's question** — the thing the audience will want answered by the end?
Write a **logline** (one or two sentences: character, goal, obstacle, stakes). If you can't,
keep researching.

## 2. Research
- **Desk research:** press archives, academic work, court records, public filings, prior
  films (what's been done; what's the fresh angle?).
- **Map contributors:** main characters, experts, opponents, family members, officials —
  and who controls access to each.
- **Pre-interviews** (phone or in person, usually unfilmed): listen for how they talk, what
  they'll reveal, whether they're comfortable on camera. Take careful notes; don't promise
  what the film will say.
- Fact-check central claims early — a film built on a story that later collapses is
  expensive.

## 3. Negotiating access
- Be honest about what the film is, who it's for (if known), how long filming may take, and
  that they won't control the edit (if that's the arrangement). Ambiguity here causes most
  later disputes.
- Institutions (hospitals, police, schools, companies) usually need a written **access
  agreement**: what you can film, who must consent, legal review points, factual-accuracy
  checks versus editorial control. Don't give away editorial control; fact-checking access
  is often a reasonable compromise.
- Build trust with time: visit without cameras, keep promises, return calls.
- Have alternatives: if your main access fails, is there still a film?

## 4. Ethical groundwork with participants
- **Informed consent:** they understand the film's purpose, distribution (TV, streaming,
  worldwide, forever online), and risks to them. Re-confirm consent as circumstances change.
- **Vulnerable contributors** (minors, people in crisis, those at legal or physical risk):
  assess duty of care; broadcasters often require a risk assessment and psychological
  support plan. Consider anonymisation from the start.
- Payment: practices differ (some outlets forbid paying contributors; expenses are usually
  fine). Check the commissioner's guidelines.
- Never stage events and present them as real; reconstructions must be signposted.

## 5. The treatment (2–10 pages, typically)
1. **Title and logline.**
2. **Synopsis** — the story as the viewer will experience it, in present tense, with the
   main characters and arc.
3. **Characters** — who they are, what they want, why we'll care.
4. **Structure** — beginning/middle/end or episode outline for a series.
5. **Style and approach** — vérité, interview-led, archive-driven, presenter-led; visual
   references; tone.
6. **Access** — what's secured and how.
7. **Why now / why us.**
8. **Team** — director, producer credits.
9. **Budget level and schedule** (for funders; commissioners may want this later).
Pair with a **pitch deck** (images, characters, key beats) and, if possible, a short
**taster tape** (2–5 minutes) showing characters on camera — often decisive.

## Gotchas
- Pitching a topic ("a film about loneliness") instead of a story.
- Overpromising contributors approval rights you can't keep.
- Exclusive access agreements nobody put in writing.
- Researchers bonding with subjects and losing distance — keep checking claims.

## Verify
- [ ] A logline states character, goal, obstacle and stakes.
- [ ] Main contributors have been pre-interviewed and agreed in principle; access terms written.
- [ ] Consent explanations covered purpose, distribution and editorial control.
- [ ] Vulnerable-contributor risks assessed with a support plan.
- [ ] Treatment covers synopsis, characters, structure, style, access and team.
