# Narrative podcast structure and scene recording

**Goal:** an audio story where listeners *see* places through sound, feel momentum from
scene to scene, and trust a host who sounds like a person, not a script.

## 1. Structure for the ear
- The basic building block, often taught in public-radio circles, is **anecdote +
  reflection**: a sequence of action ("and then… and then…") that builds curiosity, followed
  by a moment of meaning ("Here's why that mattered"). Alternate them.
- **Open in a scene**, not with context. Context arrives once the listener cares.
- Pose a **question** early that the episode (or series) answers; plant smaller questions
  to carry each segment.
- Listeners can't skim back easily: **signpost** ("There are two things you need to know
  about Dan"), repeat key names, and keep the number of characters small.
- Episode length follows the story; many narrative episodes land at 25–50 minutes, with
  acts of 8–15 minutes.
- Series: each episode needs its own arc plus a reason to press play on the next.

## 2. Recording scenes in the field
- **Kit:** a portable recorder (Zoom/Tascam/Sound Devices class) with a **shotgun or
  cardioid handheld mic**, closed-back headphones, a windshield (and a furry cover outdoors),
  spare batteries and cards. Record WAV at 48 kHz/24-bit (common broadcast standard).
- **Get close** — 15–30 cm from a voice. Distance is the biggest quality killer.
- **Monitor always**: listen for wind, handling noise, clipping. Set levels so speech peaks
  around −12 to −6 dBFS and use a −12 dB safety track if your recorder offers it.
- **Record the scene, not just the talk:** arrival, doors, footsteps, the kettle, the
  machine starting. These sounds let the listener into the place and give the editor
  transitions.
- **Ambience/room tone:** 1–2 minutes of each location for building beds and covering edits.
- **In-scene narration:** have the host or subject describe what they see ("We're on the
  roof; the whole valley is under water") — it's gold in the edit.
- For sit-down interviews, use a soft room (sofa, curtains, closet of clothes) — reverb
  sounds amateur and can't be removed fully.

## 3. Interviews for audio
Same principles as film interviews (open questions, silence after answers), plus: ask for
**stories, not opinions** ("Take me to that morning"), ask for sensory detail, and ask people
to restate in full sentences. Avoid crosstalk and verbal "mm-hm"s.

## 4. Host narration (tracking)
- Write **for the ear**: short sentences, one idea each, spoken contractions, active verbs,
  no clauses that delay the point.
- Write *around* tape: set up the clip so the listener knows who's speaking and why it
  matters, then get out of the way; after the clip, don't repeat it.
- Be yourself on mic: the host's curiosity and doubt are part of the story in many narrative
  podcasts (but not all formats suit a present host).
- Record tracks in a quiet, dead space; stand or sit up straight; mark up the script for
  emphasis; re-read lines rather than patching syllables.
- Read the script aloud with the tape before recording final tracks — and rewrite.

## 5. Sound-led editing
- **Script/edit loop:** paper edit (see
  media-communication.documentary-and-nonfiction-media.nonfiction-scripting.paper-edits-from-transcripts-and-narration-writing),
  rough mix, table read with an editor, revise. Narrative episodes typically go through
  several edits.
- **Music** marks shifts in time or mood; use it sparingly and let it end with purpose
  ("button" before a key line). License it properly.
- **Loudness:** target a consistent integrated loudness — around −16 LUFS for stereo podcasts
  is widely used (some platforms normalise to about −14); broadcast often uses −23/−24 LUFS
  depending on region.
- Ethics match film: don't cut answers to change meaning; signpost reconstructions or
  re-voiced quotes.

## Gotchas
- Opening with five minutes of throat-clearing context.
- Recording interviews from a metre away in a kitchen with tile floors.
- Too many voices that sound alike — listeners lose track.
- Narration that describes the tape the listener just heard.

## Verify
- [ ] Episode opens in a scene and poses a question in the first minutes.
- [ ] Field tape includes scene sound and ambience, not just interviews.
- [ ] Voice recordings close-mic'd, monitored and free of clipping and wind.
- [ ] Narration reads naturally aloud and doesn't repeat clips.
- [ ] Final mix meets the target loudness consistently.
