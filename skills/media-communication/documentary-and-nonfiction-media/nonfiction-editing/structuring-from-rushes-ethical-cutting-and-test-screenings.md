# Structuring from rushes, ethical cutting and test screenings

**Goal:** turn a mountain of footage (often 100:1 shooting ratio or more) into a structured,
emotionally coherent film that's fair to the people in it — and know when it works by
watching it with an audience.

In documentary, the edit is where the film is largely written; plan months, not weeks, for
features.

## 1. Organise before cutting
- Ingest with consistent naming (date_camera_card), sync multi-cam and dual-system sound,
  and attach transcripts.
- **Bins** by scene/day, by character, interviews, archive, B-roll, music.
- **Log** every clip with a one-line description and markers on key moments. Logging feels
  slow and saves weeks later.
- The director or producer should watch everything at least once; the editor should too.

## 2. String-outs → scenes
- For each scene, make a **string-out**: all the usable material in order, trimmed of dead
  time. Then cut each into a **scene** that works on its own (a beginning, a turn, an end).
- For interviews, make **theme string-outs**: every answer about X from every contributor.
  Patterns, contradictions and the best bites become obvious.
- Rate scenes (A/B/C) for strength.

## 3. Structure with cards
- Write each scene on a card (physical or digital board) with its story function:
  *establishes character, raises stakes, reveals, complicates, resolves*.
- Arrange into acts on a wall. Ask: Where's the hook? When does the audience learn what the
  film is about (usually within the first 5–10 minutes of a feature)? Where's the midpoint
  shift? What's the climax? Does the ending answer or reframe the opening question?
- Expect to rebuild structure several times. The **assembly** (everything in order) will be
  long and slow; the **rough cut** finds the shape; the **fine cut** polishes rhythm; then
  **picture lock**.
- Kill darlings: a great scene that doesn't serve the story goes.
- Chronology is one option; parallel timelines, a frame story, or an investigation's
  order of discovery may serve better — but don't mislead about what happened when.

## 4. Cutting interviews honestly
- **Frankenbites** (stitching words from different parts of an interview into one
  sentence) are tempting and dangerous. A tight edit that keeps meaning is normal; creating a
  statement the person never made is a breach of trust and of most broadcasters' guidelines.
- Cover edits with cutaways or a visible jump cut; don't hide a meaning change.
- Reaction shots must come from the same moment, or at least not imply a reaction the person
  didn't have.
- Don't let music or juxtaposition make a contributor look ridiculous or guilty unfairly.
- Contributors who'll be portrayed critically should, under most editorial codes, be given
  an opportunity to respond to significant allegations (right of reply) — flag this to
  producers before lock.

## 5. Test screenings
- Show rough cuts to **people who don't know the material**: a small group (5–15) plus
  trusted filmmakers. Watch the audience, not the screen: where do they shift, check phones,
  laugh, lean in?
- Afterwards ask open questions first: "What was the film about? Who did you care about?
  Where were you confused? Where were you bored?" Then specific ones.
- Listen for **problems**, not solutions — audiences are good at telling you *where* it's
  broken and bad at prescribing fixes.
- Screen more than once; each structure change needs fresh eyes.
- Before lock, do a **compliance/legal review** (defamation, privacy, archive clearance,
  music) — see media-communication.documentary-and-nonfiction-media.archival-sourcing.archive-research-licensing-and-fair-use-judgement.

## Gotchas
- Starting to cut before logging, and forgetting the best moment exists.
- Falling in love with the first assembly's structure.
- Over-scoring with music to manufacture emotion.
- Test screening only with the crew's friends.

## Verify
- [ ] All footage logged and transcripts attached before structural editing.
- [ ] Every scene card states its story function.
- [ ] No interview edit changes what a contributor meant.
- [ ] At least two screenings with fresh audiences, with notes on confusion and boredom points.
- [ ] Legal/compliance review done before picture lock.
