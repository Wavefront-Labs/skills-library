# Building and working a beat

**Goal:** within a few months, you know your patch's people, papers and calendar well
enough that tips come to you and you see stories in routine material before anyone else.

## 1. Week one: map the beat
Make a beat map (one document) covering:
- **Institutions:** who has power, money and decision rights (agencies, companies,
  courts, councils, unions, regulators, NGOs, academics).
- **Decision calendar:** board/council meetings, court lists, budget cycles, earnings,
  annual reports, inspection reports, statistical releases.
- **Document streams:** agendas and minutes, court dockets, regulatory filings, planning
  applications, inspection databases, procurement notices, company registries.
- **Past coverage:** read 1-2 years of your outlet's and competitors' clips. Note
  unresolved stories — they're your first leads.

## 2. Build the contact book
- **Go meet people without needing a story.** "I'm new on the beat, what should I
  understand?" is the best question you'll ever ask. Coffee beats phone; phone beats email.
- Cultivate at **every level**: the official spokesperson, but also clerks, deputies,
  union reps, retired insiders, critics, lawyers who litigate in the area. The best
  information sits below the top.
- For each contact record: role, how you met, best channel, what they know, what they
  care about, last contact date. Revisit contacts you haven't spoken to in ~6 weeks
  (rough cadence; adapt to your beat).
- **Protect the relationship by being fair and accurate,** not by being soft. Sources
  respect a reporter who gets it right, including when the story hurts them. Tell them
  before a critical story runs (right of reply), never let them be surprised.

**Gotcha:** don't let a small set of friendly sources become your beat. Capture is gradual —
if you find yourself writing their framing, call their critics.

## 3. Weekly routines
- **Monday scan:** every agenda, docket and release calendar for the week. Flag items
  with money, conflict, change or affected people.
- **Read documents in full**, especially appendices and consultant reports attached to
  agendas — that's where the buried numbers live.
- **Set alerts:** saved searches on court systems, regulators' sites, company filings,
  and keyword alerts (Google Alerts, RSS) for key names.
- **Keep a story list:** ideas, status, next step. Aim to always have one quick story, one
  medium story, and one long project in progress.

## 4. Spotting stories competitors miss
- **Follow the money:** budget lines that change sharply, contracts without competition,
  consultants hired repeatedly.
- **Compare over time:** this year's inspection results vs last year's; promises made at
  launch vs outcomes.
- **Read what's missing:** a regular report that didn't appear, a meeting item moved to
  closed session, a person who stopped appearing.
- **Talk to the people affected,** not just the institution. Your beat's users are sources too.
- **Use data** the institution publishes but nobody analyses (see data-journalism skills).

## 5. Developing exclusives
Hold a tip until it's confirmed by a second independent source or document. Ask the
"who else knows" question to judge how long you have before it leaks elsewhere.

## Verify
- [ ] Beat map lists institutions, calendar and document streams.
- [ ] Contact book has 30+ contacts across levels within the first months (rough benchmark).
- [ ] Weekly scan done; story list has quick/medium/long items.
- [ ] At least one recent story came from documents or tips rather than press releases.
- [ ] Critics of your main sources are in your contact book.
