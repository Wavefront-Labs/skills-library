# Structuring a narrative feature or profile

**Goal:** a long piece that reads like a story — characters, scenes, tension — while
every detail is as reported and sourced as hard news.

## 1. Report for scenes, not just facts
Features fail at the reporting stage more than the writing stage. While reporting, collect:
- **Scenes:** moments with a place, time, people and action. Be there if you can.
- **Sensory detail you witnessed** — what the room looked/sounded like, what people did
  with their hands. Note it at the time; you can't invent it later.
- **Dialogue and exact words**, with who said them.
- **Documents and timelines** that let you reconstruct scenes you missed.
- For profiles: the subject *doing* things, plus 5-10 people who know them from different
  angles (allies, critics, family, colleagues past and present).

**Rule of thumb (approximate):** you'll use perhaps a tenth of what you gather. That's the
point — the best detail is selected from abundance.

## 2. Find the spine
Before outlining, write two sentences:
1. **The nut graf:** why this story, why now, why should a reader care? It usually
   appears after the opening scene, around paragraphs 3-6.
2. **The question the story answers** (or the change the character undergoes). This is
   what keeps a reader going through 4,000 words.

## 3. Choose a structure
- **Anecdotal open → nut graf → body → kicker.** The workhorse. Opening scene must
  illustrate the larger point, not just be colourful.
- **Chronological narrative.** Strong when there's a clear arc (a rescue, a trial). Open
  at a high-tension moment, then flash back to the start.
- **Braided / alternating threads.** Two timelines or characters converging. Hard to
  control; label sections clearly.
- **Profile structure:** scene with subject → nut graf (why now) → biography in brief →
  themed sections (work, conflict, private life) each opened with a scene → the
  revealing moment → kicker.

Outline on index cards or a list of section headers with the scene, the purpose and the
key fact for each. If a section has no purpose, it goes.

## 4. Scene reconstruction — the accuracy trap
- Reconstructed scenes (events you didn't witness) need **multiple sources or
  documentation** for each detail: who said what, the weather, the time.
- Don't put thoughts in people's heads unless they told you what they were thinking,
  and attribute it ("she remembers thinking…").
- Composite characters and compressed timelines are not acceptable in mainstream
  journalism; if you must compress, say so in the text.
- Keep a sourced annotation for every scene detail — fact-checkers will ask.

## 5. Pacing and voice
- Alternate **scene** (slow, detailed, in the moment) with **summary** (fast, explanatory).
  Three expository sections in a row and readers drop off.
- Put context in small doses where the reader needs it, not as a front-loaded history block.
- Voice: confident, specific, restrained. Let detail carry emotion; adjectives rarely do.
- Quotes in features should reveal character — the strangest, truest line, not the
  most informative.

## 6. The ending
Common kickers: return to the opening scene changed; a forward-looking moment; a quote
that resonates with the nut graf. Avoid summarising or moralising.

## Verify
- [ ] Nut graf exists and appears early enough that the reader knows why they're reading.
- [ ] Each section has a stated purpose and opens with something concrete.
- [ ] Every reconstructed detail has a source in your annotation file.
- [ ] No unattributed interior thoughts; no composites or undisclosed compression.
- [ ] Profile includes critical as well as friendly voices.
- [ ] Ending doesn't summarise; it lands.
