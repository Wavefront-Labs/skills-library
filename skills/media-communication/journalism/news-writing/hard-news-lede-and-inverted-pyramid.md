# Hard-news lede and inverted pyramid

**Goal:** a reader who stops after any paragraph still has the most important facts so
far, and an editor can cut from the bottom without breaking the story.

## 1. Find the news before you type
Answer in one sentence: **what happened that wasn't true yesterday, and who does it
affect?** If you can't, you're not ready to write. Rank the five Ws + how by what *this*
audience needs first — usually what happened and to whom, then when/where, then why.

## 2. The lede
- **One sentence, roughly 25–35 words.** Longer ledes almost always contain two stories.
- Subject–verb–object, active voice, a strong concrete verb: "The council voted 7-2 on
  Tuesday to close…" not "There was a decision made…".
- **Lead with the newest development**, not the background. In a running story, today's
  change goes up top; the original event drops to paragraph 3-4.
- Avoid: starting with a time element or a dependent clause, question ledes, quotes as
  ledes, "officials announced" when the announcement isn't the news.
- **Attribution in the lede** when the claim is contested or comes from one party:
  "…police said." Uncontested, verifiable facts don't need it.

## 3. Building the pyramid
Typical order for a 400-600 word hard-news piece:
1. Lede (the news).
2. Second graf: the most important supporting fact or the "so what" (impact, numbers).
3. Best quote, attributed — one that adds emotion or authority, not one that repeats facts.
4. Key details: who/when/where/how specifics.
5. Context/background: why this matters, what happened before.
6. Reaction, other side, secondary details.
7. What happens next (if not already higher).

**Gotcha:** "what happens next" is often more useful to readers than background; don't
bury a vote date or deadline at the bottom where it will be cut.

## 4. Attribution and quotes
- **"Said"** is neutral; prefer it to "claimed", "admitted", "insisted" (each implies
  judgement). Wire style: name first, then "said": "…, Smith said."
- Full name and title on first reference, surname after (house style varies).
- Paraphrase facts; quote opinion, emotion, and distinctive phrasing.
- Every accusation needs a response from the accused, or a sentence saying you sought one:
  "X did not respond to requests for comment on Tuesday."
- Don't stack quotes; put a transition sentence between speakers.

## 5. Tight copy on deadline
- Write the lede, the next two grafs, and **file a short version first** (a 3-graf
  "first take"). Then expand. Wire services work this way for good reason.
- Cut: "in order to" → "to"; "currently"; "that" where not needed; adjectives that
  editorialise; numbers past what the reader needs (one key figure per sentence).
- Paragraphs of one or two sentences in news style.
- Numbers, dates, ages and spellings: house style (AP, Reuters, or the outlet's own — the
  conventions differ, e.g. numerals vs words for under ten; check yours).

## 6. Rewrites as news moves
Each new development → new lede, demote the old one, update the timestamp and the "what
next". Re-read the whole story for contradictions: a casualty figure updated in the lede
but not in graf 6 is a common error.

## Verify
- [ ] The lede alone tells the news, in ≤35 words, active voice.
- [ ] Cutting the last three paragraphs loses nothing essential.
- [ ] Every contested claim is attributed; every accused party has a response or a
      "did not respond" line.
- [ ] Numbers in the lede match numbers in the body.
- [ ] House style checked for numerals, titles and dates.
