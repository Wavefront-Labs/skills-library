# Editing reporters' copy for accuracy and legal risk

**Goal:** the story that publishes is clearer, fairer and legally safer than the one
filed — and still recognisably the reporter's.

## 1. Before you touch it: does it stand up?
Read the full story once without editing. Answer:
- What's the news, in one sentence? Is that what the lede says?
- **How do we know?** For each key claim: document, named source, anonymous source,
  or inference?
- Who is harmed if this is wrong? Have they had a chance to respond?
- What's missing — the obvious question a reader will ask?
If the answers are weak, the fix is more reporting, not editing.

## 2. Three passes
**Pass 1 — Structure.** Lede carries the news; nut/context early; best material high; no
section repeats another; ending doesn't editorialise. Move blocks before polishing sentences.

**Pass 2 — Accuracy.** Check names, titles, numbers (recompute percentages), dates,
places, quote attribution. Compare against the reporter's notes or documents for anything
high-risk. Make sure the headline, standfirst, captions and social copy match the body —
legal problems often live in display copy written by someone else.

**Pass 3 — Legal and fairness.** Read as the subject's lawyer would.

## 3. Legal red flags (jurisdiction matters — consult your media lawyer)
- **Defamation:** any statement lowering someone's reputation. Ask: can we *prove* it,
  with admissible evidence, in its natural and ordinary meaning (including what it
  implies)? Strong verbs to scrutinise: *fraud, lied, corrupt, stole, covered up,
  bribed, cheated.* Replace with what the evidence shows ("records show…").
- **Juxtaposition and implication:** a photo or sentence placement can defame even if each
  sentence is true.
- **Allegations:** "alleged" doesn't cure defamation if you repeat an unprovable charge;
  attribution to privileged sources (court documents, parliament) is safer — rules vary.
- **Contempt / sub judice (notably UK, Australia, Ireland, Canada):** once someone is
  arrested or charged, avoid prejudicial material — previous convictions, confessions,
  assertions of guilt, identifying photos where ID is in issue. US practice is much looser.
- **Automatic anonymity:** victims of sexual offences, and juveniles in many courts,
  can't be identified — including via jigsaw identification across details.
- **Privacy and confidentiality:** health, children, private addresses.
Escalate anything uncertain to the lawyer before publication, not after.

## 4. Working with the reporter
- Ask questions, don't just rewrite: "How do we know this?" "What did they say when you
  put it to them?" "Is 'dozens' 24 or 60?"
- Make changes visible (track changes or CMS revisions) and discuss substantive ones.
- Preserve voice in features; tighten aggressively in news.
- Never insert facts the reporter hasn't confirmed.

## 5. News judgement and story selection
When choosing what runs and where: weigh impact (how many affected, how badly), novelty,
proximity to your audience, conflict, prominence, and whether you can stand it up *now*.
Being second and right beats being first and wrong — especially with names and casualties.

## 6. Sign-off
Before publishing confirm: right of reply done and reflected; legal reviewed if flagged;
headline/display copy checked against body; corrections from the reporter merged; the
story has a named responsible editor.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state how we know every allegation in the story.
- [ ] Headline, dek, captions and social copy are as accurate as the body.
- [ ] Defamation-risk words replaced with evidenced descriptions.
- [ ] Contempt and anonymity rules checked for any criminal-justice story.
- [ ] Subject's response is in the story.
- [ ] Reporter has seen substantive changes.
