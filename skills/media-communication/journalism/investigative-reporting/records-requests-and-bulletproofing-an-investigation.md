# Records requests and bulletproofing an investigation

**Goal:** an investigation whose every allegation is documented, fairly put to the
subject, legally reviewed, and published without exposing a source.

## 1. Write the hypothesis memo first
One page: the allegation you suspect is true, what evidence would prove it, what would
disprove it, who'd know, and which records exist. Revisit it weekly. Investigations die
from drift; they also go wrong when the reporter stops looking for disproof.

## 2. Public-records (FOI/FOIA) requests
Laws differ by country and state (US FOIA and state laws, UK FOIA 2000, Australia FOI Act
1982, etc.). Deadlines, fees and exemptions vary — *check your jurisdiction's statute*.
Practical method across most regimes:
- **Know the record exists** before asking. Find it referenced in a budget, an audit,
  a retention schedule, minutes or a prior release. Ask for it by name.
- **Narrow the request:** date range, named custodians, document types. Broad requests
  get refused on cost grounds or take a year.
- **Ask for data in native format** (spreadsheet/CSV, not PDF printouts).
- Calendar the statutory deadline; follow up the day it lapses; appeal refusals —
  exemptions are often over-applied and appeals work more often than people expect.
- Keep a request log: ID, date sent, deadline, contact, status, outcome.

## 3. Document analysis
- **Build a master timeline** (spreadsheet: date, event, source document, page). Most
  investigative findings emerge from contradictions on the timeline.
- Build a **people/entities sheet**: names, roles, companies, addresses, relationships.
- Corporate and property registries, court records, campaign finance, procurement
  portals and sanctions lists are the usual cross-references.
- Track **provenance** for every document: where it came from, when, how verified.

## 4. Source protection
- Assume communications can be subpoenaed or seized. Move sensitive sources to
  end-to-end-encrypted channels (e.g. Signal with disappearing messages) early.
- Don't contact a confidential source from work accounts or devices tied to their employer.
- Leaked documents can carry **identifying traces**: printer tracking dots, unique
  edits, watermarks, metadata. Never publish raw scans of a leak without checking —
  retype or describe where possible.
- Keep source identities out of shared drafts and notes; use codes.

## 5. Right of reply ("no surprises" letter)
Before publication, send each person or organisation you'll criticise a **written** list
of the specific allegations and facts you intend to publish, with a reasonable deadline
(often several working days for complex stories; norms vary). Include every adverse claim.
Their answer may correct you — that's the point. Report their response fairly.

## 6. Bulletproofing
- **Line-by-line evidence memo:** each sentence → supporting documents/sources. Any
  sentence without backing is cut or softened.
- **Legal review** by your outlet's media lawyer. Defamation law varies sharply by
  jurisdiction (UK and Australia are more claimant-friendly than the US); the lawyer will
  look at meaning, truth/justification, public interest defences and your conduct.
- Choose words for what you can **prove**: "records show payments to…" not "bribed".
- Pre-plan publication: response to likely legal letters, follow-ups, and what you'll do
  if the subject pre-empts you.

## Verify
- [ ] Hypothesis memo includes the disproving evidence you looked for.
- [ ] Request log is current; refusals appealed where justified.
- [ ] Every allegation in the draft maps to documents in the evidence memo.
- [ ] Every subject received a written, specific right-of-reply with a fair deadline.
- [ ] Leaked material checked for identifying traces; sources not named in shared files.
- [ ] Legal sign-off obtained on the final text, headline and images.
