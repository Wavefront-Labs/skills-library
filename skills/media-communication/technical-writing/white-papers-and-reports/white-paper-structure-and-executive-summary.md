# White paper structure and the executive summary

**Goal:** a long-form document whose main point a decision-maker gets in one minute,
whose argument a specialist can check, and whose evidence is honest about its limits.

## 1. Define the reader and the decision
Write, before drafting:
- **Reader:** role, expertise, what they already believe.
- **Decision or change** you want: approve a budget, adopt an approach, understand a risk.
- **Question** the document answers, in one sentence.
- **Answer** in one sentence. If you can't write it yet, you're not ready to draft —
  you're still researching.
Types differ: a *vendor white paper* argues for an approach (and should still be fair);
a *technical report* documents work and findings; a *policy/technical brief* is short
(2–4 pages) and recommendation-led.

## 2. Structure: answer first (pyramid principle)
Top-down, as in Barbara Minto's pyramid principle and the military's BLUF ("bottom line
up front"):
1. **Executive summary** (standalone).
2. **Context / problem** — why this matters now, with the one or two numbers that prove it.
3. **Main argument** — 3–5 key points, each a section whose heading *states the point*
   ("Batch processing costs 40% more at our volumes"), not a topic label ("Costs").
4. **Evidence** for each point — data, cases, tests, sources.
5. **Counterarguments and limitations** — address the strongest objections.
6. **Recommendations** — specific, owned, sequenced; with costs/risks.
7. **Appendices** — methods, full data, glossary. Keep the body readable.
Check the structure by reading only the headings: they should tell the whole story.

## 3. The executive summary (write it last, polish it most)
- ½–1 page for a 10–30 page document. Many readers stop here.
- Contains: the problem, the answer/key findings (with the key numbers), the
  recommendation(s), and what's being asked of the reader.
- Stands alone: no "see section 4", no undefined acronyms, no new information not in the body.
- Test: hand only the summary to a colleague; can they say what you recommend and why?

## 4. Handling evidence
- Cite every factual claim (footnotes or numbered references, consistent style).
- Prefer primary sources (original study, dataset, standard) over articles about them.
- State the **strength** of the evidence: a single case study ≠ a randomised trial.
  Words like "proves" are rarely earned; "suggests", "is consistent with" are often honest.
- Disclose methods: sample, timeframe, how data were collected, what was excluded.
- Distinguish your analysis from the source's findings.
- Vendor papers: disclose who funded/wrote it; overclaiming is the fastest way to lose a
  technical reader.

## 5. Charts and tables
- Each chart makes **one point**, stated in its title ("Error rates fell after
  the change"), with source and date beneath.
- Label axes with units; start bar-chart axes at zero; avoid 3D and decorative charts.
- Tables for exact lookup, charts for patterns. Number figures and refer to them in text.

## 6. Style
Short paragraphs led by a topic sentence; define terms once; consistent terminology
(see `media-communication.technical-writing.style-guides.house-style-and-terminology-management`);
active voice for actions and responsibilities ("The team will…", not "It is recommended that…").

## Gotchas
- Chronological structure ("first we did X, then Y") — readers want findings, not your diary.
- An executive summary that's actually an introduction.
- Recommendations with no owner, cost or timeframe.
- Over-designed layouts that make the PDF inaccessible; tag the PDF and add alt text.

## Verify
- [ ] Reader, decision, question and one-sentence answer are written down.
- [ ] Headings alone tell the argument.
- [ ] Executive summary passes the colleague test and introduces nothing new.
- [ ] Every factual claim is cited; limitations section exists.
- [ ] Each chart's title states its point; axes and sources labelled.
