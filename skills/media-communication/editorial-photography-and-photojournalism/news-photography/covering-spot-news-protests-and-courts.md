# Covering spot news, protests and courts

**Goal:** an accurate, publishable news picture filed while it's still news — without
getting hurt, arrested or breaching a court order.

## 1. Kit for news
Two bodies (wide zoom ~24–70 and tele ~70–200 equivalent) save lens changes; spare
batteries and cards; camera set to RAW+JPEG with JPEG at a size your desk accepts;
correct clock and time zone; copyright and contact pre-loaded in camera/IPTC template.
Phone with tethering and a power bank for filing.

## 2. Spot news (fire, crash, incident)
- **Overall, medium, close**: get the establishing wide first (it may be all you get
  before a cordon moves you), then tighter frames and people.
- Shoot the **human moment** (reaction, rescue) but respect the dead and injured;
  many outlets won't run identifiable victims — check house style, shoot options.
- Stay outside cordons; follow emergency-service instructions. Your right to
  photograph in public places is usually strong but differs by country — know yours.
- Note names, ages, times, places for captions **as you shoot** (voice memo works).

## 3. Press conferences
Arrive early for position (centre, slightly low, or the riser). Shoot the speaker at
the moment of expression, not mid-blink or mouth contorted; watch for props, gestures,
the reveal. Get the wide showing the room and the backdrop context too.

## 4. Protests
- **Safety first:** plan an exit, stay at the edges with a wall behind you, avoid
  being between police and crowd lines. Consider helmet, eye protection and press
  markings where clashes are possible; know when markings make you a target instead.
- Work with a partner where you can; agree a regroup point.
- Photograph both the crowd and the response; show scale honestly (a tight crop can
  make 50 people look like 5,000 — or the reverse).
- **Identifying protesters can endanger them** in some contexts. Ethics and editors
  may call for avoiding identifiable faces of bystanders or minors; decide with the desk.
- Don't stage, direct or ask people to repeat actions.
- If police order you to stop or delete images: comply with lawful orders, stay calm,
  state you are press, never delete — ask for a supervisor; legal advice afterwards.

## 5. Courts
**Rules vary sharply by jurisdiction.** In England and Wales, for example, photography
is prohibited inside courts and their precincts, and contempt laws restrict publication
of pictures that could prejudice a trial or identify protected people (children,
sexual-offence complainants). Many US courts allow cameras at the judge's discretion,
often via a pool. So:
- Check the court's rules and any reporting restrictions **before** going.
- Typical shots: defendants/witnesses arriving or leaving, from public space outside
  the precinct.
- Never publish images of jurors; take care identifying anyone under a reporting
  restriction.

## 6. Filing fast
1. Edit in camera or on phone/laptop: pick 1–3 frames that tell it.
2. Minimal, honest adjustment only (exposure, crop) — see picture-editing skill.
3. Caption: who, what, where, when (with date), why; credit.
4. Send via the outlet's FTP/app/upload; confirm received. Then keep shooting and
   send updates.

## Gotchas
- Leaving geolocation on can reveal your position or a source's; decide deliberately.
- Drones over crowds and emergency scenes are restricted in many places.
- *Uncertain:* legal points above are illustrative, not legal advice; check local law
  and your outlet's lawyers.

## Verify
- [ ] Establishing wide plus medium and close frames shot.
- [ ] Caption facts noted at the scene; names spelled and checked.
- [ ] Court and reporting restrictions checked before publication.
- [ ] Images filed with full caption, credit and only ethical adjustments.
- [ ] Exit plan and partner check-in used at protests.
