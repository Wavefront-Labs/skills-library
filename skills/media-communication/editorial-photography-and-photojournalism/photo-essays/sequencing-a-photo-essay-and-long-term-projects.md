# Sequencing a photo essay and long-term projects

**Goal:** a set of 8–15 pictures (typical for a print or web feature; *varies by
outlet*) that reads as a story — with a beginning, change and an end — not a portfolio
of good frames.

## 1. Know the story before the pictures
Write a one-line premise ("A family farm decides whether to sell after the third
drought") and the **change** or tension the essay follows. An essay without tension is
a gallery. Revisit the premise as you learn; real stories move.

## 2. The shot types an essay usually needs
The classic picture-story checklist, used as prompts not a formula:
- **Establishing/overall** — place and scale.
- **Portraits** — the main people, environmental and close.
- **Interaction** — people with each other.
- **Action / signature moment** — the one image that could stand alone (often the lead).
- **Details** — hands, objects, textures that carry meaning.
- **Sequence** — a moment unfolding across 2–3 frames.
- **Closer** — resolution, or an open question.
Check the list at the end of each shooting day; note gaps for next time.

## 3. Editing for narrative
1. First pass: flag every frame that's technically sound and true to the story.
2. Second pass: pick the strongest per **situation** (one frame per moment, rarely two).
3. Print small (or use a lightbox/edit wall view) and lay them out physically.
4. Find the **lead** (strongest, most representative) and the **closer**.
5. Sequence the middle for **rhythm**: alternate wide and tight, busy and quiet,
   horizontal and vertical; avoid two near-identical frames side by side.
6. Cut anything that repeats information. A beautiful frame that says nothing new goes.
7. Have someone who hasn't seen the work read the sequence and tell you the story back.

## 4. Long-term projects
- **Access is the project.** Spend time without the camera first; explain what you're
  doing, how pictures will be used, and agree limits. Written consent where people are
  vulnerable or minors are involved, and as your outlet requires.
- Revisit regularly; the good pictures come when people stop noticing you.
- Keep a log: dates, places, names, consent notes, story developments.
- Fund it: grants, staged publication (a short piece now, a long one later), book,
  exhibition. Note rights carefully when selling one chapter — don't sign away the
  whole project.
- Revisit your own ethics: are you still representing people fairly as their
  situation changes?

## 5. Working with writers and editors
- Agree the premise together before reporting; share findings as you go.
- Pictures shouldn't illustrate paragraphs literally — they should add what words can't.
- Provide a **caption set** with full facts for every image; captions carry the
  reporting load in a photo essay.
- Lay out with the designer: the lead image needs size; the sequence may need to
  survive mobile scrolling as a single column. Web, print and social sequences can
  differ.

## Gotchas
- Too many similar portraits is the most common essay weakness.
- Don't fill a gap by setting up a scene; set-ups presented as candid are a firing
  offence in documentary work.
- Composite or heavily altered images break documentary trust.

## Verify
- [ ] One-line premise with tension written down.
- [ ] Lead and closer identified; middle alternates scale and pace.
- [ ] No two frames carry the same information.
- [ ] A cold reader can retell the story from the pictures plus captions.
- [ ] Consent and caption facts logged for every published image.
