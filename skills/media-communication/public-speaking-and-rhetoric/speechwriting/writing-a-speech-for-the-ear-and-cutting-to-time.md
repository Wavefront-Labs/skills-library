# Writing a speech for the ear and cutting it to time

**Goal:** a script that sounds natural in the speaker's mouth, lands one idea the
audience can repeat afterwards, and fits the slot to within about 10%.

## 1. Before you write a word
Get answers to: who is in the room (and who watches the clip later), the slot length,
what comes before and after, what the speaker must announce or avoid, and the **one
sentence** the audience should leave with. If the principal can't agree a single
sentence, the speech isn't ready to write — say so now, not at draft three.

## 2. Budget the time in words
Spoken pace for a prepared text is roughly **120–150 words per minute**; slower for
ceremonial or translated speeches, faster for confident conversational speakers. Time
your speaker on an old recording if you can (words ÷ minutes) and use their number.
A 10-minute slot at 130 wpm is ~1,300 words — then leave room for applause and ad-libs
by aiming ~10% under.

## 3. Structure the ear can follow
Readers can skim back; listeners can't. So:
- **Open** with something concrete (a scene, a fact, a person) within 30 seconds — not
  thanks lists. Put protocol acknowledgements in a short, fast block or after the hook
  if the occasion allows (check local protocol; some ceremonies require them first).
- **Three movements** at most for under 15 minutes. Signpost them out loud: "Three
  things have changed…", "The second…".
- **Repeat the core line** at least twice — once early, once at the close. Repetition
  that looks redundant on paper is what makes it stick in the ear.
- **Close** on the core sentence or a callback to the opening image. Never close on
  "thank you for your time" alone — put thanks just before the last line.

## 4. Sentences for speaking
- Short sentences; one idea each. Put the strong word at the **end** of the sentence,
  where the voice naturally lands.
- Concrete nouns and numbers rounded for the ear ("nearly a third", not "31.4%").
- Devices that work aloud: **tricolon** (three parallel items, the last longest),
  **anaphora** (repeated openings), **antithesis** ("not X, but Y"), and **callbacks**.
  Use them for the two or three moments that matter, not every paragraph.
- Avoid tongue-twisters, stacked sibilants, parentheticals, and acronyms the speaker
  must spell out. Read every line aloud yourself.

## 5. Writing for someone else
Collect their real voice: transcripts of unscripted interviews, their emails, phrases
they always use. Match sentence length and register; note words they won't say. Give
them a **draft read-through session** — where they stumble or rephrase, the text is
wrong, not the speaker. Keep their ad-libbed improvements.

## 6. Cutting to time
Cut whole sections before trimming adjectives — sentence-level trims rarely save a
minute. Order of cuts: second examples, statistics that repeat a point, history
lessons, the "also" paragraph. Re-time after each round.

## 7. The reading copy
Large type (16–18 pt+), double-spaced, one paragraph never split across pages, pages
numbered, no staples (slide pages aside). Mark pauses with `/` and emphasis with
underline if the speaker likes it. Phonetic spellings for names in brackets.

## Gotchas
- Embargoed or market-sensitive lines need legal/comms sign-off; get it before the
  final reading copy, not after.
- Teleprompter copy is formatted differently (short lines, caps conventions per
  operator) — ask the operator.
- *Uncertain:* the wpm range is a working rule of thumb; individual speakers vary widely.

## Verify
- [ ] The core sentence exists and appears at least twice.
- [ ] Word count ≈ slot minutes × the speaker's own wpm, minus ~10%.
- [ ] A full read-aloud by the speaker came in on time with no stumbles.
- [ ] No unexplained acronyms, no number the ear can't hold.
- [ ] Sensitive lines cleared; names have phonetic guides.
