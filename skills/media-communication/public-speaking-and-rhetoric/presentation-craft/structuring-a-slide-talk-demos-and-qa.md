# Structuring a slide talk, demos and Q&A

**Goal:** slides that support the speaker instead of replacing them, a demo that
can't sink the talk, and a Q&A that adds rather than derails.

## 1. Storyboard on paper first
Write the talk's **one-sentence point** and the audience's starting belief. Then sketch
the path on sticky notes or a text outline — one note per slide idea — before opening
PowerPoint/Keynote/Google Slides. Software first produces "slides about topics";
outline first produces an argument.

Workable skeleton for 20–40 minutes:
1. Hook: problem, story or surprising fact (≤ 2 min).
2. Promise: what they'll be able to do or believe by the end.
3. 2–4 sections, each: claim → evidence/example → "so what".
4. Recap + one call to action. Then Q&A, then a **final 30-second close** so the
   talk doesn't end on the last random question.

Timing rule of thumb: ~1–2 minutes per content slide; a 30-minute talk rarely needs
more than 20–30 slides (rapid image builds excepted). *Rule of thumb, not a law.*

## 2. Slides
- **One idea per slide**; headline as a full sentence stating the point (the
  "assertion–evidence" style): "Latency doubled after the cache change", not "Latency".
- The body is evidence: a chart, photo, diagram — not paragraphs. If the slide can be
  read as a document, the audience reads instead of listening.
- Text minimum ~24 pt for rooms; check from the back row or by viewing at 25% zoom.
- Charts: one message, highlight the bar/line that matters, strip gridlines.
- Use **builds** to reveal points as you speak them.
- Need a leave-behind? Make a separate document; don't overload slides to double as one.

## 3. Slide–speech interplay
- Say the point **as** the slide appears, not after reading it to them.
- Use presenter view with notes and a next-slide preview; know your transitions
  ("That's why…" leads into the next slide).
- Blank the screen (`B` in PowerPoint/Keynote slideshow) during stories or discussion
  to pull attention back.

## 4. Live demos
- Rehearse on the **actual** machine, network and resolution. Bump font size (editor
  and terminal ≥ ~20 pt equivalent) and turn off notifications.
- Script the demo; pre-type long commands in a snippet file.
- Have a **fallback**: a recorded video or screenshots of each step, embedded in the
  deck. Decide the bail-out point in advance (e.g. 30 seconds of failure → switch).
- Use local/offline data where possible; conference Wi-Fi is the classic killer.

## 5. Q&A
- Repeat or paraphrase each question into the mic (the room and recording can't hear it).
- Answer briefly, to the whole room, then end eye contact with the asker to move on.
- "I don't know — I'll find out" is fine; follow up afterwards.
- **Hostile or rambling question:** acknowledge the concern, answer the fair part,
  offer to continue afterwards. Don't argue the premise at length on stage.
- Seed one question with a colleague or prepare "A question I often get is…" for silence.
- Prepare a backup slide set (after the closing slide) for likely deep questions.

## Formats
- **Keynote:** fewer, bigger ideas; story-heavy; very few words on screen.
- **Conference session:** concrete takeaways; share slides link at start and end.
- **Lightning (5 min / auto-advance):** rehearse to the auto-timer; cut to one point.

## Gotchas
- Aspect ratio and fonts: bring a PDF backup; embedded fonts don't always travel.
- Video playback and audio from the laptop need checking in the room's AV.
- Clicker batteries and a spare adapter (HDMI/USB-C) save talks.

## Verify
- [ ] Outline exists independently of the slides; each slide headline is a full-sentence point.
- [ ] Back-of-room legibility check passed.
- [ ] Demo rehearsed on the venue setup; fallback recording in the deck.
- [ ] Timed run-through leaves time for Q&A and the final close.
- [ ] PDF backup and adapters packed.
