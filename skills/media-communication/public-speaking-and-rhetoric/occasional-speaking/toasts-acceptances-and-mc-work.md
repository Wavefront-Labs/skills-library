# Toasts, acceptances and MC work

**Goal:** say the right thing for the moment, briefly, so the room feels the occasion
was honoured — and, as MC, keep the event moving. For full funeral services see
`religion-philosophy.celebrancy-and-officiating.funeral-celebrancy.leading-a-funeral-from-family-meeting-to-committal`.

## 1. The universal rule: short and about them
Occasional speeches fail by running long and by being about the speaker. Targets:
| Occasion | Typical length |
|---|---|
| Toast | 1–2 min |
| Wedding speeches (each) | 3–7 min |
| Leaving/retirement | 3–5 min |
| Award acceptance | 30–90 s (check the organiser's limit) |
| Eulogy | 3–7 min unless the family asks for more |
*Lengths are conventions, not rules; ask the organiser and keep to their number.*

## 2. A structure that works for almost all of them
1. **Who you are** in one line (relationship to the person).
2. **One or two specific stories** that show a quality of theirs — a story beats a list
   of adjectives ("generous, loyal, funny").
3. **The turn** to the present: what this occasion means.
4. **The ask:** raise glasses / welcome them / thank them — said clearly, with the
   name, so the room knows what to do.

## 3. By type
- **Toast:** end with an explicit cue: "Please stand and raise your glasses to…".
  Check everyone has a drink first.
- **Wedding speech:** humour that the grandparents and the new in-laws can all enjoy.
  The test for any joke: would the couple be happy to see it on video in ten years?
  No exes, no in-jokes that exclude the room, no embarrassment of the couple.
- **Leaving/retirement:** contribution plus character; one line of the organisation's
  thanks; don't relitigate why they're leaving.
- **Award acceptance:** thank in groups (team, organisation, family), not a list of
  twenty names; one line about why the work matters; done. Have it on a card even if
  you think you won't win.
- **Eulogy:** agree with the family which stories are welcome; include the person's
  ordinary habits, not just achievements. Write it out in full — emotion takes words
  away. Have a backup reader who has a copy.

## 4. MC / compère work
- Own a **running order** with times, who's next, and what they need (mic, slides,
  walk-up music). Brief each speaker on their time and how you'll signal (a card,
  a light, standing up).
- **Introductions:** 30–60 seconds; name, why they're relevant to *this* audience, the
  title of their talk, their name again as they walk on. Check pronunciation in person.
- Handle logistics without fuss: fire exits, toilets, timings, phones on silent.
- **Fill** when things slip: have 2–3 ready items (a sponsor thank-you, an audience
  question, a story about the cause). Never say "we're having technical problems" at length.
- **Overrunning speaker:** agree the signals in advance; if ignored, stand and move
  towards the stage; final resort, a warm interruption: "Let's thank X…".

## 5. Reading the room
Before you speak, watch: energy level, drink level, time of night, whether earlier
speeches ran long. Tired or late room → cut. Grieving room → slow, simple, no forced
lightness. Laughs not landing → drop remaining jokes, go to the sincere part.

## Gotchas
- Alcohol: one drink before speaking at most; better after.
- Microphones at venues: hold it at the chin, don't wave it.
- Names: wrong pronunciation of the honouree is the error everyone remembers.
- Culture and faith shape what's appropriate (toasting with alcohol, humour at
  funerals, order of honour) — ask the hosts.

## Verify
- [ ] Timed read-through within the organiser's limit.
- [ ] At least one specific story; no inside jokes or embarrassing material.
- [ ] Clear closing cue (glasses up / welcome / thanks).
- [ ] Names and pronunciations checked; card copy in a pocket.
- [ ] As MC: running order, speaker briefs, fill material and overrun signal ready.
