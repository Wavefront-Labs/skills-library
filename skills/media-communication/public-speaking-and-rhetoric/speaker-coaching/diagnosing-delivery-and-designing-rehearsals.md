# Diagnosing delivery and designing rehearsals

**Goal:** the speaker improves measurably on the thing that matters most for this
talk, without being buried in notes. Uses the delivery techniques in
`media-communication.public-speaking-and-rhetoric.speech-delivery.pacing-pause-presence-and-managing-nerves`.

## 1. Baseline
Record a full run-through on video, no interruptions, before giving any feedback.
Ask the speaker first: "How did that feel? What would you change?" — their answer tells
you what they can already see and what they're anxious about.

Watch the recording three ways:
1. **Sound off** — stance, hands, eye line, movement, face.
2. **Audio only** — pace (count words per minute on a 60-second sample), fillers per
   minute, pitch variety, sentence endings, volume drop-offs.
3. **Content** — can you state their point in one sentence afterwards? If not, it's a
   structure problem, and no delivery fix will help.

## 2. Pick one or two things
Rank problems by audience impact, not by how easy they are to name. Typical order:
1. Unclear message or structure.
2. Inaudible / too fast.
3. No eye contact (reading the script).
4. Distracting habits (pacing, swaying, fillers, clicking a pen).
5. Polish (vocal variety, gesture size).
Fix the top one or two per session. Ten notes produce self-consciousness and nothing sticks.

## 3. Feedback that sticks
- Be specific and observable: "You said 'um' 14 times in the first two minutes",
  not "you seem nervous".
- **Show** the clip of the moment, then a clip where they did it well.
- Give the replacement behaviour, not just the ban: "Pause and breathe where you'd say 'so'".
- Keep what works — name strengths explicitly so they don't coach them out.

## 4. Designing rehearsals
- **Chunk drills:** rehearse the opening 60 seconds and the close five or more times each;
  they carry the most weight and the most nerves.
- **Constraint drills:** deliver a section with no notes; deliver it at half speed; say
  it to one person across the room; pause a full 2 seconds at every full stop.
- **Interruption drills:** fire questions, a dead clicker, a fake "five minutes left"
  card. Big-moment speakers need practice at recovering, not just at the clean run.
- **Dress rehearsal** in the real room or a close copy, with the real mic, clicker,
  autocue and lights, at the scheduled time of day if possible.
- Space practice over days; one long session the night before helps least.

## 5. Prepping non-speakers for a big moment
(Experts, executives, someone receiving an award.)
- Reduce the load: shorten the talk, give them bullet cards, write the first and last
  lines verbatim to memorise.
- Rehearse the logistics: walking on, where to stand, where to look, how to exit.
- Prepare 3–5 likely questions with short answers (and the "bridge" back to their
  point — see press-office media training).
- On the day: warm-up routine, water, one reassurance note only. No new feedback in
  the final hour.

## Gotchas
- Coaching a senior leader: agree in advance that candid feedback is wanted; give it
  privately.
- Don't impose your style. A quiet, precise speaker shouldn't be coached into a
  motivational shouter.
- Some "delivery" issues (stammer, hearing loss, second-language speaking) need
  accommodation, not correction — adapt format (e.g. slides with captions, Q&A by
  moderator), and refer to a speech-language professional where appropriate.
- *Uncertain:* there are no standard thresholds for "too many" fillers; compare the
  speaker to their own baseline.

## Verify
- [ ] Baseline recording exists and was reviewed sound-off, audio-only and for content.
- [ ] No more than two focus areas named for the session, with replacement behaviours.
- [ ] Second recording shows measured change (wpm, filler count, eye-contact time).
- [ ] Opening and close rehearsed repeatedly; interruptions practised.
- [ ] Dress rehearsal done with the real kit; no new notes in the final hour.
