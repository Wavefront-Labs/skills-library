# Pacing, pause, presence and managing nerves

**Goal:** the audience hears every word, feels the speaker means it, and never notices
the nerves. Pairs with
`media-communication.public-speaking-and-rhetoric.speechwriting.writing-a-speech-for-the-ear-and-cutting-to-time`.

## 1. Breath and voice
- Breathe low (belly expands, shoulders still). Shallow chest breathing raises pitch
  and shortens phrases.
- **Breathe at punctuation**, not mid-phrase. Mark breath points on the script.
- Project to the back row, not the front: aim the voice "past" the furthest person.
  With a microphone, keep a constant distance (handheld ~a fist from the mouth; lavalier
  clipped mid-chest on the side you'll turn towards) and don't drop volume — the mic
  amplifies, it doesn't articulate.
- Warm up 5 minutes before: hums, lip trills, slow tongue-twisters, yawns to open the throat.

## 2. Pace and the pause
Nervous speakers speed up by 20–30% without noticing. Countermeasures:
- **Pause before the important line** (anticipation) and **after it** (absorption).
  A 2-second pause feels endless to the speaker and normal to the room.
- Pause instead of filler. "Um" usually fills the space while the brain finds the next
  sentence; train by practising silence at transitions.
- Vary pace: slower for numbers, names and the core message; quicker for narrative.
- Land sentences **down** in pitch. Rising endings ("uptalk") make statements sound
  like questions — fine in casual speech, weakens a call to action.

## 3. Presence
- **Plant** before the first word: feet hip-width, weight even, a breath, look at a
  friendly face, then speak. Don't start talking while walking to the lectern.
- Eye contact: hold one person per thought (a sentence or so), then move. Cover the room
  in zones, including the back corners.
- Gestures: let hands rest at your sides or lightly on the lectern between gestures;
  make gestures bigger than feels natural in a large room.
- Move with purpose (to change section) and stand still to deliver key lines.

## 4. Notes, script or autocue
| Mode | When | Trick |
|---|---|---|
| Full script | Precise wording matters (policy, legal) | Look down at the end of a sentence, look up to say the next; never read while looking down |
| Bullet cards | Most talks | One card per section, numbered; key quote/stat written in full |
| No notes | Short, rehearsed talks | Memorise the structure and opening/closing lines, not every word |
| Autocue/teleprompter | Broadcast, big keynotes | Rehearse with the operator; they follow your pace — don't chase the text |

## 5. Nerves
Nerves are arousal, not a defect; the aim is to channel them.
- **Rehearse aloud, standing, timed**, at least three full run-throughs; the first
  30 seconds ten times. Most panic happens at the opening.
- Before going on: slow exhale longer than the inhale (e.g. in 4, out 6–8) for a
  minute; shake out hands; water at room temperature nearby.
- Reframe: "I'm excited" tends to work better than "calm down" (*evidence is mixed;
  use what works for you*).
- If you lose your place: pause, look at notes, take a breath, recap the last point.
  The audience reads it as a deliberate pause.
- Persistent, disabling anxiety is worth raising with a professional; some speakers use
  prescribed medication under medical advice — not a coaching call.

## Gotchas
- Lectern lights wash out notes printed small or in light ink.
- Lavalier mics pick up necklaces and fabric rub; check in the sound check.
- Recording yourself is the fastest feedback; watch once with sound off for body
  language, once audio-only for pace.

## Verify
- [ ] Recorded run-through measured: pace inside your target wpm, especially the opening.
- [ ] At least three deliberate pauses landed around the key lines.
- [ ] Filler words counted and fewer than on the previous run.
- [ ] Sound check done with the actual mic; notes legible under stage light.
- [ ] Recovery move rehearsed for losing your place.
