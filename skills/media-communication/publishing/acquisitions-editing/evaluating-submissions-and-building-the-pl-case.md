# Evaluating submissions and building the P&L case

**Goal:** acquire books that fit your list and earn out, with an advance you can defend
in numbers — and say no fast and kindly to everything else.

## 1. Triage submissions quickly
Most submissions come via agents (trade publishing) or direct proposals (academic,
professional, many independents). For each, answer in order:
1. **Fit:** does it belong on *our* list and channel? A great book in the wrong house fails.
2. **Hook:** can you state what it is and who buys it in one sentence?
3. **Execution:** read the opening pages; for nonfiction, the proposal's chapter outline
   and sample chapter. Is the writing at publishable level or one edit away?
4. **Platform/credibility:** for nonfiction especially, why this author? Audience,
   expertise, media reach.
5. **Competition:** what's out there, and why will this one win?
Reply even to declines; agents remember responsiveness.

## 2. Comp titles — honest ones
Choose 3-6 **comparable titles**: similar audience, format and price point, published in
the last few years, from a comparable author profile. Pull their sales (in the US, Circana
BookScan is the common source; other markets have their own panels; note that panels miss
some channels).
Gotchas: comps that are mega-bestsellers prove nothing; comps that flopped need a reason
yours won't. Debut authors should be comped against debuts.

## 3. Build the P&L
A title P&L (template comes from your finance team) typically models:
- **Format(s), list price, discount** to retailers/wholesalers → net receipts per copy.
- **Sales forecast** by format (hardback/paperback/ebook/audio) over the first year or
  more, derived from comps — not hope.
- **Unit cost** (print, paper, binding) at your planned print run; the run size itself.
- **Royalties** at contract rates (rates vary widely by format and sector; use your house
  standard), **returns** allowance (often material in trade print — use your house
  history), plant costs (editing, design, typesetting), marketing spend, overhead allocation.
- **Advance**: the maximum you can offer so that projected royalties earn it out within
  your house's acceptable horizon. The P&L tells you the ceiling; strategy decides the offer.
- **Subsidiary rights income** (translation, audio, serial) only if realistic — don't
  balance a weak P&L with hypothetical foreign deals.
Run **scenarios:** conservative, expected, upside. If only the upside works, it's a pass
or a lower offer.

## 4. The acquisitions meeting
Pitch in under two minutes: the one-line hook, the audience, comps with numbers, author
platform, what makes it publishable now, the P&L headline (projected margin, advance
asked), and the risks. Bring answers for sales, marketing and finance objections.
Get sales/marketing enthusiasm before the meeting; a lone editor's passion rarely wins.

## 5. Negotiation with agents
- Know your **walk-away** number before the call.
- Terms beyond the advance matter: territories, formats, subsidiary rights split, delivery
  date, payout schedule (e.g. on signing / delivery / publication), option clause.
- In auctions, decide in advance whether you'll bid on "best bid" rounds and your cap.
- Get everything agreed in a deal memo before the contract team drafts.

## 6. List building
Track the list as a portfolio: spread of formats, price points, seasons, risk levels. One
big bet per season is common; ten is a crisis.

## Verify
- [ ] One-sentence hook and named audience written for every acquisition.
- [ ] Comps are recent, comparable and include at least one modest performer.
- [ ] P&L run at three scenarios; advance earns out on the expected case.
- [ ] Sales/marketing input gathered before the meeting.
- [ ] Deal memo covers rights, territories, payouts and delivery.
