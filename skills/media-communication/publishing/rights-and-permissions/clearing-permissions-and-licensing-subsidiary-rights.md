# Clearing permissions and licensing subsidiary rights

**Goal:** every piece of third-party material in a book is cleared for all the formats
and territories you'll publish in, and every right you sell is defined so precisely that
nobody can dispute it later.

*Not legal advice. Copyright exceptions (fair use, fair dealing) and terms differ by
country; use your publisher's lawyer for anything uncertain.*

## Part A — Clearing permissions (inbound)

### 1. Build the permissions log at manuscript stage
One row per item: location (chapter/page), description, type (text quote, image, chart,
lyric, map), source and rights holder, whether permission is needed, status, fee, credit
line required, restrictions, date cleared. Ask the author to supply it — most contracts
make clearance (and often the fees) the author's responsibility; check the contract.

### 2. Decide if permission is needed
- **Public domain?** Depends on author death date and jurisdiction; a 19th-century text
  may be public domain but a modern translation or edition of it isn't.
- **Exceptions:** short quotation for criticism/review may fall under fair use (US) or
  fair dealing (UK and others), but these are judged case by case. **Song lyrics, poetry,
  epigraphs and images** are high-risk; many publishers clear them regardless of length.
- **Own photos/images:** still need model releases for identifiable people in some uses,
  and property releases for some locations/artwork.
- "It's on the internet" is not permission.

### 3. Request properly
A request letter specifies: exact material (attach it), book title, author, publisher,
**formats** (print, ebook, audio, all), **territory** (world / named territories),
**languages**, print run or term, publication date, and proposed credit line. Ask for a
grant covering future editions where possible. Use rights holders' online portals where
they exist (many large publishers and image libraries have them).

### 4. Gotchas
- Clearing print only, then publishing an ebook — the commonest gap.
- Getting permission from someone who doesn't own the rights (e.g. the author of an
  article whose publisher holds copyright).
- Images: the photographer, the agency and the owner of the depicted artwork may all hold
  separate rights.
- Unanswered requests: document every attempt; whether to proceed is a risk decision for
  the publisher, not the editor.

## Part B — Licensing subsidiary rights (outbound)

### 5. Know what you hold
Check the author contract: which rights the publisher controls (e.g. translation, audio,
first serial, book club, film/TV — often retained by the author/agent), the territory, and
the split of income with the author.

### 6. Sell them
- Prepare a **rights guide** (title, pitch, author bio, sales track record, reviews,
  available rights) for buyers and scouts; book fairs such as Frankfurt and London are the
  main marketplaces.
- Send material to publishers and sub-agents in each territory; track submissions.

### 7. Licence terms to define
- **Rights granted:** language, format(s) (print, ebook, audio), territory (exclusive?),
  **term** (often a fixed number of years from publication or signature).
- **Advance and royalties**, payment schedule, accounting periods and statements.
- **Publication deadline** and **reversion**: rights return if the licensee doesn't publish
  by a date or lets the book go out of print.
- Approvals (translator, cover), credit lines, copies to supply.
- Who supplies files/materials and at what cost.
Keep a **rights database**: every licence, terms, expiry and reversion dates, so nothing
lapses unnoticed and nothing is sold twice.

## Verify
- [ ] Permissions log complete; every item cleared or removed before typesetting.
- [ ] Grants cover all formats, territories and languages you'll publish.
- [ ] Credit lines placed exactly as required.
- [ ] Each sub-rights licence states rights, territory, term, money and reversion.
- [ ] Rights database updated with expiry/reversion dates.
