# Drafting in a leader's voice for staff

**Goal:** a message staff believe the leader actually wrote, that tells them what's
happening and what it means for them, and that would survive being screenshotted to
the press.

## 1. Build a voice profile
Before the first draft, collect 5–10 samples of the leader's **unedited** writing
(emails they wrote themselves, chat messages, transcripts of town-hall answers). Note:
- typical sentence length and paragraph length;
- greeting and sign-off ("Hi all" / "Team," / first name only);
- words and phrases they use, and ones they never would ("synergy", "family");
- humour level; how they show emotion; whether they use "I" or "we".
Keep it as a one-page profile and update it. Confirm with the leader: "Is this how you
sound?"

## 2. The standard structure
1. **What's happening** — in the first two sentences. No warm-up paragraph.
2. **Why** — the honest reason, in plain words.
3. **What it means for you** — by group if it differs ("If you're in finance…").
4. **What happens next** — dates, who to ask, where the FAQ is.
5. **Close** in their voice — a genuine line, not a slogan.
Length: most all-staff messages work at 200–400 words; link to detail rather than
include it.

## 3. Org announcements
(New leaders, structure changes, appointments.)
- Name, role, start date, reporting line, what they'll own — then something human
  about them.
- Thank the outgoing person specifically if relevant.
- Tell affected people **before** the all-staff send; nobody should learn about their
  own boss changing from a group email.

## 4. Sensitive news
(Poor results, a death in the team, incidents, cuts.)
- **Say the hard thing early and plainly.** Euphemism ("rightsizing", "difficult
  journey") reads as evasion.
- Acknowledge impact without performing emotion. "This is hard news, and I know it
  will cause real worry" is enough.
- Be specific about what is known, unknown, and when you'll update.
- Don't promise what you can't guarantee ("no further changes").
- Get **legal/HR review** where it touches employment, regulators or disclosure
  rules (listed companies may have market-disclosure timing constraints — *check with
  legal; this varies by jurisdiction*).
- For layoffs, sequencing matters more than wording — see
  `media-communication.internal-and-organisational-communications-content.change-communications.sequencing-reorg-and-layoff-communications`.

## 5. Assume it leaks
Internal messages often appear in the press or on social media within hours of a big
announcement. Test each line: would it read badly as a headline out of context? Avoid
jokes at anyone's expense, comments about competitors, and anything said "off the
record" in a written email.

## 6. Process
- Draft → leader edits in their own words (insist on at least one personal pass) →
  approvers (HR, legal, comms lead) → final read **aloud** by the leader.
- Keep a single owner of the document; parallel edits in multiple copies cause
  version errors.
- Schedule the send with timezones in mind; check the distribution list.

## Gotchas
- Over-polished copy sounds like comms, not the leader; leave some of their quirks.
- Reply-all storms: send from a list that blocks reply-all if possible.
- Don't attribute words to a leader who hasn't approved the final text.

## Verify
- [ ] Voice profile exists and the leader confirmed it.
- [ ] The news is in the first two sentences; "what it means for you" is explicit.
- [ ] Affected people told before the all-staff message.
- [ ] Legal/HR reviewed sensitive content; nothing reads badly as a headline.
- [ ] Leader made a personal edit and read the final aloud.
