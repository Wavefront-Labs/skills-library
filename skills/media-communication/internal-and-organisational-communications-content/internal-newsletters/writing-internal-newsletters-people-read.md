# Writing internal newsletters people read

**Goal:** staff can get what matters from the update in under a minute, and know
exactly what (if anything) they need to do.

## 1. Decide the job of this newsletter
Write one line: who it's for, what it's for, how often. "Weekly, all 400 staff in
operations: what changed, what you must do, who to thank." If it tries to serve every
team's promotional wishes, it becomes the thing nobody opens.

## 2. A fixed, scannable format
People learn a format; keep it the same every issue.
1. **Subject line** that names the most important item: "Rota changes from 4 Nov +
   new expenses tool" beats "Ops Weekly #47".
2. **Action required** box at the top: what, who, by when, link. If none, say "No
   actions this week" — that is useful information too.
3. **3–5 items**, each: a headline that states the news (not a label), 1–3 sentences,
   one link for more.
4. A **people** item (welcomes, recognition) — consistently the most-read kind of
   content in many organisations (*commonly reported; check your own data*).
5. Dates/deadlines strip and a contact line.
Rough length: readable in 1–2 minutes. Long stories go on the intranet and the
newsletter links to them.

## 3. Writing for scanning
- Lead with the point. "From 4 November, night shifts start at 21:00" — then why.
- Short paragraphs, meaningful bold on the key fact (sparingly), descriptive links
  ("expenses guide"), never "click here".
- Plain language: avoid internal acronyms or expand them once. Read-age tools
  (Hemingway, Word's readability stats) help for broad audiences.
- Write like a colleague, not a press release: "we", contractions, no superlatives.
- Accessibility: real text not images of text, alt text on images, colour not the only
  signal, works on mobile.

## 4. Gathering content without chaos
- A simple intake form with a deadline (e.g. Tuesday noon for Thursday send) and a
  rule: you edit everything, contributors approve facts only.
- Keep a content calendar for predictable items (benefits windows, results, events).
- Say no or "next issue" to low-value items; the newsletter's attention budget is finite.

## 5. Channels and cadence
- Email is still the default for official record; chat-channel posts (Slack/Teams)
  get faster reads but scroll away. A common pattern: post a short version in chat
  with a link to the full issue.
- Deskless/frontline staff may need print, screens, an employee app or a team-brief
  cascade instead of email.
- Cadence: consistent beats frequent. Skip an issue rather than send filler — but
  tell people.

## 6. Measuring honestly
Internal email platforms (e.g. Poppulo, Staffbase, or your email tool) report opens and
clicks. **Open rates are unreliable** (privacy features in some mail clients inflate
them). Better signals: clicks on action items, completion of the requested action,
questions received, a twice-yearly two-question pulse ("Do you find it useful? What's
missing?").

## Gotchas
- Sensitive news (redundancies, pay) does not go in the newsletter first — see the
  change-communications skill for sequencing.
- Attachments get lost; link to a stable page.
- Sending to "all staff" lists: check the list and test-send to yourself and a colleague.

## Verify
- [ ] Subject line names the top item.
- [ ] Action box at the top with owner, deadline and link (or "no actions").
- [ ] 3–5 items with news-style headlines; skimmable in under two minutes.
- [ ] Plain language, accessible formatting, test-sent and checked on mobile.
- [ ] Measured on actions taken and feedback, not opens alone.
