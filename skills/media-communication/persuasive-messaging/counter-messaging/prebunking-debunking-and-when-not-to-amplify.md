# Prebunking, debunking and when not to amplify

**Goal:** reduce the effect of a false or hostile narrative on the audience that matters,
without accidentally spreading it further.

## 1. First decision: respond or not?
Answer before writing anything:
- **Reach:** is the claim actually reaching your target audience, or only a small, already
  committed community?
- **Trajectory:** is it growing, crossing platforms, getting picked up by larger accounts or
  media? A rough rule used by many practitioners: respond when it is about to tip into the
  mainstream; before then, monitoring may be wiser.
- **Harm:** could belief in it cause real damage (health, safety, violence, voting)? High harm
  lowers the threshold.
- **Your credibility:** are you a trusted messenger for this audience, or will your denial
  read as confirmation?
If the claim is small, low-harm and contained, **strategic silence** plus monitoring is a
legitimate choice. Quote-tweeting a fringe post to mock it shows it to your followers.

## 2. Prebunking (before the lie lands)
Inoculation works like a vaccine: warn people that manipulation is coming and show them a
weakened example of the technique, so they recognise it later.
- **Technique-based** prebunks (teach the trick: fake experts, emotional manipulation,
  false dichotomies, scapegoating, cherry-picked data) generalise across topics and avoid
  repeating specific myths.
- **Issue-based** prebunks target a predictable false claim ("You may hear that the new
  ballot machines change votes. Here's how they're audited…").
- Format: a forewarning, a microdose of the technique, a refutation. Short videos and
  games have been tested in field studies with measurable, though modest and decaying,
  effects — plan "booster" reminders.
Best used before elections, product launches, health campaigns, or anything you can
predict will draw misinformation.

## 3. Debunking (after it's out): fact – myth – fallacy – fact
1. **Fact first:** lead with the truth, simply and memorably, if it can be said in a few
   words. The headline should not be the myth.
2. **Warn, then mention the myth once:** "A false claim circulating says…" Repeat it only
   once.
3. **Explain the fallacy:** *why* it's wrong and how it misleads (the technique, the missing
   context, who benefits). Fill the gap with an alternative explanation — people hold on to
   a myth if removing it leaves a hole in their understanding (the continued-influence
   effect).
4. **Fact again:** end on the truth.
This is sometimes called the "truth sandwich".
The once-feared "backfire effect" (corrections making belief stronger) appears to be rare in
replication studies; the bigger risk is corrections that are too weak, too late, or too
complicated (current consensus as of the 2020 handbook; research continues).

## 4. Messenger and channel
- The best debunker is a **trusted source for the audience** — a local doctor, faith leader,
  community figure, or a peer — not necessarily the institution being attacked.
- Correct where the myth spread (the same platform, group or community), with visuals that
  travel.
- Pair with action: what should people do, and where to get reliable updates.

## 5. Tone
No mockery of believers; many people sharing a rumour are worried, not malicious. Respect +
clarity persuades the watching audience, even when the original poster won't move.

## Gotchas
- Myth-busting graphics with the myth in huge letters and "FALSE" in small ones.
- Long technical rebuttals no one finishes.
- Debunking every rumour personally and becoming the amplifier.
- Fact-checking with a source the audience already distrusts.

## Verify
- [ ] Respond/don't-respond decision recorded with reach, trajectory and harm assessed.
- [ ] Predictable attacks have a prebunk ready before launch.
- [ ] Debunks lead and end with the fact; the myth appears once, flagged as false.
- [ ] An alternative explanation fills the gap.
- [ ] A messenger trusted by the target audience is delivering it.
