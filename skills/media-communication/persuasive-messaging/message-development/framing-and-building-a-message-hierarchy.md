# Framing and building a message hierarchy

**Goal:** one core message that moves a specific audience, with a hierarchy of supporting
messages and proof that can be adapted to each segment without saying different things to
different people.

## 1. Start with the audience, not the argument
Segment by **current position × movability**, not demographics alone:
- **Base** — already agree; needs mobilising, not persuading.
- **Persuadables** — open, undecided or soft. This is where messaging effort pays.
- **Opposition** — unlikely to move; mainly avoid energising them.
For the persuadables, write down: what they already believe, what they value, what they fear,
the words *they* use (from interviews, reviews, forums, focus groups). Your vocabulary is
usually the problem.

## 2. Choose the frame
A frame is the lens that says what an issue is *about* — the same policy can be framed as
fairness, freedom, safety, cost, or tradition. Rules of thumb:
- Lead with a **shared value** the audience already holds, not a fact.
- Don't repeat the opponent's frame, even to negate it ("not a tax grab" reinforces "tax
  grab"). Re-frame instead.
- Concrete beats abstract: a person, a place, a number on a human scale.
- Moral-foundations-style research suggests audiences weigh values (care, fairness,
  loyalty, authority, liberty, sanctity) differently; frames matched to the audience's
  values often persuade better than ones matched to the messenger's (effect sizes in the
  literature vary — test, don't assume; see media-communication.persuasive-messaging.message-testing.designing-and-reading-message-tests).

## 3. Structure: Value → Problem → Solution → Action
1. **Value:** "Everyone deserves to feel safe walking home."
2. **Problem** (with a cause and someone responsible, not just a sad fact): "But half the
   streetlights in the east ward have been out for a year."
3. **Solution:** specific and believable: "The council can fix them with money already
   in the budget."
4. **Action:** one concrete ask: "Sign the petition before the 12 March vote."
Hope plus a clear path outperforms fear alone; fear without efficacy tends to produce
avoidance.

## 4. The message hierarchy (message house / box)
- **Core message:** one sentence, under ~25 words, everyone uses it.
- **3 supporting messages**, each covering an objection or angle.
- **Proof points** under each: data, stories, validators.
- **Segment adaptations:** the same core and supports, with segment-specific proof and
  emphasis. Changing *emphasis and examples* per audience is fine; contradicting yourself
  across audiences will be found and quoted back.
Also a **message box** (Self says about self / Self about opponent / Opponent about self /
Opponent about us) to anticipate the other side's lines.

## 5. Language craft
- Short words, active verbs, named actors ("the water company dumped sewage", not "sewage
  was discharged").
- Say who benefits — "you", "your family", "our town".
- Remove jargon and acronyms; insiders' terms ("SNAP", "net zero pathways") mean little to
  many audiences.
- Read it aloud; if it isn't sayable, it isn't a message.

## Ethics line
Persuasion is legitimate when the claims are true and the audience can see who is speaking.
Fabricated facts, hidden sponsorship, or targeting fears you know to be false are
manipulation and in many contexts unlawful (election and advertising law).

## Gotchas
- Writing for the base and calling it persuasion.
- Ten "key" messages — nobody remembers more than three.
- Leading with statistics to people who don't share the premise.
- Untested assumptions about which frame wins.

## Verify
- [ ] Persuadable segment defined with their own words and values.
- [ ] Core message is one sayable sentence and doesn't repeat the opposing frame.
- [ ] Value → problem (with responsible actor) → solution → single ask present.
- [ ] Each supporting message has at least one proof point.
- [ ] Segment versions differ in emphasis only, never in facts.
