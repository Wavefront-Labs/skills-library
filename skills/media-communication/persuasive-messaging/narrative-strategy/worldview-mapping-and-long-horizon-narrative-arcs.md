# Worldview mapping and long-horizon narrative arcs

**Goal:** move beyond single messages to a *narrative* — a durable story about how the world
works, who the characters are, and what change looks like — that many campaigns, messengers
and years can reinforce until it becomes common sense.

Messages are what you say this week; a narrative is what your audience starts to assume.

## 1. Map the current worldview
Before writing your story, document the one already in people's heads.
- **Sources:** interviews, open-ended survey questions, focus groups, comment sections,
  media content analysis, popular culture.
- Look for **deep assumptions** about cause and responsibility: "Poverty is about bad
  choices", "Government always wastes money", "Technology fixes itself". These are the
  terrain; most messages bounce off them.
- Identify **competing narratives** already circulating, including your opponents', and
  which ones are dominant, rising or contested.
- Note **cognitive gaps** — where people have no clear model at all (common for technical
  issues like financial regulation or climate mechanisms). Gaps are easier to fill than
  entrenched models are to overturn.
Output: a short map of 3–5 dominant mental models, with quotes, for each priority audience.

## 2. Write the story of change
A narrative has characters, a conflict and a resolution:
- **Who are "we"?** (an inclusive identity the audience can join)
- **What's the problem**, and what or who causes it? Narratives that name a cause and a
  responsible actor outperform "it's sad" stories.
- **What's at stake** (values, not policy details).
- **What does the better future look like** — concrete, imaginable.
- **How do we get there** — the role ordinary people play.
Test it against the worldview map: which existing beliefs can it build *on* rather than
fight? Prefer bridging to an existing value over demanding a conversion.

A practical format for individual messengers is Marshall Ganz's *public narrative*:
story of self (why I'm called), story of us (shared values and experience), story of now
(the urgent challenge and choice).

## 3. Themes that accumulate
Choose 3–4 **recurring themes** that every campaign touches (e.g. "fairness for people who
work", "local control", "protecting what we love"). Then:
- Give every campaign, report and event a line that ties back to a theme.
- Reuse **recurring characters** (the nurse, the small farmer, the young apprentice) and
  **signature phrases** consistently over years.
- Build a **narrative toolkit** for partners: the story, themes, words to use and avoid,
  example stories, imagery guidance. Narrative power comes from many voices repeating it.
- Track adoption: do journalists, politicians, or opponents start using your terms?

## 4. Horizons
- **0–6 months:** campaign messages that each reinforce a theme.
- **1–3 years:** shift who is seen as credible on the issue; get your framing into media
  defaults.
- **3–10 years:** the narrative becomes assumed background; cultural work (TV, music,
  faith communities, sports) often matters more here than news.
Measure with periodic surveys on the underlying beliefs (not just issue support), media
content analysis of frames, and language adoption.

## Gotchas
- Confusing a slogan or brand line with a narrative.
- A story that only resonates with the base ("preaching to the choir").
- Changing themes every year as leadership changes — accumulation is the whole point.
- Villain-heavy narratives that alienate persuadables who identify with the "villain".
- Using narrative technique deceptively (inventing characters or events) — it destroys
  the trust narratives rely on.

## Verify
- [ ] A worldview map with quotes exists for each priority audience.
- [ ] The story names a "we", a cause, stakes, a future and a path.
- [ ] 3–4 themes chosen and visible in every current campaign.
- [ ] Partners have a toolkit and are using the shared language.
- [ ] Measurement tracks underlying beliefs and frame adoption over time.
