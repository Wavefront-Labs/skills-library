# Message calendars, surrogates and rapid response

**Goal:** every voice in the campaign saying the same few things, on a planned rhythm, while
reacting fast and selectively to attacks and news — so that the campaign sets more of the
agenda than it chases.

## 1. The grid (message calendar)
A rolling 2–6 week calendar, refreshed weekly, with one row per day:
- **Message of the day/week** — tied to the core message (see
  media-communication.persuasive-messaging.message-development.framing-and-building-a-message-hierarchy).
- **Moment** — the event, announcement, report, or visual that carries it.
- **Messenger** — candidate/leader, surrogate, validator, real person affected.
- **Channels** — press, paid, social, email, field scripts.
- **Known external events** — votes, debates, opponent's events, holidays, competing news.
Rules: one message per day, don't step on your own story, and leave slack for reaction.
Big announcements early in the week and morning usually give the news cycle time to pick
them up (check your local media rhythm).

## 2. Lines-to-take (LTT) documents
Short, approved, current. Per issue:
- **Top line** (one sentence to say first);
- 2–3 supporting points with facts and sources;
- **If pressed** answers for the 3 most likely follow-ups;
- **Don't say** list (phrases that repeat attacks or create new stories);
- date, version, owner. Out-of-date LTTs cause more gaffes than no LTTs — expire them.

## 3. Surrogate management
Surrogates (allies, elected officials, celebrities, experts, community voices) multiply reach.
- **Briefing pack** each week: message of the week, LTTs, the one ask of them, upcoming
  bookings, a contact for urgent questions.
- **Booking desk**: track who's going where, so two surrogates don't say different things on
  the same programme.
- Match surrogate to audience: the most credible voice for a segment is rarely the most
  famous.
- Brief them before every interview; debrief afterwards (what was asked? any new attack?).
- Have a policy for when a surrogate goes off-message or becomes a story (distance quickly,
  privately first).

## 4. Rapid response loop
1. **Monitor:** a small team watches broadcast, social, opponent channels and wires,
   with alerts on key names and issues.
2. **Triage** (minutes): Is it false/misleading? Is it reaching our persuadables, or just
   noise? Will responding amplify it to people who'd never have seen it?
   Many attacks are best ignored.
3. **Decide** the response type: correct the fact; pivot to our message; deploy a validator;
   counter-attack; or do nothing. The rule of thumb is to respond within the same news
   cycle — often within an hour for live moments like debates — if you respond at all.
4. **Clear** fast: pre-agreed sign-off for each response type, with a named approver
   available at all hours during peak periods.
5. **Distribute** to press, surrogates, and social simultaneously, then log it.
Pre-build a **rebuttal library**: researched answers to every attack you expect, with
sources, ready to deploy.

## 5. Legal and ethical boundaries
Election and advertising law apply: imprints/disclaimers on campaign material, spending
limits, coordination rules between campaigns and outside groups, and defamation law for
attacks. Rules differ sharply by country and election type — get campaign counsel to sign
off the rapid-response playbook, not each tweet at 11pm.

## Gotchas
- Responding to everything, so the opponent sets your agenda.
- Surrogates working from last month's LTTs.
- No overnight approver during the final weeks.
- "Gotcha" responses that are themselves inaccurate — the correction becomes the story.

## Verify
- [ ] A current grid exists with one message per day and named messengers.
- [ ] Every active issue has a dated, owned LTT with a "don't say" list.
- [ ] Surrogates received this week's briefing pack.
- [ ] Rapid-response triage asks "does responding amplify?" before acting.
- [ ] Counsel has approved the playbook and disclaimer rules.
