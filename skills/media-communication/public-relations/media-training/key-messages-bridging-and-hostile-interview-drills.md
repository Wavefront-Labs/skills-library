# Media training: key messages, bridging and hostile-interview drills

**Goal:** a spokesperson who answers real questions honestly *and* lands the two or three
things the audience should remember — on camera, under pressure, in 10-second chunks.

## 1. Build the message house (before any rehearsal)
- **Roof:** one overarching sentence — what you want the audience to think after the
  interview.
- **Three pillars:** key messages, each one sentence a 12-year-old would understand.
- **Foundations:** proof for each pillar — a number, an example, a human story. Proof
  makes messages quotable; bare messages sound like spin.
Test each message: *Is it true? Does the audience care? Can you say it in under 15 seconds?*

## 2. Bridging — done honestly
The ABC pattern: **Answer** (briefly, actually answer), **Bridge**, **Communicate** a key
message.
- Bridges: "What matters most here is…", "The bigger issue is…", "What I can tell you is…",
  "Let me put that in context…".
- The answer step is not optional. Bridging without answering is what makes audiences and
  journalists hostile; a politician refusing the same question 12 times is the cautionary
  tale.
- If you genuinely can't answer (legal, privacy, unknown), say why in one line and bridge:
  "I can't discuss an individual employee, but what I can tell you is what we've changed…"

## 3. Ground rules the spokesperson must know cold
- Assume **everything is on the record**, including before and after the "official"
  interview, in the lift, and on a mic you think is off.
- "Off the record", "on background" and "not for attribution" mean different things to
  different outlets and countries; agree terms explicitly *before* speaking, not after.
  When in doubt, don't.
- Never say "no comment" — give the reason you can't comment.
- Never repeat a negative frame: asked "Are you covering this up?", don't say "We're not
  covering anything up" (that becomes the headline). Say what you *are* doing.
- Don't speculate, don't answer hypotheticals about other people, don't guess numbers.
- Know the format: live vs. pre-recorded, studio vs. down-the-line, how long, who else is on.

## 4. Drill structure (half-day, typical)
1. **Briefing** on the format and outlet; review the message house.
2. **Round 1 — friendly interview**, recorded on a phone or camera. Play back; note filler
   words, eye-line, length of answers.
3. **Round 2 — tough but fair**: the questions the reporter will really ask, including the
   one leadership hopes won't come up.
4. **Round 3 — hostile**: interruptions, false premises, silence after the answer (to
   bait over-talking), the "just one more thing" after the camera seems off, a doorstep
   ambush walking to a car.
5. **Playback each round** with specific notes; repeat the weakest answer until it's clean.
Coach on delivery: sit forward, look at the interviewer (not the camera) unless down-the-line,
keep answers to ~20–30 seconds for broadcast, finish sentences cleanly so they can be clipped.

## 5. The killer-questions list
Write the 10 worst questions and a draft honest answer for each. Include personal ones
(pay, past statements), "yes or no" traps, and "what do you say to the victims?".

## Gotchas
- Over-rehearsed spokespeople sound robotic — rehearse messages, not scripts.
- Jargon and acronyms; the audience is not your org chart.
- Training the CEO once, years ago, then sending them to a crisis interview.
- Correcting the record: if you misspoke, call the reporter immediately, before publication.

## Verify
- [ ] Spokesperson can state the roof and three pillars from memory, each in under 15 seconds.
- [ ] Every pillar has at least one concrete proof point.
- [ ] Recorded answers actually answer the question before bridging.
- [ ] They have practised the killer questions out loud, on camera.
- [ ] They can explain on/off-the-record terms and default to on.
