# Writing a press release journalists actually use

**Goal:** a release that a busy reporter can read in under a minute, decide "there's a
story", and lift facts and a quote from without calling you to decode it. Most releases
fail before the first word: there is no news in them.

## 1. Pass the news test first
Before drafting, write the story as a one-sentence headline a *publication* would run
(not your marketing line). If you can't, don't send a release — post a blog or update
instead. Real news usually has at least one of: something new or first, a number that
surprises, a change that affects people the outlet serves, conflict or reversal, a local
angle, timeliness (tied to a date, report or trend), or a notable person.
Ask: *who outside our building would care, and why today?*

## 2. Structure (inverted pyramid)
1. **Release line** — `FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE` or `EMBARGOED UNTIL 00:01 GMT, Tuesday 14 October 2026`.
   Always state time *and* zone for embargoes.
2. **Headline** — under ~12 words, active verb, the actual news. Optional subhead with the
   second key fact.
3. **Dateline** — `CITY, Date —` (AP style abbreviates most months; check the house style
   of your market — UK releases often don't use AP).
4. **Lede (first paragraph)** — who, what, when, where, why in 1–2 sentences, ≤ ~35 words.
   If the reporter reads nothing else, they have the story.
5. **Second paragraph** — the most important supporting detail: the number, the impact,
   the context.
6. **Quote** — one, maybe two. See below.
7. **Supporting detail** — background, availability, price, methodology, links to data.
8. **Boilerplate** — "About <Org>": 2–4 factual sentences, kept identical across releases.
9. **Media contact** — a named human, a phone that will be answered, email. Then `###`
   or `-ENDS-` to mark the end.
Target 300–500 words. If it's longer, the extra belongs in a fact sheet or backgrounder.

## 3. Quotes that earn their place
A quote should add **opinion, emotion or meaning** — never restate facts in the paragraph
above. Bad: "We are thrilled to announce this innovative solution." Good: a specific claim
in human language a reporter might actually print ("Half our customers were driving 40
minutes to a branch. That ends this month."). Get the named person to approve it in writing;
attributing words they didn't approve is how releases get retracted.

## 4. Language
- Strip adjectives: *leading, innovative, world-class, revolutionary, thrilled, excited*.
- Spell out acronyms on first use; no internal product codenames.
- Every number sourced (link the report or say "according to <org> survey of n=1,200").
- Write so a paragraph can be cut from the bottom without breaking the story.

## 5. Distribution and embargoes
- **Personalised pitch email** to targeted reporters beats any wire. Put the release in the
  email body (attachments are often stripped or ignored), with a 2–3 line note on why it
  fits *their* beat.
- **Wire services** (Business Wire, PR Newswire, GlobeNewswire and regional equivalents)
  matter mainly for listed companies' disclosure obligations and search/syndication pickup;
  syndicated pickup is not coverage. Check the wire's formatting rules and cutoff times
  (unconfirmed per-wire specifics vary — read their current guide).
- **Embargoes** are an agreement, not a law. Only send under embargo to people who've
  agreed to it, ideally asking first. Expect occasional breaks; never embargo material that
  would be harmful if it leaked early (e.g. market-sensitive data — that follows disclosure
  rules, not PR convention).
- Include assets: hi-res images with captions and credit, spokesperson availability windows.

## Gotchas
- "Announces" headlines with no news: rewrite or don't send.
- Burying the lede under company history.
- A contact who's on leave the day it goes out.
- Sending Friday afternoon or into a big competing news event, unless the aim is to bury.
- Listed companies: anything price-sensitive must go through the formal disclosure channel
  *first* or simultaneously — involve legal/IR.

## Verify
- [ ] Headline reads like a news headline, not an ad.
- [ ] Lede alone answers who/what/when/where/why.
- [ ] Every number has a source; every quote has written sign-off.
- [ ] Embargo line has date, time and time zone (or says immediate release).
- [ ] Named contact is reachable on send day; boilerplate is current.
- [ ] A colleague outside the project can say what the news is after 30 seconds.
