# Thought leadership: bylines, speaking pitches and awards

**Goal:** make a leader known for a few specific, defensible ideas in the places their
audience pays attention — without them sounding like a press release or a ghostwriter.

## 1. Pick the territories (positioning workshop, 60–90 min)
Interview the executive. Look for the overlap of:
- what they **genuinely know** better than most (experience, data, scars);
- what the **target audience** (customers, policymakers, talent, investors) cares about;
- where the **conversation has a gap** — a view others aren't voicing.
Output: 2–3 territories, each with a one-line **point of view** that someone could disagree
with ("Most AI pilots in hospitals fail because of procurement, not technology"). If nobody
could disagree, it isn't thought leadership.
Also agree the **no-go zones** (politics, competitors, pending litigation) and a sign-off
process with realistic turnaround.

## 2. Ghostwriting in their voice
- Record a 30-minute conversation on the topic; draft from the transcript so phrases and
  examples are theirs.
- Keep a **voice sheet**: words they use and don't, sentence length, humour level, stories
  they tell often.
- Structure for an op-ed (typically 600–900 words; check each outlet): a news peg in the
  first paragraph, the argument stated by paragraph two or three, 2–3 pieces of evidence, the
  counter-argument acknowledged, a concrete recommendation.
- Ethics: the named author must have read and genuinely agree with every sentence. Many
  outlets ask whether a piece was ghostwritten or is under exclusive submission — answer
  honestly.

## 3. Pitching op-eds and bylines
- Opinion editors want **exclusive** submissions: one outlet at a time. If no reply within
  the outlet's stated window (often a few days for news-pegged pieces), withdraw politely
  and move on.
- Pitch email: the argument in two sentences, why now, why this author, word count. Often
  send the full draft for op-eds; trade publications may prefer a pitch first.
- Self-promotion kills placement: no product mentions beyond the author's bio line.

## 4. Speaking pitches
- Build a target list of conferences, podcasts and panels by audience fit; note call-for-
  papers deadlines (frequently 4–9 months before the event).
- Submission: compelling title, 100–200-word abstract with three takeaways, speaker bio,
  video of previous talks if any. Organisers book speakers who bring something specific,
  not "a fireside chat about innovation".
- Avoid pay-to-speak slots presented as editorial; at least know which ones are sponsored.

## 5. Award submissions
Judges read dozens of entries. Win by:
- answering **each criterion under its own heading**, in the order given;
- leading with results, quantified against a baseline ("cut waiting times 38% in 12 months");
- including evidence (testimonials, data, third-party verification) within the word limit;
- choosing categories where you can genuinely evidence impact, not the most prestigious one.

## 6. Social voice
LinkedIn (or the platform the audience uses) amplifies everything else. Short posts in the
executive's voice, 1–3 times a week, reacting to news in the territories, sharing others'
work, not just company announcements. If staff draft them, the executive still approves
and engages with comments personally.

## Gotchas
- Too many territories → known for nothing.
- Approval chains that take three weeks kill news-pegged pieces.
- Recycling the same byline to multiple outlets (breaches exclusivity; burns editors).
- Fake or inflated credentials in bios — easily checked, permanently damaging.

## Verify
- [ ] Each territory has a point of view a reasonable person could disagree with.
- [ ] Drafts come from the executive's own words and pass their "I'd say that" test.
- [ ] Op-eds pitched exclusively, one outlet at a time.
- [ ] Award entries answer every criterion with quantified, evidenced results.
- [ ] A 90-day calendar exists mixing bylines, speaking, awards and social.
