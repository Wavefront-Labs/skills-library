# Crisis communications: holding statements and the first 24 hours

**Goal:** say something true, fast, in the right order to the right people, without
locking yourself into facts you don't have yet — then keep updating until the story is over.

## 1. Triage in the first 15 minutes
Answer four questions with whoever knows the facts (ops, legal, security):
1. **What do we actually know** vs. suspect? Write two columns.
2. **Who is harmed or at risk**, and is anyone still in danger? Safety instructions beat
   reputation every time.
3. **Who already knows** — is it public, on social, with a reporter?
4. **Severity tier** from the playbook (e.g. 1 = local, contained; 2 = notable, media
   likely; 3 = major harm, regulators, national coverage). Tier sets who's on the call and
   who signs off.
Stand up a small crisis cell: decision-maker, comms lead, legal, the operational owner,
and a scribe keeping a timestamped log of facts, decisions and statements.

## 2. Holding statement within ~1 hour of it going public
A holding statement buys time honestly. Pattern:
- **Acknowledge** what happened in plain words (no "incident" euphemism if people were hurt).
- **Care** — concern for those affected, stated once, specifically.
- **Action** — what you are doing *now* ("we have shut the line", "we have notified the
  regulator", "we are contacting affected customers directly").
- **Instruction** — what affected people should do, with a channel.
- **Next update** — when and where ("We will update by 15:00 at <page>").
Pre-draft templates for your likely scenarios (outage, data breach, injury, recall,
executive misconduct) so this is fill-in, not blank-page.
Avoid: speculation on cause, blame (including of suppliers), numbers you'll have to revise,
"no comment" (reads as guilt), and "out of an abundance of caution" boilerplate.

## 3. Stakeholder sequencing
People who are directly affected should not learn it from the news. Typical order —
adjust per case and legal duty:
1. Emergency services / anyone needing safety action.
2. Regulators with notification duties (some have hard clocks — e.g. GDPR's 72 hours to the
   supervisory authority for personal-data breaches; check your sector and jurisdiction).
3. Affected people (customers, patients, families) — directly, not via a press release.
4. Staff, before or simultaneously with the public; give front-line staff a script.
5. Board, investors, key partners (listed companies: disclosure rules may set timing).
6. Media and public channels.
Everyone gets the **same facts**; tone and detail vary.

## 4. Cadence and channels
- Publish to one canonical page and point everything at it.
- Update at the times you promised, even if the update is "no new information; next update
  at 18:00".
- Monitor social and press; correct factual errors quickly and politely, ignore pure noise.
- Prepare a reactive Q&A covering the ten hardest questions, including the ones leadership
  doesn't want asked.

## 5. Apology and correction
Apologise when you are responsible; a credible apology names the specific failure, takes
ownership without "if anyone was offended", says what changes, and offers remedy. Clear the
wording with legal — in many jurisdictions apology laws limit liability for expressions of
sympathy but not admissions of fault (varies; confirm locally). If you said something
wrong, correct it explicitly and early: a self-correction is a one-day story; being caught
is a week.

## Gotchas
- The silent first hours get filled by someone else's narrative.
- Legal and comms fighting over every word — agree the "minimum true statement" pattern in
  advance.
- The CEO going on camera before facts are stable.
- Forgetting scheduled marketing posts — pause them.

## Verify
- [ ] Holding statement out within the target window and contains acknowledge/care/action/next update.
- [ ] Affected people and regulators heard before (or with) the media.
- [ ] Every statement is in the log with time and approver.
- [ ] Each promised update happened on time.
- [ ] Nothing published has had to be walked back — or if it did, the correction was proactive.
