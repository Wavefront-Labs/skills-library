# Earned-media campaign planning and coverage measurement

**Goal:** a campaign whose objective is a change in what an audience knows, thinks or does,
with stories engineered to earn coverage in the outlets that audience actually uses — and
evidence afterwards of whether it worked.

## 1. Objectives that measure outcomes, not output
Three levels; set at least one at the outcome level:
- **Output:** coverage pieces, reach. (Easy to count, weakly linked to results.)
- **Outtake:** audience awareness, message recall, site visits, sign-ups from coverage.
- **Outcome:** behaviour or opinion change — enquiries, donations, policy movement, trust score.
Write them SMART: "Raise prompted awareness of <programme> among UK parents of under-5s from
12% to 20% by March, measured by pre/post survey." If budget can't stretch to surveys, use
proxies (branded search volume, referral traffic, enquiries) and say so.

## 2. Audience → outlet map
For each target audience, list where they get information: specific titles, programmes,
podcasts, newsletters, creators, trade press. Then list the **specific journalists** and
what they've covered in the last 3 months. A 30-name targeted list beats a 3,000-name
database blast. Note each outlet's lead times: monthly magazines plan 3–6 months out;
daily news is days; broadcast planning desks want early notice of filmable moments.

## 3. Story engineering
Coverage comes from stories, not announcements. Hooks that reliably earn space:
- **Data:** original research or a survey (publish method and sample size; a weak survey
  gets torn apart).
- **Human:** a case study willing to be named and photographed.
- **Visual/filmable:** a stunt, event or moment broadcasters can shoot.
- **Timely:** pegged to an awareness day, report release, season or anniversary.
- **Contrarian:** a credible expert saying something unexpected.
- **Local versions:** regional data cuts multiply coverage.
Build a **story calendar**: a launch moment, then follow-on beats every 2–4 weeks (regional
data, case studies, expert op-ed, results update) so the campaign isn't one spike.

## 4. Launch sequencing
1. Brief spokespeople and case studies (see media-communication.public-relations.media-training.key-messages-bridging-and-hostile-interview-drills).
2. Offer an **exclusive** or embargoed preview to the one outlet that matters most, if that
   suits the story; others then follow.
3. Sell-in personalised pitches ~1–2 weeks ahead for features, 1–3 days for news.
4. Launch day: release out early (often 00:01 embargo for morning media), spokespeople
   on standby, social assets ready, monitoring live.
5. Follow up once, briefly, with something new — not "did you get my email?".

## 5. Measuring honestly
- Do **not** use AVE (advertising value equivalency — "this coverage would have cost £X
  in ads"). The Barcelona Principles explicitly reject it; it measures nothing about effect.
- Score coverage for **quality**: target outlet? key message included? spokesperson quoted?
  call to action or link present? tone? A small scorecard (0–2 per criterion) works.
- Track **outtakes/outcomes** against baseline: survey shifts, referral traffic with UTM
  links, enquiries, search lift.
- Report what didn't work and why; that's what improves the next plan.

## Gotchas
- Objectives set after the campaign to match what happened.
- Surveys commissioned to "prove" a pre-decided headline — journalists notice leading
  questions.
- One big launch with nothing after; coverage decays within days.
- Ignoring competing news: check the calendar for budgets, elections, big sports.

## Verify
- [ ] At least one outcome-level objective with a baseline and a measure.
- [ ] Media list is named journalists with recent relevant work, not a bulk database export.
- [ ] Story calendar has a launch plus ≥3 follow-on beats.
- [ ] Every data hook has a disclosed method and sample.
- [ ] Measurement report contains no AVE and includes outcome evidence or a stated proxy.
