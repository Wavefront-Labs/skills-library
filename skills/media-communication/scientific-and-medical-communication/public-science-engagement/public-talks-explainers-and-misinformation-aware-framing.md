# Public talks, explainers and misinformation-aware framing

**Goal:** people leave with one or two ideas they understand, remember and can repeat
accurately — and a bit more trust in how science works — whether they met you at a pub
talk, a museum, or in a 60-second video.

## 1. Start from the audience, not the content
- Who are they, what do they already know or believe, why are they here, and what's in it
  for them? A families' science festival, a retirees' society and a policy audience need
  different talks from the same research.
- Move past the **deficit model** (the idea that people only need more facts). Values,
  identity and trust shape how information is received; dialogue and listening are part of
  engagement, not an extra.
- Choose **one core message** (the "if they remember one thing" sentence) and at most
  three supporting points.

## 2. Talks and festival sessions
- **Open with a hook:** a question, a surprising object, a story, a demonstration — not your
  CV or the title slide.
- **Structure as a story:** a puzzle → how people tried to solve it → what was found →
  what it means → what we still don't know.
- **Show, don't list:** images, props, live demos and short videos beat bullet points. A
  slide should support you, not repeat what you say.
- **Pitch level:** assume a curious 14-year-old; explain every technical word or drop it.
- **Interaction:** quick polls, hands-up guesses, hands-on objects; for festivals, shorter
  bursts (10–20 minutes) and drop-in formats.
- **Q&A:** repeat the question so everyone hears it, answer briefly, admit "I don't know" or
  "nobody knows yet" when true.
- Rehearse aloud with a timer; cut 20%.

## 3. Writing explainers
- Headline as the question people actually ask ("Why do we need a new COVID vaccine
  every year?").
- **Answer early**, then explain how we know, then nuances.
- **Analogies** help, but say where they break down ("A bit like a lock and key — except
  the lock can change shape").
- Use concrete numbers on a human scale and visuals where possible.
- Link to sources; date the piece and update it as evidence changes.

## 4. Social media
- Each platform rewards different formats — short vertical video, threads, carousels,
  long-form video. Pick one or two and be consistent.
- Lead in the first seconds with the hook; put the payoff before people scroll.
- Show yourself and the process (the lab, fieldwork, failures) — authenticity builds trust.
- Expect hostility on contested topics; decide in advance what you'll engage with, block
  abusive accounts, and protect your wellbeing. Check your institution's social media
  policy.

## 5. Misinformation-aware framing
- **Don't headline the myth.** Lead with the accurate claim; if you must address a myth,
  mention it once and explain why it's wrong (see
  media-communication.persuasive-messaging.counter-messaging.prebunking-debunking-and-when-not-to-amplify).
- **Prebunk** predictable misinformation and common techniques (fake experts,
  cherry-picking) before they arrive, especially around new findings or campaigns.
- **Be open about uncertainty and change:** explain that updating conclusions when
  evidence changes is how science works — it inoculates against "they keep changing their
  story".
- **Explain the consensus and how it was reached**, not just that it exists.
- Use messengers trusted by the audience — local clinicians, community figures, peers.
- Avoid ridicule of people who hold mistaken beliefs; the watching audience notices.

## 6. Evaluation
Short exit surveys (what's one thing you learned? would you tell someone?), observation,
or follow-up questions. For online content, comments and questions are more informative than
views.

## Gotchas
- A conference talk recycled for a public audience.
- Over-simplifying into something false (e.g. "genes for" complex traits).
- Projecting certainty to sound authoritative, then losing trust when evidence changes.
- Giant myth-busting slides with the myth in bold.

## Verify
- [ ] One core message written down before preparing slides or scripts.
- [ ] Talk opens with a hook and follows a puzzle-to-meaning story.
- [ ] Every technical term explained or removed; analogies flagged where they fail.
- [ ] Myths, if addressed, appear once and after the accurate claim.
- [ ] Uncertainty and how the evidence was gathered are explained, not hidden.
