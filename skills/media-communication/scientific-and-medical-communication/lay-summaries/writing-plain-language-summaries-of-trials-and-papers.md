# Writing plain-language summaries of trials and papers

**Goal:** a summary that a person with no scientific training can read once and correctly
understand what was studied, what was found, and what it does *not* mean for them — without
being simplified into something false.

## 1. Know which kind you're writing
- **Clinical trial lay summary** (results for participants and the public). In the EU, the
  Clinical Trials Regulation (536/2014) requires sponsors to post one in the EU database,
  generally within a year of trial end (shorter for paediatric trials); Annex V lists the
  required content. Other regions have guidance rather than mandates (check current rules).
- **Plain-language summary of a publication (PLSP)** — published alongside or linked to a
  journal article; journals increasingly have their own templates and peer-review them.
- **Patient-facing explanation** in leaflets or websites.
All are **non-promotional**: no marketing claims, no brand emphasis, balanced benefits and harms.

## 2. A question-led structure (adapt to template)
1. Why was this study done? (the health problem, in a sentence or two)
2. What treatment/question was studied?
3. Who took part? (number, ages, sex, where)
4. What happened during the study? (design: randomised? placebo? how long?)
5. What were the results? (main outcome first)
6. What side effects/harms were seen?
7. How has this study helped? What are the limitations?
8. Are more studies planned? Where can I learn more?
Headings as questions help readers navigate. One study's results should not be presented
as the definitive answer; say so explicitly.

## 3. Plain words
- Aim for roughly a **reading age of 11–13 years** (US grade 6–8) for general audiences;
  check with readability formulas (Flesch–Kincaid etc.) but don't write *to* the formula —
  short words in confusing order still fail.
- Replace jargon: "placebo" → explain once ("a dummy treatment that looks like the medicine
  but has no medicine in it"); "adverse event" → "medical problem"; "efficacy" → "how well it
  worked". Keep a glossary for unavoidable terms.
- Short sentences (≈15–20 words), active voice, "you" only if addressing participants.
- One idea per paragraph; bullet lists for side effects.
- Avoid tables of p-values; use simple charts or icon arrays if the template allows.

## 4. Honest numbers
- Natural frequencies: "About 3 in 10 people (30%)". Use the same denominator when comparing
  groups.
- Give results for **both** groups ("In the medicine group, 24 of 100 people… In the dummy
  treatment group, 31 of 100…").
- Don't report only relative differences.
- Report serious and common side effects with the same care as benefits.
- Statistical significance: describe as "the difference was large enough that it was
  unlikely to be due to chance" or, if not, "the study could not show a difference" —
  never "didn't work" when it was underpowered, nor "works" when it didn't meet its main goal.

## 5. Accuracy under simplification
- Every simplified statement must still be **true**. If simplifying would mislead, keep the
  nuance and explain it.
- Have a **scientific reviewer** (an author or medical lead) check accuracy, and a **lay
  reviewer** (patients or members of the public) check understanding: ask them to explain
  back what they think it says. Revise where they misunderstand.
- Avoid causal claims the study can't support, and claims about individual benefit.
- Include a sentence like "Participants should not change their treatment based on this
  summary; talk to your doctor."

## 6. Practical bits
Translate (with back-translation or review) for countries where participants were enrolled;
check accessible formats (large print, screen readers, alt text for figures).

## Gotchas
- Promotional language sneaking in ("an exciting new treatment").
- Results presented for the drug group only.
- Writing "simple" text that a lay reader still can't explain back.
- Forgetting the limitations section because it "worries" readers.

## Verify
- [ ] Follows the required template/question headings for its type.
- [ ] Reading level checked and jargon replaced or explained.
- [ ] Results and harms reported for every group using natural frequencies.
- [ ] Scientific accuracy signed off; lay readers could explain it back correctly.
- [ ] No promotional wording; limitations and "talk to your doctor" line present.
