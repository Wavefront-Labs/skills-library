# Appraising papers and reporting uncertainty without hype

**Goal:** decide quickly whether a study is worth covering, report what it actually shows
(and doesn't), and leave readers better calibrated rather than more confused.

## 1. Fifteen-minute triage of a paper
Read the **abstract, then the methods, then the results tables** — not the press release.
1. **What kind of study?** Rough strength ladder for causal health claims: systematic
   review/meta-analysis of RCTs > randomised controlled trial > cohort > case-control >
   cross-sectional > case series/anecdote > lab/animal/cell studies. Expert opinion is
   evidence too, but the weakest kind.
2. **Who/what was studied?** Humans or mice? How many? Representative of whom?
3. **What was measured?** A hard outcome (deaths, heart attacks) or a **surrogate** (a blood
   marker, a scan finding)?
4. **How big is the effect** — with confidence intervals? A statistically significant
   effect can be tiny.
5. **Compared with what?** Placebo, standard care, nothing?
6. **Peer-reviewed or preprint?** Preprints can be important and fast, but say so clearly.
7. **Funding and conflicts** — in the disclosures section.
8. **Does it fit the wider evidence**, or is it a single surprising result?

## 2. Red flags for hype
- Press release claims that go beyond the paper's conclusions (especially causal language
  from observational data — "X causes Y" when the paper found an association).
- **Relative risk without absolute risk**: "doubles your risk" may mean 1 in 10,000 → 2 in
  10,000.
- Animal or cell results headlined as if about people.
- Small samples, many outcomes tested, one "significant" finding.
- "Breakthrough", "cure", "miracle" — almost never accurate.
- Composite or surrogate endpoints presented as patient benefit.
- The only experts quoted are the authors and their institution.

## 3. Converting numbers
- Always give **absolute numbers**: "Out of 1,000 people like those in the study, about 12
  on the drug and 18 on placebo had a heart attack over five years."
- **Number needed to treat** (NNT = 1 / absolute risk reduction) is useful: from the example,
  ARR = 0.6% → NNT ≈ 167.
- Natural frequencies ("3 in 100") are understood better than percentages and far better
  than odds ratios.
- Mention harms and costs alongside benefits.

## 4. Interviewing
- **Authors:** ask what the study *can't* tell us, what surprised them, what the next step
  is, and what they'd want readers not to conclude.
- **Independent experts** — at least one who wasn't involved. Science media centres in
  several countries (UK, Australia, NZ, Germany and others) circulate expert reactions to
  embargoed papers; use them, but also find your own.
- Ask them to rate the strength of evidence in plain terms.

## 5. Writing uncertainty
- State the finding, then **how confident we should be** and **why** ("This was a small
  early trial; larger ones are under way").
- Use calibrated words consistently (suggests / shows / proves), and avoid "proves" almost always.
- Don't create false balance: one contrarian against a strong consensus is not a 50/50 debate.
- Put caveats high, not in the last paragraph.
- Headlines must not overclaim even if the body is careful — work with editors on them.

## 6. Embargoes
Journals and services (EurekAlert! etc.) send embargoed papers for preparation time.
Honour embargoes; use the time to read the paper and get independent comment — not just
to rewrite the release.

## Gotchas
- Writing from the press release alone.
- Reporting each study as a flip-flop ("coffee is bad / good this week") without context.
- Ignoring that the journal or conference abstract may have no full data yet.
- Unsourced "studies show".

## Verify
- [ ] Study type, population, outcome, effect size and funding identified from the paper itself.
- [ ] Absolute risks given whenever relative risk is mentioned.
- [ ] At least one independent expert quoted.
- [ ] Limitations appear in the top third of the story.
- [ ] Headline matches the strength of the evidence.
