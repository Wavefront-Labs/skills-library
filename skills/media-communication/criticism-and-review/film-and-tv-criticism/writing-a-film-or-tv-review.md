# Writing a film or TV review

**Goal:** produce a review that tells readers whether and why the work is worth their
time, makes an argument rather than a plot summary, and respects embargoes and spoilers
— often within hours of a screening.

## 1. Watching like a critic
- Watch with a notebook (dim screen or paper in a cinema). Note **timestamps/moments**,
  lines of dialogue verbatim, shots or cuts that struck you, and your reactions as they
  happen — reactions fade fast and you need them to reconstruct the experience.
- Record the factual basics: title, director, writer(s), principal cast, runtime, rating/
  certificate, release date and platform. Check spellings against the press kit.
- If it's a screener link, watch on the largest screen available. Judging cinematography
  on a phone is unfair to the film; say if you did.

## 2. Find the argument (before writing)
Answer in one sentence: **"What is this trying to do, and how well does it do it?"**
That sentence is your thesis. Then three supporting points, each anchored to specific
evidence from the work (a scene, a performance choice, an editing pattern).
Judge the work on its ambitions first, then on whether those ambitions were worth having.

## 3. Structure (a reliable default)
1. **Lede** — an image, a moment or a provocation that sets up the judgement. Not
   "In this film...".
2. **Context + setup** — premise in 2–4 sentences, *only* what's needed; where it sits in
   the director's work, genre or the source material.
3. **Argument** — 2–4 paragraphs of evidence: performance, writing, direction, craft.
4. **Reservations / counterpoint** — the strongest case against your view.
5. **Verdict** — who it's for, and the rating if your outlet uses one.
Lengths: a capsule is 100–200 words; a standard review 500–900; a lead review longer.

## 4. Use vocabulary precisely
Name *what* produces the effect: a long take, a match cut, shallow focus, handheld,
blocking, production design, score vs needle drops, sound mix, pacing in the edit,
an actor's restraint. "Beautifully shot" is a reaction; "shot in low, raking light that
makes every interior feel like late afternoon" is criticism. Don't use jargon to sound
expert — every term should help the reader see.

## 5. Spoilers
- Default: reveal nothing past the end of the first act or what's in the trailer.
- If discussion needs a spoiler, flag it clearly (a "Spoilers follow" line before the
  section) or write a separate spoiler piece after release.
- TV: reviewing a season from screeners — the network's embargo letter usually lists
  which episodes and plot points may be discussed. Follow it.

## 6. Embargoes, screeners and festivals
- An **embargo** is an agreed publication time; breaking it can lose your outlet access.
  Confirm exact date/time and timezone in writing. *Embargo terms vary by distributor;
  never assume.*
- Screener links are watermarked/personal; don't share them.
- Festivals: plan a schedule, write capsules same day, flag world-premiere status; note
  the film may change (re-edits) before release and say so if you review a festival cut.

## 7. Ethics
Disclose any relationship with filmmakers. Don't accept travel or gifts beyond your
outlet's policy. Review the work, not the person, and don't mock actors' bodies.

## Gotchas
- Plot summary creep: if over a third of the review is synopsis, cut.
- Reviewing the film you wanted instead of the one made.
- Star rating and prose disagreeing — readers notice; align them.
- TV: judging a whole series on the first two episodes; say how many you saw.

## Verify
- [ ] One-sentence thesis exists and the verdict matches it.
- [ ] Every judgement is anchored to a specific moment or craft choice.
- [ ] Synopsis ≤ about a third of the word count; no unflagged spoilers.
- [ ] Credits, names and runtime checked against the press notes.
- [ ] Embargo time confirmed; for TV, number of episodes seen is stated.
