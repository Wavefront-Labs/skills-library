# The reference interview and search strategy

**Goal:** turn "Do you have anything on climate?" into the information the person
actually needs, find it efficiently across databases, and leave them more capable of
searching next time.

## 1. The reference interview
The first question is rarely the real one. People ask broadly (or for a specific item
they think will help) because they don't know what exists. RUSA's behavioural
guidelines frame it as: **approachability › interest › listening/inquiring › searching
› follow-up**.
- **Approachable:** look up, acknowledge immediately (in person, chat or email).
- **Open questions** to learn the need: "What are you working on?", "What would the
  perfect answer look like?", "What have you found so far?"
- **Neutral questions** (Dervin's sense-making approach) avoid presuming: "What will
  you use this for?" — ask about the situation, not the topic.
- Clarify the constraints: **purpose** (essay, decision, curiosity), **level** (child,
  undergrad, specialist), **amount** and **type** (one fact, overview, peer-reviewed
  only), **timeframe/currency**, **deadline**, languages, format.
- **Paraphrase back:** "So you need 3–5 peer-reviewed studies since 2018 on heat-related
  hospital admissions in older adults, for a literature review due Friday?"
- Privacy: patron questions are confidential; don't discuss them with others, and
  minimise what you record.

## 2. Building the search strategy
1. **Split the question into concepts** (usually 2–4): *heat waves* | *hospital admissions*
   | *older adults*.
2. **Synonyms and variants per concept:** heat wave, heatwave, extreme heat, high
   temperature; elderly, older adults, aged, seniors.
3. **Combine:** OR within a concept, AND between concepts:
   ```
   ("heat wave*" OR heatwave* OR "extreme heat") AND
   (hospitali* OR "hospital admission*") AND
   (elderly OR "older adult*" OR aged)
   ```
   Phrase quotes, `*` truncation, and NOT sparingly (it silently drops relevant items).
   Syntax differs by platform (EBSCO, ProQuest, PubMed, Web of Science) — check help pages.
4. **Controlled vocabulary:** find the database's subject terms (MeSH in PubMed, the
   EBSCO/ERIC thesauri, LCSH in catalogues) from one relevant record and add them —
   they catch items your keywords miss.
5. **Limit** with filters: date, peer review, language, publication type.
6. **Iterate:** too many → add a concept, use subject terms as major topic, narrow dates;
   too few → more synonyms, drop the least essential concept, broaden terms.
7. **Chain citations:** from 1–2 great articles, check their references (backward) and
   "cited by" in Google Scholar/Web of Science/Scopus (forward). "Pearl growing" from
   good records' subject terms.
Choose sources by need: catalogue for books, subject databases for scholarship,
statistical agencies for data, news databases for current events, grey literature for policy.

## 3. Teach as you search (information literacy)
Narrate what you're doing and why ("I'm adding 'aged' because that's PubMed's term"),
let the user drive the keyboard when possible, and leave a written strategy they can
rerun. Touch on evaluation: who produced it, evidence, currency, purpose — the ACRL
Framework's "authority is constructed and contextual" in practice.

## 4. Follow-up
Always close with: **"Does this answer your question?"** and an invitation to come back.
For complex queries, offer a follow-up appointment or email the strategy and links
(respecting licensing — share via the library's link resolver, not copies of PDFs).

## Gotchas
- Answering the first question asked, fast and wrong.
- Jargon: "databases", "peer-reviewed", "interlibrary loan" may need explaining.
- Overwhelming with 500 results; give the user a manageable, relevant starter set.
- Medical/legal questions: provide information and sources, not advice or interpretation.
