# Writing community guidelines and an enforcement ladder

**Goal:** rules members actually read and understand, plus an internal version detailed
enough that two moderators make the same call on the same post.

This is the community-manager scale (a server, forum, subreddit, brand community). For
platform-scale policy see law-government.platform-and-community-governance.platform-policy-drafting.writing-enforceable-content-policies-and-community-guidelines
and law-government.platform-and-community-governance.trust-and-safety-rulemaking.designing-penalty-ladders-and-strike-systems.

## 1. Two documents, not one
- **Public guidelines** — short (aim for something readable in two minutes), friendly,
  positive where possible. 5–10 rules, each a one-line headline plus one or two sentences
  and an example. Start with the community's *purpose* ("This is a place for beginner
  woodworkers to ask questions without being mocked") — purpose resolves most edge cases.
- **Internal mod handbook** — definitions, examples of what does and doesn't cross the line,
  enforcement actions per rule, escalation contacts, and the precedent log.

## 2. Writing rules that can be enforced
- Prefer **observable behaviour** over intent: "No slurs or insults aimed at another member"
  is enforceable; "Be nice" is not (keep "be kind" as a value statement, not a rule).
- Define fuzzy terms in the handbook: what counts as "spam" (e.g. self-promo more than once a
  week outside #showcase), "off-topic", "harassment" (repeated unwanted contact after being
  asked to stop, or targeting one person across threads).
- Include the **non-negotiables** separately: illegal content, doxxing, threats, sexual
  content involving minors — immediate removal, ban and, where required, reporting to the
  platform or authorities.
- Make room for context: allow quoting a slur to report it, reclaimed language, academic
  discussion — and say who decides.
- Align with the host platform's own terms (Discord, Reddit, etc.): your rules can be
  stricter, never looser.

## 3. The enforcement ladder
A default progression the mod team follows unless the rule says otherwise:
1. **Nudge** — public or private reminder, content left or edited.
2. **Removal + warning** — logged, with the rule cited.
3. **Timeout / mute** — e.g. 24 hours, then 7 days.
4. **Temporary ban** — e.g. 30 days.
5. **Permanent ban.**
Severe violations jump straight to 4 or 5. Decide how long warnings "count" (e.g. strikes
expire after 90 days of good behaviour) so old mistakes don't follow people forever.
Always tell the member which rule, what action, and how to appeal — templated messages help.

## 4. Appeals
Keep it light but real: a form or a modmail address, reviewed by a moderator *other* than
the one who acted, with a target response time (e.g. 7 days). Record the outcome. Overturned
decisions are feedback on the rules or training, not embarrassment.

## 5. Precedent log and updates
- Log each tricky call: the content (summarised, or a link if safe), the rule, the decision,
  the reasoning. Review new mods against it.
- When the same edge case appears three or more times, update the handbook — and the public
  rules if members need to know.
- Announce rule changes with a reason and a date they take effect; don't retroactively punish.
- Review the full set at least annually or after a big incident.

## Gotchas
- Rules copied from another community whose purpose differs.
- Twenty rules nobody reads; or one vague rule ("don't be a jerk") everyone interprets
  differently.
- Mods enforcing on friends differently — log and peer-review.
- No record kept, so ban evaders and repeat offenders reset each time.

## Verify
- [ ] Public guidelines start from purpose and fit on one screen.
- [ ] Each rule has a handbook definition and examples on both sides of the line.
- [ ] Ladder written down, with jump-straight-to-ban list and strike expiry.
- [ ] Every enforcement message names the rule and the appeal route.
- [ ] Precedent log exists and is reviewed when updating rules.
