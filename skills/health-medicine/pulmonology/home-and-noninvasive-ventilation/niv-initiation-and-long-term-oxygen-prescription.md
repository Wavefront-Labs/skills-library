# NIV initiation and long-term oxygen prescription

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** start NIV in the right patient at effective settings, recognise failure early,
and prescribe home oxygen or home ventilation only when the evidence says it helps.

## 1. Acute NIV — who
- Acute hypercapnic respiratory failure: **pH < 7.35 and PaCO2 > 6.5 kPa (49 mmHg)**
  persisting after ~1 h of optimal medical therapy (COPD exacerbation is the strongest
  evidence; also OHS, neuromuscular, chest wall disease).
- pH < 7.25 → higher risk of failure; manage where intubation is immediately available.
- **Decide the ceiling of care before starting** (is NIV the ceiling, or a trial before
  intubation?) and document it.
- Contraindications to weigh: inability to protect airway, untreated pneumothorax,
  facial trauma, vomiting, upper GI surgery recently.

## 2. Settings (typical adult starting point)
- Mode: spontaneous/timed bilevel. **EPAP 4–5 cmH2O, IPAP 15**, then raise IPAP by
  2–5 every 10 min toward **20–30** as tolerated (OHS/obesity often needs higher EPAP
  8–10+ to splint the upper airway). Backup rate ~ 16–20 in COPD; Ti ~ 0.8–1.2 s
  (shorter in COPD).
- Oxygen entrained to **SpO2 88–92%** in hypercapnic patients.
- **Full-face mask** first in acute settings; check leak and pressure-area (nasal bridge)
  hourly.

## 3. Checkpoints
- **ABG at 1 h and 4 h** (or venous pH/PCO2 if trend reliable). Improving pH and
  respiratory rate → continue; worsening at 1–4 h → escalate or palliate per ceiling.
- Aim for maximal use in first 24 h, then wean by daytime breaks as pH normalises.
- Look for **patient–ventilator asynchrony** (ineffective triggering, double cycling)
  before blaming the patient.

## 4. Home NIV
- COPD: persistent hypercapnia (PaCO2 > 7 kPa / 53 mmHg) ≥ 2–4 weeks after an
  exacerbation requiring NIV → consider home NIV with high-intensity settings aimed at
  reducing PaCO2.
- OHS: CPAP first if severe OSA coexists; bilevel if hypercapnia persists.
- Neuromuscular (e.g. MND/ALS): start on symptoms of nocturnal hypoventilation, FVC
  falling (< 50% predicted), or raised CO2 — do not wait for daytime failure.
- Verify with overnight oximetry/capnography and device download.

## 5. Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT)
- Qualify on **two ABGs ≥ 3 weeks apart**, stable, on optimal therapy:
  **PaO2 ≤ 7.3 kPa (55 mmHg)**, or **≤ 8.0 kPa (60 mmHg)** with polycythaemia,
  pulmonary hypertension, peripheral oedema or nocturnal hypoxaemia.
- Prescribe ≥ 15 h/day; titrate flow to PaO2 ≥ 8 kPa without unacceptable CO2 rise.
- **No survival benefit for moderate hypoxaemia** (LOTT trial) — don't prescribe
  "just in case". Ambulatory oxygen only if it improves exertional symptoms/distance on
  a formal test.
- **Smoking is a fire risk** — most services refuse LTOT to active smokers; document
  home fire-safety referral.

## Gotchas
- Giving high-flow oxygen to a hypercapnic patient before NIV ("oxygen-induced
  hypercapnia").
- Starting NIV without a ceiling-of-care decision.
- Low IPAP because the patient "didn't like it" → ineffective ventilation.
- Using SpO2 alone to qualify LTOT — needs ABG.

## Verify
- [ ] Indication ABG and ceiling of care documented.
- [ ] Settings, mask type and target SpO2 recorded.
- [ ] Repeat ABG at 1 h and 4 h; trend acted on.
- [ ] LTOT: two qualifying ABGs, smoking status, fire-safety referral recorded.
- [ ] Home NIV: download and overnight CO2 reviewed at follow-up.
