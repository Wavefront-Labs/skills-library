# Fever workup and source identification

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, local protocols or current guidelines.*

**Goal:** turn "fever" into a named source (or a confident non-infectious cause) with
the fewest, best-taken tests, and never let the workup delay treatment in a patient who
is septic.

## 1. Triage first: is this sepsis?
- Hypotension, rising lactate, new confusion, rigors with tachycardia, mottling or new organ
  dysfunction = sepsis pathway: two sets of blood cultures and source-directed samples
  **before** antimicrobials if it causes no meaningful delay, then empiric therapy within
  the time your local sepsis protocol sets (commonly within 1 hour for septic shock).
- High-risk hosts lower the threshold: neutropenia, asplenia, transplant, recent surgery,
  indwelling devices, injection drug use, returned travellers (think malaria).

## 2. Localise with a systematic sweep
History: tempo, rigors, travel, animals, sexual history, injection drug use, new drugs
(last 1-3 weeks), recent procedures, sick contacts, vaccination. Then examine every
"hidden" site deliberately — the source is often missed because nobody looked:

| Site | Look for |
|---|---|
| Lines and devices | Exit-site erythema, tunnel tenderness, line day count, catheters, drains |
| Skin | Pressure injuries, cellulitis, emboli (splinters, Janeway), injection sites |
| Chest | New infiltrate, effusion (empyema), aspiration risk |
| Abdomen | RUQ/biliary, collections post-surgery, *C. difficile* if diarrhoea + recent antibiotics |
| Urine | Only if symptoms or obstructed/catheterised — asymptomatic bacteriuria is not a source |
| Heart | New murmur: endocarditis until excluded |
| Joints, spine | Effusion, back pain + fever (epidural abscess, discitis) |
| Head, sinuses, teeth | Sinusitis in intubated patients, dental abscess, meningism |

## 3. Take cultures properly
- **Blood:** 2-3 sets from separate venepunctures, each 20-30 mL in adults (volume is the
  biggest determinant of yield). Do not draw only through a line unless paired with a
  peripheral set (differential time-to-positivity helps diagnose line infection).
- Sample the site before antibiotics where possible: sputum, urine (clean catch/catheter
  port, not the bag), aspirates, tissue. Fluid and tissue beat swabs.
- Tell the lab the clinical question (e.g. "?endocarditis", "?anaerobes", "returned traveller")
  — it changes incubation and media.

## 4. Read results critically
- One positive bottle of coagulase-negative staphylococci, *Corynebacterium*, *Cutibacterium*
  or *Bacillus* is usually contamination; the same organism in ≥ 2 sets, or in a patient with
  prosthetic material, is not.
- *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Candida* in blood is never a contaminant: repeat cultures,
  look for a focus, and involve ID (S. aureus bacteraemia consultation improves outcomes).
- CRP and procalcitonin support but never establish a diagnosis; procalcitonin is most
  useful for stopping antibiotics in respiratory infection, and is unreliable in
  renal failure, after surgery/trauma, and in some fungal infections (verify local guidance).

## 5. Non-infectious fever — keep it on the list
Drug fever (beta-lactams, anticonvulsants, sulfonamides; often relative bradycardia, eosinophilia),
venous thromboembolism, transfusion reaction, malignancy (lymphoma), autoimmune disease,
gout/pseudogout, adrenal insufficiency, thyroid storm, neuroleptic malignant / serotonin
syndrome, withdrawal, post-operative inflammation (first 48 h usually not infection).

## 6. Fever of unknown origin (FUO)
Classic definition: ≥ 38.3 °C on several occasions, > 3 weeks, no diagnosis after a
defined baseline workup (definitions vary; check which one your service uses).
1. **Re-take the history and re-examine** — repeated, it diagnoses more than any scan.
2. Baseline: FBC with film, renal/liver panel, ESR/CRP, LDH, CK, urinalysis, ≥ 3 blood
   cultures, HIV, ferritin, ANA/RF, chest imaging, CT abdomen/pelvis, TB test as appropriate.
3. Look for "potentially diagnostic clues" and chase those first; avoid shotgun serology.
4. If no clues: FDG-PET/CT is now commonly the next step; consider temporal artery
   ultrasound/biopsy in older adults, marrow/liver biopsy when cytopenias or abnormal LFTs.
5. **Avoid empiric antibiotics or steroids in a stable FUO patient** — they obscure the diagnosis.

## Red flags — escalate now
Septic shock; S. aureus or Candida bacteraemia; fever + neutropenia; fever + new murmur;
fever + back pain or neurological signs; fever in a returned traveller from a malaria area
(same-day malaria testing); necrotising soft-tissue signs (pain out of proportion, crepitus).

## Gotchas
- Fever can be absent in the elderly, on steroids, or in uraemia; hypothermia in sepsis is worse.
- Antibiotics started "to cover" before cultures sterilise the very samples you needed.

## Verify
- [ ] Sepsis screened for and, if present, cultures + empiric therapy within protocol time.
- [ ] Every site in the table examined and documented, including lines and skin.
- [ ] ≥ 2 adequately filled blood culture sets before antimicrobials where feasible.
- [ ] Each positive culture classified as true pathogen vs contaminant, with reasons.
- [ ] Non-infectious causes and recent drug changes reviewed.
- [ ] For FUO: baseline set complete, clues listed, empiric therapy withheld if stable.
