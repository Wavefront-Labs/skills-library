# Multimodal analgesia, PCA and opioid stewardship

> General clinical/professional reference, not patient-specific advice. Doses and contraindications must be checked against the patient and local formulary.

**Goal:** good function (deep breathing, mobilising, sleeping) with the fewest opioid-
related harms, and no new long-term opioid users created by the operation.

## 1. Build a multimodal plan
Baseline for most adults unless contraindicated:
- **Paracetamol** regular (weight/hepatic adjusted).
- **NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor** regular for a short course (check renal function, bleeding,
  GI history, CV risk, asthma sensitivity, surgeon's view on bone/anastomotic healing).
- **Dexamethasone** intraoperatively (also PONV benefit).
- **Regional anaesthesia or wound infiltration** wherever effective for the procedure.
- Opioid for **breakthrough**, oral preferred, lowest effective dose.
Add-ons with selective evidence: ketamine (opioid-tolerant, major spine/abdominal
surgery), IV lidocaine infusion (some abdominal surgery; LAST precautions), gabapentinoids
(now used sparingly — sedation and respiratory depression with opioids, especially elderly).
Use **PROSPECT** procedure-specific recommendations rather than one recipe for everything.

## 2. PCA (IV opioid)
- Typical adult morphine PCA: bolus 1 mg, lockout 5 min, **no background infusion** in
  opioid-naive patients (background infusions increase respiratory depression).
  Hydromorphone and fentanyl PCAs use different, much smaller bolus amounts.
- Only the patient presses the button (no "PCA by proxy").
- Standardised concentrations and pre-filled syringes; independent double-check of
  pump programming; anti-syphon and antireflux valves.
- Antiemetic and naloxone prescribed alongside.

## 3. Monitoring
- Pain scores **plus function** (can they cough, walk?).
- **Sedation score** is the early warning for opioid-induced ventilatory impairment — it
  precedes respiratory rate drop. E.g. Pasero (POSS): 3 = frequently drowsy, drifts off
  mid-conversation → stop opioids, assess, monitor closely; 4 = minimal response →
  naloxone, emergency.
- Higher risk: OSA, elderly, renal impairment (morphine metabolites), concurrent
  sedatives/gabapentinoids, first 24 h; consider continuous capnography/oximetry.

## 4. Special groups
- **Opioid-tolerant:** continue baseline opioid (or equivalent) and add analgesia on
  top; expect higher requirements; regional and ketamine are useful; involve APS.
- **Buprenorphine** (OUD or pain): usually continue (sometimes with dose adjustment)
  and add multimodal + full-agonist opioids as needed — stopping it risks relapse.
  Coordinate with the prescriber.
- **Methadone:** continue, confirm dose with the dispensing service, watch QTc.
- Renal failure: avoid morphine/codeine accumulation; prefer oxycodone/hydromorphone
  with caution, or fentanyl.

## 5. Stewardship at discharge
- Prescribe the **smallest supply** that fits the procedure's expected course (many
  institutions use procedure-specific quantities), immediate-release only, no
  long-acting opioids for acute pain.
- Written plan: step down to paracetamol/NSAID, when to stop, safe storage and disposal,
  no driving, constipation prevention.
- Communicate with primary care; avoid open-ended refills.

## Gotchas
- Codeine and tramadol: genotype-dependent effect and serotonergic/seizure risk.
- A comfortable but over-sedated patient is not a success.
- Undertreated pain in opioid-tolerant patients is often mislabelled drug-seeking.

## Verify
- [ ] Multimodal regimen prescribed; contraindications checked.
- [ ] PCA programming double-checked; no background infusion in opioid-naive.
- [ ] Sedation and respiratory observations charted at set intervals.
- [ ] Opioid-tolerant/buprenorphine plan documented with APS input.
- [ ] Discharge opioid quantity limited with written taper and disposal advice.
