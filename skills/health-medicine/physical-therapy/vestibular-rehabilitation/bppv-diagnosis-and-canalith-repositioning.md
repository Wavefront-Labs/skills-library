# BPPV diagnosis and canalith repositioning

> General clinical/professional reference. Related ENT workup: `health-medicine.otolaryngology.ent-diagnosis.vertigo-and-neck-lump-workup`. Manoeuvres must be learned hands-on.

**Goal:** BPPV is the commonest cause of vertigo and usually curable in one to three
visits. Identify the canal from the nystagmus pattern, do the right manoeuvre, and catch
the uncommon central mimic.

## 1. History that fits BPPV
Brief (<1 min) spinning vertigo triggered by **head position change relative to
gravity** — rolling in bed, lying down, looking up, bending. No hearing loss, no
neurological symptoms, normal between episodes (residual unsteadiness common). Risk:
older age, head injury, prior vestibular neuritis, prolonged bed rest.

## 2. Screen for red flags first (central or other causes)
New severe headache, neck pain after trauma, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia,
limb ataxia, facial or limb weakness/numbness, inability to walk, continuous vertigo
(not positional), hearing loss → medical/ED review (stroke workup; HINTS by trained clinicians).
Positional nystagmus that is **purely down-beating, not fatigable, without latency, or not
suppressed by fixation, or no vertigo with strong nystagmus** suggests a **central** cause.
Check neck ROM, vascular history and precautions (cervical instability, severe neck pain,
recent neck surgery) before positioning.

## 3. Test (use Frenzel/video goggles if available)
**Dix–Hallpike (posterior canal, ~80–90% of BPPV):** sitting, head turned 45° to the test
side, bring quickly to supine with head ~20–30° extended over the edge; hold ≥30 s.
Positive: latency of a few seconds, **up-beating torsional nystagmus** (top pole toward the
lower ear), crescendo–decrescendo, lasting <60 s, with vertigo; reverses on sitting up.
**Supine roll test (horizontal canal):** supine, head flexed ~30°, rotate head quickly 90°
each side.
- **Geotropic** (beats toward the ground both sides) → canalithiasis; affected side = side
  with *stronger* nystagmus.
- **Apogeotropic** (beats away from ground) → cupulolithiasis or anterior-arm debris;
  affected side = side with *weaker* nystagmus.

## 4. Treat by canal
- **Posterior canal:** **Epley** (canalith repositioning): from positive Dix–Hallpike
  position, rotate head 90° to the other side, then roll body to side-lying with nose
  ~45° down, then sit up; hold each position ~30 s or until nystagmus stops. **Semont**
  (liberatory) is an equivalent alternative.
- **Horizontal canal, geotropic:** **barbecue (Lempert) roll** toward the unaffected side, or
  **Gufoni** manoeuvre; forced prolonged positioning (lying on unaffected side overnight).
- **Apogeotropic:** Gufoni variant or head-shake manoeuvres to convert to geotropic, then treat.
- Re-test after treatment; repeat in the same session if still positive (commonly 1–3 cycles).
- Post-manoeuvre restrictions (sleeping upright etc.) are **not** supported by evidence.

## 5. After treatment
Review in ~1 month or sooner if symptomatic; recurrence ~15–50% over years (estimates vary).
Residual dizziness or imbalance → **vestibular rehabilitation exercises** (habituation,
balance). Assess falls risk in older adults. Medications (vestibular suppressants)
are not recommended as routine treatment for BPPV.

## Gotchas
- Lying the patient back without the 20–30° extension underestimates posterior canal BPPV.
- Bilateral or multi-canal BPPV after head trauma — test all canals.
- Anterior canal BPPV is rare; down-beating positional nystagmus warrants central consideration first.
- Nausea: warn patients; have a sick bowl; antiemetic per prescriber if severe.

## Verify
- [ ] Red flags and neck precautions screened before testing.
- [ ] Dix–Hallpike both sides and supine roll test performed; nystagmus direction, latency and duration recorded.
- [ ] Canal and side identified; matching manoeuvre performed and re-tested.
- [ ] Any atypical (central-pattern) nystagmus referred for medical review.
- [ ] Follow-up and residual-dizziness/falls plan arranged.
