# Post-stroke gait and balance retraining

> General clinical/professional reference. Medical stability, cardiovascular limits and early mobilisation timing must follow the stroke team's guidance and local protocols.

**Goal:** walking recovery after stroke depends on **how much and how hard** people
practise walking. Measure, then deliver lots of repetitions of the real task at a
challenging intensity, safely.

## 1. Baseline (and repeat every 2–4 weeks)
- **10-metre walk test** (comfortable and fast speed): <0.4 m/s household, 0.4–0.8
  limited community, >0.8 m/s community ambulator (approximate categories). MCID roughly 0.1–0.16 m/s.
- **6-minute walk test** (endurance), **Berg Balance Scale** or **Mini-BESTest** (balance;
  Berg <45 suggests increased fall risk — threshold debated), **Functional Ambulation
  Category (FAC 0–5)**, Timed Up and Go, and the patient's walking goals.
- Impairments contributing: weakness (hip flexors, ankle dorsiflexors and plantarflexors),
  spasticity, sensory loss, neglect, cognition, cardiovascular deconditioning, fear of falling.

## 2. Gait training — what the evidence favours
- **High-intensity, variable, task-specific stepping practice** (APTA CPG strong recommendation)
  — target **~70–80% heart-rate reserve or RPE 15–17** (Borg 6–20), for up to ~40 min of
  stepping per session, as tolerated and medically cleared.
- Overground walking and treadmill (with or without body-weight support/harness) are
  both used; increase **speed, distance, step count**, and add variability: turns, obstacles,
  slopes, stairs, different surfaces, dual-task.
- **Count steps** (pedometer/wearable) — many inpatients take very few steps/day in
  therapy; a step-count target makes dose visible.
- Low-value approaches to minimise: prolonged static standing, sitting balance
  without progression, isolated passive work in an ambulant patient, virtual reality alone
  as a substitute for walking practice.

## 3. Balance training
Progress from wide to narrow base, eyes open to closed, firm to compliant surface,
static to dynamic (reaching, stepping, perturbations), single task to dual task.
Include reactive balance (stepping responses) and **practise getting up from the floor**.
Train in the environments the person will live in.

## 4. Orthoses and adjuncts
- **Ankle-foot orthosis (AFO)** for foot drop/instability — improves speed and safety;
  reassess as recovery changes. **Functional electrical stimulation (FES)** to the
  peroneal nerve is an alternative or complement.
- Walking aids: choose the least restrictive aid that is safe; reassess frequently.
- Spasticity limiting gait → liaise with medical team (positioning, stretching, botulinum
  toxin, see `health-medicine.physical-medicine-and-rehabilitation.spasticity-management.goal-directed-spasticity-oral-botulinum-toxin-and-intrathecal-baclofen`).

## 5. Safety
Guard appropriately (gait belt, harness on treadmill), monitor BP/HR/SpO2 and symptoms,
know exercise stop criteria (chest pain, severe breathlessness, BP limits set by the medical
team, new neurological signs). Falls risk plan for the ward and home; teach carers safe assistance.

## Gotchas
- "Wait for tone to normalise before walking" is outdated — walking practice is the therapy.
- Hemiplegic shoulder: support it during gait training to prevent subluxation pain.
- Neglect and pusher behaviour need specific strategies; walking speed may lag balance.
- Intensity targets assume medical clearance; beta-blockers blunt HR — use RPE.

## Verify
- [ ] Baseline 10MWT, 6MWT, balance scale and FAC recorded with dates.
- [ ] Session dose recorded (steps, minutes, HR/RPE intensity), not just "gait practice".
- [ ] Practice progressed in variability and challenge; balance includes reactive tasks.
- [ ] AFO/aid choice reassessed as function changes.
- [ ] Outcomes re-measured; change compared against MCID.
