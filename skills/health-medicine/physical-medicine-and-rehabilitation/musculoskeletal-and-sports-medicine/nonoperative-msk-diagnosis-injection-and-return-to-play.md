# Non-operative MSK diagnosis, injection and return to play

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** reach a structural diagnosis, decide who needs imaging or surgery, use
injections only where they help, and return the person to activity on criteria, not dates.

## 1. Diagnosis
1. **History:** mechanism (contact, twisting, overuse), load change in the last 4–6 weeks
   (training volume, footwear, surface), night pain, systemic symptoms.
2. **Red flags first:** fever, weight loss, history of cancer, unremitting night pain,
   neurological deficit, saddle anaesthesia, hot swollen joint (septic arthritis until
   aspirated), suspected fracture or compartment syndrome.
3. **Examination:** look–feel–move–special tests, always examine the joint above and below,
   neurovascular check. Use decision rules: **Ottawa ankle/knee rules** for X-ray.
4. **Imaging:** X-ray for bone; **ultrasound** for tendon, bursa, effusion and guided
   procedures; **MRI** for intra-articular, marrow (bone stress) or surgical planning —
   not as a first test for non-specific low back pain without red flags.

## 2. Injections (corticosteroid, the commonest)
- **Indications with reasonable evidence:** subacromial bursitis, adhesive capsulitis
  (early), trigger finger, de Quervain, carpal tunnel (short-term), knee/hip OA flares,
  greater trochanteric pain.
- **Typical doses (verify locally):** triamcinolone acetonide or methylprednisolone
  40 mg for a large joint (knee/shoulder), 10–20 mg small joints/tendon sheaths, mixed
  with 1–5 mL lidocaine 1 %.
- **Technique:** consent (infection ~1 in 10–50 thousand, fat atrophy/skin depigmentation,
  steroid flare 24–48 h, transient hyperglycaemia in diabetes, tendon rupture risk);
  no-touch aseptic technique with chlorhexidine; **ultrasound guidance** improves
  accuracy for hip, glenohumeral and small structures; aspirate before injecting into a
  joint; never inject against high resistance (intratendinous).
- **Limits:** avoid intratendinous injection of Achilles and patellar tendons; space
  injections ≥ 6–12 weeks, commonly ≤ 3–4 per joint per year; avoid within ~3 months
  before joint replacement. Steroid gives short-term relief in lateral epicondylalgia but
  worse 1-year outcomes than wait-and-see — prefer loading.
- Hyaluronic acid and PRP: evidence mixed; label as such when offering.

## 3. Exercise prescription (FITT)
- **Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type**, plus progression. Tendinopathy: isometric then
  heavy-slow resistance (3 × 15 → 3 × 6 over 12 weeks) with pain ≤ 3–5/10 settling by
  next morning.
- Track load with the **acute:chronic workload ratio** (aim ~0.8–1.3) as a rough guide,
  not a rule.

## 4. Return to play (criteria-based)
Use a continuum — return to participation → return to sport → return to performance.
Criteria commonly include: full or near-full ROM, strength ≥ 90 % limb symmetry index,
hop tests ≥ 90 % LSI, sport-specific drills without symptoms, and psychological readiness
(e.g. ACL-RSI). **Concussion:** no same-day return; graded return-to-sport strategy
(6 stages, ≥ 24 h per stage) after symptom-limited activity.

## Gotchas
- Missing **bone stress injury** in a runner with focal bony pain — check energy
  availability (RED-S), menstrual history and vitamin D.
- Injecting a hot joint without aspirating for culture.
- Diabetic patients not warned about glucose rise for several days.
- Returning on a calendar date when strength symmetry is still 70 %.

## Verify
- [ ] Red flags documented as absent or acted on.
- [ ] Imaging choice matched to the suspected tissue.
- [ ] Injection: indication, drug/dose, guidance, consent and aftercare recorded.
- [ ] Exercise plan has dose and a progression rule.
- [ ] Return-to-play decision references objective criteria.

*Uncertainty note:* injection doses and frequency limits vary by guideline and
institution; confirm with local protocols and current product information.
