# Allergic rhinitis and chronic urticaria: stepwise management

> General clinical/professional reference, not patient-specific advice. Drug choices, doses and biologic access rules vary by country — check current guidelines and labelling.

**Goal:** control the allergist's commonest chronic conditions with the least treatment
burden, escalate in defined steps, and recognise the linked diseases (asthma,
eczema) that change the plan.

## Allergic rhinitis
**Classify** (ARIA): intermittent vs persistent; mild vs moderate-severe (sleep, work,
daily activity affected). Confirm sensitisation that matches the seasonal/perennial pattern.

**Steps**
1. Allergen reduction where practical (HDM covers have weak evidence alone; pet
   removal is effective but hard).
2. Mild/intermittent: oral or intranasal second-generation antihistamine.
3. Moderate-severe or persistent: **intranasal corticosteroid daily**, started before
   the season; fixed combination with intranasal azelastine if insufficient.
4. Add-ons: saline irrigation, montelukast (limited benefit; neuropsychiatric warning),
   short intranasal decongestant courses only (< 5–7 days; rebound rhinitis).
5. Uncontrolled despite adherence and technique: reconsider diagnosis, refer for
   immunotherapy (see `health-medicine.allergy-and-immunology.allergen-immunotherapy.scit-and-slit-build-up-and-reaction-management`).

**Spray technique** (common failure point): head slightly forward, nozzle aimed away
from the septum (right hand for left nostril), gentle sniff, no hard snort.

**Always ask about asthma** — rhinitis and asthma are one airway; treating rhinitis
improves asthma control, and uncontrolled asthma is a contraindication to SCIT.
Unilateral symptoms, bleeding, anosmia or pain → ENT examination, not more steroid.

## Chronic urticaria
**Define:** wheals and/or angioedema for > 6 weeks. Split into **spontaneous (CSU)** and
**inducible** (dermographism, cold, cholinergic, pressure, solar).
- Workup is minimal: FBC, CRP/ESR; consider total IgE and anti-TPO. Extensive allergy
  testing is rarely useful — CSU is usually not allergic.
- Wheals lasting > 24 h, bruising or systemic features → consider urticarial vasculitis (biopsy).
- Angioedema **without** wheals → think ACE inhibitor and bradykinin/hereditary angioedema
  (C4 level); antihistamines and adrenaline don't work for bradykinin angioedema.

**Steps** (international guideline)
1. Standard-dose non-sedating H1 antihistamine daily (not as needed).
2. Up-dose to **up to 4×** the licensed dose (off-label in many regions) for 2–4 weeks.
3. Add **omalizumab** (300 mg 4-weekly; interval/dose adjustments per response).
4. Ciclosporin for omalizumab non-responders (monitor BP and renal function).
Short oral steroid bursts for flares only. Track with **UAS7** and UCT scores.

## Atopic dermatitis links
Emollients, trigger avoidance, topical steroids/calcineurin inhibitors, and biologic or
JAK inhibitor escalation (usually with dermatology). Food allergy rarely drives eczema
in adults; in infants with severe eczema consider it but avoid test-driven elimination.

## Gotchas
- Sedating first-generation antihistamines impair driving and learning; avoid for routine use.
- Nasal steroid "failure" is often non-adherence or poor technique.
- Patients stop CSU treatment when wheals settle; taper slowly — relapse is common.

## Verify
- [ ] Rhinitis classified; asthma screened; red flags excluded.
- [ ] Spray technique observed, adherence checked before escalating.
- [ ] Urticaria duration, subtype and angioedema type documented.
- [ ] Antihistamine up-dosing trial completed before biologic referral.
- [ ] Control scores (UAS7/UCT, symptom scores) recorded at each review.
