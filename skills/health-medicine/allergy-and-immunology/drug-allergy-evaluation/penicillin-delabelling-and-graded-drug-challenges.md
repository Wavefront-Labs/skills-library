# Penicillin de-labelling and graded drug challenges

> General clinical/professional reference, not patient-specific advice. Protocols, thresholds and setting requirements vary by institution; follow local policy.

**Goal:** remove inaccurate drug allergy labels safely, identify the true allergies, and
give patients who genuinely react a safe route to the drug they need.

## Why it matters
Around 10% of people carry a penicillin allergy label; **> 90% tolerate penicillin on
testing**. The label pushes prescribers toward broader, costlier, more toxic antibiotics
and is associated with more C. difficile and resistant infections.

## 1. Phenotype the index reaction
Ask: which drug, how long ago, timing after first dose, what exactly happened, what
treatment was given, has the patient tolerated the drug (or a related one) since?
- **Immediate (≤ 1–6 h), IgE-type:** urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension.
- **Delayed benign:** maculopapular rash days into the course without systemic features.
- **Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR):** SJS/TEN (mucosal involvement,
  blistering, skin sloughing), DRESS (fever, rash, eosinophilia, organ involvement),
  AGEP. **Never challenge** with the culprit or close relatives.
- **Non-allergic labels:** GI upset, headache, family history, "unknown in childhood" —
  often removable by history alone after confirming subsequent tolerance.

## 2. Risk stratification (PEN-FAST)
- **F**ive years or less since reaction (2 points), **A**naphylaxis/angioedema or
  **S**CAR (2), **T**reatment required (1). PEN = the patient reports penicillin allergy.
- Score < 3 = low risk (very low positive-test rate in validation cohorts); many
  services proceed to direct oral challenge. Validation is strongest in adults.

## 3. Direct oral challenge
- Low-risk history: single therapeutic dose of amoxicillin (e.g. 250–500 mg adult) or
  a two-step (10% then 90%), observe ~30–60 min.
- Delayed reactions can follow; some protocols extend dosing over several days
  (practice varies; evidence for multi-day challenge is mixed).
- Document clearly and **remove the label from every record** (hospital, primary care,
  pharmacy) — otherwise it reappears.

## 4. Skin testing
- For moderate/high-risk immediate histories: prick then intradermal with
  benzylpenicilloyl (major determinant), penicillin G, and amoxicillin where available;
  positive and negative controls. Negative skin test → oral challenge to confirm.
- Reagent availability differs by country; local protocols dictate concentrations.

## 5. Cross-reactivity
- Penicillin–cephalosporin cross-reactivity is driven mostly by **R1 side chains**, not
  the beta-lactam ring; true cross-reactivity is ~1–2%. Choose a cephalosporin with a
  dissimilar side chain (e.g. cefazolin has a unique side chain).
- Carbapenem cross-reactivity < 1%; aztreonam shares a side chain with ceftazidime.

## 6. Graded challenge versus desensitisation
- **Graded challenge (test dose):** for patients judged *unlikely* allergic — 2–3 steps
  confirm tolerance; it does not change the immune response.
- **Desensitisation:** for patients *likely or proven* IgE-allergic who need the drug —
  12+ step protocols induce temporary tolerance that lasts only while dosing continues.
  Not for SCAR.

## Gotchas
- Challenging on beta-blockers or during unstable illness complicates rescue.
- "Rash on amoxicillin during a viral illness" (e.g. EBV) is usually not allergy.
- A negative test today does not cover a different drug class; de-label specifically.

## Verify
- [ ] Index reaction phenotyped; SCAR excluded before any challenge.
- [ ] Risk score and pathway documented.
- [ ] Challenge done with anaphylaxis treatment available and observation timed.
- [ ] Label updated or removed in all records; patient given written result.
- [ ] Desensitisation only for confirmed/likely IgE allergy with essential need.
