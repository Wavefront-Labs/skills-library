# Best possible medication history (BPMH) and medication reconciliation

> General professional reference, not a substitute for local policy or clinical judgement. Related: `health-medicine.internal-medicine.multimorbidity-and-pharmacotherapy.structured-polypharmacy-review` (what to do once the list is right).

**Goal:** roughly half of hospital admissions carry at least one unintentional medication
discrepancy, and many arise at transitions. Reconciliation is the step that makes the
list true; every later decision is built on it.

## 1. Gather the BPMH — at least two sources, one of them the patient
- **Patient/carer interview** (structured, not "any medicines?"):
  - prescription medicines: name, strength, dose, route, frequency, **what they actually take**;
  - "forgotten" forms: inhalers, eye/ear drops, creams, patches, injections (weekly/monthly
    — methotrexate, GLP-1 agonists, depot antipsychotics, bisphosphonates, B12), insulin;
  - OTC, herbal, supplements, recreational substances;
  - recent changes, stopped meds and why; allergies with reaction type; adherence.
- **Second sources:** community pharmacy dispensing record, GP/primary-care list or
  shared record (e.g. Summary Care Record), PDMP, previous discharge summary,
  medication packs/vials brought in, care-home MAR.
- Record source and date for each item; flag what could not be verified.

## 2. Reconcile at every transition
Compare the BPMH against the **current orders** (admission), **transfer orders**, or
**discharge prescription**. For each difference classify:
- **Documented intentional** — changed with a recorded reason. No action.
- **Undocumented intentional** — deliberate but not recorded. Ask prescriber to document.
- **Unintentional** — omission, wrong dose/frequency, commission, duplication. Resolve.
Prioritise by harm potential: **anticoagulants, insulin/antidiabetics, opioids,
antiepileptics, Parkinson's drugs (time-critical), immunosuppressants, antiretrovirals,
steroids (adrenal crisis risk)** first.

## 3. Communicate and close the loop
- Discuss unresolved discrepancies with the prescriber (ward round or direct contact);
  record the outcome, not just "discussed".
- At discharge: list every medicine with status **new / changed / stopped / unchanged**
  and the reason; send to primary care and community pharmacy; counsel the patient on
  the changes; reconcile against what they have at home.
- Timing standard (local policy, often within 24 h of admission) — track compliance.

## Gotchas
- A dispensing record shows what was supplied, not what is taken; a GP list shows what is
  prescribed, not what is taken. Only the patient can close that gap.
- Patients rarely mention eye drops, contraceptives, or injections given in clinic.
- "As directed" on a label hides the real dose — ask.
- Medicines withheld on admission (e.g. antihypertensives in sepsis, metformin) need an
  explicit restart decision before discharge, or they are silently lost.
- Brand vs generic names cause duplicates (two names, one molecule).

## Red flags / escalate immediately
Missed time-critical doses (Parkinson's, insulin, antiepileptics, anticoagulation for
mechanical valves), suspected adverse drug event as cause of admission, doses that suggest
an overdose, or a discrepancy involving a high-alert drug the prescriber cannot be reached about.

## Verify
- [ ] BPMH uses ≥2 sources including a patient/carer interview; sources recorded.
- [ ] Every discrepancy classified and resolved or escalated; outcome documented.
- [ ] High-risk and time-critical medicines checked first and charted on time.
- [ ] Discharge list states new/changed/stopped with reasons and was sent onward.
- [ ] Patient counselled on changes and understands what to stop/start at home.
