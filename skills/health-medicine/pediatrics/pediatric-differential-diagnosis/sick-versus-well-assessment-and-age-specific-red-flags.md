# Sick versus well: the febrile child under 5 (NICE traffic lights)

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement or current local guidelines. Based on NICE NG143 Fever in under 5s; use your local pathway and age-appropriate observation charts.

**Goal:** decide quickly how sick a febrile child is, act on the risk group, and leave
low-risk children with a proper safety net.

## 1. Assess with the traffic-light table
Measure and record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate and capillary refill time.
Measure blood pressure if heart rate or capillary refill is abnormal and facilities exist.
For children with learning disabilities, interpret the table in light of the individual child.

**Red (high risk):** pale, mottled, ashen or blue; no response to social cues, appears ill,
does not wake or stay awake, weak, high-pitched or continuous cry; grunting, respiratory rate
over 60 per minute, moderate or severe chest indrawing; reduced skin turgor; age under
3 months with temperature 38 C or higher; non-blanching rash, bulging fontanelle, neck
stiffness, status epilepticus, focal neurological signs, focal seizures.

**Amber (intermediate risk):** pallor reported by parent; not responding normally to social
cues, no smile, wakes only with prolonged stimulation, decreased activity; nasal flaring;
tachypnoea (over 50 per minute at 6 to 12 months, over 40 per minute above 12 months); oxygen
saturation 95% or less in air; crackles; tachycardia (over 160 per minute under 12 months, over
150 at 12 to 24 months, over 140 at 2 to 5 years); capillary refill 3 seconds or more; dry
mucous membranes; poor feeding in infants; reduced urine output; age 3 to 6 months with
temperature 39 C or higher; fever for 5 days or more; rigors; swelling of a limb or joint;
non-weight-bearing limb.

**Green (low risk):** responds normally, content or smiles, stays awake or wakes quickly,
strong normal cry, normal colour, moist mucous membranes, none of the amber or red features.

## 2. Pointers to specific illness (NICE table 3)
- Non-blanching rash, especially with an ill-looking child, lesions over 2 mm, capillary
  refill 3 s or more or neck stiffness -> meningococcal disease.
- Neck stiffness, bulging fontanelle, reduced consciousness, convulsive status epilepticus ->
  bacterial meningitis; classic signs are often absent in infants.
- Tachypnoea, crackles, nasal flaring, chest indrawing, cyanosis, low saturation -> pneumonia.
- Dysuria, frequency, foul or cloudy urine, haematuria, abdominal or loin pain -> urinary
  tract infection.
- Swollen limb or joint, not using an extremity -> septic arthritis.
- Fever for 5 days or longer -> consider Kawasaki disease; do not use fever duration alone to
  predict serious illness.

## 3. Act on the risk group
- Some vaccinations can induce fever in under-3-month-olds; still treat the high-risk group seriously.
- Do not give oral antibiotics for fever without apparent source; give parenteral antibiotics
  at the earliest opportunity for suspected meningococcal disease.
- Specialist assessment of red features includes urine testing; amber features also need
  urine testing unless an experienced paediatrician deems it unnecessary; green children
  referred to a specialist still have urine tested and are assessed for pneumonia.
- Follow the NICE guideline and local pathway for investigations, fluids and antibiotics
  rather than this skill.

## 4. Safety net
If the child is not admitted and no diagnosis is reached, but red or amber features were
present, give a safety net (NICE lists the options in its section on management by the paediatric specialist) rather
than discharge without advice.

## Gotchas
- Cut-offs differ by age; adult "normal" heart and respiratory rates do not apply.
- Verify against the current NICE version and your local pathway before relying on any figure.
