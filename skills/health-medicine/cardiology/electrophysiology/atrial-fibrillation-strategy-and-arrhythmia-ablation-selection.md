# Atrial fibrillation strategy and arrhythmia ablation selection

> General clinical/professional reference, not patient-specific advice. Antiarrhythmic and anticoagulant dosing depends on renal function, interactions and structural heart disease; check current labelling and guidelines.

**Goal:** prevent stroke and sudden death, control symptoms, and choose ablation when it
beats drugs — matching the arrhythmia mechanism to the right treatment.

## 1. Atrial fibrillation: the integrated approach
ESC 2024 frames AF care as **AF-CARE**: Comorbidity and risk-factor management,
Avoid stroke and thromboembolism, Reduce symptoms by rate and rhythm control,
Evaluation and dynamic reassessment.
- **Risk factors:** hypertension, obesity (weight loss reduces AF burden), sleep apnoea,
  alcohol reduction, diabetes, activity.
- **Stroke prevention:** CHA2DS2-VA (ESC 2024 removed sex) or CHA2DS2-VASc (ACC/AHA).
  Anticoagulate at score ≥ 2; consider at 1. **DOACs preferred** over warfarin except
  mechanical valves and moderate-severe mitral stenosis. Dose by label criteria (renal
  function, age, weight, interacting drugs) — under-dosing is common.
- Bleeding scores identify modifiable risks; they're not reasons to withhold anticoagulation.
- LAA occlusion for those who can't take long-term anticoagulation.

## 2. Rate versus rhythm
- **Rate control:** beta-blocker, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker (avoid in
  HFrEF), digoxin; lenient target (< 110 bpm at rest) acceptable if asymptomatic.
- **Early rhythm control** (within ~1 year of diagnosis) reduced cardiovascular
  outcomes in EAST-AFNET 4; consider in most patients with recent AF and risk factors.
- **Cardioversion:** AF > 24–48 h (thresholds differ by guideline) without ≥ 3 weeks of
  anticoagulation → TOE to exclude LAA thrombus or anticoagulate first; continue
  anticoagulation ≥ 4 weeks after.
- **Antiarrhythmic drugs by substrate:**
  - No/minimal structural disease: flecainide or propafenone (with AV-nodal blocker),
    dronedarone, sotalol (QT/renal monitoring).
  - Coronary disease (no HFrEF): dronedarone, sotalol, amiodarone — **avoid class IC**.
  - HFrEF: amiodarone (thyroid, liver, lung, eye monitoring).

## 3. When to ablate
- **AF:** pulmonary vein isolation is first-line in selected symptomatic paroxysmal AF
  and after antiarrhythmic failure; strong evidence in AF with HFrEF (CASTLE-AF). Pulsed-
  field ablation is widely adopted (shorter procedures, lower oesophageal risk; long-term
  comparative data still accumulating).
- **Typical atrial flutter:** cavotricuspid isthmus ablation — high success; often
  first-line.
- **SVT (AVNRT, AVRT/WPW):** ablation is curative in most (~95%), low risk; first-line
  for recurrent symptomatic SVT and **pre-excited AF** or high-risk accessory pathways.
- **VT:** in structural heart disease with recurrent ICD therapies; idiopathic outflow
  tract VT/frequent PVCs causing symptoms or cardiomyopathy.

## 4. Acute management highlights
- Regular narrow-complex SVT: vagal manoeuvre (modified Valsalva), adenosine with ECG
  recording, synchronised cardioversion if unstable.
- Wide-complex regular tachycardia: treat as VT; unstable → synchronised DC cardioversion.
- Pre-excited AF: no AV nodal blockers; procainamide/ibutilide or cardioversion.

## 5. EP study basics
Intervals (AH, HV — prolonged HV > ~70 ms suggests infranodal disease), programmed
stimulation to induce and map tachycardia, activation and entrainment mapping, 3D
electroanatomical maps. Complications: vascular, tamponade, AV block (septal ablation),
stroke, oesophageal injury (posterior wall AF ablation).

## Gotchas
- Stopping anticoagulation after "successful" AF ablation based on rhythm alone.
- Class IC drugs without an AV-nodal blocker → 1:1 flutter conduction.
- Amiodarone–warfarin/digoxin interactions.
