# Designing exposure and behavioural experiments in CBT

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.*

**Goal:** turn a maintenance formulation of an anxiety problem into a sequence of
exposures and behavioural experiments that test the client's feared predictions, drop
safety behaviours, and generalise beyond the therapy room.

## 1. Start from the maintenance cycle
- Map the vicious cycle with the client: trigger → feared prediction (in their words)
  → anxiety → safety behaviours/avoidance → no disconfirmation → belief maintained.
- Write the **core prediction** with a belief rating (0-100%): "If I stay on the train
  with my heart racing, I will collapse (80%)."
- List every safety behaviour, including subtle ones (gripping rails, sitting near exits,
  rehearsing sentences, carrying water, reassurance seeking, mental neutralising in OCD).

## 2. Build the hierarchy
- 10-15 situations rated on SUDS (0-100). Vary them along the dimensions that drive fear:
  distance, duration, being alone, crowding, body sensations.
- Example (panic): 20 sit with a racing heart after stairs → 40 one-stop train with
  partner → 60 one stop alone → 80 rush-hour, no water, five stops → 90 express train.
- Include **interoceptive exposures** for panic: 30 s hyperventilation, 60 s spinning,
  straw breathing, running on the spot.

## 3. Run each exposure as an experiment
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Before | State the specific prediction and rate belief; agree which safety behaviours to drop |
| During | Stay until the prediction has been tested (not necessarily until SUDS falls); attend to the feared stimulus |
| After | What happened? Was the prediction confirmed? Re-rate belief; what did you learn? |

- **Inhibitory learning principles:** maximise expectancy violation; vary contexts and
  stimuli; combine fear cues ("deepened extinction"); remove safety signals; do not
  require habituation within a session. Older protocols used "stay until SUDS drops by
  50%" — either can work, but frame the aim as learning, not calm.
- Start around SUDS 40-60 with high success likelihood, then climb; skipping steps is
  fine once confidence grows.

## 4. Behavioural experiments for cognitive beliefs
- **Hypothesis-testing:** compare two theories (Theory A "I am going mad", Theory B
  "I am anxious and monitoring my body") and design a test that discriminates them.
- **Survey experiments:** ask others (e.g. "Do people notice when someone blushes, and
  what do they think?") for social-anxiety beliefs.
- **Manipulation experiments:** deliberately increase then drop a safety behaviour
  (e.g. self-focused attention in social anxiety) and compare the outcome.

## 5. Homework and generalisation
- Agree exactly what, when, how often (daily exposure practice is typical; 45-60 min or
  several shorter trials) and how it will be recorded. Anticipate obstacles.
- Review homework first in every session; praise attempts, troubleshoot avoidance.
- Track an outcome measure each session (e.g. PDSS, LSAS, Y-BOCS, GAD-7).

## Gotchas
- Covert safety behaviours (distraction, reassurance, "just in case") quietly block learning.
- Exposure that is too easy teaches little; too hard leads to escape and sensitisation.
- Escape mid-exposure: debrief, do not end on it if possible — return to a lower step.
- Medical conditions (cardiac, asthma, pregnancy, epilepsy) need clearance before
  interoceptive work; check with the medical team.
- Benzodiazepines taken as needed can act as a safety behaviour (discuss with the prescriber).

## Verify
- [ ] Maintenance cycle written with specific, rated predictions.
- [ ] Safety behaviours listed and a plan agreed to drop them.
- [ ] Hierarchy of 10-15 steps with SUDS ratings, including interoceptive items if relevant.
- [ ] Each exposure has before/after belief ratings and a learning statement.
- [ ] Homework is specific and reviewed; a standard outcome measure is falling.
