# Delivering behavioural parent training

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.*

**Goal:** help parents of a child with oppositional or conduct problems change the coercive
cycle at home, using a sequenced social-learning programme with measurable change.

## 1. Who and which format (NICE CG158)
- Offer a **group** parent training programme to parents of children aged 3 to 11 who are at
  high risk of, or have, oppositional defiant disorder or conduct disorder (1.5.1).
- Offer an **individual** programme when the parents cannot take part in a group (1.5.3);
  for severe and complex problems, individual parent and child training (1.5.5).
- Group programmes should involve both parents where possible, typically have 10 to 12
  parents, use a social learning model with modelling, rehearsal and feedback, and typically
  run 10 to 16 meetings of 90 to 120 minutes (1.5.2).
- Do not treat a neurodevelopmental history such as ADHD as a barrier to assessment (1.3.3).

## 2. Assess and engage
- Baseline: the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire completed by parent, carer or
  teacher (1.3.4); NICE also lists the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory among outcome
  measures. Look up the current scoring and cut-offs in the manual for the edition you use.
- Screen for coexisting problems (ADHD, autism, learning difficulties, parental mental
  health or substance misuse) and refer when significant complicating factors are present.
- **Safeguarding first:** follow local child-protection procedures if concerns arise.
- Explain the coercive cycle: child demand, parent gives in or escalates, both learn that
  escalation works. The programme changes where attention goes.
- Agree 2-3 target behaviours with observable definitions.

## 3. Sequence the skills (positives before limits)
1. Child-led play: parent describes, imitates and praises, without questions or commands.
2. Labelled praise.
3. Clear, specific, positively phrased instructions, with follow-through.
4. Reward charts for 1-2 target behaviours, small and immediate, phased out gradually.
5. Planned ignoring for minor attention-seeking behaviour; warn about an extinction burst.
6. Brief, calm, consistent consequences or time-out as your programme manual specifies.
Introduce limit-setting only after positive interaction is established.

## 4. Session structure and wider system
- Review homework, teach one skill with modelling and role-play, set specific homework.
- With consent, share the plan with the teacher and align praise and reward language.
- Use a manualised programme with supervision, direct observation and routine outcome
  measures (NICE 1.1 supervision wording).

## 5. Monitor and end
- Repeat the outcome measures at mid-point and end; plan for setbacks and boosters.

## Gotchas
- Parents often skip special time once limits are introduced; behaviour then relapses.
- Extinction bursts make things look worse for a week or two; warn parents in advance.
- Time-out should never involve locking a child in, frightening spaces or physical
  restraint; adapt or avoid for trauma-exposed or autistic children.
- Parental depression, conflict or substance use predicts drop-out; address or refer.

## Verify
- [ ] Baseline measures recorded; safeguarding screen documented.
- [ ] Targets defined in observable terms.
- [ ] Positive skills established before limit-setting.
- [ ] Homework reviewed each session; school informed with consent.
- [ ] Post-programme measures show change or a revised plan is in place.
