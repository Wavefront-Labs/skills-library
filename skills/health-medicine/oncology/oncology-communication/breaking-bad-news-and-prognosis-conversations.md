# Breaking bad news and prognosis conversations

**Goal:** deliver serious cancer information so that the patient understands it, feels
supported, and can take part in decisions that reflect their own values.

## 1. Prepare (before entering the room)
- Know the facts: histology, stage, treatment options, intent, and what remains
  uncertain. Agree the message with the team.
- Private room, phones silenced, time protected, tissues; invite the patient to bring
  someone. Book a **professional interpreter** when needed; never use family to
  interpret bad news.
- Have the specialist nurse or key worker present if possible.

## 2. Break the news with SPIKES
| Step | What you do | Example phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| **S**etting | Sit down, introductions, eye level | "Who have you brought with you today?" |
| **P**erception | Find out what they already know or suspect | "What is your understanding of why we did the scan?" |
| **I**nvitation | Ask how much detail they want | "Are you someone who likes all the details, or the big picture?" |
| **K**nowledge | Warning shot, then the news in plain words, in small chunks | "I'm afraid the results are not what we hoped. The biopsy shows cancer." |
| **E**motion | Pause; respond to emotion before giving more facts | "This is a lot to take in." |
| **S**trategy/summary | Next steps, check understanding, follow-up | "Can you tell me what you will tell your family?" |

Use the word "cancer" clearly; avoid euphemisms ("growth", "shadow") and jargon
("positive margin", "progression") without explanation.

## 3. Respond to emotion (NURSE)
- **N**ame: "You seem frightened."
- **U**nderstand: "I can see why this is so hard."
- **R**espect: "You have handled so much already."
- **S**upport: "We will work through this with you."
- **E**xplore: "Tell me more about what worries you most."
Silence is useful. Patients flooded with emotion do not process facts; information
given then will often not be retained.

## 4. Discuss prognosis honestly
- **Ask first:** "Would it help to talk about what this might mean for the future?"
  Some people want numbers, some do not, and preferences change.
- Give ranges, not single numbers: "Most people in your situation live months to a
  couple of years; some longer, some shorter." Avoid median survival presented as a
  personal expiry date.
- **Best case / worst case / most likely** framing helps.
- Acknowledge uncertainty and pair it with a plan: "**hope for the best, prepare for
  the worst**" (hope-and-worry statements: "I hope this treatment works well, and I
  worry it may not").
- Distinguish **intent**: curative vs life-prolonging vs symptom-focused, explicitly.

## 5. Discuss treatment trade-offs (shared decision-making)
- Lay out options including **no anticancer treatment** with best supportive care.
- For each: likely benefit (absolute numbers or natural frequencies, not only relative
  risk), main harms, time spent in hospital, impact on what matters to the patient.
- Elicit values: "What is most important to you if time is limited?" "What would be
  unacceptable?" (Serious Illness Conversation Guide questions).
- Make a recommendation when asked; do not abandon the patient to a menu.

## 6. Transition to palliative intent
- Signal early in the illness that the conversation may come; revisit at progression,
  falling performance status, or when treatment burden exceeds benefit.
- Frame it as a change of goals, not "nothing more can be done": "We will focus on
  keeping you as well and comfortable as possible."
- Introduce specialist palliative care early (concurrently with anticancer therapy where
  appropriate); start advance care planning, preferred place of care, and resuscitation
  decisions per local law and policy.

## Gotchas
- Information overload: one key message per consultation; follow up in writing.
- Family requests to withhold the diagnosis (collusion): explore the concern, but the
  patient's right to know and preferences take precedence (cultural and legal context
  varies).
- Over-optimism is common in oncologists' prognostic estimates; check against data.
- Do not deliver bad news by phone or results portal unless previously agreed.

## Verify
- [ ] Facts and team message agreed; interpreter booked if needed.
- [ ] Patient's existing understanding and information preferences elicited.
- [ ] Emotion acknowledged before more information given.
- [ ] Intent, options (including no treatment) and trade-offs explained in plain terms.
- [ ] Understanding checked (teach-back); written summary and follow-up arranged.
- [ ] Palliative care and advance care planning raised when goals change.
