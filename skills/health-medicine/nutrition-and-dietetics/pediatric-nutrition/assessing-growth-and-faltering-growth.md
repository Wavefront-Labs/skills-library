# Assessing growth and faltering growth

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement or current guidelines. Thresholds follow NICE NG75 (UK); definitions vary internationally.

**Goal:** measure and plot a child's growth accurately, recognise when growth is
genuinely faltering, find contributors in the feeding history, and agree a plan with goals.

## 1. Weight loss in the early days of life
- Some weight loss is normal; it usually stops after about 3 or 4 days and most infants
  are back to birth weight by 3 weeks.
- If an infant loses more than 10% of birth weight: clinical assessment for dehydration or
  illness, a detailed feeding history, consider observed feeding by someone trained, and
  offer feeding support. Re-weigh no more often than daily.
- Consider paediatric referral for illness, marked weight loss, no response to feeding
  support, or not back to birth weight by 3 weeks.

## 2. Thresholds for concern after the early days
On UK-WHO growth charts (a centile space is the gap between adjacent centile lines):
- a fall across 1 or more weight centile spaces if birth weight was below the 9th centile
- a fall across 2 or more if birth weight was between the 9th and 91st centiles
- a fall across 3 or more if birth weight was above the 91st centile
- current weight below the 2nd centile, whatever the birth weight.

## 3. When there is concern
- Weigh; measure length (birth to 2 years) or height (over 2 years); plot with previous
  measurements to see weight change and linear growth over time.
- Concern about length or height: get parents' heights and work out the mid-parental height
  centile. More than 2 centile spaces below it may suggest undernutrition or a primary growth disorder.
- Over 2 years: determine BMI centile. Below the 2nd centile may be undernutrition or small
  build; below the 0.4th centile suggests probable undernutrition needing assessment.
- Record measurements in the parent-held child health record.
- Do a clinical, developmental and social assessment and a detailed feeding or eating
  history; consider observing feeding or mealtimes (by a trained person).
- Consider testing for urinary tract infection, and for coeliac disease if gluten has been
  eaten. Further tests only if indicated: they rarely find a cause in a well-appearing child.
- Associated factors: preterm birth, neurodevelopmental concerns, maternal postnatal
  depression or anxiety.

## 4. Feeding history prompts
- Milk-fed infants: effectiveness of suckling or bottle feeding, feeding patterns and
  routines, environment, aversion, how carers respond to feeding cues, physical disorders.
- Older children: mealtime arrangements, types of foods offered, food aversion or avoidance,
  appetite, parent-child interaction at meals.
- Consider a food and mealtime diary kept by parents or carers.

## 5. Plan
- Agree a management plan with parents and carers, with specific goals, monitoring and a
  date to reassess.
- Offer feeding support; if formula supplements a breastfed infant, support continued
  breastfeeding, advise expressing, and give available breast milk first.
- Relaxed enjoyable mealtimes, eating with others, avoiding coercion or punitive approaches,
  regular schedule such as 3 meals and 2 snacks.
- Food choices suited to developmental stage that optimise energy and nutrient density; if more is needed,
  short-term fortification with energy-dense foods or referral to a paediatric dietitian.
- Too many energy-dense drinks, including milk, can reduce appetite for food.
- A trial of oral nutritional supplement only if faltering continues despite the above, with regular reassessment.

## Gotchas
- A single low reading is not a trend; re-measure before acting.
- Preterm birth is an associated factor: interpret charts with that in mind.
