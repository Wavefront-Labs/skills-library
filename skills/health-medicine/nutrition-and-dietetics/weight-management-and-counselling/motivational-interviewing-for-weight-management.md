# Motivational interviewing for weight management

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** help a person choose and sustain changes that improve health, using a
counselling style that builds their own motivation — without stigma, and without
missing an eating disorder.

## 1. Set up the conversation
- **Ask permission:** "Would it be OK to talk about your weight today?"
- Use **person-first, neutral language**: "weight", "higher weight", "a person with
  obesity" — not "obese person", "fat", "fail". Ask which terms they prefer.
- Right-sized chair, cuff and scales; weigh in private; weight is optional for the conversation.
- Explore their story: weight history, previous attempts (what worked, what didn't),
  medical issues, medications that promote weight gain (some antipsychotics, insulin,
  sulfonylureas, steroids, certain antidepressants), sleep, mood, work and family.

## 2. Screen before you prescribe a deficit
- Ask about binge eating, compensatory behaviours (vomiting, laxatives, excessive
  exercise), rigid restriction. A quick screen such as **SCOFF** (≥2 positive answers
  suggests further assessment). Binge-eating disorder is common in weight-management
  settings and needs a different approach — refer.
- History of eating disorder → involve the specialist team before any restriction.

## 3. Motivational interviewing (MI) — OARS
- **O**pen questions: "What would be different if your health improved?"
- **A**ffirmations: "You've already cut down on takeaways — that took effort."
- **R**eflections (more than questions; aim ~2 reflections per question):
  "You'd like more energy to play with your kids, but evenings are when it falls apart."
- **S**ummaries that collect their reasons and plans.
- Listen for and evoke **change talk** (desire, ability, reasons, need → commitment).
  Respond to "sustain talk" and discord by reflecting, not arguing — the "righting
  reflex" (telling people what to do) increases resistance.
- Importance and confidence rulers: "On 0–10, how important… why not lower? What
  would take you up one point?"

## 4. Make a plan together
- **Realistic targets:** 5–10% loss improves blood pressure, glucose and lipids;
  ~0.5–1 kg/week is a common pace with a ~500–600 kcal/day deficit.
- Choose an eating pattern the person can sustain (Mediterranean-style, portion
  control, meal replacements, time-restricted eating) — adherence matters more than the type.
- **SMART goals**, 1–3 at a time: "Pack lunch on 4 workdays this week" beats "eat healthier".
- **Self-monitoring:** food diary/app, regular (e.g. weekly) self-weighing, steps.
- Activity: build towards ~150–300 min/week moderate activity plus strength work; reduce sitting.
- Plan for lapses: identify high-risk situations and if-then plans.

## 5. Support and escalate
- Regular follow-up improves outcomes; group programmes are effective.
- Discuss pharmacotherapy (e.g. GLP-1 receptor agonists) and bariatric surgery when
  criteria are met; on these, the dietitian's focus shifts to protein adequacy,
  micronutrients, hydration, GI symptoms and preserving muscle.

## Gotchas
- Weight-neutral goals (blood pressure, fitness, mood) are valid outcomes too.
- Regain after stopping is expected biology, not personal failure — frame long-term care.
- Children: family-based approach; avoid weight talk directed at the child.

Uncertainty note: MI technique per Miller & Rollnick; targets per common NICE/ADA
guidance — adapt for age, ethnicity and comorbidity (BMI thresholds differ for some populations).

## Verify
- [ ] Permission asked and neutral language used.
- [ ] Eating-disorder screen done before setting a deficit.
- [ ] Reflections outnumbered questions; change talk elicited.
- [ ] 1–3 SMART goals agreed in the person's words.
- [ ] Self-monitoring and follow-up arranged.
