# Renal diet planning in CKD

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** use diet to slow CKD progression and control potassium, phosphate, sodium
and fluid — while keeping the patient adequately nourished. Over-restriction is a
common way renal diets cause harm.

## 1. Know the stage and treatment
- CKD stage (eGFR, albuminuria), diabetes, dialysis modality (haemodialysis vs
  peritoneal), residual urine output, and transplant status all change the targets.
- Pull recent labs: potassium, phosphate, calcium, PTH, bicarbonate, albumin,
  glucose/HbA1c, urine albumin:creatinine ratio.

## 2. Protein
| Situation | Typical target (adults) |
|---|---|
| CKD 3–5, not on dialysis, metabolically stable | ~0.55–0.8 g/kg/day (with dietitian supervision; very low with keto-analogues only in specialist settings) |
| CKD with diabetes | ~0.6–0.8 g/kg/day |
| Haemodialysis / peritoneal dialysis | ~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day (PD loses protein into dialysate) |
Use adjusted/ideal body weight per local practice in obesity. Protein restriction
must never push the patient into malnutrition — energy ~25–35 kcal/kg/day.

## 3. Potassium — treat the lab, not the label
- Restrict only if hyperkalaemic; blanket restriction removes fruit and vegetables
  (fibre, alkali) needlessly.
- Check non-dietary causes first: ACE-i/ARB/MRA, constipation, metabolic acidosis,
  poor glycaemic control, missed dialysis, blood transfusion.
- High-potassium foods to moderate: potatoes, tomatoes/purée, bananas, dried fruit,
  chocolate, juices, **potassium-based salt substitutes** (avoid). Boiling and
  draining vegetables lowers potassium content.

## 4. Phosphate
- Target per stage (typically towards the normal range).
- Biggest lever: **phosphate additives** in processed foods (look for "phos" in
  ingredients — absorbed almost completely), colas, processed cheese and meats.
  Plant phosphate (phytate) is less absorbed than animal phosphate.
- **Binders** (calcium-based, sevelamer, lanthanum, iron-based) must be taken **with
  meals and snacks containing phosphate**, not on an empty stomach; match dose to the
  phosphate load of the meal.

## 5. Sodium and fluid
- Sodium commonly <2–2.3 g/day (~5–6 g salt) — helps BP, proteinuria and thirst.
- Fluid: haemodialysis — often ~500–750 mL/day **plus** previous day's urine output;
  aim interdialytic weight gain below ~2–2.5 kg (or <4% of dry weight). Count soups,
  jelly, ice, sauces. PD and non-dialysis CKD: usually less restrictive.
- Thirst control: low salt, sucking ice cubes from the allowance, chilled fruit.

## 6. Other points
- Diabetes: integrate carbohydrate guidance; some phosphate- and potassium-dense
  "healthy" foods may need swapping.
- Correct metabolic acidosis (medical) — and more fruit/veg where potassium allows.
- Vitamins: renal-specific water-soluble supplements on dialysis; avoid
  high-dose vitamin A and unsupervised herbal products.

## Gotchas
- Patients often carry outdated diet sheets — reassess each time labs change.
- Low albumin on dialysis reflects inflammation as much as intake; use full nutrition assessment.
- Transplant reverses most restrictions; post-transplant focus shifts to weight,
  glucose, food safety (immunosuppression).

Uncertainty note: targets summarised from KDOQI 2020 nutrition guideline and common
renal dietetic practice; individualise and follow local renal unit protocols.

## Verify
- [ ] Stage, modality and current labs reviewed.
- [ ] Protein and energy targets set and nutrition status checked.
- [ ] Potassium restriction only if lab-indicated; other causes addressed.
- [ ] Phosphate additive education given; binder timing explained.
