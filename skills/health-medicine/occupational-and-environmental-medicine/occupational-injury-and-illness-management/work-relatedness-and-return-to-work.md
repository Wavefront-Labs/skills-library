# Work-relatedness and return to work

**Goal:** treat the injured or ill worker so that they get back to safe, productive work
as early as is clinically sensible — because prolonged absence itself harms health — and
document causation clearly enough to survive a compensation claim.

## 1. The occupational history (do it every time)
Ask, and write down verbatim where possible:
- **Mechanism**: what they were doing, load, posture, the moment of injury; or for illness,
  the exposure (substance, duration, controls, respirator, ventilation).
- **Temporal pattern**: onset relative to work; better on weekends/holidays? (classic for
  occupational asthma and contact dermatitis).
- **Co-workers affected**? Same symptoms in others is strong evidence.
- **Job demands** now and the employer's capacity for modified duties.
- **Previous episodes, other jobs, hobbies** (alternative exposures).

## 2. Judging work-relatedness
Use a structured frame (adapted from Bradford Hill-style reasoning):
1. Is the **diagnosis** secure?
2. Is there **evidence of exposure** or a credible mechanism at work?
3. Is the **exposure sufficient** (dose, duration) by the published evidence?
4. Is the **temporal relationship** right?
5. Are **non-occupational causes** plausible, and have you weighed them?
6. Conclusion in the legal language of the jurisdiction — usually "more likely than not"
   (balance of probabilities), *not* scientific certainty. Say "work contributed to /
   aggravated" when that is the honest answer — aggravation of a pre-existing condition
   is often compensable. Check local rules; definitions differ by scheme.

Reporting duties also differ: e.g. UK RIDDOR (specified injuries to workers, reportable occupational diseases, exposure to
carcinogens, mutagens and biological agents, and dangerous occurrences); US OSHA
recordkeeping (Form 300) uses its own "recordable" definition (29 CFR 1904.7: medical treatment beyond first aid, days away, restricted work or job transfer), which is not the same as
compensability. Know which one the employer is asking about.

## 3. Treat for function
- For non-specific low-back pain and most soft-tissue injuries: reassurance, stay
  active, simple analgesia per current guidance, avoid bed rest, avoid early imaging
  without red flags (cauda equina, fracture, infection, malignancy, progressive deficit) (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- Screen **yellow flags** (fear-avoidance, catastrophising, low expectations of recovery),
  **blue flags** (perceived work stress, poor supervisor support) and **black flags**
  (system obstacles: compensation disputes, no modified duties). These predict prolonged
  absence better than the injury does.
- Set an expected recovery timeline at the first visit and say it out loud.

## 4. Build the graded return-to-work plan
1. Name the **target job** and its demands.
2. Write current **capacities** in measurable terms (lift 10 kg floor-to-waist occasional;
   sit 30 min then change position; no ladders).
3. Agree **modified duties** with the employer — real work, not "sitting in the canteen".
4. **Grade hours and load** — a typical example: weeks 1–2 at 50% hours on modified
   duties, weeks 3–4 at 75% with partial original tasks, then full duties. This is
   illustrative; set steps by tolerance, not calendar.
5. **Review dates** and who is accountable (worker, supervisor, case manager, clinician).
6. Plan what happens if a step fails (hold, not restart from zero).

## 5. Treating-doctor reports
State diagnosis (if consented), work-relatedness opinion with reasoning, current
capacity, restrictions, treatment plan, expected date of full capacity, next review.
Avoid "off work until further notice" — it ends conversations.

## Gotchas
- "Totally unfit" certificates for minor injuries prolong disability; prefer
  "fit for modified duties" with specifics.
- Recording a diagnosis as "work-related" is a medicolegal opinion — reason it, don't
  default to the worker's or the employer's view.
- Missing the early window: the longer someone is off, the lower the chance of ever
  returning (the commonly cited figure is that return becomes unlikely after many months
  off; exact percentages vary by source, treat them as indicative) (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
