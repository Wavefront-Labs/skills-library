# Aircrew medical certification and altitude physiology

**Goal:** decide whether a pilot or aircrew member can safely perform their flying role,
grounding the decision in the physiology of flight and the regulator's standard rather
than general clinical fitness.

## 1. Know the framework
- **ICAO Annex 1** sets international minimum standards; states implement them, e.g.
  **EASA Part-MED** (Class 1 commercial, Class 2 private, LAPL) and **FAA 14 CFR Part 67**
  (first, second, third class; 14 CFR Part 68 BasicMed for some private flying). EASA Part-MED class structure (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- Certificate validity shortens with age and class; check the current regulator schedule.
- Only an authorised aeromedical examiner (AME) or the authority's medical section may
  certify; borderline cases are deferred to the authority.

## 2. Apply the incapacitation-risk approach
The widely used **"1% rule"** holds that a multi-crew pilot's annual risk of sudden or
subtle incapacitation should be below about 1% (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) (single-pilot operations are usually judged more strictly).
For each condition ask:
1. Could it cause **sudden incapacitation** (seizure, syncope, arrhythmia, hypoglycaemia)?
2. Could it cause **subtle impairment** (cognition, vision, vestibular, mood, sedation)?
3. Is it **aggravated by the flight environment** (hypoxia, pressure change, acceleration,
   fatigue, circadian disruption)?
4. Does the **treatment** impair (sedating antihistamines, opioids, many psychotropics)?

Common deferral areas: cardiovascular disease, epilepsy or unexplained loss of
consciousness, insulin-treated diabetes (protocol-based certification now exists in several
regimes), psychiatric illness and antidepressants (some regulators run SSRI protocols),
substance misuse, and visual or colour-vision deficits.

## 3. Altitude and pressure physiology
- **Hypoxia** (Dalton's law): falling partial pressure of oxygen. Transport-category pressurised cabins must provide a cabin pressure altitude of not more than 8,000 ft under normal
  operation (14 CFR 25.841(a)); a healthy person's SpO2 at that altitude is roughly 90% (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked); patients with lung or heart disease may desaturate further.
- **Time of useful consciousness** after decompression shortens steeply with altitude
  (approximate textbook values: FL250 3-5 min, FL300 1-2 min, FL350 30-60 s, FL400 15-20 s (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) — FAA PHAK chapter 17 carries a table; not retrievable this session);
  rapid decompression and exertion shorten it further.
- **Boyle's law:** trapped-gas problems on ascent and especially descent — otic and sinus
  barotrauma (do not fly with an upper respiratory infection blocking the Eustachian tube),
  dental barotrauma, untreated pneumothorax, recent gas-introducing surgery.
- **Altitude decompression sickness** is uncommon below about 18,000 ft (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) but more likely
  after diving. FAA AIM chapter 8 recommends waiting at least 12 hours after dives not requiring
  decompression stops and at least 24 hours after dives that did, before flying at altitudes up to
  8,000 ft MSL, and at least 24 hours after any dive before flying above 8,000 ft MSL (actual flight
  altitude, not cabin altitude); EASA and DAN/UHMS guidance differ, so check the applicable one.
- **Passenger fitness to fly:** a hypoxic challenge test (breathing about 15% oxygen
  approximates 8,000 ft (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)) helps decide in-flight oxygen for lung-disease patients.
- Other stressors: spatial disorientation, positive-G and G-LOC in high-performance
  aircraft (anti-G straining manoeuvre, G-suits), noise, fatigue and jet lag.

## 4. Reach a disposition
| Disposition | Use when |
|---|---|
| Fit | Meets standard without restriction |
| Fit with limitation | e.g. must wear corrective lenses, multi-pilot operations only, safety-pilot requirement |
| Temporarily unfit | Acute, self-limiting illness or medication washout |
| Defer/refer | Outside AME authority, needs specialist work-up or special issuance/waiver |
| Unfit | Risk cannot be reduced to an acceptable level |

Document the evidence, the risk reasoning and any review interval.

## Gotchas
- Pilots under-report symptoms for fear of losing their licence; build trust and know
  peer-support programmes.
- Over-the-counter sedating medicines and recent alcohol are common, avoidable hazards.
- "Clinically fit" is not "aeromedically fit": stable conditions can still carry an
  unacceptable sudden-incapacitation risk.
- Regulations change frequently; never certify from memory of an old standard.

## Verify
- [ ] Correct certificate class and current regulator standard identified.
- [ ] Sudden and subtle incapacitation risks assessed, including treatment effects.
- [ ] Flight-environment aggravators considered (hypoxia, pressure, G, fatigue).
- [ ] Disposition, limitations and review date recorded; deferrals sent to the authority.
