# Fitness-for-duty certification — answer the employer's question, protect the worker's privacy

**Goal:** decide whether a person can do a *specific* job safely and sustainably, and
communicate that as an outcome (fit / fit with restrictions / temporarily unfit / unfit)
without disclosing their diagnosis.

## 1. Get the job before you get the patient
Ask the referrer for, and do not start without:
- **Job title is not enough** — a written description of physical, cognitive and
  environmental demands: lifting weights and frequency, postures, shift pattern, night
  work, driving, working at height, respirator use, lone working, weapons, patient contact.
- **Whether the role is safety-critical** (others are endangered if the worker has a
  sudden incapacity: drivers, pilots, crane operators, rail track workers, armed staff).
- **The applicable standard**, if any. Many roles have a published medical standard that
  overrides your general judgement — e.g. national driver-licensing standards (UK DVLA
  "Assessing fitness to drive: a guide for medical professionals"; US FMCSA rules for commercial drivers), aviation (see
  aircrew-medical-certification-and-altitude-physiology), seafarer, rail and diving
  standards. Use the current edition; thresholds change.
- **The specific question** ("Can she return to night shifts?" beats "Please assess").

## 2. The assessment
1. **Consent and role clarity** first: explain you are assessing for the employer, what
   will be reported (functional outcome and restrictions) and what will not (diagnosis,
   unless the worker consents). Document it.
2. **History aimed at function**: conditions that cause *sudden incapacity* (seizure,
   syncope, hypoglycaemia on insulin/sulfonylureas, arrhythmia, sleep disorder),
   *progressive limitation* (MSK, respiratory, cardiac capacity), *impaired judgement*
   (substance use, sedating medication, cognitive or psychiatric illness) and
   *communicable risk* (for healthcare/food roles).
3. **Medication review** for sedation and hypoglycaemia risk — often the decisive factor.
4. **Targeted examination and tests** proportionate to the role: corrected visual
   acuity and fields, colour vision (Ishihara) where colour signals matter, audiometry
   for hearing-critical roles, spirometry before respirator use, BP, BMI/neck size for
   sleep-apnoea screening in drivers, urinalysis for glucose if indicated (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
5. **Functional testing** where the history leaves doubt: observed lifting, step test,
   or a referral for functional capacity evaluation (see the occupational-therapy
   work-rehabilitation skills).

## 3. Decide using a risk frame
For each condition ask: **likelihood** of an adverse event at work × **consequence** if it
happens there. A 2% annual seizure risk is irrelevant for desk work and disqualifying for
heavy goods driving. Prefer the least restrictive outcome that controls the risk.

Outcomes:
- **Fit** — no restrictions.
- **Fit with restrictions/adjustments** — time-limited where possible, with a review date.
- **Temporarily unfit** — with expected review interval and what would change the answer.
- **Unfit** for this role — say which demand cannot be met, and whether alternative duties
  would be suitable.

## 4. Writing restrictions people can act on
Bad: "Light duties." Good: "No lifting over 10 kg; no work at height; no driving company
vehicles; day shifts only; review in 6 weeks." Each restriction should be **specific,
measurable, tied to a demand, and dated**. Note that the employer decides whether the
adjustment is operationally possible — you advise, they decide.

## Gotchas
- Pre-placement assessment must not be a disguised screen-out: in many jurisdictions
  (e.g. UK Equality Act 2010 section 60, which bars asking about an applicant's health before
  a job offer, subject to listed exceptions) health questions are restricted before a job offer;
  the US ADA position and the duty to consider reasonable adjustments (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- A treating doctor's "fit to work" note is not a fitness-for-duty decision against a
  safety standard — you may legitimately reach a different conclusion.
- Self-report under-discloses when a licence or job depends on it; corroborate with the
  GP/treating records (with consent) for sudden-incapacity conditions.
- Do not certify against a standard you have not read in its current version.

## Verify
- [ ] You had the job demands and the governing standard before deciding.
- [ ] Consent and the reporting boundary are documented.
- [ ] Every sudden-incapacity risk was considered against the role's safety-criticality.
- [ ] The report states an outcome and restrictions, not the diagnosis.
- [ ] Every restriction is measurable and has a review date.
