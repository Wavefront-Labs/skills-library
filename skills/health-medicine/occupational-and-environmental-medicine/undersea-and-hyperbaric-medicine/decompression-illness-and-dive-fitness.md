# Decompression illness and dive fitness

**Goal:** recognise decompression illness early, get the diver onto oxygen and to a chamber,
use hyperbaric oxygen only for evidence-supported indications, and keep unfit divers out
of the water.

## 1. Recognise decompression illness (DCI)
DCI covers two mechanisms that are treated the same way:
- **Decompression sickness (DCS):** inert-gas bubbles forming on ascent. Traditionally
  **Type I** (joint pain, skin marbling, lymphatic swelling) and **Type II** (neurological,
  inner-ear, cardiorespiratory "chokes").
- **Arterial gas embolism (AGE):** pulmonary barotrauma lets gas into arterial blood;
  onset usually within minutes of surfacing with stroke-like signs or collapse, and can
  follow even a shallow ascent with breath-holding.
Any new symptom within about 24 hours of a dive (or after flying post-dive) is DCI until
proven otherwise. Record the dive profile, gas, computer data and symptom timeline.

## 2. First aid and evacuation
1. **100% oxygen** at the highest available concentration (demand valve or tight
   non-rebreather), continued until handover. Oxygen relieves symptoms but does not
   replace recompression.
2. Lay flat (supine or recovery position); head-down positioning is no longer advised.
3. Oral fluids if fully conscious, or isotonic glucose-free IV fluids, for hydration.
4. Neurological examination, repeated and documented.
5. Call the regional hyperbaric unit or **DAN emergency line** early for advice and chamber location.
6. **Air evacuation** with cabin altitude as low as possible (pressurised to sea level or
   low-flying helicopter) (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
In-water recompression is generally not recommended outside trained, equipped expeditions.

## 3. Recompression
- Standard first-line treatment is **US Navy Treatment Table 6** (oxygen breathing at
  60 fsw with air breaks, then a stage at 30 fsw; extendable). Stage depths, durations and
  total time are per the current US Navy Diving Manual (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- **Table 5** is reserved for pain-only or skin DCS resolving fully within the first
  10 minutes at depth (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- Repeat treatments for residual deficits until a plateau. Chamber choice, extensions and
  saturation tables are specialist decisions.

## 4. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) indications
The UHMS list (check the current edition) includes (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked): DCS and gas embolism, carbon monoxide
poisoning, clostridial myonecrosis, necrotising soft-tissue infection, crush injury and
compartment syndrome, selected problem (e.g. diabetic) wounds, delayed radiation injury,
compromised grafts and flaps, refractory osteomyelitis, intracranial abscess, severe
anaemia where transfusion is impossible, thermal burns, central retinal artery occlusion
and idiopathic sudden sensorineural hearing loss.
- **Absolute contraindication:** untreated pneumothorax (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- **Relative:** bullous lung disease, uncontrolled seizures, URTI or Eustachian dysfunction,
  claustrophobia, certain drugs (e.g. bleomycin history, doxorubicin, disulfiram; check a
  current reference).
- **Complications:** middle-ear barotrauma (most common), CNS oxygen toxicity seizures,
  pulmonary oxygen toxicity with long exposures, reversible myopia.

## 5. Assess fitness to dive
- **Recreational:** screening questionnaire (e.g. RSTC/UHMS diver medical form) with
  physician review of positive answers; UK guidance from the UK Diving Medical Committee.
- **Commercial:** statutory medicals by an approved medical examiner of divers (e.g. UK
  HSE under the Diving at Work Regulations), usually annual.
Key issues:

| Area | Concern |
|---|---|
| Lungs | Previous spontaneous pneumothorax, bullae, asthma that is cold/exercise-triggered or poorly controlled |
| Neurology | Epilepsy, unexplained syncope |
| Heart | Ischaemic disease, arrhythmia, exercise capacity; PFO assessment after shunt-type DCS |
| ENT | Inability to equalise, perforated eardrum, previous inner-ear barotrauma |
| Metabolic | Insulin-treated diabetes (protocol-based diving accepted by some bodies) |
| Mental health/medication | Panic disorder, sedating or seizure-threshold-lowering drugs |

## Gotchas
- Divers often deny or minimise symptoms; ask directly and examine thoroughly.
- Inner-ear DCS and inner-ear barotrauma look alike but need different management; get specialist input.
- A normal dive profile does not exclude DCS.
- Return to diving after DCI needs specialist review and a defined interval.
