# Structuring an advanced practice consultation

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** as a nurse practitioner or advanced clinical practitioner seeing
undifferentiated patients, reach a defensible working diagnosis and plan, stay inside
scope, and leave the patient knowing exactly when to come back.

## 1. Open and gather (Calgary-Cambridge-style)
- Open-ended start, then let the patient talk for the first minute or two without interruption.
- Elicit **ideas, concerns and expectations (ICE)** — often why they came today.
- Focused history: onset, course, character, associated features, systematic red
  flags for the presenting complaint, PMH, **medications incl. OTC and herbal**,
  allergies, social (smoking, alcohol, occupation, who's at home), pregnancy possibility.
- Summarise back: "So, three days of… have I got that right?"

## 2. Examine with a hypothesis
Before examining, name your top three differentials; examine to confirm or exclude
them, including the **one dangerous diagnosis you must not miss** (e.g. in sore
throat: epiglottitis, peritonsillar abscess; in back pain: cauda equina, AAA,
malignancy, infection). Full vital signs every time.

## 3. Reason explicitly
- Write the differential in the record with what argues for and against each.
- Use validated decision tools where they exist (e.g. Centor/FeverPAIN, Wells,
  Ottawa ankle rules, PERC) — and know their populations.
- **Test thresholds:** order a test only if the result would change management.
  Every test has a pre-test probability; low-probability positives are mostly false.
- Ask: "What would make me wrong? What else could this be?"

## 4. Decide and prescribe within scope
- Check your **scope of practice**, local formulary, and prescribing authority
  (registration body, employer protocols, any controlled-drug limits).
- Check interactions, renal/hepatic function, pregnancy/breastfeeding, allergies.
- If outside your competence or scope: consult or refer — and document who and why.
  Knowing the boundary is a core advanced-practice skill, not a weakness.

## 5. Share the decision
Explain the likely diagnosis in plain language, the options (including watchful
waiting), benefits and harms, and agree the plan. Check understanding with
teach-back.

## 6. Safety-net specifically
Not "come back if worse" — instead: **what** symptoms, **how soon**, and **where**
to go. "If your temperature stays above 38 after three days, or you get a stiff neck,
rash that doesn't fade under a glass, or become drowsy — go to the ED straight away."
Document the safety-netting advice given.

## 7. Document
History with pertinent negatives (red flags asked and absent), exam findings,
differential, plan, prescriptions, safety-netting, follow-up and any referral.

## Gotchas
- Premature closure and anchoring are the commonest reasoning errors — revisit the
  diagnosis when the story doesn't fit or the patient re-attends.
- Re-attenders and "third presentation" patients deserve a fresh look, ideally by a
  senior colleague.
- Antibiotic stewardship: delayed/back-up prescriptions are a legitimate tool.
- Supervision and peer review are part of maintaining safe autonomous practice.

Uncertainty note: scope and prescribing rights vary widely by country, state and
employer; this describes generic consultation structure.

## Verify
- [ ] ICE explored and addressed.
- [ ] Must-not-miss diagnosis named and examined for.
- [ ] Differential and reasoning documented with pertinent negatives.
- [ ] Prescribing checked against scope, interactions and patient factors.
- [ ] Specific safety-netting and follow-up documented.
