# Teach-back and safe discharge

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** make sure the patient (and carer) can actually do what keeps them well at
home, and that nothing important falls through the gap between hospital and community.
Many readmissions trace back to misunderstood medications or missed warning signs.

## 1. Plan discharge from admission
- Record the expected date of discharge and what must happen before it (clinical
  criteria, equipment, package of care, transport, who is at home).
- Identify early: home oxygen, mobility aids, home adaptations, community nursing,
  social care referrals — these take days.
- Involve the carer/next of kin with the patient's consent.

## 2. Teach well
- **Assume limited health literacy for everyone** ("universal precautions"); many
  people won't say they didn't understand.
- **Plain language:** "blood thinner" not "anticoagulant"; "high blood pressure" not "hypertension".
- **Two or three key messages per session**, most important first. Use the patient's
  own goals ("so you can get back to walking the dog").
- **Chunk and check:** teach a chunk, check understanding, then the next chunk.
- **Show, then have them do it:** inhalers, insulin pens, injection technique, stoma
  care, wound dressings — watch them perform it.
- Back it with written/visual material at an easy reading level, in their language;
  use a professional interpreter, not family, for clinical teaching.

## 3. Teach-back (the core check)
Ask the patient to explain or demonstrate in their own words — it tests **your**
explanation, not their intelligence.
- "I want to make sure I explained this clearly. Can you tell me how you'll take
  your new water tablet tomorrow?"
- "What would you do if your weight goes up 2 kg in two days?"
If wrong or incomplete, re-explain differently and **teach back again** until correct.
Avoid "Do you understand?" or "Any questions?" — they invite a polite "yes".

## 4. Discharge checklist
- **Medication reconciliation:** compare pre-admission, inpatient and discharge
  lists; explain every new, changed and stopped drug and why. Make sure they have
  the supply and devices.
- **Red-flag symptoms:** specific, condition-based ("chest pain, breathless at rest,
  ankle swelling rising") with **who to call and when**.
- **Follow-up:** appointments booked (date/time given), tests to be done, results
  to chase and by whom.
- **Lines and devices** removed (cannulas!) or plans for those staying.
- **Discharge summary** sent to GP/primary care and community services; handover
  phone call for complex patients.
- Transport, keys, house warm and food available for frail/older patients.
- Teach-back done and documented.

## Gotchas
- Discharge teaching squeezed into the last 10 minutes, with the patient dressed and
  waiting for transport, is poorly retained — teach across the stay.
- Anticoagulants, insulin, opioids and diuretics cause the most post-discharge harm —
  prioritise them.
- Cognitive impairment, delirium on the ward, and hearing/vision problems change
  how you teach; involve carers and adapt materials.
- Friday-afternoon discharges often lack community follow-up — check what's actually in place.

Uncertainty note: teach-back method per AHRQ health-literacy toolkit; discharge
checklists vary by organisation.

## Verify
- [ ] Expected discharge date and needs recorded early.
- [ ] Key messages limited and prioritised.
- [ ] Teach-back (or demonstration) done and documented for each key skill/medicine.
- [ ] Medication reconciliation complete with changes explained.
- [ ] Red flags, contact numbers and follow-up given in writing; summary sent.
