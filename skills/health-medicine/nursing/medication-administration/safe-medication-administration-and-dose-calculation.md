# Safe medication administration and dose calculation

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** get the right drug, dose, route and time into the right patient, with the
checks concentrated where errors cause the most harm.

## 1. The checks at the bedside
The "rights": right **patient** (two identifiers — name and date of birth or hospital
number, checked against the wristband, never the room number), **drug**, **dose**,
**route**, **time**, plus right **reason**, **documentation**, **response**, and the
patient's right to refuse.
Also check: **allergies** (and the reaction type), the last dose given, relevant
observations or labs (e.g. HR before digoxin/beta-blocker, glucose before insulin,
INR before warfarin, potassium and renal function before potassium supplements),
and that the patient can swallow.

## 2. Dose calculations — show the working
- **Tablets/liquids:** dose = (wanted ÷ stock strength) × stock volume.
  Example: 62.5 mg wanted; stock 125 mg/5 mL → (62.5 ÷ 125) × 5 = **2.5 mL** (recomputed).
- **Weight-based:** mg/kg × kg. Use a **measured** weight in kg, recorded today where possible.
- **Infusion rate (mL/h):** (dose per hour ÷ concentration).
  Example: heparin 1,000 units/h; bag 25,000 units in 250 mL = 100 units/mL →
  1,000 ÷ 100 = **10 mL/h** (recomputed).
- **mcg/kg/min to mL/h:** mL/h = (mcg/kg/min × kg × 60) ÷ concentration (mcg/mL).
  Example: 5 mcg/kg/min, 80 kg, 1,600 mcg/mL → 5×80×60 = 24,000 ÷ 1,600 = **15 mL/h** (recomputed).
- **Gravity drip (drops/min):** (volume mL × drop factor) ÷ minutes.
- Sense-check: does the answer need more than ~2–3 tablets (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) or an unusual volume? Stop and recheck.

## 3. High-alert medicines
Insulin, anticoagulants, opioids, concentrated potassium and other electrolytes,
chemotherapy, IV sedatives, neuromuscular blockers, and paediatric doses.
- **Independent double check:** a second nurse recalculates from the prescription
  without seeing your answer, then you compare.
- Insulin: write "units" in full — never "U" (misread as 0 (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked); the Joint Commission and ISMP lists of unsafe abbreviations could not be fetched). Use insulin syringes or pens only.
- Concentrated potassium: use pre-mixed bags; never IV push.
- Opioids: check sedation score and RR before and after; naloxone available.

## 4. Technology helps, but doesn't think
- **Barcode scanning** of wristband and product catches wrong-patient and wrong-drug
  errors — don't scan a label stuck on a clipboard or override alerts without reading them.
- Use the infusion pump's drug library (see the bedside devices skill).

## 5. Error or near miss
Assess and protect the patient first; monitor; inform the prescriber and senior nurse;
document facts; report through the incident system. Being open with the patient (duty
of candour) is expected. Reporting is about fixing systems, not blame.

## Gotchas
- Interruptions during drug rounds drive errors — use "do not disturb" tabards or
  protected time if your unit has them.
- Look-alike/sound-alike drugs (hydralazine/hydroxyzine; different strengths of the
  same drug) — read the label three times: picking up, preparing, returning.
- Crushing modified-release or enteric-coated tablets can cause overdose or damage —
  check with pharmacy.
- "Held" doses need a documented reason and prescriber awareness.

Uncertainty note: list of "rights" and high-alert drugs varies by organisation (ISMP
lists are a common reference); follow local policy.

## Verify
- [ ] Two patient identifiers matched to the wristband.
- [ ] Allergy status confirmed and relevant obs/labs checked.
- [ ] Calculation written out and sense-checked.
- [ ] High-alert drug independently double-checked.
- [ ] Administration documented immediately; response reassessed.
