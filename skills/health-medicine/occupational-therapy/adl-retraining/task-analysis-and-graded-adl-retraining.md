# Task analysis and graded ADL retraining

**Goal:** move a person from current to target performance on a priority daily task
through structured, graded, repeated practice, using compensation where recovery is
unlikely or too slow.

## 1. Analyse the task
Break the chosen task (e.g. upper-body dressing) into observable steps, then note for
each: required motor, sensory, cognitive and perceptual demands; objects and setting;
time; safety. Watch the person perform it and mark the steps that fail and why.

| Step | Demand | Observed problem | Likely cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orient shirt | Perception, sequencing | Picks up inside out | Visuoperceptual/neglect |
| Affected arm into sleeve | Range, sitting balance | Loses balance | Trunk control |
| Fasten buttons | Fine motor, sensation | Unable | Reduced dexterity |

## 2. Choose the approach
- **Remediation/restoration** - practise to recover the underlying skill; suits expected
  recovery (e.g. early post-stroke).
- **Compensation/adaptation** - change the method, equipment or environment; suits
  permanent impairment, discharge pressure or safety risk.
- Usually both: compensate now to enable participation, keep remediating in parallel.
Agree the choice with the person; acceptance determines carry-over.

## 3. Grade the task
Adjust one variable at a time:
- **Physical** - sitting vs standing, support surface, object weight/size, range demanded.
- **Cognitive** - number of steps, choices offered, distractions, verbal vs written cues.
- **Assistance** - hand-over-hand, physical prompt, gesture, verbal cue, supervision,
  independent. Fade the least intrusive cue that still achieves success.
- **Environment** - quiet clinic, ward bathroom, then own home.

**Chaining:** in **backward chaining** the therapist does all but the last step and the
person completes it, so every attempt ends in success; add earlier steps progressively.
**Forward chaining** teaches step 1 first. Backward chaining is often preferred where
cognitive impairment or low motivation is present.

## 4. Teach compensatory techniques (examples)
- **One-handed dressing (hemiplegia):** affected limb in first, out last; dress seated
  with feet supported; loose front-opening garments, elastic laces, button hook.
- **Lower-body dressing with hip precautions or reduced reach:** long-handled reacher,
  sock aid, long shoe horn; follow the surgeon's/local precaution protocol.
- **Neglect/visual field loss:** consistent layout, anchoring cues, scanning to the
  affected side.

## 5. Energy conservation and pacing
For fatigue (COPD, heart failure, MS, long COVID, cancer-related fatigue), teach the
**4 Ps**: **Prioritise**, **Plan**, **Pace**, **Position**.
- Sit for tasks where possible; keep frequently used items between hip and shoulder height.
- Spread heavy tasks across the day/week; build in rest *before* exhaustion.
- Coordinate breathing with effort (exhale on exertion) in respiratory clients.
- Use an activity diary to identify boom-bust patterns.

## 6. Practise, repeat, transfer
- Practise at the real time of day in the real context where possible.
- Provide written/pictorial instructions and train carers in the same method.
- Re-measure against the baseline (assistance level, time, COPM) at each review.

## Gotchas
- Equipment issued without practice is frequently abandoned; train with it and check use.
- Doing the task for the person on a busy ward undoes retraining; agree the method with
  the whole team and document it.
- Grading too fast causes failure and loss of confidence; too slowly wastes rehab time.
- Fatigue and pain can masquerade as poor motivation.

## Verify
- [ ] Task analysed step-by-step with failure points and causes.
- [ ] Remediation/compensation choice agreed and documented.
- [ ] Grading and cue level recorded, with a plan to fade support.
- [ ] Carers/staff trained in the same method.
- [ ] Outcome re-measured against baseline.
