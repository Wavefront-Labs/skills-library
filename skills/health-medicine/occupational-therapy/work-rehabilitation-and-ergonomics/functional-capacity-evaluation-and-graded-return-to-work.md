# Functional capacity evaluation and graded return to work

**Goal:** match what the person can safely do to what the job requires, then close the
gap with a timed, agreed, graded return plan and workplace adjustments.

## 1. Understand the job
Do a **job demands analysis**: tasks, frequency (occasional/frequent/constant), weights
lifted and carried, postures, shift pattern, cognitive and interpersonal demands, travel.
Get the job description and, ideally, observe the workplace or photos/videos.
Classify physical demand level using a recognised system, e.g. the US Dictionary of
Occupational Titles strength levels (sedentary, light, medium, heavy, very heavy).

## 2. Assess the person
- Medical status, restrictions from treating clinicians, red flags cleared.
- **Psychosocial (yellow) flags**: fear-avoidance, low expectation of return, low mood,
  poor workplace relationships; screening tools such as the **Orebro** questionnaire
  help predict delayed return.
- Worker's goals and the employer's capacity to adjust.

## 3. Functional capacity evaluation (FCE)
A standardised battery (several commercial systems exist) testing lifting, carrying,
pushing/pulling, positional tolerances (sitting, standing, kneeling, reaching), hand
function and endurance, usually over one or two days.
- Use **kinesiophysical** observation (body mechanics, accessory muscle use, heart rate)
  to judge safe maximum rather than only self-limited psychophysical end-points.
- Report **consistency of effort** across repeated and related tests; avoid labelling
  "malingering" - inconsistency has many causes (pain, fear, misunderstanding). Single
  "effort" tests (e.g. grip-curve patterns on a Jamar dynamometer) have limited validity
  in isolation.
- Compare results to job demands item by item; a one-day test predicts sustained
  capacity only partly.

## 4. Build a graded return-to-work plan
- Start below current tolerance and increase in planned steps (hours, then tasks), e.g.
  reduced hours on modified duties, increasing over several weeks to full duties.
- State for each phase: hours, tasks included/excluded, review date, who monitors,
  and what to do if symptoms flare.
- Agree with worker, employer and GP/occupational physician; in the UK this aligns with
  the fit note "may be fit for work" options (phased return, altered hours, amended
  duties, workplace adaptations). Legal duties (e.g. reasonable adjustments under UK
  Equality Act, ADA accommodations in the US) vary by jurisdiction.
- Work conditioning/hardening (graded, job-simulated exercise) can bridge large gaps.

## 5. Workstation and task ergonomics
- **Office/DSE:** feet supported, hips slightly above knees, elbows roughly at desk
  height, monitor about arm's length with top line at or slightly below eye level,
  keyboard and mouse close; regular posture changes and breaks.
- **Manual tasks:** screen postural risk with **RULA** (upper limb) or **REBA** (whole
  body); assess lifting with the **NIOSH lifting equation** (recommended weight limit
  from a load constant adjusted by horizontal/vertical distance, travel, asymmetry,
  frequency and coupling). Prefer engineering controls (lift aids, height adjustment)
  over training alone.

## Gotchas
- Return delayed is harder: likelihood of returning falls the longer someone is off work;
  early contact with the employer matters.
- An FCE is a snapshot; medications, sleep and test day pain affect results.
- Plans fail when line managers are not briefed; give them a one-page plan.
- Confidentiality: share functional capacity, not diagnosis, with employers unless
  consent is explicit.

## Verify
- [ ] Job demands documented with frequencies and weights.
- [ ] Medical clearance and psychosocial flags assessed.
- [ ] FCE findings compared to job demands, with effort consistency described neutrally.
- [ ] Written graded plan with phases, review dates and flare-up plan, agreed by all parties.
- [ ] Ergonomic risks scored and controls recommended (engineering before training).
