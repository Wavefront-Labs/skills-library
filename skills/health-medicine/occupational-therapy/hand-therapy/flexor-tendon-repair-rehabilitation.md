# Flexor tendon repair rehabilitation

**Goal:** protect the repair from rupture while moving the tendon enough to limit
adhesions, then restore active range, grip and functional use on a staged timeline
agreed with the surgeon.

## 1. Gather the surgical facts before the first session
- **Zone** (Verdan/IFSSH zones I-V; zone II = "no man's land" within the
  fibro-osseous sheath, highest adhesion risk; thumb zones T I-T III).
- Tendons repaired (FDP, FDS, both), **strand count** of core suture and epitendinous
  suture, pulley venting, associated nerve/digital artery repair, fractures.
- Surgeon's preferred protocol and any restrictions. Repair strength and tissue quality
  decide how aggressive early motion can be.

## 2. Fabricate the protective orthosis
A forearm-based **dorsal blocking orthosis** (thermoplastic), typically:
- Wrist in neutral to slight flexion (some protocols allow slight extension).
- MCP joints in flexion (commonly cited around 40-70 degrees).
- IP joints allowed to extend fully to the hood (prevents flexion contracture).
Exact angles vary by protocol and surgeon; follow the agreed regimen. Worn full-time,
usually around 6 weeks. Check pressure areas, nerve repair tension and fit at each visit.

## 3. Choose the early motion regimen

| Regimen | Principle | Typical candidate |
|---|---|---|
| **Early active motion** (e.g. controlled active motion, place-and-hold, "Saint John" / relative motion variants) | Gentle active flexion within orthosis from first days | Strong multi-strand repair, reliable patient |
| **Early passive motion** (Kleinert, Duran-type) | Passive flexion, active extension to hood | Weaker repair, associated injuries, surgeon preference |
| **Immobilisation** | Static for ~3-4 weeks | Children, unable to comply, complex reconstruction |

Common early components: passive flexion warm-up to reduce resistance; active or
place-and-hold flexion to a partial fist; active IP extension within the orthosis;
oedema control (elevation, compression wraps); frequent short sessions (hourly is
common). Avoid simultaneous wrist and finger extension (overstretches the repair).

## 4. Progress by time and by tendon glide
Approximate stages (confirm against local protocol):
- **0-~4 weeks:** protected motion as above.
- **~4-6 weeks:** add tenodesis and tendon-gliding exercises (hook, straight, full fist);
  if active flexion lags badly (adhesions), progression may be earlier with surgeon input.
- **~6-8 weeks:** discontinue orthosis; light functional use; blocking exercises;
  address extension deficit (gentle passive extension, possibly a night extension orthosis).
- **~8-12 weeks:** graded strengthening and resistive grip; return to heavy work usually
  around 12 weeks or later.

Measure **total active motion (TAM)** and compare passive vs active range: a large
active-passive gap suggests adhesion; a sudden loss of active flexion suggests rupture.
Report outcomes with a recognised system (e.g. Strickland or ASSH TAM classification).

## Gotchas
- **Rupture risk** is highest in the early weeks while the repair is weakest; an abrupt
  loss of active flexion or a "pop" needs same-day surgical review.
- Patients remove orthoses to wash or work; teach and document safe hygiene technique.
- Associated digital nerve repair changes positioning and limits extension.
- Smokers, crush injuries and delayed repair have worse outcomes; set expectations.
- Children rarely comply with early active protocols; casts or immobilisation are usual.

## Verify
- [ ] Zone, tendons, repair type and surgeon's protocol documented.
- [ ] Orthosis angles recorded and checked for fit, pressure and nerve tension.
- [ ] Patient demonstrates exercises correctly and states rupture warning signs.
- [ ] TAM and active-passive gap recorded each review; progression justified.
- [ ] Strengthening and return to heavy use deferred until protocol timeline allows.
