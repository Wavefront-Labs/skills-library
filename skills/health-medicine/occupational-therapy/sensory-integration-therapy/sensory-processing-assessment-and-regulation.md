# Sensory processing assessment and regulation

**Goal:** understand how a person's sensory processing affects participation, and
reduce that impact through regulation strategies, environmental change and, where
appropriate, clinic-based Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI).

## 1. Start from participation
Identify the occupations affected: meltdowns at transitions, refusing to wear clothes,
avoiding playground equipment, poor attention in class, restricted diet, sleep. Ask
when, where and with whom it happens; record triggers and what helps.

## 2. Assess sensory processing

| Tool | What it gives |
|---|---|
| **Sensory Profile 2** (Dunn) | Caregiver/teacher questionnaire; quadrants of seeking, avoiding, sensitivity, registration |
| **Sensory Processing Measure (SPM-2)** | Home, school and other settings; praxis and social participation scales |
| **SIPT** / **EASI** | Performance-based tests of sensory integration and praxis; require certified training |
| Clinical observations | Postural control, ocular-motor, bilateral coordination, praxis, responses to movement and touch |
| Adolescent/Adult Sensory Profile | Self-report for older clients and adults |

Interpret patterns, not single items. Distinguish **modulation** problems (over- or
under-responsivity, seeking) from **discrimination** and **praxis** (motor planning)
problems; they need different approaches.

## 3. Build a formulation
Link the pattern to behaviour in context: e.g. auditory over-responsivity plus a noisy
dining hall explains lunchtime refusal. Consider alternative or co-occurring explanations
(anxiety, pain, communication difficulty, trauma, sleep deprivation, medical problems).

## 4. Intervene
**Environmental adaptation** (often first): reduce noise (ear defenders, quiet space),
lighting changes, seating options, predictable routines, visual schedules, warning
before transitions.

**Self-regulation programmes:** e.g. Zones of Regulation or the Alert Program, teaching
the person to recognise their state and choose a strategy.

**Sensory strategies / "sensory diet":** a planned set of activities scheduled through
the day, e.g. heavy work and proprioceptive input (carrying, pushing, wall push-ups)
before seated work; calming deep pressure; movement breaks. Plan with the family and
school, tie to known trigger times, and review against agreed goals.

**Ayres Sensory Integration (clinic-based):** individually tailored, play-based,
child-directed activity with suspended equipment, providing graded vestibular,
proprioceptive and tactile challenges in a "just-right" challenge. Evidence is
strongest when delivered with fidelity (ASI Fidelity Measure) and measured with
individual goals (e.g. Goal Attainment Scaling) - mostly in autistic children.

## Gotchas
- "Sensory integration therapy" is used loosely; isolated weighted vests, brushing
  protocols or passive sensory input are not ASI and have weak or mixed evidence. Be
  explicit about what is being delivered.
- Safety: weighted items must follow local guidance (weight limits, supervision, never
  for restraint); suspended equipment needs safe set-up and matting.
- A sensory explanation must not delay assessment for medical, safeguarding or mental
  health causes.
- Questionnaires reflect the respondent's perception; triangulate home and school.
- Respect neurodiversity: aim for participation and comfort, not suppression of
  harmless stimming.

## Verify
- [ ] Participation problems and goals defined before assessing sensory processing.
- [ ] Standardised measure(s) used, scored per manual, triangulated across settings.
- [ ] Formulation considers non-sensory explanations.
- [ ] Strategies are specific, scheduled and reviewed against goals.
- [ ] ASI, if used, delivered by trained therapist with fidelity and outcome measurement.
