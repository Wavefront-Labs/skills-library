# Recognising dying and care in the last days of life

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement. Anticipatory medicine choices and doses must follow your current local end-of-life prescribing guidance and formulary; verification and certification of death follow local law.*

**Goal:** notice when someone is probably entering the last days of life, say so
gently and clearly to them and those close to them, and refocus everything on comfort,
dignity and what matters to them — there is only one chance to get this right.

## 1. Recognising that someone may be dying
In a person with a progressive illness, signs that often appear over days include:
increasing weakness and time spent asleep, reduced interest in food and drink,
difficulty swallowing medicines, reduced awareness, changes in breathing pattern,
cool or mottled peripheries, and reduced urine output.
- **Look for reversible causes first** (infection, hypercalcaemia, opioid toxicity,
  dehydration, hypoglycaemia) when treating them fits the person's goals.
- Uncertainty is normal. Say "I think she may be dying, possibly within days" rather
  than waiting for certainty; reassess at least daily — some people stabilise.
- Make it a **senior, team decision** where possible, documented with the reasoning.

## 2. Talking with the person and family
- Use plain, kind words: "I'm sorry, but I think he is now dying." Check what they have
  noticed; many families already sense it.
- Explain what changes they might see (sleeping more, less eating and drinking, changes in breathing,
  noisier breathing) and that these are part of the natural process and usually not distressing to the person.
- Ask about wishes: place of care, people to contact, religious or cultural practices,
  how they want to be updated (including at night).
- Offer practical support: open visiting, somewhere to rest, a named nurse.

## 3. Review and de-escalate interventions
Stop what no longer helps comfort, explaining why:
- Routine observations, blood tests, blood glucose monitoring (simplify rather than stop
  abruptly in insulin-dependent diabetes), and non-essential medicines (statins,
  antihypertensives, many others).
- Review implantable cardioverter-defibrillators: **deactivate shocks** after discussion.
- Confirm a resuscitation decision is documented.
- **Hydration and nutrition:** support oral intake if wanted and safe; consider clinically
  assisted hydration case by case — discuss benefits and burdens with family; neither starting
  nor withholding should be automatic. Good mouth care relieves thirst more than fluids.

## 4. Anticipatory ("just in case") prescribing
Prescribe as-needed subcutaneous medicines **in advance** for the common symptoms so
nobody waits for a prescriber:
| Symptom | Drug class (per local guidance) |
|---|---|
| Pain / breathlessness | Opioid (dose depends on current opioid use and renal function) |
| Agitation / distress | Benzodiazepine (e.g. midazolam), ± antipsychotic |
| Nausea / vomiting | Antiemetic |
| Respiratory secretions | Antimuscarinic |
- Convert regular oral medicines to a **subcutaneous syringe driver** when swallowing fails.
- Doses must come from local guidance and be individualised; check opioid conversion with a pharmacist.

## 5. Comfort care in practice
- Position for comfort; pressure-area care at a frequency that does not disturb.
- Mouth care regularly (moist swabs, lip balm). Eye care.
- **Noisy breathing** from secretions: repositioning first; explain to family that it is
  usually not distressing to the person; antimuscarinics may help if started early.
- **Restlessness:** check for pain, full bladder, constipation, discomfort; then medicines.
- Continue spiritual and emotional support; hearing may persist, so encourage family to talk.

## 6. Care after death
- Verification of death by an authorised professional per local policy; record time.
- Allow family time; respect cultural and religious practices for care of the body.
- Personal care of the body with dignity; tags/identification per policy; deactivate devices.
- Notify the GP/primary team and other services; cancel appointments and deliveries.
- Know when a death must be reported to the coroner/medical examiner (rules vary).
- Offer bereavement information and support; check on staff as well.

## Gotchas
- Not recognising dying leads to burdensome tests and missed goodbyes.
- No anticipatory medicines = crises at night and weekends.
- "Stopping treatment" is never "stopping care" — say so explicitly.

## Verify
- [ ] Recognition of dying documented with reasoning and daily review.
- [ ] Family informed, questions answered, wishes and contacts recorded.
- [ ] Non-beneficial interventions stopped; ICD shocks addressed; resuscitation status clear.
- [ ] Anticipatory medicines prescribed for pain, agitation, nausea and secretions per local guidance.
- [ ] Mouth care, positioning and hydration plan in place.
- [ ] Care-after-death and notification steps completed; bereavement support offered.
