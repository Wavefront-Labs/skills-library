# Opioid titration, rotation and symptom control in serious illness

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement. Opioid conversion ratios are approximate and differ between references; all doses, conversions and syringe-driver combinations must be checked against a current palliative formulary (e.g. PCF, local guidelines) and with a specialist palliative care team or pharmacist.*

**Goal:** relieve suffering promptly and safely: treat each symptom by its likely cause,
titrate to effect, anticipate side effects, and review often, because needs change quickly
in advancing illness.

## 1. Assess before you prescribe
- Ask the person (or observe, if they cannot report): site, character, timing, what helps,
  impact on sleep and function, and what *they* hope for (comfort vs alertness trade-offs).
- **Mechanism:** nociceptive (somatic, visceral), **neuropathic** (burning, shooting, allodynia),
  bone, incident (movement-related), or total pain — psychological, social and spiritual
  distress amplify physical pain and need their own attention.
- Look for treatable causes: fracture, cord compression, bowel obstruction, urinary retention, constipation.

## 2. Opioid titration principles
- Start low in opioid-naive patients and frail/elderly; use **regular dosing plus an
  as-needed (breakthrough) dose**, commonly around 1/10 to 1/6 of the 24-hour regular dose (check local guidance).
- Review every 24 h: add up breakthrough doses used, fold a proportion into the regular
  dose (typical increments 30-50%, unit practice varies), keep the breakthrough dose in step.
- **Renal impairment:** morphine metabolites accumulate (drowsiness, myoclonus, confusion).
  Prefer agents with less renal dependence per local guidance and reduce dose/frequency.
- Prescribe a **laxative with every regular opioid** and an antiemetic available for the first days.

## 3. Opioid toxicity — recognise early
Drowsiness, new confusion or hallucinations, **myoclonic jerks**, pinpoint pupils,
slowed breathing. Reduce or hold the dose, hydrate if appropriate, look for the cause
(renal decline, rapid escalation, interactions). Reserve naloxone for life-threatening
respiratory depression and titrate small doses to avoid precipitating severe pain and withdrawal.

## 4. Rotation and conversion
Rotate when there is dose-limiting toxicity with inadequate relief, a route change
(oral → subcutaneous → transdermal), or renal decline.
1. Calculate the total 24 h dose of the current opioid **including breakthrough doses used**.
2. Convert with a **current equianalgesic table** — ratios are approximate.
3. **Reduce the calculated dose** (often 25-50%) for incomplete cross-tolerance when switching
   between different opioids, unless pain is severe; reassess within 24 h.
4. Transdermal fentanyl/buprenorphine: slow onset and offset (~12-24 h). Not for unstable pain
   or titration. Overlap/bridging per local guidance; heat increases absorption.
5. **Methadone** has a long, variable half-life and non-linear conversion — specialist-only.

## 5. Adjuvants and non-opioid options
Paracetamol; NSAIDs with care (renal, GI, bleeding); neuropathic agents (gabapentinoids,
antidepressants) — titrate slowly; corticosteroids for liver capsule pain, nerve compression,
raised intracranial pressure; radiotherapy for bone pain; nerve blocks/interventions for selected
pain; and non-drug measures (positioning, heat/cold, psychological support).

## 6. Other common symptoms — treat by cause
- **Breathlessness:** treat reversible causes (effusion, anaemia, infection, heart failure);
  fan to face, positioning, breathing techniques, calm environment; low-dose opioids reduce
  the sensation of breathlessness; benzodiazepines for associated anxiety. Oxygen helps only if hypoxaemic.
- **Nausea/vomiting:** match antiemetic to mechanism — chemical/drug-induced (central-acting
  dopamine antagonists), gastric stasis (prokinetic, unless obstruction), raised intracranial
  pressure (steroids, cyclizine-type), vestibular/movement, bowel obstruction (specialist plan).
- **Delirium/agitation:** look for reversible causes (drugs, retention, constipation,
  hypercalcaemia, infection, hypoxia); non-drug measures first; antipsychotics for distressing symptoms.
- **Subcutaneous syringe driver (CSCI):** when the oral route fails; check drug
  compatibility in a current reference before mixing.

## When to refer to specialist palliative care
Pain uncontrolled after two titration steps or one rotation, neuropathic or incident pain
not responding, complex renal/hepatic dosing, suspected opioid-induced hyperalgesia,
methadone/ketamine consideration, and any distressing symptom you cannot control within 24-48 h.

## Gotchas
- Missing a dose of a regular opioid causes avoidable crises — prescribe rescue doses.
- Patch + fever, heat pads or hot baths can produce toxicity.
- "Allergy to morphine" is usually nausea or itch — clarify before rotating.
- Families often fear opioids hasten death; appropriately titrated opioids for symptoms do not.

## Verify
- [ ] Pain mechanism and patient goals documented.
- [ ] Regular + breakthrough opioid, laxative and antiemetic prescribed.
- [ ] 24 h breakthrough use reviewed and regular dose adjusted.
- [ ] Renal function checked; toxicity signs assessed.
- [ ] Conversions and syringe-driver mixes double-checked against a current formulary.
- [ ] Specialist palliative care referral made where criteria met.
