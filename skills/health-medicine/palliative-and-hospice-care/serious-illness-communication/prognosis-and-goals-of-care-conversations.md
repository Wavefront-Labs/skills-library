# Prognosis and goals-of-care conversations

*General professional reference on communication skills. Law and policy on capacity, surrogates and treatment decisions vary by jurisdiction; follow local guidance and seek ethics or legal advice when needed.*

**Goal:** help a seriously ill person and those close to them understand where things
are heading, say what matters most to them, and shape care around that — while being
honest, kind and clear. Good conversations are a clinical skill that can be learned and practised.

## 1. Prepare (5 minutes that change the conversation)
- Know the medical facts and the realistic options; agree the message with the team so
  the family does not hear different stories.
- Private room, seats, phones silenced, enough time, interpreter booked (a professional,
  not a family member) and the right people present — ask the patient whom they want there.
- Decide your **headline**: one plain sentence about the situation.

## 2. A reliable structure (SPIKES / REMAP blend)
1. **Set up** and **ask what they understand**: "What have the doctors told you so far?"
   "What is your sense of how things are going?"
2. **Ask how much they want to know**: some want numbers, some want the big picture,
   some want family to hear details. "Some people like lots of detail, others prefer
   the overview. What's right for you?"
3. **Give the headline**, then pause: "I'm afraid the scans show the cancer has grown
   despite treatment." Short sentences. No jargon. Then **stop talking**.
4. **Respond to emotion** before adding information. Silence is a tool. Use NURSE:
   **N**ame ("This is a lot to take in"), **U**nderstand, **R**espect, **S**upport
   ("We'll keep caring for you whatever happens"), **E**xplore ("Tell me more about what's worrying you").
   Information delivered into strong emotion is not heard.
5. **Map the future and explore values**: "If time were shorter than we hope, what
   would be most important to you?" "What are your biggest worries?" "What abilities are so
   important that you can't imagine living without them?" "How much are you willing to go through for the possibility of more time?"
6. **Recommend** a plan that fits their stated values: "Given what you've told me
   matters most — being at home and comfortable — I'd recommend…" Making a recommendation
   relieves families of the burden of choosing alone; they can still disagree.
7. **Summarise and next steps**; check understanding with ask-tell-ask.

## 3. Talking about prognosis and uncertainty
- Use ranges, not single numbers: "weeks to months", "hours to days". Tie to trajectory:
  "If things keep changing at the pace we've seen…"
- **Hope and worry** together: "I hope the treatment holds things steady. I also worry that
  time may be short, and I want us to be prepared." This respects hope without misleading.
- Acknowledge uncertainty honestly; do not use it to avoid the conversation.
- "I wish" statements help when the person asks for something medicine cannot give:
  "I wish we had a treatment that would cure this."

## 4. Family meetings
- Pre-meeting with the team: agree on facts, recommendation, and who leads.
- Introductions and the purpose; start with what the family understands.
- With a person who lacks capacity, guide the family to **substituted judgement**: "What
  would your mother say if she could join us?" — not "What do you want us to do?"
- Spend more time listening than talking.
- Close with a clear plan, the next update time, and a named contact.

## 5. Navigating conflict
Common roots: different information, mistrust, guilt, grief, cultural or religious
values, or unrealistic hope. Approaches: explore the meaning behind a request ("Tell me
what 'everything' means to you"), find shared goals ("We all want her to be comfortable"),
time-limited trials with clear review points, second opinions, and early involvement of
palliative care, chaplaincy, ethics or mediation. Clinicians are not obliged to offer
treatment that cannot achieve its goal, but how that is explained matters enormously.

## 6. Cultural humility
Ask, do not assume: "How do you and your family usually make medical decisions?" Confirm early
the patient's own wishes about family-centred disclosure.

## Gotchas
- Ending with a menu of options and no recommendation leaves families carrying the decision alone.
- "Do you want us to do everything?" is a misleading question — everything for what goal?
- The conversation is a process, not a single event; revisit as things change.

## Verify
- [ ] Patient's understanding and information preferences explored before sharing news.
- [ ] Headline given in plain language, followed by space for emotion.
- [ ] At least one empathic response documented or observed.
- [ ] Goals, worries and trade-offs elicited in the person's own words.
- [ ] A recommendation offered and linked to their values.
- [ ] Conversation documented where the whole team can find it, including surrogate details.
