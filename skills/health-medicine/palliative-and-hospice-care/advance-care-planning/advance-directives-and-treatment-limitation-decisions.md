# Advance directives and treatment-limitation decisions

*General professional reference. The legal force of advance directives, proxy appointments and resuscitation orders differs between countries and states, and forms differ between regions — check your jurisdiction's law, local policy and approved forms.*

**Goal:** help people think ahead about the care they would and would not want, name
who should speak for them, and record it in a form that actually reaches the clinicians
at 3 a.m. in an emergency department.

## 1. What advance care planning is (and is not)
ACP is an ongoing **process** of understanding a person's values, goals and preferences
for future care, sharing them with family and clinicians, and revisiting them as health
changes. It is **not** a single form, and not only about resuscitation. The best outcome
is a well-prepared proxy who knows the person's values.

## 2. When to start
- Any adult can do basic planning (naming a proxy).
- Prompt it at **trigger points**: a new serious diagnosis, progression, an unplanned
  admission, frailty or care-home move, starting dialysis or ventilatory support, or when
  the "surprise question" applies ("Would I be surprised if this person died in the next 12 months?").
- Earlier is better: capacity can be lost suddenly (stroke, delirium).

## 3. The conversation
1. **Values first:** what makes life worthwhile, what worries them, what trade-offs they
   would accept (see the serious-illness conversation skill,
   `health-medicine.palliative-and-hospice-care.serious-illness-communication.prognosis-and-goals-of-care-conversations`).
2. **Name a proxy:** who, and does that person know and accept the role? Encourage the
   person to tell them their wishes directly. Formal appointment (healthcare proxy,
   power of attorney for health, lasting power of attorney) follows local legal process.
3. **Specific treatments** in the context of their likely illness course: hospital
   admission, intensive care, ventilation, dialysis, artificial nutrition, antibiotics,
   CPR, preferred place of care and death.
4. **Document and share.**

## 4. Types of documents (names vary by jurisdiction)
| Document | Who completes | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Advance statement / values statement | Person | Wishes and values; guides decisions, usually not legally binding |
| Advance directive / living will / advance decision to refuse treatment | Person with capacity | Refuses specified treatment in specified circumstances; may be legally binding if valid and applicable (formal requirements apply, especially for life-sustaining treatment) |
| Proxy appointment (healthcare power of attorney / lasting power of attorney) | Person with capacity | Names a decision-maker; scope set by law and document |
| Portable medical order (POLST/MOLST, ReSPECT, treatment escalation plan) | Clinician with patient/proxy | Actionable clinical orders/recommendations that travel across settings |
| DNACPR / DNR order | Clinician | Specific to CPR only |

## 5. Resuscitation and ceilings of care
- A DNACPR decision is **only about CPR**. It does not mean "no treatment": antibiotics,
  fluids, symptom relief, even ICU may still be appropriate. Write the ceiling of care separately.
- Where CPR would not work (clinically futile), clinicians are generally not obliged to
  offer it, but in many jurisdictions they must **inform** the patient (or those close
  to them) of the decision unless doing so would cause harm — check local legal duties.
- Where CPR might work but carries burdens, it is a shared decision based on the person's values.
- Describe CPR honestly and gently: what it involves, realistic chances of survival and
  of returning to previous function, given their condition.

## 6. Capacity and when the person cannot decide
Assess capacity for the specific decision at the specific time (understand, retain,
weigh, communicate — test wording varies by jurisdiction). Without capacity: follow a
valid applicable directive, involve the legal proxy, and otherwise decide in the person's
**best interests** or by **substituted judgement**, depending on the jurisdiction.

## 7. Making plans portable
Copies to the patient, proxy, GP/primary care and hospital record; electronic shared
care records where available; a recognised form kept at home; review at each transition.

## Gotchas
- A directive found too late is no directive: check the record at every admission.
- Vague wording ("no heroic measures") is hard to apply; tie wishes to situations.
- Families may not know about a directive; disagreement about it needs early, careful discussion.
- Never let DNACPR quietly become "no escalation" without a documented decision.

## Verify
- [ ] Values and goals recorded in the person's own words.
- [ ] Proxy named, aware of the role and contact details recorded.
- [ ] Resuscitation decision and ceiling of care documented separately and clearly.
- [ ] Capacity assessment documented for any decision made.
- [ ] Documents on the correct local forms, shared across settings, with a review date.
