# Pediatric palliative care: parallel planning and family-centred care

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement. Children's medicine doses are weight- and age-based and differ from adult practice: always use a current paediatric palliative formulary (e.g. APPM) and specialist advice.*

**Goal:** add an extra layer of support for a child with a life-limiting or
life-threatening condition and their whole family — alongside, not instead of,
treatment aimed at cure or life extension — and plan for every likely path, so the
family is never caught unprepared.

## 1. Who it is for
Children's palliative care differs from adult care: many conditions are rare, non-cancer,
and have long, unpredictable courses over years. One widely used grouping (Together for Short Lives):
1. Conditions where curative treatment may be feasible but can fail (e.g. some cancers, organ failure).
2. Conditions where premature death is inevitable but long periods of normal activity are possible (e.g. some muscular dystrophies).
3. Progressive conditions without curative options, treatment palliative from diagnosis (e.g. some metabolic disorders).
4. Irreversible, non-progressive conditions with complex needs (e.g. severe cerebral palsy).
Perinatal palliative care may begin before birth, after an antenatal diagnosis.

**Refer at diagnosis of a life-limiting condition**, not only at end of life.

## 2. Parallel planning
Families can hold two plans at once: "We hope for the best, and we also plan for the worst."
- Plan A: the treatment pathway and its goals.
- Plan B: what happens if things get worse — preferred place of care, which interventions
  would and would not help, symptom plans, people to call.
- Record it in a child/family advance care plan that travels between hospital, hospice,
  school and home; review at each change in condition.
- Concurrent care (curative and palliative together) is the norm in children; in the US,
  children can receive hospice while continuing disease-directed treatment (federal provision).

## 3. Communicating with children
- Match to developmental stage: young children may see death as temporary; by around
  school age many understand it is permanent; adolescents think abstractly and value autonomy.
- Children often know more than adults think and may protect their parents by not asking.
  Ask parents how their child communicates and what they already know; offer to help
  parents talk with the child rather than excluding them.
- Seek the child's **assent** and listen to their views; adolescents may want to be part of
  decisions and to plan things that matter (legacy, friends, school).
- Use play specialists, child-life specialists and psychologists.

## 4. Symptom management in children
- **Pain assessment** by developmental stage: observational tools (e.g. FLACC) for pre-verbal
  or non-verbal children, faces scales for young children, numeric scales for older children.
  Parents are often the best interpreters of their child's distress.
- Children with neurological impairment have distinctive problems: neuro-irritability,
  spasticity, seizures, secretions, feeding intolerance, gut dysmotility, visceral hyperalgesia.
- Principles mirror adults (assess cause, regular plus as-needed, anticipate side effects,
  non-drug measures) but **dosing is per kg**, formulations differ, and routes matter
  (buccal, intranasal, gastrostomy, subcutaneous).

## 5. The whole family
- **Siblings:** age-appropriate information, inclusion, attention to school and their own
  grief; they may feel guilt or jealousy. Sibling support groups help.
- Parents: practical help, respite (children's hospices often offer respite stays),
  financial advice, care coordination with a named key worker, attention to the parents' relationship and mental health.
- Cultural, spiritual and religious needs; grandparents and extended family.
- **Memory-making** (handprints, photographs, recordings) offered sensitively, not imposed.
- Bereavement support continuing long after death, including for siblings and school communities.

## 6. Ethical and team issues
Decisions to limit life-sustaining treatment in children involve parents and, as
appropriate, the child; disagreement may need second opinions, ethics review, mediation,
or courts in rare cases. Staff caring for dying children also need support and debriefing.

## Red flags — specialist input now
Uncontrolled pain or seizures, rapid deterioration, family–team disagreement about
goals, safeguarding concerns, a family without a plan for a predictable crisis.

## Gotchas
- Waiting for "end of life" to refer misses years of possible support.
- Adult conversion tables and doses do not apply to children.
- Protecting a child from all information can leave them alone with their fears.

## Verify
- [ ] Condition category and referral at diagnosis documented.
- [ ] Parallel plan written, shared across settings and dated for review.
- [ ] Child's developmental stage, voice and assent considered.
- [ ] Pain assessed with an age-appropriate tool; doses checked against a paediatric palliative formulary.
- [ ] Siblings, parents and key worker included in the plan.
- [ ] Bereavement support arranged for the family.
