# Hospice eligibility and home crisis planning

*General professional reference. Hospice eligibility, funding and prescribing rules are country- and payer-specific (e.g. the US Medicare Hospice Benefit vs UK NHS-funded hospice and community services) — check the current rules that apply to you. Crisis-kit contents and doses must follow local guidance.*

**Goal:** identify people who would benefit from hospice or community palliative care
early enough to help, and make home a safe place to be ill — so that a symptom at 2 a.m.
is managed by a plan, not an ambulance.

## 1. Identify people early
- **Surprise question:** "Would I be surprised if this person died in the next 12 months?"
  If not, consider palliative needs. Sensitive but imperfect — use it as a prompt, not a test.
- Structured tools: **SPICT** or **Gold Standards Framework** indicators (general decline,
  repeated unplanned admissions, weight loss, dependence, plus disease-specific indicators
  for cancer, heart, lung, kidney, liver, neurological disease, frailty and dementia).
- Function scales track trajectory: **Palliative Performance Scale (PPS)**, Karnofsky,
  ECOG. Falling scores over weeks are more informative than a single value.

## 2. Hospice eligibility (example: US Medicare Hospice Benefit)
- Two physicians certify a terminal illness with **life expectancy of 6 months or less if
  the illness runs its normal course**; the person elects hospice and agrees to forgo
  curative treatment for the terminal diagnosis (concurrent care rules differ for children).
- Disease-specific guidance (local coverage determinations) lists supporting criteria
  — function, weight loss, recurrent infections, organ-specific markers. Recertification occurs
  at defined benefit periods; a person can be discharged if they stabilise and re-elect later.
- Prognosis is uncertain: certification needs reasonable clinical judgement, not certainty.
- Outside the US, access is usually by **need** rather than prognosis; check local referral criteria.
- Common barrier: referral too late (days before death). Refer when needs appear.

## 3. Structuring a home visit
1. **Symptoms:** systematic review (pain, breathlessness, nausea, bowels, mouth, skin,
   sleep, mood, confusion). Use a validated scale (e.g. IPOS/ESAS) to track change.
2. **Medicines:** what is actually taken vs prescribed; simplify; check supplies will last
   through weekends and holidays.
3. **Function and safety:** mobility, falls, equipment (bed, commode, pressure mattress),
   home oxygen safety (no smoking).
4. **Carer:** who provides care, how they are coping, sleep, respite needs, their own health.
5. **Goals and plans:** preferred place of care and death, advance care plan status,
   resuscitation documentation present **in the home**.
6. **What to do if…:** agree contact numbers and the first actions for likely crises.

## 4. Crisis medication kits (comfort kits / just-in-case boxes)
- Prescribe and **deliver to the home in advance** small supplies of as-needed medicines
  for pain, breathlessness, agitation, nausea and secretions, usually subcutaneous, with
  administration authorisation/charts so visiting nurses can give them.
- Include equipment (syringes, needles, sharps bin) and clear instructions.
- Store safely; controlled-drug records and disposal after death per local law.
- Doses and drug choices per local guidance; review when needs change.

## 5. Out-of-hours planning
- Share a concise summary with out-of-hours services and ambulance: diagnosis, goals,
  ceiling of care, resuscitation decision, medicines and kit location, key contacts.
- Give the family **one number to call first** (hospice or community team) rather than emergency services,
  unless they want hospital care for that situation.
- Anticipate predictable crises: uncontrolled pain, bleeding risk, seizures, breathlessness,
  hypercalcaemia, spinal cord compression, blocked catheter, and what to do for each.
- For people at risk of major bleeding: dark towels at home and a crisis medicine plan, discussed sensitively.

## 6. Supporting carers
Practical training (repositioning, giving medicines, mouth care), respite care options,
financial and benefits advice, emotional support, and bereavement follow-up. Carer
breakdown is a common reason for unwanted admission at the end of life.

## Gotchas
- Medicines in the house but no signed authorisation = nurse cannot give them.
- The resuscitation form in the hospital notes does not help the ambulance crew at home.
- Hospice discharge after stabilising can feel like abandonment; explain it as good news and keep a plan.

## Verify
- [ ] Palliative needs identified with a structured tool; trajectory documented.
- [ ] Eligibility checked against current local/payer rules; referral made when needs appeared.
- [ ] Home visit covered symptoms, medicines, safety, carer and plans.
- [ ] Crisis medicines and authorisation in the home; staff know where.
- [ ] Out-of-hours summary shared; family has a first-call number.
- [ ] Carer support and respite discussed.
