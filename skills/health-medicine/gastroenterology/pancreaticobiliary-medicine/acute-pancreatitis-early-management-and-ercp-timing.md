# Acute pancreatitis: early management (NICE NG104)

> General clinical reference, not patient-specific advice. Fluid volumes, analgesia and local pathways must follow current guidelines and formulary. ERCP timing is not covered by NG104; use current specialist guidance.

**Goal:** confirm the diagnosis, find the cause, recognise severity, feed early, avoid
needless antimicrobials, and involve a specialist centre for complications.

## 1. Diagnosis
Typical presentation is sudden-onset abdominal pain, often with nausea and vomiting and a
history of gallstones or heavy alcohol use; signs include epigastric tenderness, fever and
tachycardia. Confirm with blood lipase or amylase, usually raised (raised levels can occur in
other conditions). If levels are not raised, abdominal CT may confirm pancreatic inflammation.

## 2. Find the cause
- Do not assume it is alcohol-related just because the person drinks.
- If gallstones and alcohol are excluded, look for metabolic causes (hypercalcaemia,
  hyperlipidaemia), prescription drugs, microlithiasis, hereditary causes, autoimmune
  pancreatitis, ampullary or pancreatic tumours, and anatomical anomalies (pancreas divisum).

## 3. Severity terms (revised Atlanta, as used by NICE)
- **Moderately severe:** organ failure resolving within 48 hours, or local or systemic
  complications without persistent organ failure.
- **Severe:** single or multiple organ failure persisting more than 48 hours.

## 4. Early management
- **Antimicrobials:** do not offer prophylactic antimicrobials.
- **Fluids:** follow the NICE intravenous fluid therapy guidelines (adults; children and
  young people).
- **Nutrition:** do not make people nil-by-mouth or withhold food without a clear reason
  (for example vomiting). Offer enteral nutrition to severe or moderately severe disease,
  starting within 72 hours of presentation. Offer parenteral nutrition only if enteral
  nutrition has failed or is contraindicated.

## 5. Complications and referral
- Infected or suspected infected necrosis: offer an endoscopic approach when anatomically
  possible, a percutaneous approach otherwise; balance prompt debridement against the benefits
  of delay.
- Necrotic, infective, haemorrhagic or systemic complications: seek advice from a specialist
  pancreatic centre in the referral network and discuss transfer.
- Children: seek advice from paediatric gastroenterology or hepatology and a specialist
  pancreatic centre, and discuss transfer.

## 6. Talking to patients and families (severe disease)
Explain that a hospital stay of several months, including critical care, is relatively
common; recovery can take at least 3 times as long as the stay; local complications may take
weeks to declare and it can be safer to delay intervention so a collection can mature; relapse
can occur after starting to recover; NICE states approximately 15% to 20% of adults with
severe acute pancreatitis die in hospital.

## 7. After the acute episode
- Advise people with alcohol-related pancreatitis to stop drinking; for other recurrent
  disease, advise that alcohol might exacerbate it. Smoking is linked to chronic pancreatitis.
- Give written and verbal information on the condition, pain, nutrition (including enzyme
  replacement if needed), follow-up and psychological care.
- Chronic pancreatitis: NICE advises HbA1c at least every 6 months, bone density assessment
  every 2 years in adults, and monitoring for exocrine insufficiency and malnutrition at
  least every 12 months (every 6 months under 16); this information should reach the GP.

## Gotchas
- Lipase or amylase can be raised in other conditions.
- Persistent organ failure beyond 48 hours defines severe disease; reassess at that point.

## Verify
- [ ] Diagnosis supported by lipase or amylase (CT if not raised).
- [ ] Cause sought beyond alcohol and gallstones if neither explains it.
- [ ] No prophylactic antimicrobials; not nil-by-mouth without reason.
- [ ] Enteral nutrition started within 72 hours in moderately severe or severe disease.
- [ ] Specialist pancreatic centre contacted for necrotic, infective, haemorrhagic or systemic complications.
