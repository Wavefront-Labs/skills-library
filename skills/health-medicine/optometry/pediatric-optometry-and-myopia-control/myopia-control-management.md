# Myopia control management

**Goal:** slow axial elongation in progressing myopic (or pre-myopic) children to reduce lifetime risk of myopic maculopathy, retinal detachment, glaucoma and cataract, while keeping vision clear and the plan acceptable to the family.

## 1. Identify who needs it
- **Risk factors**: one or both parents myopic, early onset (younger age at onset means faster progression and higher final myopia), East Asian ethnicity, high near work, low time outdoors.
- **Pre-myopia**: less hyperopia than expected for age is a predictor; age-specific cut-offs are in IMI papers.
- Discuss with families at the first myopic prescription, not after years of progression.

## 2. Baseline measurements
1. **Cycloplegic refraction** (cyclopentolate is commonly used; agent and dose per local protocol/verified reference) to avoid over-minussing.
2. **Axial length** by optical biometry where available; this is the preferred progression metric because it is not affected by accommodation and responds to treatments that refraction may underreport.
3. Binocular vision and accommodation (lag, near phoria) can influence lens choice.
4. Dilated fundus exam, especially for higher myopes; document myopic changes.
5. Compare axial length with age- and sex-specific growth centile charts where available.

## 3. Options (evidence and availability vary by country and regulator)
| Intervention | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Low-dose atropine** | Effect appears dose-dependent; higher concentrations carry more photophobia, near blur and rebound. Formulation, strength and licensing differ by jurisdiction; prescribe per verified reference and local regulations |
| **Orthokeratology** | Overnight reverse-geometry rigid lens; slows axial growth; requires excellent hygiene; small but serious microbial keratitis risk |
| **Dual-focus / extended-depth-of-focus soft lenses** (e.g. daily disposable dual-focus designs) | Daytime wear; good for children who manage lens handling |
| **Myopia control spectacles** (DIMS, HAL/highly aspherical lenslets, contrast-modulating designs) | Non-invasive; must be worn full-time and well centred |
| **Outdoor time** | Supports prevention of onset (around two hours per day is a commonly cited target); smaller effect on progression |
| Undercorrection | Not recommended: does not slow, and may speed, progression |
| Single vision full correction | Standard of care for vision but not myopia control |

Combination therapy (e.g. atropine plus ortho-k or defocus lenses) is used for fast progressors; evidence is still developing.

## 4. Choose with the family
Weigh age and maturity, refraction and astigmatism, progression rate, lifestyle, cost, hygiene capacity, and family preference. Document informed consent, including off-label status where applicable.

## 5. Monitor
- Review about every 6 months: axial length, refraction (cycloplegic at least periodically), acuity, compliance and side effects (pupil size, near vision for atropine; corneal health for lenses).
- Judge response against expected untreated growth for age rather than expecting zero change; consider switching or combining if progression stays fast.
- Plan when to stop (often mid-to-late teens once progression is stable) and watch for rebound.

## Gotchas
- Non-cycloplegic refraction in children overestimates myopia.
- Ortho-k flattens the cornea, so refraction during treatment does not reflect progression: use axial length.
- Poorly centred or part-time-worn defocus spectacles lose their effect.
- Treatment efficacy percentages from trials do not translate directly into individual outcomes; set realistic expectations.

- [ ] Cycloplegic refraction and axial length at baseline.
- [ ] Options explained, including risks, cost and off-label status; consent documented.

- [ ] Stopping and rebound plan discussed.
