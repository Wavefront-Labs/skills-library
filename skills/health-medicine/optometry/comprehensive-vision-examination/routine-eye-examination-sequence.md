# Routine eye examination sequence

**Goal:** complete a safe, reproducible eye examination that answers three questions: can the patient see well enough for their needs, are the eyes healthy, and does anything need correction, monitoring or referral.

## 1. Case history (drives everything else)
- **Presenting complaint**: onset, duration, one or both eyes, distance vs near, constant vs intermittent.
- **Red flags to ask directly**: sudden vision loss, new flashes/floaters or a curtain, painful red eye, diplopia of recent onset, headache with visual symptoms, jaw claudication in older adults.
- **Ocular history**: previous correction, amblyopia/patching, surgery, trauma, glaucoma.
- **General health and medication**: diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune disease; drugs with ocular effects (hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids, tamsulosin, amiodarone).
- **Family history**: glaucoma, macular degeneration, strabismus, high myopia.
- **Visual tasks**: driving, occupation, screen use, hobbies.

## 2. Vision and preliminary tests (before any drops or bright light)
1. **Visual acuity** monocular then binocular, habitual correction, distance and near. Record logMAR (ETDRS) or Snellen; use **pinhole** if below expected — improvement suggests refractive cause.
2. **Pupils**: size, shape, direct/consensual reflexes, **swinging flashlight test for RAPD**. Do this before dilation.
3. **Cover test** distance and near, then **ocular motility** (H-pattern), noting diplopia or nystagmus.
4. **Confrontation fields** or a screening field, plus Amsler grid where macular disease is suspected.
5. Optional per presentation: colour vision (Ishihara), stereopsis, near point of convergence and accommodation.

## 3. Refraction
Objective (retinoscopy or autorefraction) then subjective refraction and near addition; see `health-medicine.optometry.refraction-and-prescribing.subjective-refraction-and-prescribing-decisions`.

## 4. Ocular health assessment
| Area | Method | Look for |
|---|---|---|
| Lids, lashes, conjunctiva | Slit lamp, diffuse | Blepharitis, lesions, injection |
| Cornea, tear film | Slit lamp + fluorescein, TBUT | Staining, dry eye, dystrophy |
| Anterior chamber angle | Van Herick before dilation; gonioscopy if narrow | Angle-closure risk |
| Lens | Retroillumination after dilation | Cataract type and grade |
| Fundus | Volk/indirect lens or direct ophthalmoscope | Disc (cup:disc, rim, haemorrhage), macula, vessels, periphery |

- **IOP**: Goldmann applanation is the reference; non-contact tonometry is a screen. Record time and method.
- **Dilation**: grade the angle first. Short-acting mydriatic per local protocol (commonly tropicamide); warn about driving and glare.

## 5. Record, explain, decide
- Document every test, including normal findings and what could not be done.
- Plan: prescription, recall interval (risk-based; jurisdictions differ), monitoring, or referral with stated urgency (emergency/same-day, urgent, routine) per local referral pathways.

## Gotchas
- Dilating before pupil testing or Van Herick loses the RAPD and angle assessment.
- Pinhole that does not improve acuity points to pathology or amblyopia, not refraction.
- Non-contact tonometry overreads with thick corneas and squeezing; confirm suspicious readings with applanation and pachymetry.
- An undilated direct ophthalmoscopy misses peripheral retinal breaks; dilate when flashes/floaters are reported.

## Verify
- [ ] Red-flag questions asked and documented.
- [ ] Acuity recorded per eye with correction type; pinhole used where reduced.
- [ ] Pupils and angle assessed before dilation.
- [ ] Disc, macula and periphery examined and described.
- [ ] IOP recorded with method and time.
- [ ] Clear plan with recall interval or referral urgency communicated to the patient.
