# Chronic ear disease and cholesteatoma surgery

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** separate the "safe" discharging ear from cholesteatoma, control infection
before operating, choose the operation that gives a dry, safe ear first and hearing
second, and know when hearing-restoration or implant pathways apply.

## 1. Classify the ear
- **Mucosal CSOM (inactive/active):** central perforation, mucoid discharge when active.
- **Squamous disease (cholesteatoma):** attic or posterosuperior retraction with keratin,
  marginal perforation, foul-smelling scanty discharge, granulation/polyp. Polyp in the
  canal = cholesteatoma until proven otherwise; don't avulse it in clinic (may sit on
  the facial nerve or stapes).
- Grade retraction pockets (e.g., Tos/Sadé), note whether the base is visible.

## 2. Medical control first
1. **Microsuction aural toilet** under the microscope — drops can't work on debris.
2. Topical quinolone (e.g., ofloxacin/ciprofloxacin drops) for active CSOM with a
   perforation; aminoglycoside drops carry ototoxicity concern with an open middle ear —
   follow local policy and counsel.
3. Water precautions; fungal otitis externa if white/black debris after antibiotics.
4. Persistent discharge despite this → culture, CT, consider surgery.

## 3. Investigations
- **Pure-tone audiogram** with masking (air–bone gap; bone thresholds = cochlear reserve).
- **CT temporal bone** (high-resolution): extent, ossicles, tegmen, lateral canal
  fistula, facial canal dehiscence, sigmoid/jugular anatomy — a surgical road map.
- **Non-EPI DWI MRI:** detects cholesteatoma (bright on DWI) down to ~3 mm — used for
  diagnosis in equivocal cases and as surveillance instead of routine second-look surgery.

## 4. Choosing the operation
| Situation | Typical procedure |
|---|---|
| Dry central perforation, normal ossicles | Myringoplasty/type I tympanoplasty (temporalis fascia or cartilage) |
| Perforation + ossicular erosion | Tympanoplasty + ossiculoplasty (PORP/TORP, incus interposition) |
| Limited cholesteatoma, good tube function | Canal wall up mastoidectomy ± staged second look or DWI surveillance |
| Extensive/recurrent, poor follow-up, only-hearing-ear issues | Canal wall down/modified radical; or CWU with obliteration |

- Facial nerve monitoring in revision/cholesteatoma surgery is widely used; know the
  tympanic segment course over the oval window.
- Cartilage grafts resist re-retraction better than fascia in poor-tube-function ears.

## 5. Hearing restoration pathways
- **Otosclerosis:** conductive loss, normal drum, absent stapedial reflexes. Options:
  hearing aid vs stapedotomy (small fenestra + piston). Counsel ~1% risk of dead ear.
  Operate the worse ear; never the only-hearing ear.
- **Bone-conduction implants:** chronic discharging ears, canal atresia, single-sided
  deafness where conventional aids fail.
- **Cochlear implant referral:** bilateral severe–profound SNHL with limited benefit
  from well-fitted aids; candidacy criteria differ by country (word-recognition or
  threshold criteria) — check the current local standard.

## Gotchas
- Operating on a wet ear: graft take falls; dry it first unless cholesteatoma forces it.
- Missing a lateral canal fistula: positive fistula test or CT erosion — leave matrix on
  the fistula until last or for a second stage.
- Vertigo or facial weakness in a cholesteatoma ear = complication, urgent imaging.
- Post-op audiograms should be compared on the same equipment; quote air–bone gap closure.

## Verify
- [ ] Ear classified mucosal vs squamous with otomicroscopy findings recorded.
- [ ] Audiogram with masking and HRCT reviewed before listing.
- [ ] Consent covers hearing loss, dead ear, taste change (chorda tympani), facial palsy, recurrence.
- [ ] Surveillance plan (DWI MRI or second look) documented for cholesteatoma.
