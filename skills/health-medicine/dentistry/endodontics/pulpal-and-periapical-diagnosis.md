# Pulpal and periapical diagnosis

**Goal:** a reproducible two-part diagnosis — one **pulpal** term plus one **apical** term — for
the correct tooth, so the treatment (monitor, vital pulp therapy, RCT, extraction) follows
from evidence rather than from where the patient points.

## 1. History first
- Character, onset, duration, what provokes and relieves it, night pain, analgesic response.
- **Stimulated pain that stops within seconds** of removing the stimulus suggests reversible
  pulpitis; **lingering** thermal pain (often quoted as more than ~30 s; definitions vary),
  spontaneous pain or pain waking the patient suggests irreversible pulpitis.
- Pain on biting/release, sharp and localised → think **cracked tooth**.
- Recent restorations, trauma, periodontal treatment, swelling, sinus tract, bad taste.
- Referred pain is common (across arches, upper↔lower same side); pulpal pain is poorly
  localised until inflammation reaches the periapical tissues.

## 2. Clinical tests (always include control teeth)
Test a contralateral and an adjacent healthy tooth **first** so the patient knows what
"normal" feels like, then the suspect tooth. Record each result.
1. **Cold** — refrigerant spray (e.g. tetrafluoroethane) on a cotton pellet, on the middle
   third of the buccal surface. Note response yes/no, intensity, and **lingering time**.
2. **Electric pulp test (EPT)** — dry tooth, conductive medium, incisal/cusp-tip third, avoid
   restorations and gingiva. A positive means nerve fibres respond; it does **not** measure
   blood supply or grade inflammation.
3. **Heat** — reserve for the complaint of heat-provoked pain; rubber dam and gutta-percha or
   a heated instrument, one tooth at a time.
4. **Percussion and palpation** — tenderness = apical tissue involvement.
5. **Bite test** (cusp-by-cusp, bite stick) — pain on release localises a crack.
6. **Periodontal probing and mobility** — an isolated narrow deep pocket suggests vertical
   root fracture or a sinus tract draining via the sulcus.
7. **Sinus tract** — trace with a gutta-percha cone and radiograph to find the source tooth.

## 3. Radiographs
- At least one **paralleling periapical**; a second at a different horizontal angle separates
  overlapping roots and canals (SLOB/same-lingual-opposite-buccal rule).
- Look for: depth of caries/restoration to pulp, PDL widening, lamina dura loss, periapical
  radiolucency or radiopacity, resorption, calcified canals, previous root fillings.
- Early apical periodontitis is often radiographically invisible — bone loss must reach the
  cortex to show. **Limited-field-of-view CBCT** is reserved for complex cases (suspected
  resorption, missed anatomy, unclear source, surgical planning).

## 4. Assign the AAE terms
**Pulpal:** normal pulp · reversible pulpitis · symptomatic irreversible pulpitis ·
asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis (e.g. deep caries likely to expose, no symptoms) ·
pulp necrosis · previously treated · previously initiated therapy.

**Apical:** normal apical tissues · symptomatic apical periodontitis (percussion/biting pain,
± radiolucency) · asymptomatic apical periodontitis (radiolucency, no symptoms) · chronic
apical abscess (sinus tract, little discomfort) · acute apical abscess (rapid onset,
swelling, pain, ± systemic signs) · condensing osteitis (diffuse radiopacity).

Example: "Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis with symptomatic apical periodontitis, LL6."

## 5. Map to treatment (broadly)
- Reversible pulpitis → remove cause, restore, review.
- Irreversible pulpitis → RCT or extraction; in selected cases with modern evidence, full
  pulpotomy (MTA/calcium silicate) is an option (indications vary — check current guidance).
- Necrosis/periapical disease → RCT or extraction. Acute abscess → drainage (through tooth or
  incision) plus source removal; antibiotics only for systemic involvement or spreading infection.

## Gotchas
- **False negatives:** recently traumatised teeth, immature apices, heavy calcification, large
  restorations/crowns, premedication with analgesics.
- **False positives:** anxious patients, contact with gingiva or metal restorations, liquefaction
  necrosis in multi-rooted teeth (one canal vital, another necrotic).
- Never diagnose from one test; never open a tooth the tests did not identify.
- A sinus tract opposite one tooth may drain from another — always trace it.
- Cracked teeth may test vital; the prognosis depends on crack extent, not just pulp status.

## Verify
- [ ] Control teeth tested before the suspect tooth, results recorded with lingering times.
- [ ] At least two different tests agree on the pulpal status.
- [ ] Periapical radiograph (± second angle) reviewed and reported.
- [ ] Diagnosis written as one pulpal plus one apical AAE term, with tooth number.
- [ ] Probing depths recorded to exclude a periodontal or fracture cause.
