# Impacted mandibular third molar removal

**Goal:** remove the tooth only when indicated, with the nerve risk assessed and consented,
through a flap and bone removal sized to the impaction, and manage the predictable sequelae.

## 1. Is removal indicated?
UK NICE TA1 advises **against prophylactic removal** of pathology-free third molars.
Indications include: unrestorable caries in the third molar, untreatable pulpal/periapical
disease, cellulitis/abscess/osteomyelitis, resorption of the tooth or adjacent tooth,
fracture, cyst/tumour or follicular disease, tooth in a surgical field (e.g. fracture,
orthognathic surgery), and **pericoronitis** — a severe episode or recurrent episodes.
Distal caries in the second molar from a mesioangular impaction is a widely accepted modern
indication (guidance varies by country — check local).

## 2. Classify the impaction (panoramic radiograph)
- **Winter (angulation):** mesioangular (commonest, usually easier), vertical, horizontal,
  distoangular (often hardest in the mandible — path of withdrawal into the ramus).
- **Pell & Gregory:** ramus space Class I–III (crown fully/partly/fully within ramus);
  depth A–C relative to the second molar occlusal plane/cervical line.
- Root number, curvature, bulbous or hooked apices, ankylosis, bone density (age).

## 3. Nerve risk
**Rood & Shehab signs** of inferior alveolar canal proximity: darkening of the root, deflected
roots, narrowing of the root, dark and bifid apex; interruption of the canal white lines,
diversion of the canal, narrowing of the canal. Darkening, interruption and diversion are the
most predictive. With signs present: **CBCT** to define the relationship and consider
**coronectomy** (remove crown, leave roots ≥ ~3 mm below the crest; contraindicated if the
roots are infected or mobile).

## 4. Consent (state figures from your local data where possible)
Pain, swelling, trismus, bleeding, infection, dry socket, damage to adjacent teeth, jaw
fracture (rare), and **IAN and lingual nerve injury** — temporary in a small percentage,
permanent in well under 1% in most series (quoted figures vary widely; use your unit's data).

## 5. Technique
1. Anaesthesia: IANB + lingual + long buccal (or sedation/GA by case).
2. **Flap:** envelope (sulcular from second molar) or **three-cornered** with a mesial
   relieving incision. Keep the distal incision angled **buccally** up the external oblique
   ridge — the lingual nerve may lie at or above the lingual crest.
3. Full-thickness mucoperiosteal reflection; protect lingual tissues; **avoid lingual bone removal**.
4. **Buccal and distal bone removal** with a surgical bur under copious sterile saline
   (irrigation prevents thermal necrosis), creating a buccal gutter.
5. **Section** the tooth (crown from roots; split roots) along planes that free the path of
   withdrawal rather than removing more bone. Do not cut through to the lingual cortex.
6. Elevate with controlled force (Coupland, Cryer, Warwick James); no force on the second molar
   beyond its tolerance.
7. Debride follicle and debris, smooth bone edges, irrigate, check haemostasis, suture.
8. Post-op: analgesia (e.g. ibuprofen + paracetamol unless contraindicated — verify against
   local formulary/guideline), cold packs, written instructions, emergency contact.
Routine prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended for most healthy patients (evidence and
practice differ — check local guidance).

## 6. Dry socket (alveolar osteitis)
Onset typically **day 2–4**, severe throbbing pain radiating to the ear, empty or grey socket,
halitosis. Management: exclude other causes (radiograph if in doubt), gentle saline irrigation,
analgesic/antiseptic dressing (e.g. iodoform/butamben pastes such as Alvogyl), analgesia,
review. Preoperative chlorhexidine rinse reduces incidence; smoking increases it.

## Gotchas
- Lingual nerve injury is a flap-design and lingual-retraction problem — keep away.
- Distoangular teeth fight you: section early rather than lever harder.
- Persistent numbness after the LA wears off needs documenting and early review/referral
  (some nerve repairs are time-sensitive).
- Excess force near a thin angle risks mandibular fracture, especially in older patients.

## Verify
- [ ] Documented indication matching guidance, not "prophylactic".
- [ ] Winter and Pell & Gregory classification and Rood & Shehab signs recorded.
- [ ] CBCT/coronectomy considered when nerve signs were present.
- [ ] Written consent includes nerve risks with figures.
- [ ] Lip and tongue sensation checked and recorded at review.
