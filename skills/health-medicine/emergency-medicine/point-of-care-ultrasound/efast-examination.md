# eFAST examination in trauma

**Goal:** in under five minutes at the bedside, answer three binary questions — is there
free fluid in the abdomen, fluid around the heart, or air/blood in the pleural space? — so
the unstable patient goes to the right place (theatre, drain, pericardial intervention) without waiting for CT.

## 1. Set up
- Probe: curvilinear (2–5 MHz) for abdomen and haemothorax; phased array for subxiphoid if
  windows are poor; **linear** (high-frequency) is best for lung sliding but curvilinear is acceptable.
- Patient supine; screen visible to the team leader; the scan happens during the primary
  survey (C – circulation) without interrupting resuscitation.
- Depth set so the deepest structure of interest sits near the bottom of the screen.

## 2. The windows (do them in a fixed order)
1. **Right upper quadrant (hepatorenal / Morison's pouch):** coronal plane, mid-to-posterior
   axillary line around ribs 8–11. Sweep the whole interface **including the caudal liver tip**
   — the most sensitive spot for early fluid — and above the diaphragm for haemothorax
   (loss of mirror image, visible spine above diaphragm = "spine sign").
2. **Left upper quadrant (splenorenal):** more posterior and cephalad than you expect
   ("knuckles on the bed"). Fluid collects first in the subphrenic space, above the spleen.
3. **Pelvis (suprapubic):** transverse and longitudinal; best with a full bladder. Fluid
   lies posterior to the bladder (rectovesical pouch in males, pouch of Douglas in females).
4. **Subxiphoid cardiac:** probe flat under the xiphoid aiming at the left shoulder, using the
   liver as a window; parasternal long axis if subxiphoid fails. Look for anechoic pericardial
   fluid and RV diastolic collapse.
5. **Anterior lungs (e-component):** probe in sagittal orientation at the 2nd–4th intercostal
   space mid-clavicular line both sides (the highest point in a supine patient). Normal:
   **lung sliding**, B-lines, lung pulse. M-mode: "seashore" (normal) vs "barcode/stratosphere" (no sliding).

## 3. Interpretation
- **Free fluid** appears anechoic (black) and dependent; clotted blood can be echogenic and missed.
- **Pneumothorax:** absent sliding + absent B-lines + **lung point** (specific); absent
  sliding alone is not specific (mainstem intubation, pleurodesis, apnoea, contusion/ARDS).
- **Haemothorax:** anechoic fluid above the diaphragm with spine sign.
- Positive FAST in an unstable patient → laparotomy/damage control pathway; stable with
  positive FAST → CT to characterise; negative FAST in the unstable patient → look for other
  sources (pelvis, chest, retroperitoneum, long bones) and **repeat the scan**.

## 4. Document
Each window as positive / negative / indeterminate (with reason, e.g., subcutaneous
emphysema), time of scan, and operator.

## Gotchas
- FAST is **specific but not sensitive** for intra-abdominal injury; a negative scan does not
  exclude injury, especially early (small volumes undetectable — often cited ≥~200 mL in
  Morison's pouch; figures vary) and for retroperitoneal, hollow-viscus and solid-organ
  injuries without haemoperitoneum.
- Pericardial fat pad, perinephric fat, seminal vesicles, ascites and physiological pelvic
  fluid in women of reproductive age are common false positives.
- A large left haemothorax can mimic pericardial fluid; scan from more than one window.
- Surgical emphysema and obesity degrade images — call it "indeterminate", not "negative".
- Serial scans beat a single scan; re-FAST after deterioration.

## Verify
- [ ] All windows imaged including the caudal liver tip and LUQ subphrenic space.
- [ ] Lung sliding assessed bilaterally with M-mode where unclear.
- [ ] Each window recorded as positive, negative or indeterminate.
- [ ] Result communicated to the team leader and integrated with haemodynamics.
- [ ] Repeat scan performed after any deterioration or if initial scan was early/negative.
