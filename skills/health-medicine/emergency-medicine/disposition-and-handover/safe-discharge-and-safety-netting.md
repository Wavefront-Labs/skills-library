# Safe discharge and safety-netting from the emergency department

**Goal:** discharge only patients whose dangerous diagnoses have been adequately excluded
and whose ongoing needs can be met at home — and leave each one knowing exactly what would
make them come back, and how.

## 1. The discharge "time-out" (before you say "you can go")
Ask yourself, and document the answers:
1. **What is the worst thing this could be, and have I reasonably excluded it?** (e.g.,
   headache → SAH, dissection; abdominal pain in older adults → AAA, mesenteric ischaemia).
2. **Are the vital signs normal now?** Unexplained tachycardia, hypoxia, fever or new
   confusion at the point of discharge is a classic precursor of harm. Repeat a full set.
3. **Do the results make sense?** Every ordered test reviewed, including the ones still
   pending; abnormal incidental findings have a named owner.
4. **Can they cope at home?** Mobility (have you seen them walk?), cognition, social
   support, ability to take medications, access to transport and phone.
5. **Has a senior reviewed** the high-risk groups? Many departments mandate senior review
   for unscheduled return within 72 hours, atraumatic chest pain in older adults, fever in
   infants, abdominal pain in over-70s and patients leaving against advice (local lists vary).

## 2. Choosing a disposition
- **Admit** when ongoing treatment, monitoring or investigation cannot be safely delivered
  elsewhere, or when risk remains unacceptably high after ED work-up.
- **Observation / short-stay or ambulatory pathway** when a time-limited period of
  monitoring or a repeat test will settle the question (e.g., serial troponins, minor head
  injury observation, ambulatory PE pathway).
- **Discharge with follow-up** when the residual risk is low and a defined follow-up
  (clinic, GP/primary care, virtual ward, repeat test) is booked or clearly arranged.
- Use validated tools where they fit (e.g., HEART score for chest pain, PESI/sPESI or Hestia
  for PE outpatient management, CURB-65 for pneumonia) as support, not substitutes, for judgement.
- Use **shared decision-making** for grey-zone risks: explain the options and residual risk in plain numbers.

## 3. Safety-netting: make it specific
Good safety-netting covers three things:
- **What** to look out for — concrete symptoms, not "if worse" (e.g., "pain moving to the
  right lower abdomen, vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down, or fever above 38°C").
- **When** to act — the expected course ("the cough should settle within three weeks") and
  time-bound triggers ("if not improving in 48 hours").
- **How** to get help — which service, phone number, and that they can return to the ED any time.
Give it verbally **and** in writing (condition-specific leaflet), use **teach-back** ("tell me
what you'll watch for"), and involve carers or interpreters as needed.

## 4. Documentation and communication
- Diagnosis (or working diagnosis stated honestly, e.g., "non-specific abdominal pain —
  appendicitis not excluded"), what was ruled out and how, discharge vitals, safety-netting
  given and understood, follow-up plan and who owns pending results.
- Discharge summary to the primary care clinician with clear actions.
- For handover of patients staying in the department, use a structured format (SBAR/ISBAR)
  including outstanding tasks and the contingency plan.

## 5. Special situations
- **Leaving against advice:** assess capacity for this decision, explain risks, offer
  alternatives and partial treatment, document, and leave the door open to return.
- **Did not wait / left before results:** follow your department's recall process for
  abnormal results.
- **Night-time discharge** of frail older adults is associated with harm; consider waiting until morning.

## Gotchas
- "Diagnosis momentum" — accepting a label from triage or a referral without re-examining it.
- A second ED visit for the same complaint is a red flag: re-evaluate from scratch.
- Analgesia can mask a surgical abdomen; re-examine before discharge.
- Generic leaflets without verbal explanation are rarely read or understood.
- Pending results with no owner are a recurrent root cause in incident reviews.

## Verify
- [ ] Worst-case diagnoses listed and reasoning for exclusion documented.
- [ ] Repeat observations normal (or abnormalities explained) at discharge.
- [ ] All results reviewed; pending results assigned to a named person/process.
- [ ] Functional and social safety confirmed (walked, coping, transport, support).
- [ ] Specific what/when/how safety-netting given verbally and in writing, with teach-back.
- [ ] Follow-up arranged and discharge summary sent.
