# Biomechanical assessment and orthotic prescription

> General clinical/professional reference. Evidence for orthoses is moderate for some
> conditions (e.g. short-term plantar heel pain, RA forefoot pain, diabetic pressure
> relief) and weak for others; prefabricated devices often perform similarly to custom.

**Goal:** identify which tissue is overloaded and why, and change the load with the
simplest device or footwear that works — not "correct" a foot to a theoretical neutral.

## 1. Reasoning model
- Prefer the **tissue-stress model**: name the painful structure, the movement or load
  that stresses it, and an intervention that reduces that stress. The older Root
  "subtalar neutral" model has poor reliability and weak predictive validity; use its
  vocabulary with caution.
- Ask: what changed (load, footwear, weight, training)? Orthoses rarely fix a
  load-management problem alone.

## 2. Assessment battery (reliable-enough tests)
- **Foot Posture Index (FPI-6)**: six observations, score −12 to +12; roughly 0 to +5
  normal, ≥ +6 pronated, ≤ −1 supinated (ranges per manual — check).
- **Weight-bearing lunge test**: ankle dorsiflexion (cm wall-to-toe or inclinometer
  angle); < ~35–40° or a side-to-side difference is clinically relevant.
- **Windlass / Jack's test**: hallux dorsiflexion and arch rise; reduced in functional
  hallux limitus.
- First MTPJ range, single-leg heel raise (endurance and inversion — tibialis posterior),
  navicular drop, hip/knee control in single-leg squat.
- Gait: barefoot and shod; video where possible; running assessment for runners.
- Footwear: wear pattern, heel counter, toe box, fastening, heel-to-toe drop.

## 3. Choosing a device
| Need | Device direction |
|---|---|
| Medial tissue stress (tib post, plantar fascia in pronated foot) | medial heel skive, medial wedge, arch fill |
| Lateral instability/cavus | lateral wedge, valgus forefoot post, lower medial arch |
| Plantar forefoot pressure | metatarsal dome/bar, cut-outs, accommodative top-cover |
| Heel pain | heel cup, cushioning, arch support |
| Diabetic/neuropathic pressure | total-contact accommodative insole, ideally pressure-verified |
- **Prefabricated first** for many presentations; custom when the foot is atypical,
  prefab failed, or pressure relief must be precise.

## 4. Capturing the foot
- **Plaster casting** (non-weight-bearing, held position), **foam box** (semi-weight-
  bearing), or **3D scanning**. Capture method changes the shell — be consistent and
  specify the position on the prescription.

## 5. Writing the prescription
Specify: shell material and stiffness, length (sulcus vs full), arch fill/height,
rearfoot post or skive (angle/mm), forefoot posting, heel cup depth, extrinsic
additions, top-cover material, and the shoe it must fit. Ambiguous prescriptions get
the lab's defaults.

## 6. Issue and review
- Wear-in schedule (e.g. increasing over 1–2 weeks); warn about transient arch/knee
  ache.
- Review at 4–6 weeks: symptom score, fit, wear marks, new pressure areas. Modify or
  stop if no benefit.
- Footwear advice is often worth more than the device: lace-up, firm heel counter,
  adequate depth, appropriate drop.

## Red flags / refer
- Rigid flat foot in a child or adolescent (tarsal coalition), progressive adult flat
  foot with loss of single heel raise (tibialis posterior failure stage), new cavus
  deformity (neurological cause — e.g. CMT, spinal lesion), unilateral rapidly changing
  foot shape in diabetes (Charcot).

## Gotchas
- Asymptomatic flat feet in children generally need no orthoses.
- Hard, rigid devices on a neuropathic foot can ulcerate it; prefer accommodative.
- A device that doesn't fit the patient's actual shoes won't be worn.

## Verify
- [ ] The overloaded tissue and its mechanism are written down.
- [ ] FPI-6, lunge and hallux tests recorded with numbers.
- [ ] Prescription specifies every variable, capture method and target shoe.
- [ ] Review booked with an outcome measure; device changed or stopped if no benefit.
- [ ] Structural red flags screened and referred.
