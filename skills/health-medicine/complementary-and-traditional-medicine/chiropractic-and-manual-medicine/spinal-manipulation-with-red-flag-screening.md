# Spinal manipulation with red-flag screening

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.*

**Goal:** decide whether manual therapy is appropriate, rule out serious pathology and vascular risk, deliver the least forceful effective technique with consent, and measure the result.

## 1. Red-flag screen (lumbar and thoracic)
| Suspect | Features | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Cauda equina | Saddle anaesthesia, new bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg symptoms, sexual dysfunction | Same-day emergency referral |
| Malignancy | Cancer history, age >50 with new pain, weight loss, night pain | Urgent medical referral |
| Infection | Fever, IV drug use, immunosuppression, recent surgery | Urgent referral |
| Fracture | Trauma, osteoporosis, long-term steroids, older age | Imaging/referral; no HVLA |
| Inflammatory | <45 y, morning stiffness >30 min, improves with exercise | Rheumatology referral |
| Visceral/vascular | Pulsatile abdominal mass (AAA), chest pain | Emergency referral |

Single flags have low predictive value; act on clinical picture (IFOMPT/JOSPT red-flags framework).

## 2. Cervical region: vascular screening
Before cervical manipulation, take a history for cervical artery dysfunction: sudden new severe neck pain or headache "unlike any before", the 5 Ds and 3 Ns (dizziness, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, drop attacks; nystagmus, nausea, numbness), recent neck trauma, vascular risk factors. Positive → no manipulation, urgent medical referral. Positional "VBI tests" have poor diagnostic accuracy and do not clear the patient — the IFOMPT framework prioritises history and clinical reasoning.

## 3. Examination
Observation, active and passive range of motion, segmental palpation/motion assessment, neurological exam (myotomes, dermatomes, reflexes; straight-leg raise; upper-limb tension), and provocation tests. Stratify low back pain (e.g. STarT Back) and record a baseline (NRS 0–10, Oswestry for back, NDI for neck).

## 4. Choose the technique
- Least-force-first: soft tissue → mobilisation (graded I–IV) → HVLA thrust if indicated and consented.
- Contraindications to HVLA: red-flag pathology, osteoporosis, inflammatory instability (e.g. rheumatoid atlanto-axial), anticoagulation (relative), recent fracture, myelopathy, Down syndrome (upper cervical laxity), patient refusal.
- NICE NG59 places manual therapy only as part of a package with exercise, not alone.

## 5. Consent
Explain: common transient soreness or stiffness (up to roughly half of patients, resolving in 24–48 h); rare serious events (cervical artery dissection, stroke, cauda equina, fracture) — the causal link for dissection is debated but must be disclosed. Offer alternatives. Document.

## 6. Delivery and aftercare
Position for comfort; pre-manipulative hold — stop if symptoms reproduce (dizziness, paraesthesia). Single thrust per segment; no audible cavitation is not a failure — do not keep re-thrusting to obtain one. Afterwards: re-test, advise activity and exercise, warn what new symptoms require urgent help.

## 7. Review
Reassess at each visit; expect meaningful change in 4–6 sessions for most acute/subacute problems. No improvement → reconsider diagnosis or refer.

## Gotchas
- Evidence: manipulation gives small-to-moderate short-term benefit for low back pain, similar to other recommended therapies (Rubinstein 2019); not an evidence-based treatment for non-musculoskeletal conditions (asthma, colic, otitis media).
- Patients presenting with a dissection may already be having one — new neck pain with headache is the warning.
- Avoid routine X-ray for non-specific back pain.
- Long maintenance plans without reassessment are not justified.

## Verify
- [ ] Red flags and, for neck, vascular history documented.
- [ ] Neurological exam and baseline outcome measure recorded.
- [ ] Technique choice justified; HVLA contraindications excluded.
- [ ] Consent including serious risks recorded.
- [ ] Exercise/self-management included; review interval set.
