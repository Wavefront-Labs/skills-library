# Healer–clinic liaison and safety assessment

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.*

**Goal:** when a patient is also being treated by a traditional or ritual healer (diviner, spiritual healer, bonesetter, traditional birth attendant, religious healer), understand what it means to them, protect them from specific harms, and create a working relationship with the healer — without judging, appropriating or performing the practice yourself.

## 1. Stance
- The healing system belongs to its community. Clinicians do not adopt, imitate or reinterpret its rituals; they learn enough to work alongside it.
- Use cultural humility: the patient is the expert on their meaning; ask, do not assume from ethnicity or religion.
- Use a trained interpreter, not family members, for sensitive topics.

## 2. Elicit the explanatory model
Kleinman's questions (or the DSM-5-TR Cultural Formulation Interview core 16 questions):
1. What do you call the problem?
2. What do you think caused it? Why now?
3. What does it do to you; how severe is it?
4. What treatment do you think you should receive? What are you hoping for?
5. Who else is helping — healer, family, religious leader — and what have they advised?

Note idioms of distress (e.g. spirit affliction, "thinking too much") that may correspond to depression, anxiety, psychosis or epilepsy, and those that do not map onto biomedical categories — record them in the patient's words.

## 3. Practice-specific safety screen
Ask neutrally what the treatment involves. Specific harms to look for:

| Practice | Possible harm | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Oral or rectal herbal preparations | Hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, interactions (antiretrovirals, anticoagulants, antiepileptics), enema-related injury | Identify the plant if possible; check LFTs/renal function; adjust drug monitoring |
| Scarification, cutting, tattooing | Bloodborne virus transmission, infection | Discuss single-use blades; offer hepatitis B/HIV testing |
| Bonesetting with tight splints | Compartment syndrome, malunion | Teach warning signs (pain, numbness, colour change); offer X-ray |
| Fasting, purging, prolonged ritual | Dehydration, hypoglycaemia (diabetes), missed medication | Plan medication timing; sick-day rules |
| Restraint or exorcism for mental illness | Physical injury, neglect, abuse | Safeguarding procedure; urgent psychiatric assessment |
| Traditional birth practices | Delayed referral in obstructed labour or haemorrhage; harmful cord care | Agree danger signs and a facility-referral plan with the birth attendant |

Most ritual practices (prayer, song, protective objects, cleansing ceremonies) are low-risk and can support wellbeing; harm usually comes from delayed care or specific physical procedures. Apply safeguarding law for children and vulnerable adults regardless of cultural framing; female genital mutilation is illegal in many jurisdictions and must be reported per local law.

## 4. Build the liaison
- With the patient's consent, contact the healer; explain your role and ask theirs.
- Agree specifics: danger signs that mean the healer sends the patient to clinic (fever in infants, breathlessness, bleeding in pregnancy, confusion, seizures); which medicines must not be stopped (ART, insulin, antiepileptics, TB treatment).
- Offer two-way referral slips and a named clinic contact. Community programmes (e.g. HIV collaborations in sub-Saharan Africa) show healers can refer effectively when respected.
- Never ask the patient to choose; support concurrent care where safe.

## 5. Document and follow up
Record the explanatory model, practices, harms screened, agreements with the healer, and consent for sharing information. Revisit at each visit; meanings and practices change.

## Gotchas
- Dismissing a healer drives patients to conceal use — the single biggest safety risk.
- Not every unfamiliar belief is pathology; assess psychosis on form and impairment, not content alone.
- Avoid filming or describing ceremonies without community permission.
- Herbal identity is often unknown; treat unexplained organ dysfunction as possible herb toxicity.

## Verify
- [ ] Explanatory model recorded in the patient's words, via a trained interpreter if needed.
- [ ] Each practice screened for specific physical harms; tests ordered where indicated.
- [ ] Essential medicines and danger signs agreed with patient (and healer, with consent).
- [ ] Safeguarding concerns acted on per local law.
- [ ] Referral pathway and named contact documented.
