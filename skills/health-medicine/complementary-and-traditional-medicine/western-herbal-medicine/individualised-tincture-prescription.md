# Individualised tincture prescription

*General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.*

**Goal:** produce a liquid herbal prescription (typically 3–6 herbs) whose each herb has a stated reason, a dose within monograph ranges, no unmanaged interaction, and a review date.

## 1. Case review
Record presenting complaint, existing medical diagnosis, all medicines and supplements, allergies (especially Asteraceae), pregnancy/breastfeeding, liver/kidney disease, alcohol use (tinctures contain ethanol), and upcoming surgery. Red flags or undiagnosed symptoms → refer for medical assessment first.

## 2. Choose herbs by action
Build the formula by role: **primary** herbs for the main complaint, **supporting** herbs for contributing systems, and sometimes a **corrective/harmoniser** for taste or tolerance. Justify each from the materia medica and where possible from monograph or trial evidence (EMA/HMPC, ESCOP). Be explicit when use is "traditional use only".

## 3. Dose calculation
Herbalists usually prescribe a weekly volume (often 60–100 mL/week for 1:2 extracts at 5 mL twice daily — confirm with your dispensary standard) and apportion it.

1. Look up each herb's weekly dose range for its extract ratio (e.g. Bone's tables list ranges in mL/week of 1:2 or 1:5).
2. Allocate a share to each herb so it falls within range: a 100 mL blend taken over a week at 5 mL three times daily delivers ~105 mL.
3. Check: (mL of herb in the bottle) ÷ (weeks the bottle lasts) ≥ the lower bound of its weekly range, otherwise it is sub-therapeutic; also ≤ the upper bound.

Example worked line: 1:2 Withania, weekly range 20–40 mL; in a 100 mL weekly blend allocate 30 mL → within range.

Ratio caveat: 1:2 means 1 g dried herb per 2 mL; 1:5 tinctures are 2.5× weaker per mL. Never mix ratios without converting.

## 4. Interaction and safety screen
| Concern | Examples |
|---|---|
| Enzyme induction | St John's wort (Hypericum) induces CYP3A4 and P-gp: reduces ciclosporin, oral contraceptives, warfarin, HIV drugs, digoxin; serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs/triptans. Usually avoid with prescription drugs. |
| Bleeding | Ginkgo, high-dose garlic, ginger, Salvia with anticoagulants; stop ~2 weeks before surgery (verify local advice) |
| Hepatotoxicity | Kava, comfrey (internal use, pyrrolizidine alkaloids), black cohosh (rare reports) |
| Blood pressure/potassium | Liquorice >~100 mg glycyrrhizin/day: hypertension, hypokalaemia |
| Pregnancy | Avoid emmenagogues and most herbs lacking safety data |

Use a dedicated interaction reference for every drug on the list; if data are absent, say "unknown" rather than "safe".

## 5. Dispense and label
Label: patient name, herbs, ratio and volumes, dose (e.g. "5 mL in a little water twice daily"), ethanol content, date, practitioner and contact, and "keep out of reach of children". Prefer suppliers with GMP and identity testing; in the UK, licensed THR products carry a THR mark (verify regime in your jurisdiction).

## 6. Review
Review at 2–4 weeks: symptom score versus baseline, adherence, adverse effects. Adjust one or two herbs at a time so effects are attributable. Report suspected reactions (e.g. MHRA Yellow Card).

## Gotchas
- Alcohol-based tinctures are inappropriate for people in alcohol recovery, children (use glycerites), or with metronidazole/disulfiram.
- "Natural" ≠ safe; adulteration and misidentification occur, so source matters.
- Too many herbs dilutes each below its effective dose.
- Tell the patient to inform their doctor and pharmacist.
