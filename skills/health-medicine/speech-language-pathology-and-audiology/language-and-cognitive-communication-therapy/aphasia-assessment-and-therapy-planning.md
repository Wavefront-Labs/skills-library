# Aphasia assessment and therapy planning

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** describe what a person with aphasia can and can't do across modalities, then
deliver therapy at enough intensity to change everyday communication.

## 1. Acute screen
- Screen early after stroke (e.g. Frenchay Aphasia Screening Test, bedside language
  screen): spontaneous speech, auditory comprehension (yes/no reliability, commands),
  naming, repetition, reading, writing.
- Separate aphasia from **dysarthria** (motor speech, language intact), **apraxia of
  speech** (motor planning; groping, inconsistent errors), and **cognitive-communication**
  disorder (attention/executive; e.g. right-hemisphere or TBI).
- Establish a **reliable yes/no** and a communication method for consent, pain and
  basic needs — first priority.
- Give the team communication strategies on day one.

## 2. Full profile
- Formal battery: Western Aphasia Battery-R, Comprehensive Aphasia Test (CAT, includes
  disability questionnaire), Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Exam. Add naming tests (BNT),
  discourse sample (picture description, procedural narrative).
- Classify roughly (Broca's, Wernicke's, conduction, anomic, global, transcortical) for
  communication, but plan therapy from the **processing profile** (e.g. semantic vs
  phonological naming breakdown) and the person's goals.
- Patient-reported measures: communication confidence, participation, mood — depression
  after stroke is common and affects engagement.

## 3. Therapy options (choose by profile and goal)
- **Semantic feature analysis (SFA):** for semantic naming errors — generate features
  (group, use, action, properties, location, association) around target pictures.
- **Phonological components analysis / cueing hierarchies:** for phonological errors;
  fade cues from most to least supportive.
- **Constraint-induced aphasia therapy (CIAT/ILAT):** spoken responses only, intensive
  (e.g. ~3 h/day over 2 weeks), in group communication games.
- **Script training / scenario training:** rehearse real-life exchanges the person
  wants (ordering coffee, phone call).
- **Melodic intonation therapy:** selected non-fluent patients with good comprehension.
- **Supported conversation (SCA) and communication partner training:** partners learn to
  reveal and acknowledge competence — writing key words, gesture, yes/no checks.
- **Total communication:** AAC, drawing, gesture as additional channels.
- Reading/writing: anagram and copy treatment, text-to-speech.

## 4. Intensity and timing
- Evidence (Cochrane 2016; RELEASE 2022) favours **higher intensity/dose and
  frequency**: something like 2–9 h/week was associated with better outcomes;
  very low doses show little change. Therapy remains effective in chronic aphasia.
- Combine clinician-led sessions with home practice apps and family practice.

## 5. Measure what matters
Baseline and post-treatment probes on treated and **untreated items** (to show
generalisation), discourse measures, and a functional/participation outcome.

## Gotchas
- Treating "word lists" without connection to the person's goals.
- Testing comprehension with yes/no when yes/no is unreliable.
- Assuming poor speech means poor cognition — test non-verbally.
- Ignoring partner training: the environment is half the communication.

## Verify
- [ ] Reliable yes/no and basic communication method established and shared.
- [ ] Aphasia separated from dysarthria, AOS and cognitive-communication deficits.
- [ ] Processing profile and personal goals recorded.
- [ ] Dose planned and logged; generalisation probes scheduled.
- [ ] Partner training delivered; mood screened.
