# Result disclosure and cascade testing

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** help a patient understand a genetic result, what it means for them and their
relatives, and act on it — while respecting their autonomy and emotional state.

## 1. Pre-test (sets up everything that follows)
- Elicit why they're testing now and what they expect it to show.
- Explain possible results: **positive** (P/LP), **negative** (true negative if the
  familial variant is known; *uninformative* if not), **VUS**, and **secondary/incidental
  findings** (opt-in choice).
- Implications for relatives, insurance (in the US, GINA covers health insurance (Title I) and employment (Title II);
  its position on life, disability and long-term-care insurance (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked); rules differ elsewhere).
- Turnaround, cost, how results will be given (in person/phone/telehealth), and who
  else may be present. Document consent.
- **Predictive testing for untreatable adult-onset conditions** (e.g. Huntington
  disease): multi-session protocol with psychological assessment and a support person;
  generally avoid testing minors for adult-onset conditions without medical benefit.

## 2. Disclosing results
1. Prepare: review the report, classification evidence, and management guidelines
   before the session. Confirm the right patient and sample.
2. Check in on how they're feeling; ask who is with them.
3. Give the headline early and plainly: "The test found a change in the BRCA2 gene that
   increases cancer risk." Pause.
4. Respond to emotion first (guilt, fear, relief, anger); normalise. Parents often feel
   guilt about passing on variants — address it explicitly.
5. Explain meaning in **absolute risk** with a timeframe and comparison: "More than 60 out
   of 100 women with this change develop breast cancer in their lifetime (NCI), compared with about 13
   out of 100 in the general population." Use natural frequencies or pictographs; avoid
   relative risk alone ("5 times higher").
6. Management plan: surveillance, risk-reducing options, referrals — or for a negative
   result, what residual risk remains based on family history.
7. **Teach-back:** "So I know I've explained this well, can you tell me what this
   means for you?"
8. Provide a written summary and the lab report.

### VUS results
Say clearly: "We don't currently know whether this change matters; we treat you based on
your personal and family history, not this result." Explain that most VUS are later
reclassified as benign (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked), and how they'll be told if it changes.

## 3. Cascade testing
- **Map at-risk relatives** on the pedigree: first-degree (50% for autosomal dominant),
  then second-degree; for recessive conditions, carrier risk for siblings/partners'
  implications.
- Provide a **family letter**: the condition, gene and exact variant (HGVS), the lab,
  why testing matters, and how relatives can access counselling locally. Attach a copy
  of the report (with patient consent).
- Discuss who will tell whom and barriers (estrangement, cultural factors, fear).
  Offer to contact relatives directly where law, policy and consent allow.
- Relatives need only **targeted testing** of the known familial variant (cheaper,
  definitive).
- Follow up at ~3–6 months: who has been informed and tested?
- **Duty to warn**: patients generally control disclosure; clinician disclosure without
  consent is exceptional and jurisdiction-specific — involve ethics/legal.

## 4. Documentation
Session summary: attendees, result and classification, risks discussed (numbers used),
management recommendations, psychosocial concerns, cascade plan, follow-up date.

## Gotchas
- "Negative" without a known familial variant is not reassuring if family history is
  strong — say so.
- Unexpected findings (non-paternity, consanguinity) generally aren't disclosed unless
  clinically necessary; agree the policy pre-test.
- Interpreters should be professional; family interpreters filter bad news.
- Results can change (reclassification); give the patient responsibility and a route
  to recontact.
