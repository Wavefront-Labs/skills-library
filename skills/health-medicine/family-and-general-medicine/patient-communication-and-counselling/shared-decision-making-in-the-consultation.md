*General clinical/professional reference on consultation technique, not patient-specific advice; local consent law and guidance take precedence.*

# Shared decision-making in the consultation

**Goal:** for a decision where reasonable people could choose differently (start a statin?
PSA test? knee surgery vs physio? stop a medicine?), the patient leaves understanding the
real options including doing nothing, the size of the benefits and harms in numbers they
grasp, and having made a choice that matches what matters to them — and the record shows it.

SDM is **not** for emergencies or decisions with one clearly right answer; there, explain
and recommend. Use it for **preference-sensitive** decisions.

## 1. Set it up (ICE first)
Elicit **Ideas, Concerns and Expectations** early — a decision framed around the wrong
worry fails. "What were you hoping we'd do about this today?"

## 2. Three-talk model (Elwyn)
1. **Team talk** — signal that there's a choice and you'll make it together.
   "There's more than one sensible way to handle this, and the right one depends on what
   matters to you. Can we go through them?"
2. **Option talk** — list the options (including *no change / watchful waiting*), then
   compare benefits, harms and practicalities. Use a decision aid or Option Grid if one
   exists (NICE patient decision aids, Mayo statin aid, screening leaflets).
3. **Decision talk** — explore preferences, check readiness, decide or defer.
   "Having heard that, which way are you leaning, and why?"

## 3. Communicate risk properly
- Use **absolute** risks with a common denominator and time frame: "Out of 100 people like
  you, about 10 will have a heart attack or stroke in 10 years without a statin; with one,
  about 7. So 3 in 100 avoid one; 97 take it with no change in that outcome."
- Avoid relative risk alone ("cuts risk by 30%") — it inflates perceived benefit.
- Present harms with the same denominator ("about 1 in 10,000 get serious muscle damage").
- **Icon arrays** (100-person pictograms) beat percentages for most people.
- Positive and negative framing both ("93 won't, 7 will") to reduce framing bias.
- NNT is useful for clinicians, often poorly understood by patients — translate it.

## 4. Check understanding (teach-back)
"I've thrown a lot of numbers at you — so I know I've explained it well, how would you
describe the choice to your partner tonight?" Correct and re-check; this tests your
explanation, not the patient.

## 5. Decide, defer, or safety-net
- It's fine to **defer**: give the decision aid, book a follow-up or phone call.
- If the patient asks "what would you do?", you can share a view — after options are laid
  out — and say what your view is based on.
- Respect a choice you disagree with if the patient has capacity and is informed.

## 6. Document
Options discussed (including no treatment), key numbers given, decision aid used, the
patient's stated reasons/values, decision made or deferral plan, and review date. Under
UK **Montgomery** the test is what a reasonable patient would want to know, plus what
this patient would consider material — record what you disclosed.

## Gotchas
- Asking "any questions?" at the end is not SDM; the patient's values must be elicited.
- Patients often defer to the doctor out of politeness — "whatever you think" usually
  means they need more time or clearer options, not that they've chosen.
- Health literacy and numeracy vary widely; frequencies ("1 in 20") beat percentages.
- Interpreters, not family members, for anything consequential.
- Time pressure is the commonest barrier — split across two appointments rather than rush.
- Uncertainty: evidence that SDM changes hard outcomes is mixed; its strongest support is
  for knowledge, decisional conflict and risk-perception accuracy.

## Verify
- [ ] "No change / wait" was offered as an option.
- [ ] Benefits and harms given as absolute numbers with the same denominator and time frame.
- [ ] Teach-back done and misunderstandings corrected.
- [ ] Patient's reasons for their choice recorded in their words.
- [ ] Decision aid or written information given; follow-up or review set.
