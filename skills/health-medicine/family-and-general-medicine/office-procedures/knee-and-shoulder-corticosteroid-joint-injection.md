# Knee and shoulder corticosteroid injection in the office

**Goal:** a safe, landmark-guided injection into the right space, done for the right
indication, with documented consent and a plan if it doesn't help. Landmark accuracy is
imperfect (shoulder especially) — ultrasound guidance improves placement where available.

## 1. Select the patient
Reasonable indications: knee OA flare with effusion or pain limiting rehab; subacromial
pain (rotator-cuff tendinopathy) not settling with 6+ weeks of exercise; frozen shoulder in
the painful phase (glenohumeral injection).

**Do not inject** if: suspected septic arthritis or overlying cellulitis, prosthetic joint
(refer), bacteraemia, fracture, unexplained hot swollen joint (aspirate and send for
culture/crystals first), patient on the same joint ≥3–4 times/year, or within ~3 months of
planned arthroplasty (check local orthopaedic rule — often 3 months, some say 6).

Caution: diabetes (warn of glucose rise for 2–5 days), anticoagulation (usually OK on
warfarin with INR in range or on DOACs — follow local policy), immunosuppression, recent
vaccination plans in some protocols.

## 2. Consent (document it)
Benefits: short-to-medium term pain relief (weeks to a few months); not disease-modifying.
Risks: post-injection flare (~1 in 10–20, 24–48 h), skin depigmentation or fat atrophy
(more with superficial placement), facial flushing, transient hyperglycaemia, tendon
weakening, infection (rare, roughly 1 in 10,000–50,000), and possible effect on later
joint-replacement infection risk. Advise relative rest of the joint for 24–48 h.

## 3. Typical doses (confirm against your formulary)
| Site | Corticosteroid | Local anaesthetic |
|---|---|---|
| Knee | Triamcinolone acetonide 40 mg or methylprednisolone acetate 40–80 mg | Optional 2–5 mL 1% lidocaine |
| Subacromial | Triamcinolone 20–40 mg or methylprednisolone 40 mg | 4–5 mL 1% lidocaine (diagnostic as well) |
| Glenohumeral | Triamcinolone 40 mg or methylprednisolone 40 mg | 2–5 mL 1% lidocaine |

## 4. Technique
**Aseptic no-touch technique:** mark the site, clean (chlorhexidine-alcohol, allow to
dry), do not re-palpate the cleaned site, single needle pass where possible.

- **Knee, superolateral:** knee extended and relaxed. Insert ~1 cm superior and lateral to
  the superolateral corner of the patella, aiming under the patella toward the
  intercondylar notch. 21G green needle. Aspirate first if effusion — send cloudy fluid
  for MC&S and crystals and **do not inject**.
- **Subacromial, posterior:** patient seated, arm relaxed. Palpate the posterolateral
  corner of the acromion; insert ~2 cm inferior, aiming anteriorly toward the coracoid.
  Should inject without resistance; resistance means you're in tendon — withdraw slightly.
- **Glenohumeral, posterior:** insert ~2–3 cm inferior and 1–2 cm medial to the
  posterolateral acromion, aiming at the coracoid; 21G 1.5-inch (longer in larger patients).

## 5. Aftercare
Observe 15–30 min (vasovagal, anaphylaxis). Safety-net in writing: increasing pain,
redness, heat or fever after 48 h = urgent review for infection. Rehab/exercise continues —
injection is a window for physiotherapy, not the treatment. Review in 6 weeks.

## Gotchas
- A pain-free shoulder 5 minutes after subacromial lidocaine supports the diagnosis; no
  change suggests misplacement or wrong diagnosis.
- Superficial steroid causes visible skin atrophy — never inject while withdrawing.
- Mixing steroid and lidocaine in one syringe is common practice but check product
  compatibility statements (some advise against for certain preparations).
- Don't inject the same knee repeatedly for OA without re-assessing for referral.
- Uncertainty: dose ranges and repeat-injection limits vary between sources and countries.
