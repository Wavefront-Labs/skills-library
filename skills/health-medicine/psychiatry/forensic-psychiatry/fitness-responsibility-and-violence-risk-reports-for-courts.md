# Fitness, responsibility and violence-risk reports for courts

> General professional reference. Legal tests named here are jurisdiction-specific
> and evolve through case law and reform (e.g. Law Commission proposals on unfitness in
> England and Wales). Always work to the instructing party's question and the local
> legal test, and seek legal clarification when uncertain.

**Goal:** give the court an independent, evidence-based opinion on a defined
psychiatric–legal question, in a report that survives cross-examination.

## 1. The role (and its traps)
- Your duty is to the **court**, overriding any duty to the instructing party; stay
  within expertise; distinguish fact, opinion and the basis for each.
- **Dual agency**: you are not the person's treating doctor. Explain at the start who
  instructed you, what the report is for, and that it is **not confidential** in the
  usual sense. Document their understanding.
- Avoid assessing someone you treat, where possible.

## 2. Information base
Instructions/letter of questions, charge sheet/indictment and case summary, witness
statements, CCTV/interview transcripts, previous psychiatric and medical records, GP
records, prison records, collateral interviews. List every source in the report.

## 3. Fitness to plead / competency to stand trial
- England and Wales: **Pritchard** criteria as modernised in case law — ability to
  understand the charge, decide how to plead, challenge jurors, instruct counsel,
  follow proceedings and give evidence.
- US: **Dusky** — sufficient present ability to consult with counsel with a reasonable
  degree of rational understanding, and rational and factual understanding of the
  proceedings.
- Assess each element with specific questions; consider whether **adjustments**
  (intermediary, breaks, simplified language) could make the person fit. Distinguish
  inability from unwillingness.

## 4. Criminal responsibility
- **Insanity** (e.g. M'Naghten in England and many US states: defect of reason from a
  disease of the mind such that they didn't know the nature/quality of the act or that
  it was wrong; tests vary — some US states use ALI/Model Penal Code or have abolished
  the defence).
- **Diminished responsibility** (England and Wales, homicide only): abnormality of
  mental functioning from a recognised medical condition substantially impairing
  specified abilities and providing an explanation for the conduct.
- Reconstruct mental state **at the time of the offence** from contemporaneous
  evidence, not current presentation alone. Intoxication rules are complex — flag the
  legal point rather than resolving it.

## 5. Violence risk
- Use **structured professional judgement** (e.g. **HCR-20 V3**: historical, clinical,
  risk-management items) to build a formulation and scenarios (what violence, against
  whom, under what conditions, how likely, how serious, how imminent) and the
  management that would reduce it.
- Actuarial tools (VRAG-R, Static-99R for sexual offending) give group-level estimates;
  explain their limits — they don't tell you about this individual's management needs.
- State uncertainty explicitly; avoid "dangerous" without definition.

## 6. Feigning and exaggeration
Consider in all forensic assessments: inconsistencies across sources, atypical
symptom patterns, symptom validity testing (e.g. SIRS-2, TOMM, by trained users).
Feigning and genuine illness can coexist.

## 7. Report structure
Instructions and questions → sources → interview circumstances → history → MSE →
investigations/testing → diagnosis (with criteria) → opinion answering **each
question in turn** → limitations → statement of truth/declaration as required by local
rules → CV/qualifications summary.

## 8. Secure-setting care
- Levels of security (low/medium/high) matched to risk; relational, procedural and
  physical security.
- Care planning integrates treatment, risk management and legal restrictions; leave
  and discharge decisions often involve tribunals or ministry/justice approval.

## Gotchas
- Answering the ultimate legal question when the court must decide it (e.g. "guilty")
  — give the psychiatric opinion relevant to the test.
- Copying records without critical appraisal.
- Overconfident risk statements that fall apart under cross-examination.
