# Structured risk formulation and safety planning

> General clinical/professional reference for trained clinicians, not patient-specific
> advice. Involuntary-care criteria are set by local mental-health law; follow your
> service's crisis and escalation policy. This skill deliberately covers the clinical
> assessment and safety-planning process only.
>
> If you or someone you are with is in immediate danger, contact local emergency
> services or a crisis line now (e.g. 988 in the US, 116 123 Samaritans in the UK/ROI).

**Goal:** understand this person's risk well enough to act on it — a formulation
that drives a collaborative plan — rather than assigning a "low/medium/high" label.

## 1. What the evidence says about prediction
- Risk scales and checklists **do not predict** suicide or violence in individuals
  with useful accuracy; most people who die by suicide were rated low risk. NICE NG225
  advises **against** using risk tools/scales to predict future self-harm or suicide
  or to decide who gets treatment or discharge.
- Use structured instruments (e.g. **C-SSRS** screening items, **HCR-20** for violence
  in specialist settings) as *aide-memoires* for a thorough history, not as scores.

## 2. The assessment conversation
- Ask directly and non-judgementally about suicidal thoughts: asking does **not**
  increase risk. Cover: current thoughts (frequency, intensity, controllability),
  intent, any planning or preparatory behaviour, recent self-harm and its context,
  previous attempts (most serious), and what has stopped them so far.
- **Access to means**: ask whether the person has access to things they have
  considered using (including stockpiled medication and firearms), as the basis for
  means-safety planning — no detail beyond what's needed to reduce access.
- Context: mental state (hopelessness, agitation, psychosis, command hallucinations,
  intoxication), recent losses or humiliation, debt, legal problems, isolation,
  chronic pain, recent discharge from inpatient care (a high-risk period).
- **Collateral** from family/carers and records, with appropriate confidentiality
  handling — many inquiries cite failure to listen to families.
- Violence: past violence (strongest marker), current threats, targets, substance
  use, paranoid ideation with distress, access to weapons, supervision.

## 3. Formulation, not a level
Write: **static** factors (history), **dynamic/modifiable** factors (current state,
substance use, social crisis), **protective** factors (reasons for living, family,
engagement, faith, future plans — note these can change), **triggers/scenarios** that
would increase risk, and **what would reduce it**. End with the plan each factor maps
to.

## 4. Collaborative safety plan (Stanley–Brown structure)
Written *with* the person, in their words, kept on their phone/paper:
1. Personal warning signs (thoughts, moods, situations).
2. Internal coping strategies they can use alone.
3. People and social settings that provide distraction.
4. People they can ask for help.
5. Professionals and services, including 24-hour crisis numbers.
6. **Making the environment safer** — agreed steps to reduce access to means (e.g. a
   family member holding medication, firearm storage away from the home). Lethal-means
   counselling is one of the better-evidenced suicide-prevention interventions.
Add reasons for living. Share with carers if the person agrees.

## 5. Crisis care and de-escalation
- Calm, low-stimulus space; one lead clinician; respect personal space; open posture;
  listen and acknowledge; offer choices; avoid arguing with delusional content.
- Medical causes of acute behavioural disturbance (delirium, intoxication, hypoxia,
  hypoglycaemia) must be considered.
- Restrictive interventions only as a last resort, proportionate, per local policy,
  with debrief and review. Rapid tranquillisation follows local protocol (**doses per
  current local guideline**).

## 6. Involuntary-care decisions (framework)
Most mental-health laws require: a **mental disorder**, a degree of **risk** (to
health, safety or others), that treatment is **necessary/appropriate**, and that a
**less restrictive** alternative isn't suitable. Document each element, capacity, the
person's views, carers' views and the alternatives considered (home treatment/crisis
team, voluntary admission).

## 7. Follow-up
- Contact within ~72 hours of discharge after self-harm or from inpatient care
  (commonly used standard, e.g. UK NCISH recommendation — check local).
- Treat underlying disorders; psychological therapy for self-harm (e.g. CBT-informed,
  DBT) where indicated.

## Gotchas
- A single "denies SI" line is not a risk assessment.
- Chronic suicidality (e.g. in some personality disorders) needs a longer-term plan,
  not repeated short admissions by default.
- Staff anxiety drives over-restriction; patient distress drives under-reporting.

## Verify
- [ ] Direct questions about thoughts, intent, planning and past attempts documented.
- [ ] Access to means explored and a means-safety step agreed.
- [ ] Collateral sought; formulation covers static, dynamic, protective and triggers.
- [ ] Written safety plan given to the person (copy in notes).
- [ ] Legal framework elements documented if involuntary care considered.
- [ ] Follow-up contact time set and owner named.
