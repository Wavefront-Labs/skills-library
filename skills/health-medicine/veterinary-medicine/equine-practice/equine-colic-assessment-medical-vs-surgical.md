# Equine colic: medical vs surgical assessment

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** identify the strangulating or surgical colic early enough for surgery to work,
and manage the majority medical cases safely on farm.

## 1. History and safety first
- Duration, feed changes, water intake, faecal output, previous colic or surgery,
  medications already given (owner-given flunixin masks pain and heart rate),
  pregnancy, crib-biting, worming.
- Colicky horses throw themselves down — position yourself safely, use a handler,
  consider sedation before rectal exam.

## 2. Triage examination
- **Heart rate:** > 60 bpm concerning; persistent HR > 60 despite analgesia strongly
  suggests a surgical lesion or endotoxaemia.
- Mucous membranes (pink → injected → toxic line/purple), CRT > 2.5 s, PCV/total
  protein, **blood lactate**.
- **Pain severity and response to analgesia:** pain that returns quickly after
  xylazine or isn't controlled by flunixin + alpha-2 is a key referral trigger.
- Gut sounds per quadrant; abdominal distension; rectal temperature.

## 3. Nasogastric intubation (diagnostic and life-saving)
- Pass the tube in every moderate–severe colic; establish a siphon by priming with water.
- **Net reflux > 2 L** is significant (small intestinal obstruction, ileus, proximal
  enteritis); leave tube in or re-intubate regularly — gastric rupture is fatal.
- Don't give oral fluids/oils if reflux present.

## 4. Rectal examination
- Sedate (e.g. xylazine or detomidine ± butorphanol per local dosing), buscopan to relax
  the rectum, lots of lubricant; tail tied/held aside. Stop if horse strains hard
  (rectal tear risk — the most litigated complication).
- Findings: distended small-intestinal loops (surgical until proven otherwise in adults),
  pelvic flexure impaction (doughy, often medical), nephrosplenic entrapment (colon
  over the nephrosplenic ligament), taut bands, large colon distension/torsion signs.

## 5. Ancillary tests
- **FLASH (fast localised abdominal sonography):** distended, amotile small intestine
  (> 5 cm, turgid loops), free fluid, colon wall thickness (> 9 mm ventral colon
  suggests large colon volvulus), nephrosplenic window.
- **Abdominocentesis:** serosanguinous fluid, **peritoneal lactate > blood lactate**
  (or rising on repeat) suggests ischaemia. Serosanguineous + high lactate → refer.

## 6. Refer for surgery (or hospital) if
Uncontrollable or recurring pain, HR persistently > 60, net reflux, distended small
intestine on rectal or ultrasound, abnormal belly tap/peritoneal lactate, worsening
mucous membranes or PCV, no improvement in 4–6 h of medical treatment. **Discuss
insurance and costs early** — delays for financial conversations cost horses.

## 7. Medical management
- Analgesia: flunixin meglumine (NSAID — note it masks signs for hours), alpha-2
  agonists, butorphanol.
- Impactions: enteral fluids via NG (e.g. repeated boluses of water/electrolytes if no
  reflux), withhold feed, walk; IV fluids for severe/dehydrated.
- Re-examine within a fixed time (e.g. 1–2 h) and set clear call-back triggers for the owner.

## Gotchas
- Relying on gut sounds alone.
- Giving flunixin before assessment and then misreading improvement.
- Skipping the NG tube in a painful horse.
- Rectal exam on an unsedated, straining horse.

## Verify
- [ ] HR, membranes, CRT, PCV/TP, lactate recorded and trended.
- [ ] NG tube passed; reflux volume recorded.
- [ ] Rectal, FLASH and belly tap findings documented when performed.
- [ ] Referral criteria explicitly checked; finances discussed.
- [ ] Recheck time and owner call-back triggers set.
