# Hyperkalaemia and hyponatraemia emergency management

> General clinical/professional reference, not a substitute for independent clinical judgement, current guidelines, or verified dosing references.

**Goal:** prevent death from the two commonest electrolyte emergencies in kidney
medicine — protect the heart in hyperkalaemia, protect the brain in hyponatraemia
(from the low sodium *and* from correcting too fast) — while finding the cause.

Doses are commonly cited adult regimens for illustration; use local protocols.

## Part A — Hyperkalaemia
### Assess
- Repeat if haemolysed or unexpected (but don't delay treatment if ECG changes).
- **12-lead ECG:** peaked T waves, PR prolongation, loss of P waves, wide QRS, sine wave,
  bradyarrhythmias. ECG may be normal even at dangerous levels.
- Severity (UK Kidney Association/Renal Association guideline, confirmed): mild 5.5–5.9, moderate 6.0–6.4, severe ≥ 6.5 mmol/L.
- Causes: AKI/CKD, drugs (ACE-I/ARB, MRA, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, CNIs,
  potassium supplements), tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis), acidosis,
  insulin deficiency (DKA), adrenal insufficiency.

### Treat in three steps
1. **Stabilise the myocardium** (if ECG changes or K⁺ ≥ 6.5): **calcium gluconate 10%
   30 mL IV** (the guideline recommends calcium chloride or gluconate at an equivalent dose of 6.8 mmol, given when there is ECG evidence of hyperkalaemia; 30 mL of 10% gluconate is about 6.8 mmol) over 5–10 min (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) (or calcium chloride 10% 10 mL via central/large vein (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked));
   repeat if ECG changes persist. Onset minutes, lasts ~30–60 min (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked). It does not lower K⁺.
2. **Shift K⁺ into cells:**
   - **Insulin–glucose:** 10 units soluble insulin in 25 g glucose (e.g. 50 mL 50% or
     125 mL 20%) over 15 min. Lowers K⁺ ~0.6–1.0 mmol/L in 30–60 min (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked). **Monitor glucose
     at regular intervals up to 12 h** (the guideline schedule is 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 360, 480 and 720 min; hypoglycaemia common, especially in CKD); the guideline gives 10% glucose 50 mL/h for 5 h (25 g) if pre-treatment glucose < 7.0 mmol/L.
   - **Salbutamol** 10–20 mg nebulised as adjunct (guideline: recommended for severe, suggested for moderate; caution with tachyarrhythmias/IHD).
   - Sodium bicarbonate only with significant metabolic acidosis.
3. **Remove K⁺ from the body:** stop culprit drugs; potassium binders (sodium zirconium
   cyclosilicate, patiromer); loop diuretics if volume-replete and urine output; treat
   AKI cause; **dialysis** for refractory hyperkalaemia, especially with oliguria.
Recheck K⁺ at 1, 2, 4, 6 and 24 h (guideline schedule, confirmed); rebound occurs as shift therapies
wear off.

## Part B — Hyponatraemia
### Classify fast
- **Symptoms first:** severe (vomiting, seizures, reduced consciousness,
  cardiorespiratory distress) or moderately severe (confusion, headache, nausea).
- Confirm hypotonic (serum osmolality < 275 (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)); exclude hyperglycaemia/pseudohyponatraemia.
- **Acute** (< 48 h, e.g. post-op, marathon, MDMA, polydipsia) vs **chronic** (> 48 h or
  unknown — assume chronic).
- Workup: urine osmolality (< 100 → excess water intake/low solute (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)), urine sodium
  (< 30 suggests low effective arterial volume (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)), volume status, TSH, cortisol, drugs
  (thiazides, SSRIs, carbamazepine, PPIs).

### Severe symptoms → hypertonic saline
- **3% NaCl 150 mL IV over 20 min** (European) or **100 mL bolus** (US), repeat up to
  2–3 times (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked), checking sodium after each, until symptoms improve or Na⁺ rises **4–6 mmol/L** (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
  — which is usually enough to reverse cerebral oedema.
- Then stop hypertonic saline and limit further correction.

### Limit the correction rate (all chronic cases)
- **Do not exceed 10 mmol/L in the first 24 h** (8 mmol/L in high-risk patients: Na⁺
  ≤ 105, alcohol use, malnutrition, hypokalaemia, liver disease) and 8 mmol/L in each
  subsequent 24 h (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked). (The hyponatraemia guideline texts were not readable from a free source in this review.)
- Watch for **aquaresis** once the cause is removed (e.g. volume repletion, stopping
  thiazide, cortisol replacement): urine output > ~100 mL/h means sodium can rise fast (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- Overcorrection → re-lower with 5% dextrose (e.g. 10 mL/kg over 1 h (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)) ± desmopressin
  2 µg IV (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked); some clinicians use a proactive desmopressin "clamp".
- Check sodium every 2–4 h during active correction.

### Mild/asymptomatic chronic
Treat the cause: fluid restriction (SIADH, e.g. < 800–1000 mL/day (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)), stop offending
drugs, isotonic saline only if hypovolaemic, urea or tolvaptan per specialist.

## Gotchas
- Calcium gluconate doesn't lower potassium — don't stop at step 1.
- Insulin–dextrose hypoglycaemia can be delayed several hours.
- Giving 0.9% saline to SIADH can worsen hyponatraemia (urine more concentrated than saline).
- Osmotic demyelination appears days later — prevention is the only treatment.

## Verify
- [ ] ECG obtained; calcium given if ECG changes or K⁺ ≥ 6.5.
- [ ] Shift therapy given with glucose monitoring ≥ 6 h; removal plan in place.
- [ ] K⁺ re-checked on schedule; culprit drugs stopped.
- [ ] Hyponatraemia classified by symptoms, tonicity, acuity; severe symptoms got hypertonic bolus.
- [ ] Correction target and limit written; Na⁺ checks every 2–4 h; urine output watched.
