# Grading cheese with an iron and diagnosing defects — score it, then explain it

**Goal:** a repeatable judgement of each batch against its style, with every defect named and
traced to a cause the maker can fix.

## 1. Taking the sample
- **Iron (trier):** a curved, sharpened half-tube. Insert at a consistent spot (side of a wheel,
  away from the centre on a block), twist half a turn, withdraw the plug.
- Look first: openings, colour uniformity, rind thickness, any rind-to-paste discolouration.
- Break the plug — note how it breaks (smooth bend, crumbly, short, rubbery).
- Smell the freshly broken surface, then taste a piece from the middle of the plug (not the rind end).
- **Return the rind end** of the plug to the hole and seal (cheese wax, rind paste) to keep the wheel sound.
- Soft cheeses: cut a wedge or sacrificial unit; cut through centre to see ripening from rind inward.
- Temper samples to ~15–18 °C; cold cheese hides aroma and over-states firmness.

## 2. Scoring
Use a sheet with weighted categories — competition and grading schemes differ but generally cover:
- **Appearance / rind / finish** — shape, rind integrity, colour, mould cover appropriate to style.
- **Body and texture** — firmness, smoothness, how it breaks and melts in the mouth.
- **Aroma and flavour** — intensity, balance (salt, acid, sweet, bitter, umami), cleanness, length.
- **Overall / typicity** — does it match the style benchmark or brand standard?
Score against the **style**: an aged Gouda's crunchy tyrosine crystals are a plus; in a young semi-soft
they'd be odd. Taste a reference (a known good batch or benchmark cheese) at the start to calibrate.

## 3. Defect lexicon and likely causes
| Defect | Description | Usually traces to |
|---|---|---|
| Acid / sour | Sharp sourness, often with crumbly, short body | Too much/too fast acid, too wet curd |
| Bitter | Lingering bitterness | Excess rennet, wet curd, low salt, some cultures |
| Flat / bland | Lacks development | Too little acid, too cold cave, too young |
| Pasty / sticky | Smears on the thumb | High moisture + high acid |
| Rubbery / corky | Springs back, tough | Low acid, low moisture, overcooked curd |
| Mealy / grainy | Gritty mouthfeel | High-heat cooking, acid, protein changes |
| Mechanical openings | Irregular holes | Cold/dry curd at moulding, poor knitting |
| Gassy / pinholes / splits | Many small holes, or splits weeks later | Coliforms early; yeasts; late-blowing clostridia |
| Fruity / fermented | Pineapple, solvent notes | Ethyl esters — yeasts, low-acid make (cheddar) |
| Rancid / soapy | Goaty-soapy beyond style | Lipolysis — damaged or old milk, excess lipase |
| Ammoniated | Sharp ammonia, browned rind | Over-ripe soft cheese, poor ventilation |
| Unclean / barny | Dirty, cowshed | Milk hygiene, silage, environment |
| Salt uneven | Patches too salty/bland | Salt mixing or brining |

## 4. Turning a grade into action
- Grade each batch (e.g. premium / standard / reject or a numeric score) and record it with batch
  ID, age, sampling spot and grader.
- Tie defects back to the **make sheet** (pH timings, temperatures, moisture) and **cave log** —
  patterns across several batches matter more than one bad wheel.
- Re-grade at set ages (e.g. 3, 6, 12 months) to see how batches develop and choose release timing.
- Any sign of contamination (unexpected gas, off odours, pink discolouration) → hold the batch and test,
  don't blend it into sales.

## Gotchas
- Graders differ; use two people and compare, and keep your own blind spots in mind (bitterness
  sensitivity varies a lot).
- Don't over-sample a wheel destined for sale — every plug is a potential mould entry point.
