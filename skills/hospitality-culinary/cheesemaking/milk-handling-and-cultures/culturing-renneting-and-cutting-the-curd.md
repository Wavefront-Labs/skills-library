# Culturing, renneting and cutting the curd — the first hours decide the cheese

**Goal:** a consistent coagulum and acid development make after make, so later steps (cooking,
draining, pressing, ripening) behave predictably. For dairy-plant milk treatment see
manufacturing-production.food-beverage-processing.dairy-processing.separation-standardisation-pasteurisation-and-homogenisation.

## 1. Milk
- **Quality first:** fresh, low bacterial count, no antibiotic residues (they kill cultures — test
  if unsure), from healthy animals. Off-flavours in milk end up concentrated in the cheese.
- **Raw vs pasteurised:** raw milk gives more complexity but carries pathogen risk (*Listeria*,
  *E. coli* O157, *Salmonella*, *Brucella*); many jurisdictions require pasteurisation or a minimum
  aging period (e.g. US 60 days at ≥ 1.7 °C for raw-milk cheese — check yours). Low-temperature
  vat pasteurisation (~63 °C for 30 min, cool fast) is gentler on curd than HTST (72 °C/15 s).
- **Avoid UHT** (won't set) and avoid homogenised milk for most cheeses (weak, pasty curd).
- **Calcium chloride** (e.g. ~0.02% of milk weight, diluted in water) restores setting strength in
  pasteurised or cold-stored milk. Add before rennet, never mixed with it.

## 2. Culture
- **Mesophilic** (lactococci, ~20–32 °C): cheddar, gouda, most soft cheeses.
- **Thermophilic** (*S. thermophilus*, lactobacilli, up to ~45–55 °C): alpine, Italian hard, mozzarella.
- Direct-vat-set (DVS) freeze-dried cultures: dose per supplier per litre; sprinkle on the surface,
  wait 2–5 min, stir in. **Ripen** (hold at temperature) typically 30–60 min, until pH/TA begins to fall.
- **Bacteriophage** (viruses of starter bacteria) stall acid production: rotate culture strains, keep
  whey away from the make room, sanitise thoroughly.

## 3. Rennet and the flocculation method
- Dilute rennet in cool, non-chlorinated water (~20× volume) just before use; stir in for ~1 min,
  then **still the milk** completely (top-stir briefly with cow's milk to stop cream rising).
- **Flocculation time:** float a light plastic lid or bowl on the milk and spin it gently every
  minute. When it stops spinning freely (milk starts to gel), note the minutes since renneting.
- **Cut time = floc time × multiplier:** ~2–3 for hard, low-moisture cheeses; ~3–4 for semi-soft;
  ~4–6+ for soft, high-moisture cheeses (uncertain; recipe-specific). This auto-corrects for milk variation.
- **Clean break check:** slide a finger or knife in at an angle, lift — the curd should split
  cleanly with clear (not milky) whey filling the split.

## 4. Cut, heal, stir, cook
- Cut vertical then horizontal (curd harps) or a knife grid + angled cuts to an even size:
  ~2–3 cm for soft cheese, ~1–1.5 cm for cheddar, rice-grain size for alpine/parmesan. **Even size
  matters more than exact size** — uneven curd drains unevenly.
- Let curd **heal** 5 min, then stir gently (curds are fragile at first).
- **Cook** by raising temperature slowly (about 1 °C every 5 min) to the recipe target; fast heating
  case-hardens curds and traps whey.

## 5. Track acidity
Log pH (calibrated meter; spear probe for curd) and/or titratable acidity of whey at each step on a
**make sheet** with times and temperatures. Target pH drops are recipe-specific (e.g. cheddar milling
around pH 5.3–5.4). Acid timing controls moisture, texture and safety.

## Verify
- [ ] Milk source, treatment (time/temperature) and antibiotic status logged.
- [ ] Culture type/dose and ripening time logged; pH falling before rennet.
- [ ] Floc time measured and cut time calculated with the recipe multiplier.
- [ ] Clean break; curds even in size; cook ramp followed.
- [ ] Make sheet complete with pH/TA at each step.
