# Assessing intoxication and refusing service

**Goal:** stop serving before someone is intoxicated, refuse when you must without
starting a fight, and keep both the guest and the licence safe. **Laws differ by
jurisdiction** (e.g. RSA in Australia, TIPS/ServSafe-type programs and dram-shop
liability in the US, licensing conditions in the UK). Complete your local certified
course; this page is the practical layer on top.

## 1. Watch early and continuously
Count drinks per guest roughly and notice pace. Signs commonly taught (several together
matter more than one):
- **Behaviour**: louder, over-friendly or aggressive, rude, swearing, annoying others,
  repeating themselves.
- **Coordination**: swaying, stumbling, fumbling money or phone, spilling, knocking
  things over, difficulty picking up change.
- **Speech**: slurred, slow, loud, losing train of thought.
- **Appearance**: glassy eyes, drowsiness, dishevelled, vomiting.
- **Judgement**: ordering doubles/shots fast, buying for strangers, drinking others'
  drinks, planning to drive.
Medication, disability or fatigue can mimic some signs; you still can't serve someone
who appears intoxicated, but be respectful and check whether they need help.

## 2. Slow down before cutting off
- Offer water and food; pace orders ("I'll bring water with this one").
- Avoid serving doubles or shots to someone who's escalating.
- Tell the team and the manager about a guest you're watching.

## 3. Refusing service — the script
1. **Involve the manager** where policy requires, and give door staff a heads-up.
2. Approach **privately**, calm and quietly, not across a crowded bar.
3. **Use "I" and the law**, not the person's behaviour: "I'm not able to serve you any
   more alcohol tonight — I'd lose my licence." Avoid "You're drunk."
4. Be **firm and brief**; don't negotiate or explain repeatedly. Offer alternatives:
   water, soft drink, food, a cab.
5. Don't let friends buy for them; make sure the whole team knows.
6. If they become aggressive, step back, don't argue, call security/manager. Your
   safety comes first.

## 4. Checking ID
- Check anyone who looks under the age your policy says (many use "under 25" or
  "under 30" challenge policies).
- **Accept only legally valid** IDs for your jurisdiction (passport, licence, proof-
  of-age card).
- **Feel and look**: photo matches (ears, nose, chin don't change with hair/makeup),
  date of birth, expiry, holograms/tactile features, lamination tampering, edges.
- Ask questions only the real holder would answer quickly (date of birth, postcode).
- Watch for **secondary supply**: an adult buying for minors.
- If in doubt, refuse. "No ID, no service."

## 5. Duty of care after refusal
- Don't eject a vulnerable, very intoxicated person alone onto the street. Help arrange
  a taxi, a friend, or a safe place. Discourage driving; you may need to call police if
  someone insists on driving drunk.
- Medical red flags (unresponsive, vomiting while drowsy, breathing problems): call
  emergency services, recovery position, stay with them.

## 6. Record it
Write an **incident log** entry: time, description of the person, signs observed,
action taken, who was involved, outcome. Regulators and courts look for this.

## Gotchas
- Serving the "one last drink" after noticing signs.
- Refusing loudly in front of friends — escalates fast.
- Accepting an ID because the queue is long.
- Assuming door staff will handle it without telling them.
