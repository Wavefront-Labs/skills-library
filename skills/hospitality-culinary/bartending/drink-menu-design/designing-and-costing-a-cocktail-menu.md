# Designing and costing a cocktail menu

**Goal:** a drinks list that sells, fits the venue, can be made fast by the team, and
hits the margin the business needs.

## 1. Concept first
Answer in one sentence: who drinks here, when, and why? A neighbourhood bar after work,
a hotel lounge, a dining-room aperitif list — each wants different drinks, prices and
speed. The concept decides length (often 8–14 cocktails works for most venues), style
and price ceiling.

## 2. Structure for balance
Cover the range guests actually want:
- **Light/refreshing** (highballs, spritzes), **sour/citrus**, **spirit-forward/stirred**,
  **rich/dessert**, and **low and no alcohol** options.
- Spread **base spirits** so one isn't dominant (unless the concept is a gin bar).
- Include a couple of **familiar anchors** (a Margarita/Negroni twist) next to the
  adventurous signatures — anchors sell, signatures make the reputation.
- Group by style or flavour, not by spirit, so guests can choose by mood.

## 3. Build for speed
Before a drink goes on: can a bartender make it in under ~60–90 s in a rush? Look for
shared prep (one house syrup used in three drinks), batchable components, garnishes
that don't need fiddly work. Every new bottle and prep item adds inventory cost and
waste.

## 4. Costing a drink
For each recipe:
`ingredient cost = (pour in ml ÷ bottle ml) × bottle cost`
Sum every ingredient — spirits, modifiers, syrups (cost per ml of the batch), juice,
bitters, garnish, even the soda.
**Pour cost % = total ingredient cost ÷ selling price (excluding sales tax/VAT).**
Example: 50 ml gin at $30/700 ml = $2.14; 15 ml vermouth at $20/750 ml = $0.40;
garnish and bitters $0.15 → $2.69. At a target pour cost of ~20%, net price ≈
$2.69 ÷ 0.20 = **$13.45**, then round to a menu price and add tax where it's displayed
inclusive.
Typical cocktail pour-cost targets are often quoted around **18–25%**, but your
business sets the number. *[Industry norm varies by market and venue type.]*
Look at **cash margin** too: a $16 drink at 25% (=$12 margin) can beat a $12 drink at
18% (~$9.84).

## 5. Pricing and placement
- Price in tiers rather than a single price for all, unless that's the concept.
- Don't list prices in a right-hand column aligned for comparison; prices following
  the description reduce pure price-shopping (classic menu-engineering advice).
- Highest-margin drinks go where eyes land first (top of a section, boxed features).

## 6. Naming and descriptions
- Name memorably, but the **description must tell the guest what they're getting**:
  base spirit, 2–3 key flavours, and style. "Rye, smoked honey, lemon — bright,
  smoky sour." Avoid ingredient lists nobody can parse.
- Flag allergens (nuts, dairy, egg) and non-alcoholic clearly.

## 7. Seasonal rotation and review
- Change a portion of the list seasonally (fruit, spice, lighter in summer, darker in
  winter) and keep best sellers.
- After 4–8 weeks, **menu-engineer**: plot each drink's popularity vs margin. Keep
  high-high; reprice or reposition popular low-margin; rework or remove low-low.
- Retrain staff and update spec sheets with every change.

## Gotchas
- Costing with the list price of the bottle, not actual paid price after discounts.
- Forgetting the cost of the syrup's sugar and the garnish.
- A menu that needs 20 new SKUs — dead stock.
- Descriptions that promise flavours the drink doesn't deliver.

## Verify
- [ ] Every drink has a written spec and costed sheet with pour cost %.
- [ ] Style and spirit spread checked; at least one low/no option.
- [ ] Each drink timed in a mock rush.
- [ ] Popularity and margin reviewed after the first month.
