# Tart shells, blind baking and choux

**Goal:** crisp, even tart shells that hold their height, choux that rises hollow and
stays crisp, and smooth set fillings.

## 1. Short pastry
- **Pâte brisée** (savoury/plain): flour, cold butter, salt, water. Rub or pulse
  butter into flour to pea-size pieces, add **just enough** iced water to clump.
- **Pâte sucrée / sablée** (sweet): creamed or sanded butter and sugar with egg/yolk;
  sablée is shorter and more fragile.
- **Work cold, mix little.** Gluten from overworking + warm butter = tough pastry that
  shrinks. Stop mixing when it just comes together.
- Flatten into a disc, wrap, **rest ≥ 1 h** (overnight better) chilled.

## 2. Lining a tart ring or tin
1. Roll to an even ~3 mm on a lightly floured surface or between sheets of parchment.
2. Lift on the rolling pin; ease into the ring and **push into the corner** with a
   floured knuckle — don't stretch the walls (stretched pastry shrinks back).
3. Leave an overhang or trim flush depending on method; dock the base with a fork if
   recipe calls for it.
4. **Chill or freeze the lined shell 20–30 min** before baking — the single biggest
   cure for shrinkage.

## 3. Blind baking
1. Line with parchment or a coffee filter pressed into the corners; fill to the top
   with baking beans/ceramic weights/rice.
2. Bake ~170–180 °C (fan ~160 °C) until the edges are set and pale gold, often
   15–20 min.
3. Remove weights; bake another 5–10 min until the base is dry and evenly golden.
4. **Seal:** brush hot shell with beaten egg (or yolk) and return 1–2 min — waterproofs
   against wet fillings. For chocolate fillings, a thin coat of cocoa butter or
   chocolate works too.
5. Trim the overhang with a microplane or sharp knife while still warm (if left on).
- Perforated tart rings + perforated mats bake more evenly and are common in
  professional kitchens; some pastry chefs bake without weights in them.

## 4. Pâte à choux
1. Boil water/milk, butter, salt (and sugar) until butter melts — full rolling boil.
2. Add flour all at once; stir hard over heat until a smooth ball forms and a thin
   film coats the pan base — **dry the panade 1–3 min**.
3. Cool briefly (to ~60 °C) in a mixer with the paddle, then add **egg gradually**.
4. **Consistency test:** a finger drawn through leaves a groove that slowly closes, and
   paste falls from the paddle in a "V"/ribbon. Stop adding egg there — the exact
   amount varies with flour and drying.
5. Pipe at 90° for profiteroles, 45° for éclairs, with even pressure. Flatten tips with a
   wet finger. Optional craquelin disc on top for even round shape.
6. Bake hot then lower (e.g. 200 °C → 170 °C, deck or conventional; fan lower) **without
   opening the door** for the first ~20 min. Bake until deep golden and light;
   pale choux collapses as it cools.
7. Pierce the base to vent steam; cool on a rack.

## 5. Pastry cream and custards
- **Crème pâtissière:** hot milk tempered into yolks/sugar/starch, then **boil 1–2 min
  while whisking** — the starch must boil or the cream thins later (yolk amylase).
  Cool fast, film on the surface, chill.
- **Crème anglaise:** no starch; cook to **~82–84 °C** (coats a spoon), never boil —
  strain into a cold bowl.
- **Baked custards** (crème brûlée, tart fillings): low oven ~140–150 °C, bake until
  set with a slight wobble at the centre. Core ~78–80 °C.

## Gotchas
- Soggy base: underbaked, no seal, or filling added to a cold shell too early.
- Choux that deflates: underbaked, too much egg, or door opened.
- Pastry cream must be held chilled (≤ 5 °C), dated, short shelf life.

## Verify
- [ ] Tart shell walls are vertical, even height, no shrinkage or cracks.
- [ ] Base is evenly golden and crisp after filling and chilling.
- [ ] Cut choux is hollow inside and doesn't collapse after 10 minutes cooling.
- [ ] Pastry cream boiled and thick; anglaise coats a spoon with no curdling.
