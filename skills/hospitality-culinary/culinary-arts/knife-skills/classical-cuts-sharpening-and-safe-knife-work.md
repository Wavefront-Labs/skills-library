# Classical cuts, sharpening and safe knife work

**Goal:** produce uniform cuts at a steady pace with a knife that is actually sharp,
without cutting yourself or anyone passing behind you.

## 1. Set up before you cut
- **Board stable:** a damp cloth or non-slip mat under it. A board that slides is the
  most common cause of serious cuts.
- **Colour-coded boards** where your kitchen uses them (UK/many kitchens: red raw meat,
  blue raw fish, yellow cooked meat, green salad/fruit, brown veg, white dairy/bakery —
  check the house scheme; it varies).
- Waste bowl and a tray for finished product within reach, so you never sweep with the
  blade edge (sweep with the spine, or the bench scraper).

## 2. Grip and guide hand
- **Pinch grip:** thumb and the side of the bent index finger pinch the blade just
  ahead of the bolster; other three fingers wrap the handle. Control comes from the pinch.
- **Claw (guide hand):** fingertips tucked under, knuckles forward against the flat of
  the blade. The knuckle sets the slice thickness; the blade never rises above it.
- **Make it flat first:** square off a round vegetable (a slice off one side) so it sits
  on a flat face. Rolling produce causes slips.
- Motion: rock (tip stays down) for herbs and fine dice; push-cut forward-and-down for
  most veg; pull/draw-cut for proteins.

## 3. Classical cut sizes (common Western-school reference)
| Cut | Size | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Julienne (allumette) | 3 × 3 × 50–60 mm | sticks, from 3 mm planks |
| Brunoise | 3 mm cube | julienne turned 90° |
| Fine brunoise | 1.5 mm cube | from fine julienne |
| Batonnet | 6 × 6 × 50–60 mm | |
| Small dice | 6 mm cube | batonnet turned |
| Medium dice | 12 mm cube | |
| Large dice | 20 mm cube | |
| Paysanne | ~12 × 12 × 3 mm | flat squares/triangles/rounds |
| Tourné | ~50 mm, seven faces | barrel shape, curved paring knife |

Sizes differ slightly between schools and exams (some quote 1/8 in = 3.2 mm,
1/4 in, 1/2 in, 3/4 in); use the standard your exam or head chef sets. Uniformity
matters more than the exact millimetre — even pieces cook evenly.

**Onion dice:** halve through the root, peel, horizontal cuts toward (not through) the
root, vertical cuts likewise, then slice across. The root holds it together.

**Chiffonade:** stack leaves, roll tight, slice thin across the roll; cut basil just
before use (it blackens).

## 4. Sharpening vs honing
- **Honing** (steel or ceramic rod) realigns a rolled edge. Do it often: a few light,
  alternating strokes at the edge angle. It does not remove much metal.
- **Sharpening** (whetstone) grinds a new edge when honing no longer restores it.
  1. Soak water stones per maker (splash-and-go stones need none).
  2. Typical progression: ~1000 grit to set the edge, ~3000–6000 to refine.
  3. Hold the angle consistently: roughly 15–20° per side for Western knives, often
     10–15° for Japanese — check the maker's spec.
  4. Grind one side until you feel a **burr** along the full length of the other side,
     then switch. No burr along the full length = not finished there.
  5. Finish with alternating light strokes to remove the burr; strop if you have one.
- Flatten stones regularly; a dished stone rounds your edge.
- Pull-through sharpeners are fast but remove lots of metal and can't do single-bevel
  Japanese knives.

## 5. Safety habits (the ones that get enforced)
- A **falling knife falls** — step back, never catch.
- Carry the knife point down, blade back, along your side; say "knife" / "sharp behind".
- Never leave a knife in a sink or under food; wash, dry, put away immediately.
- Cut away from your hand when using a paring knife in the air (e.g. tourné) and keep
  the work low.
- A dull knife is more dangerous: it needs more force and skids.
- Cut-resistant glove on the guide hand for mandolines and oyster work — always.

## Gotchas
- Wet hands or an oily handle: dry before cutting.
- Glass, marble or steel "boards" ruin edges in minutes.
- Dishwashers dull and loosen handles; hand-wash.

## Verify
- [ ] Board does not move when pushed.
- [ ] Ten pieces of brunoise laid side by side are visibly the same size.
- [ ] Knife slices cleanly through a tomato skin or a hanging sheet of paper with no tearing.
- [ ] After sharpening you felt a full-length burr on each side and then removed it.
- [ ] You can describe the house board colour scheme and the falling-knife rule.
