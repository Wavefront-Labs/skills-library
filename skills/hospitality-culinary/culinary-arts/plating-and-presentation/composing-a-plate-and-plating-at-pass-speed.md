# Composing a plate and plating at pass speed

**Goal:** a plate that looks deliberate, eats well (every bite reachable, nothing
soggy), arrives hot — and the fortieth one looks the same as the first.

## 1. Design the plate (before service)
- **Choose the plate first.** Size sets the negative space; white shows colour; dark
  or textured plates suit rustic food. Rim width eats into usable space.
- **One focal point** — usually the protein — placed where the eye lands, often slightly
  off-centre or at roughly "6 o'clock" towards the guest, with starch at ~10 and veg at
  ~2 in classic "clock-face" plating. Modern plating breaks this freely; the rule is
  that the eye has somewhere to start.
- **Negative space:** leave 20–40 % of the well empty. A crowded plate reads as cheap.
- **Height** gives interest — lean, stack or quenelle — but stability matters: a tall
  stack that falls during the walk is a re-fire.
- **Odd numbers** (3 or 5 elements of a garnish) usually read as more natural.
- **Contrast:** colour (one bright accent), texture (crisp against soft), temperature.
- **Eatability test:** can the guest get every component on one fork? Is the crisp
  element sitting in sauce and going soft?

## 2. Sauce placement
- Under the protein to keep the crust dry and frame it; beside it as a pool or swoosh;
  dots/squeeze-bottle for intensity. Not over a crust you spent effort on.
- **Swoosh:** spoonful of thick purée, back of spoon pressed and dragged in one motion.
- Viscosity matters: thin sauce runs into everything. Adjust thickness for the plate,
  not just the taste.

## 3. Garnish discipline
- Every garnish is **edible and relevant** to the dish's flavours. No raw parsley sprigs
  as decoration.
- Micro-herbs and flowers: few, placed with tweezers, last thing.
- Oil drops and powders last — they bleed or dissolve.

## 4. Write the spec
- **Spec sheet with a photo** of the approved plate, component list with portion weights
  (e.g. 160 g protein, 60 g purée, 3 pieces of shallot), plate type, placement notes.
- Pin it at the station. New cooks plate from the photo, not from memory.

## 5. Plating at speed
1. **Hot plates** for hot food (plate warmer, ~50–60 °C); cold plates for cold. A cold
   plate kills a hot sauce in seconds.
2. Set out all plates for the ticket at once in a line and build **component by
   component across all plates**, not plate by plate.
3. **Order:** sauce/purée base → starch/veg → protein (last, just rested and sliced) →
   jus/sauce over → garnish → wipe.
4. Use tools: ring moulds for neat bases, offset spatula for lifting, squeeze bottles,
   portion spoons that match the spec weight.
5. **Wipe the rim** with a clean damp cloth (some use a vinegar-dampened cloth to cut
   grease) — fingerprints and drips are the most common pass rejection.
6. **Check against the photo** at the pass; head chef or sous rejects mismatches.

## Gotchas
- Food plated under heat lamps keeps cooking and skins over; plate when the runner is
  ready, not before.
- Slicing proteins too early lets juice run into the plate.
- Squeeze bottles of warm sauce need a hot bain-marie; cold, they split or set.
- "Consistency over creativity" during service — no improvising on a plated dish.

## Verify
- [ ] Spec photo and component weights are posted at the station.
- [ ] Five plates of the same dish laid side by side are indistinguishable.
- [ ] Every component can be eaten on one fork; crisp elements still crisp at the table.
- [ ] Hot food on hot plates; rims clean.
- [ ] Ticket-to-pass time for plating stays within the house target.
