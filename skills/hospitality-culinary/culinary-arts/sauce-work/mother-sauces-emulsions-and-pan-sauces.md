# Mother sauces, emulsions and pan sauces

**Goal:** a sauce at the right thickness, seasoning and temperature at the moment it
hits the plate, and the ability to rescue one that is going wrong.

## 1. Roux-thickened sauces (béchamel, velouté, espagnole)
- Roux is roughly **equal weights fat and flour**. Cook white (2–3 min, no colour) for
  béchamel/velouté, blond for richer velouté, brown for espagnole. Darker roux thickens
  less, so use more.
- Rough guide: **~60–90 g roux per litre** of liquid for a medium sauce; adjust to the
  house standard.
- **Temperature contrast prevents lumps:** hot liquid into cool roux or cool liquid into
  hot roux, whisking. Hot into hot lumps.
- Bring to the boil (starch must fully gelatinise), then simmer gently **20–30 minutes**
  to cook out the floury taste, skimming. Strain through a chinois.
- **Nappe** = coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through leaves a clean line.
- Cover the surface (cartouche, film or a film of butter) to stop a skin.
- Derivatives: béchamel → Mornay (cheese), soubise (onion); velouté → suprême (cream),
  allemande (liaison); espagnole + brown stock reduced → demi-glace. Many kitchens now
  make a reduced veal jus instead of classical espagnole — follow the house.

## 2. Warm butter emulsions
**Hollandaise / béarnaise:**
1. Reduction first for béarnaise (shallot, tarragon, vinegar, pepper), strained.
2. Whisk yolks with a little water or reduction over barely simmering water until
   thick, pale and ribbon-like — around **65–70 °C**; above ~75 °C they scramble.
3. Whisk in warm clarified (or whole) butter slowly, then faster as it takes.
4. Season, lemon, hold **warm not hot** (roughly 55–65 °C) in a bain-marie or siphon.

**Beurre blanc:** reduce wine/vinegar/shallot to a syrup, then whisk in cold diced
butter off and on low heat. Above ~60 °C it splits; below ~30 °C it sets.

**Rescue a split warm emulsion:** start a fresh yolk (or a spoon of warm water) in a
clean bowl and whisk the broken sauce into it drop by drop. If it's merely too thick,
loosen with warm water.

## 3. Cold emulsions (mayonnaise, aïoli)
- Ingredients at room temperature; yolk + mustard + a little acid first.
- Oil in a thread at the start; once it thickens you can go faster.
- Too thick → a few drops of water or lemon. Split → new yolk, whisk broken sauce in.
- Raw-egg sauces: many jurisdictions require pasteurised egg in commercial service —
  check local rules. Keep chilled and date-label.

## 4. Pan sauces (à la minute)
1. Sear the protein; remove and rest. Pour off excess fat, keep the **fond** (browned
   bits). If the fond is black, it's burnt — don't use it.
2. Sweat shallot briefly, **deglaze** with wine/brandy (flame away from you, pan off
   the burner when adding alcohol), scrape.
3. Add stock or jus, reduce to just short of the target body.
4. **Monter au beurre:** off the boil, swirl in cold butter for gloss and body. Don't
   re-boil or it greases out.
5. Add resting juices, season, acid, herbs last.

## 5. Seasoning balance
Taste for salt, acid, sweetness, bitterness, fat. Flat = usually salt or acid. Too
salty = dilute (stock, cream) — sugar doesn't fix salt. Reductions concentrate salt:
season **at the end**.

## Holding and safety
- Roux and tomato sauces held hot ≥ 63 °C (UK) or ≥ 57 °C / 135 °F (US FDA Food Code);
  check your jurisdiction.
- Egg emulsions sit in the danger zone; most kitchens make small batches and discard
  after a set time (often 2–4 hours). Unconfirmed as a universal rule — follow your
  HACCP plan.

## Verify
- [ ] Roux sauce is lump-free, glossy, at nappe and has no raw-flour taste.
- [ ] Hollandaise holds without oil pooling after 15 minutes in the bain-marie.
- [ ] You rescued a deliberately split mayonnaise with a fresh yolk.
- [ ] Pan sauce glossy, coats the plate, seasoned after reduction.
- [ ] Holding temperatures and discard times logged per your HACCP plan.
