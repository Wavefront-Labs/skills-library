# Yield testing and plate costing

**Goal:** know what one portion really costs — including what you trim and lose in the
pan — and write the recipe so anyone makes it the same way at the same cost.

## 1. AP vs EP
- **AP (as purchased):** what you pay for, as delivered.
- **EP (edible portion):** what's left to serve after trimming and cooking.
- **Yield % = EP weight ÷ AP weight × 100.**
- **EP cost per kg = AP cost per kg ÷ yield (as decimal).**
Example: whole salmon £14.00/kg AP, 58 % yield after filleting and pin-boning →
£14.00 ÷ 0.58 = **£24.14/kg EP**. A 160 g portion costs ~£3.86, not the £2.24 you'd
get by multiplying the AP price.

## 2. Yield test (do it, don't copy book figures)
1. Weigh the item as received (AP).
2. Trim/fabricate as your kitchen does it; weigh each output separately: usable
   portions, usable trim (e.g. for stock or staff meal), waste.
3. Cook a sample if it's served cooked; weigh again for **cooking loss** (roasts often
   lose 20–35 %).
4. Record: date, supplier, staff member, weights, yields. Repeat 2–3 times and average —
   yields vary by supplier, season and who's cutting.
5. Optionally assign value to usable trim (reduces the effective cost of the main cut).

Published yield tables (e.g. *The Book of Yields*) are a useful starting point but
your own test is the number to cost from.

## 3. Cost the standardised recipe
For each ingredient line: **quantity (EP) × EP cost per unit**. Sum the lines → batch
cost. Divide by number of portions → **portion cost**.
Add a **Q-factor** (sometimes called "spice/oil factor") of ~2–5 % for seasoning, oil,
garnish and small items too fiddly to cost per line — some operators prefer to cost
everything explicitly.

**Standardised recipe sheet:** name, yield (number × size), ingredients with EP
weights (grams, not "a handful"), method in numbered steps, equipment, cooking temps and
times, plating spec/photo, allergens, holding and shelf life, cost date.

## 4. Price it
- **Target food cost %** (commonly 25–35 % depending on concept; check your business).
- **Selling price (net of tax) = portion cost ÷ target food cost %.**
  £3.86 + sides £1.40 = £5.26 plate cost; at 30 % → £17.53 net; add VAT/sales tax
  and round to a menu price.
- Also check **cash contribution margin** (price − cost) — see
  hospitality-culinary.culinary-arts.menu-engineering.menu-item-mix-analysis-and-repricing.
  Percentage alone can push you to underprice premium items.

## 5. Scaling
- **Conversion factor = desired yield ÷ original yield.** Multiply every ingredient by it.
- Scale by **weight**, not volume. Convert cups/spoons to grams first.
- Things that don't scale linearly: salt and strong spices (scale and re-taste),
  thickeners, leaveners in baking, cooking times (depend on depth, not quantity),
  evaporation (wider pans reduce faster). Test-cook a scaled batch before relying on it.

## 6. Recipe development discipline
- Change one variable per test; write the result down.
- Taste panel of 2–3 people, scored; record what changed.
- Final recipe gets a version number and date; old versions archived.

## Gotchas
- Costs go stale — re-cost key items monthly or when a supplier invoice moves > ~5 %.
- Forgetting packaging, garnish and sauce on the plate makes cost look 10–20 % lower.
- Cooked-weight portions vs raw-weight portions: state which on the recipe.

## Verify
- [ ] Each protein and big-ticket veg has a dated, repeated yield test on file.
- [ ] Portion costs use EP cost, not AP.
- [ ] Recipe card weighs every ingredient in grams and lists allergens.
- [ ] A scaled batch was test-cooked and tasted before use.
- [ ] Menu price checked against both target food cost % and cash margin.
