# Running bench blending trials — build it in the glass, prove it blind, scale it exactly

**Goal:** turn many lots (varieties, blocks, barrels, press fractions) into a final wine better
than its parts — or matching a house style year after year — by a repeatable bench process,
not by instinct at the tank.

## 1. Know your components
- Gather a representative sample from every lot (thief from several barrels, mixed by volume).
  Label each: lot code, variety, block, vessel, volume available, recent analysis (alcohol, pH,
  TA, VA, RS, free SO₂).
- **Taste each component alone** and write a short profile: aroma, fruit, acid, tannin,
  oak, length, faults. Rank them A/B/C for the target wine. Anything faulty (VA, Brett, mousy)
  stays out — blending rarely hides faults and usually spreads them.
- Think in roles: core (most of the blend, most volume), structure (tannin, acid), aromatic
  lift, texture/mid-palate, and minor seasoning (press wine, a few % of something bold).

## 2. Build trials
- Tools: graduated cylinders and pipettes (or a 100 mL base with 1 mL resolution), clean glasses,
  spit buckets, water, plain crackers. Blend **by volume, measured**, never by eye.
- Start with the core and its percentage that the volumes allow; make 3–5 variants changing **one
  component at a time** in steps of ~5% (then 1–2% when fine-tuning).
- Name trials with codes (B1, B2…) and record the exact recipe for each.
- Let blends sit 15–30 min (or overnight for reds) — components "knit" and the first taste misleads.

## 3. Taste blind
- Pour coded samples; a colleague randomises the order. Include the best single component and,
  for house-style wines, last year's wine or a reference bottle as a control.
- Score and rank; triangle-test the top two if the difference is subtle.
- Taste on more than one day if it's a major decision. Palate fatigue sets in after ~10–15 wines;
  break, rinse, reset.

## 4. Check the rules before signing off
Label claims usually require minimum percentages — e.g. a varietal name on the label often needs
75–85% of that variety, a vintage 85–95% of that year, an appellation 85–100% from that area,
depending on country (check yours: US TTB, EU and Australian rules differ). Run the blend sheet
against them. Also check volume: does the chosen recipe use what you actually have, with the
remainder going somewhere sensible?

## 5. Scale to the cellar
`litres of lot X = blend % × final volume` — written on a blend sheet that a second person
checks, including topping losses and lees left behind. Blend in a clean, purged tank, mix
thoroughly, then **take a sample of the tank and compare it to the bench blend** blind. Re-analyse
(pH, TA, alcohol, SO₂); blending shifts pH and SO₂ binding, so adjust free SO₂ afterwards.
Allow some weeks for the blend to settle before fining/stabilisation trials (combining wines can
make them protein- or tartrate-unstable even if each part was stable).

## 6. Non-vintage / house style
Keep reserve wines from previous years and a library reference of the house wine. Each year,
blend to match the reference, not to make the "best" wine — consistency is the product.

## Verify
- [ ] Every component tasted and analysed; faulty lots excluded.
- [ ] Trial recipes recorded with measured volumes; final choice selected blind.
- [ ] Label-law percentages and available volumes checked on the blend sheet.
- [ ] Tank blend matches the bench blend in a blind comparison; post-blend analysis logged.
