# Barrel regimes, topping, racking and lees work — élevage without spoilage

**Goal:** between fermentation and bottling, add the oak, texture and slow oxygen the style
needs while keeping the wine full, protected and free of acetic and *Brettanomyces* spoilage.
Coopering itself (how barrels are made and toasted) is in handcrafts.coopering.toasting-charring.toast-and-char-levels-and-leak-testing.

## 1. Choosing vessels
- **Size:** 225 L barrique / 228 L Burgundy pièce gives the most oak and oxygen per litre;
  300–500 L puncheons and 2 000 L+ foudres are gentler. Stainless, concrete and clay vessels
  provide élevage with little or no oak.
- **Oak source:** French (*Quercus petraea/robur*) — finer tannin, spice; American (*Q. alba*) —
  more vanilla/coconut (lactones), sweeter. Eastern European and others in between.
- **Toast:** light (more oak tannin, fresh wood), medium/medium+ (vanilla, toast, caramel),
  heavy (coffee, smoke, less wood tannin). Head toast optional.
- **New-oak %:** new barrels give most flavour; by the 3rd–4th fill they are close to "neutral".
  Plan a rotation so each vintage gets a consistent new-oak %.

## 2. Topping (the most important routine)
Wine evaporates through the wood (the "angel's share" — 2–5% a year depending on humidity and
temperature). Headspace = oxygen = acetic bacteria and film yeast.
- Top up **every 2–6 weeks** (more often in a dry, warm room) with the same or a compatible
  wine, kept under inert gas. Never top with a wine of unknown condition.
- Bung position: bung-up with silicone bungs for active wine; "bung-over" (rolled to the side)
  keeps the bung wet and reduces topping frequency (uncertain whether it reduces ullage much).
- Barrel room: ~12–15 °C, ~70–80% RH. Very dry rooms increase losses; very humid ones grow mould on staves.

## 3. Racking
Move wine off settled lees to clarify and aerate gently.
- Typical: after MLF, then every 3–6 months for reds; less (or never, until bottling) for wines raised on lees.
- Rack with a wand or by gas pressure, not a high-shear pump; watch the sight glass and stop
  when it clouds. Purge the receiving vessel with CO₂/argon/N₂ when you want to limit oxygen.
- Taste the lees; smell for H₂S. Reductive (eggy, struck-match) wine benefits from an aerative racking.

## 4. Lees work (bâtonnage)
Stirring fine lees releases mannoproteins (rounder texture, some protein/tartrate stability) and
consumes oxygen (protects fresh fruit). Classic for Chardonnay: weekly to fortnightly at first,
then monthly. Stop if reduction (H₂S) builds; stir more if it doesn't. Gross lees (first, heavy
sediment) is usually removed first — it is more prone to H₂S.

## 5. Oxygen management in tank
Micro-oxygenation (MOX) doses tiny measured amounts of O₂ (e.g. a few mL/L/month) through a
diffuser to soften tannins and stabilise colour in reds; done in tall tanks, monitored by
taste and dissolved O₂. Over-dosing gives flat, oxidised wine.

## 6. Hygiene and *Brettanomyces*
- **Brett** produces 4-ethylphenol (barnyard, sticking-plaster, horse) and thrives in warm,
  high-pH, low-SO₂ wine with some residual sugar. Keep **molecular SO₂ adequate**, wines dry,
  barrels topped, and test regularly (lab 4-EP or PCR).
- Empty barrels: rinse, drain, dry, then burn a sulphur wick or add SO₂ gas and store bunged;
  re-sulphur every ~4–6 weeks. Before reuse, rinse and swell-test for leaks.
- Infected barrels are very hard to rescue (Brett lives several mm into the wood); isolate or retire them.
