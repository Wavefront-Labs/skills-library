# Cutting steaks and roasts to weight — consistent portions, clean presentation

**Goal:** turn a subprimal (striploin, tenderloin, rump, pork loin, lamb rack) into
portions that land within ±5 g (steaks) or ±2% (roasts) of spec, look identical on the
plate or counter, and cook evenly.

## 1. Prepare the subprimal
- Meat cold (core ≤ 5 °C, ideally 1–3 °C): it holds its shape and slices square.
- **Trim to spec first:** external fat to the agreed cover (commonly 3–6 mm on a
  striploin, or "0" for a fillet), remove the chain on a tenderloin, the "tail" or
  vein steak end on a striploin if spec says so. Weigh the trimmed piece.
- **Denude silverskin:** slip the tip under one end of the skin, grip that tab,
  angle the blade ~10–15° *up* toward the skin and push forward while pulling the
  tab back. Angle down and you gouge meat; angle too high and you leave skin.
- **Square up:** trim irregular ends into trim so every portion has the same face.

## 2. Work out thickness from weight
Cut one test steak at your best guess, weigh it, and adjust:
`thickness needed = test thickness × (target g ÷ test g)`.
Re-check every 4–5 portions because the muscle tapers. On a tapering fillet, cut
the thin end thicker (or use it for tournedos/medallions) so weights stay on spec;
butterfly thin portions rather than stacking two.

## 3. Cut
- **Across the grain.** Identify fibre direction first; a steak cut along the grain
  is chewy whatever you do later.
- Use the full length of a sharp steak knife or cimeter in **one long draw stroke**,
  not sawing. Sawing leaves ridged faces that look poor on display.
- For bone-in chops, cut between ribs with the knife, then finish through the
  chine with a bandsaw or cleaver; or have the chine removed first ("French"
  trimmed racks).
- Weigh every portion on a calibrated portion scale (tare the paper). Under-weight
  pieces go to a secondary use (stir-fry, kebab), never padded with trim.

## 4. Tying roasts
- Roll to an even cylinder so it cooks evenly. Tuck thin flaps under.
- Butcher's knot: loop string around the roast, tie a surgeon's (double-wrap) knot,
  pull to tension, then lock with a half hitch. Space ties ~2.5–3 cm apart, firm
  but not cutting into the surface. Or run a continuous loop tie (hitched
  repeatedly along the length) for speed, finishing with one lengthwise pass.
- Elastic netting is faster for volume; it marks the surface more.

## 5. Display and storage
- Steaks laid shingled, cut face up, same direction; parsley/plastic grass only
  as your local code permits. Raw poultry never above red meat; separate trays
  and tongs per species.
- Label with cut, weight, price, packed date. Air exposure blooms beef cherry red;
  vacuum-packed beef is purple until opened — that's normal, not spoilage.

## Gotchas
- **Cutting while meat is warm** gives uneven, slumped portions and drifting weights.
- **Blunt knives tear fibres** — steel before every session, sharpen daily.
- **Portion spec creep:** a 10 g overweight on a 250 g steak is a 4% margin loss
  across hundreds of covers. Keep the scale on the bench, not across the room.
- Tie strings too tight and the roast bulges between them into odd shapes.

## Verify
- [ ] Ten consecutive portions within spec tolerance on the scale.
- [ ] Faces are smooth, square and cut across the grain.
- [ ] No silverskin left; fat cover even and to spec.
- [ ] Roast holds an even diameter; knots don't slip when pulled.
- [ ] Trim weighed and logged so portion yield can be costed (see
  hospitality-culinary.butchery.primal-breakdown.seam-butchery-and-yield-tracking).
