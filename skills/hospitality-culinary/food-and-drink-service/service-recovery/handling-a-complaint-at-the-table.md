# Handling a complaint at the table

**Goal:** turn "this is wrong" into a guest who leaves feeling looked after —
quickly, proportionately, and without giving away the house or arguing with them.

## 1. The core sequence (a version of LEARN / HEARD / BLAST)
1. **Listen.** Let them finish. Don't explain or interrupt, even if you know the
   cause. Crouch or sit to eye level if the venue allows; standing over a seated
   guest reads as confrontation.
2. **Acknowledge** their feeling in your own words: "You waited 40 minutes for
   mains, that's not OK." Repeating the facts proves you heard.
3. **Apologise** for the experience, without blaming the kitchen, a colleague, or
   the guest. "I'm sorry" costs nothing; "the kitchen's slammed" sounds like an excuse.
4. **Fix it** — offer a concrete remedy, ideally a choice: "I can have the chef
   cook a fresh one, about 8 minutes, or bring you something else straight away."
5. **Follow through and check back** after the fix lands. The recovery fails most
   often here, when nobody returns.

## 2. When to get the manager
Get them early for anything involving: illness or a foreign object in food, an
allergy, injury, a large comp, a guest asking for the manager, or a guest who is
becoming aggressive. A manager visit itself signals the complaint is taken seriously.

## 3. Comp judgement — match it to the failure
| Failure | Typical proportionate response |
|---|---|
| One dish wrong / cold / overcooked | Replace it; remove from bill if they didn't eat it |
| Long wait, guest annoyed | Apology + a round of drinks, a dessert, or a starter on the house |
| Visit seriously spoiled (several failures) | Comp the affected courses or a % off; manager visit |
| Food safety (hair, glass, illness) | Remove the dish, manager handles, record incident; comp generously |
Rules of thumb: fix before you discount; comp what was actually affected; a gesture
the guest didn't ask for is worth more than a negotiated one. Know your authority
limit and the POS void/comp reason codes; every comp is logged with a reason.

## 4. De-escalation
- Lower your voice and slow down; people mirror you.
- Use their name if you know it. Don't say "calm down".
- Offer to move the conversation somewhere quieter if it's affecting other tables.
- Don't promise what you can't deliver.
- If a guest becomes abusive or threatening, safety comes first: disengage, get the
  manager or security, and don't physically intervene. House policy should cover
  refusing service.

## 5. Guests trying it on
Some complaints are attempts at a free meal (ate the whole dish, then complained).
Still be polite. Offer a fix (a replacement, a dessert) rather than a full comp, and
log it — patterns across visits are what matter. Don't accuse anyone at the table.

## 6. After the guest leaves
- Log it: table, time, what went wrong, what was given, who handled it.
- Tell the kitchen/team the specific cause so it doesn't repeat.
- Follow up if you have contact details and the failure was significant: a short
  personal note or call, an invitation back. Respond to an online review publicly
  once, briefly, without arguing facts, then take it offline.

## Gotchas
- Explaining *why* before the guest feels heard sounds defensive.
- Throwing comps at every complaint trains both guests and staff badly.
- The fix lands and nobody checks back — the guest leaves still angry.
- Arguing about the steak's doneness: if they say it's overcooked, re-cook it.

## Verify
- [ ] Guest finished speaking before you replied.
- [ ] Apology given without blaming anyone.
- [ ] A concrete fix offered (ideally a choice) and delivered.
- [ ] Comp proportionate, within your authority, logged with a reason code.
- [ ] Someone checked back after the fix.
- [ ] Cause passed to the team; incident logged.

*Uncertain:* the comp table is a common-practice guide; actual comp policy is set by
each venue.
