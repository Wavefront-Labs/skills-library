# Planning sections and running the floor in service

**Goal:** every table is greeted, fed and turned at the right pace, the kitchen
never receives 60 covers in ten minutes, and no server is drowning while another
stands at the bar.

## 1. Before service: build sections
- **Balance by covers, not tables.** A section of four 2-tops and one 6-top
  (14 seats) is a sensible starting load for a casual server; fine dining runs
  smaller. Adjust for the server's experience.
- Keep sections **physically contiguous** so a server can see all their tables
  from one point, and close to their side station.
- Give strong servers the big tables and the awkward corner; give new staff a
  tight, visible section near you.
- Assign closers, side-work, and who covers the bar or large party.
- Check the book: reservations by time slot, large parties, VIPs, allergies noted
  in the system, and walk-in capacity you're holding back.

## 2. Pre-service briefing (5–10 minutes)
Cover, in this order: covers expected and peak times · large parties and VIPs ·
specials, with **allergens** and prices · 86'd items · section assignments ·
one focus for tonight (e.g. "check-backs within two minutes"). Have the kitchen
taste specials with the floor if at all possible. Keep it short and standing.

## 3. Seating rotation and pacing
- Seat in **rotation** across sections ("one to A, one to B, one to C") so no single
  server is triple-sat (three tables in minutes, all needing greeting at once).
- Stagger arrivals into the kitchen: if the book has 30 covers at 19:00, spread
  walk-ins and hold a table for 5 minutes rather than seat everyone at once. A kitchen
  that gets orders in a wave sends food in a wave — and then everyone waits.
- Track each table's stage (sat, ordered, mains, dessert, bill) — the POS or a floor
  plan with markers. Know which tables will turn next for the waitlist.
- Quote waits honestly and slightly long; "15 minutes" that becomes 25 is worse than
  "25" that becomes 20.

## 4. Runners and the pass
- One person at the pass (expo) calls tables and seat numbers; runners deliver
  by seat number so servers stay on the floor.
- Expo checks every plate against the ticket: right dish, garnish, allergy flag,
  wipe the rim, then away. Hot food sitting under the lamp for more than a couple
  of minutes is a quality failure — find a runner or carry it yourself.
- Servers are told "your table 12 mains are up" if they're needed; otherwise
  runners just go.

## 5. Reading the floor live
Walk the room every few minutes on a loop. Look for:
- Tables with no drinks, closed menus and no server nearby.
- Finished plates not cleared; bills waiting on the edge of the table.
- A server with three tables at the same stage — jump in or send help.
- The pass backed up — slow seating for ten minutes.
Fix the problem first, debrief the cause after service.

## 6. After service
Two-minute debrief: what jammed, what to change tomorrow (section shape, seating
pace, a dish that held up the pass). Note it in the manager's log.

## Gotchas
- Seating the whole waitlist the moment tables clear = kitchen meltdown 15 minutes later.
- Sections that look equal on paper but have one 8-top — that's half the night's work.
- Managers who run food constantly stop seeing the room.
- Not telling servers about 86'd items until a guest has ordered one.

## Verify
- [ ] Sections balanced by seats and contiguous; large tables to strong servers.
- [ ] Briefing covered covers, VIPs, specials + allergens, 86s, sections.
- [ ] No server triple-sat; arrivals into the kitchen staggered.
- [ ] Nothing waits at the pass more than a couple of minutes.
- [ ] Every table's stage is known at a glance; wait quotes are honest.
- [ ] One concrete change logged after service.

*Uncertain:* section sizes and timings are typical industry rules of thumb, not
fixed standards; scale to your concept and kitchen capacity.
