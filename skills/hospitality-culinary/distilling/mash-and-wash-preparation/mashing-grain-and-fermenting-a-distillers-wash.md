# Mashing grain and fermenting a distiller's wash

**Goal:** a clean, fully fermented wash with the flavour you want in the spirit
— because the still concentrates whatever the fermenter gives it, faults included.

**Legal first:** distilling spirits requires a licence/permit in most countries
(e.g. US federal DSP permit, UK HMRC approval); home distilling is illegal in many
places even for personal use. This skill assumes a licensed operation.

## 1. Grain bill and cooking
- **Malted barley** brings its own enzymes (high **diastatic power**). Unmalted corn,
  rye or wheat bring starch but few enzymes, so a grain bill with mostly unmalted grain
  needs enough malt (often 10–15%+ malt in a bourbon-style bill — check your malt's
  spec) or added commercial enzymes.
- Starch must be **gelatinised** before enzymes can reach it. Approximate:
  corn needs cooking near the boil (~85–100 °C); rye and wheat lower (~65–75 °C);
  malted barley gelatinises at mash temperature. Typical sequence for a corn bill:
  cook corn → cool to ~70 °C, add rye/wheat → cool to ~63–65 °C, add malt.
- Rye mashes get very viscous ("gummy"); add rye slowly with good agitation, and
  consider a beta-glucanase enzyme.

## 2. Conversion
- Hold around **63–67 °C** for ~60 minutes for beta + alpha amylase working together.
  Distillers usually want **maximum fermentability** (more alcohol, less residual
  dextrin) — lean toward the lower end, unlike brewers seeking body.
- **Iodine test:** a drop of liquid on a white tile + iodine. Blue-black = starch
  remains; no colour change = converted.
- pH for mashing commonly 5.2–5.6; adjust with acid or backset.

## 3. Molasses and fruit bases
- **Molasses (rum):** dilute to a starting gravity often around 1.060–1.080; high
  sugar concentrations stress yeast. Add yeast nutrient; molasses may be sterilised
  or not depending on style (dunder/backset in some traditions).
- **Fruit (brandy, eau-de-vie):** crush or mill clean, ripe fruit; pectic enzyme
  improves juice yield, but pectin breakdown releases methanol (some pectinase
  preparations raise it), so fruit washes carry more methanol than grain.
  Pits/stones: keep crushing gentle to avoid releasing bitter compounds and cyanogenic
  glycosides — stone-fruit methods vary; **confirm with a stone-fruit-specific source.**

## 4. Fermentation
- Cool the mash to pitching temperature (commonly ~25–30 °C for distiller's yeasts;
  check the strain's spec) before pitching — hot mash kills yeast.
- Pitch a healthy, adequate yeast dose; aerate wort at pitching (yeast need oxygen
  to grow), then keep it anaerobic.
- Control temperature: fermentation generates heat; above the strain's range
  you get fusel alcohols and off-flavours.
- **Sour mash:** adding a portion of backset (spent stillage) lowers pH and helps
  consistency and infection control.
- Typical grain fermentation: 3–5 days; molasses and fruit similar or longer.

## 5. Gravity and ABV
- Measure **original gravity (OG)** before pitching and **final gravity (FG)**
  at the end with a hydrometer (temperature-corrected).
- Approximate ABV ≈ (OG − FG) × 131.25. A typical grain wash ends ~6–10% ABV.
- "Done" = gravity stable over 2 days, not bubbling stopped.
- Distil promptly; a finished wash left to sit invites acetic and lactic spoilage.

## Gotchas
- Uncooked corn → starch never converts, low yield.
- Mash temperature too high → enzymes denatured, stuck at low conversion.
- Pitching too hot — dead yeast and an infected wash.
- Infection (vinegar smell, ropy texture) → the still makes it worse, not better.
- Ignoring licensing.

## Verify
- [ ] Operation licensed for distillation.
- [ ] Each grain cooked at its gelatinisation temperature in the right order.
- [ ] Iodine test negative before cooling.
- [ ] Yeast pitched within strain's temperature range; fermentation temperature logged.
- [ ] OG and FG recorded; gravity stable before distilling.
- [ ] Wash smells clean (no vinegar or solvent).

*Uncertain:* temperatures, enzyme ratios and gravity ranges are typical values
from craft practice; follow your grain, enzyme and yeast suppliers' specs.
