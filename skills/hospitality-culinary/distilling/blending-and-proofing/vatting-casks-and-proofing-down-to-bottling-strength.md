# Vatting casks and proofing down to bottling strength

**Goal:** a batch that matches the house reference in taste, colour and ABV,
correctly labelled, stable in the bottle, and reproducible next quarter with
different casks.

## 1. Cask selection and the blend recipe
1. Pull samples of candidate casks; reduce each to the same tasting strength
   (~20% ABV) and nose/taste against the **reference sample** of the product.
2. Classify casks by role: backbone (most of the volume, house character), accent
   (sherry, heavy char, peated) and correctors.
3. Make bench blends at small scale (e.g. 100 ml total, measured by pipette or
   weight) until one matches the reference. Record proportions by volume **and ABV**
   of each component.
4. Check age statements: an age claim usually refers to the **youngest** spirit in
   the blend (true for Scotch whisky and many others — check your category).

## 2. Vatting
- Combine casks in the vatting tank per the recipe; mix thoroughly (recirculate or
  stir) and **rest** — many producers marry the vatting for days to weeks before
  reduction.
- Measure the vatting's real ABV with a calibrated density meter or alcoholmeter
  plus thermometer, temperature-corrected. **Obscuration** (dissolved solids from
  wood, sugar, colour) can make hydrometer readings read low; regulators often require
  a distillation-based method for strongly obscured products.

## 3. Proofing calculation
Ethanol and water **contract** when mixed, so volumes don't simply add. The simple
estimate:
`water to add ≈ V₁ × (A₁ / A₂) − V₁`
where V₁ = spirit volume, A₁ = current ABV, A₂ = target ABV.
Example: 1,000 L at 60% → 40%: 1000 × 60/40 − 1000 = **500 L** (approx).
Because of contraction and temperature, the result will be slightly off; add
**~90–95% of the calculated water**, mix, measure, then trim. Professional practice
uses official alcohol tables (e.g. OIML tables) or calculation software working by
mass.

## 4. Reducing in stages
- Use **demineralised or reverse-osmosis water**; mineral water causes haze and
  deposits.
- Add water slowly with mixing; large sudden reductions can shock out oils and make
  haze. Many producers reduce in steps with rest periods.
- Rest after reduction (days) before final ABV check and filtration.
- Labelled ABV tolerance is regulated (commonly about ±0.3% ABV in the EU/UK for
  spirits) — aim for the label exactly.

## 5. Chill filtration and other adjustments
- **Chill filtration:** chilling (often near 0 °C or below) and filtering removes
  fatty acid esters that cause haze when cold or diluted. Trade-off: some argue it
  strips mouthfeel/aroma; non-chill-filtered whiskies are often bottled at ≥46% ABV
  to stay clear. Test: dilute and chill a sample — does it haze?
- **Colour:** caramel (E150a) is permitted in some categories (e.g. Scotch whisky)
  and banned in others (e.g. US straight bourbon). Check before using and label as
  required.
- Additives like sugar or glycerol are regulated by category.

## 6. Batch consistency
- Keep a **reference sample** and a library of every batch.
- Taste each new batch blind against the reference with more than one taster.
- Record: casks used, volumes, ABVs, water added, final ABV, filtration, colour,
  bottle count, lot number.

## Gotchas
- Assuming volumes add — you end up overproof or underproof.
- Hydrometer read on an obscured spirit without correction.
- Tap or mineral water → calcium haze.
- Single-step big reductions → cloudiness.
- Age statement from the average age, not the youngest.

## Verify
- [ ] Bench blend matches the reference blind.
- [ ] Vatting ABV measured with temperature correction; obscuration considered.
- [ ] Water added in stages from a calculation, final ABV within label tolerance.
- [ ] Haze test done after dilution and chilling.
- [ ] Colour/additives legal for the category and labelled.
- [ ] Batch record and retained sample kept.

*Uncertain:* tolerances and additive rules vary by jurisdiction and category; the
dilution formula is an approximation — use official tables for compliance.
