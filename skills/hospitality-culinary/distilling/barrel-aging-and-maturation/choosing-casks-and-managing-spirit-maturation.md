# Choosing casks and managing spirit maturation

**Goal:** turn new-make into a matured spirit with the colour, flavour and
texture you intended — and know when a cask is ready rather than discovering it's
over-oaked.

## 1. Cask variables
- **Oak species:** American white oak (vanilla, coconut, sweet spice — the classic
  bourbon/whisky profile); European oak (more tannin, dried fruit, spice).
- **Previous fill:** new oak gives most flavour fast (US "straight bourbon" requires
  new charred oak containers); ex-bourbon is gentler; ex-sherry, wine, rum casks add
  their own character. Each refill extracts less ("first-fill", "refill").
- **Char/toast:** char levels (often #1–#4) create a charcoal layer that filters
  sulphury notes and a red layer of caramelised wood sugars below it. Toast
  (without char) gives baked/spicy notes. Heavier char = more colour and sweetness.
- **Size:** small casks (e.g. 20–60 L) have more wood surface per litre and extract
  fast, but can give a raw, woody spirit that doesn't round out. Standard barrel
  ~200 L; hogshead ~250 L; butts and puncheons ~500 L.
- **Legal:** many categories have minimum ages and container rules (e.g. Scotch
  whisky: at least 3 years in oak casks ≤700 L in Scotland). Check yours.

## 2. Filling strength
Entry ABV changes extraction: lower entry (e.g. ~55–63%) is often said to extract
more water-soluble sugars and colour; higher entry more alcohol-soluble compounds and
fewer casks needed. Some categories cap it (e.g. US bourbon ≤125 proof / 62.5% ABV
at entry). Record entry ABV for every cask.

## 3. Warehouse
- **Dunnage** (low, earthen floor, stone walls): stable, cool, humid; slow, even
  maturation.
- **Racked / rickhouse** (multi-level): top floors hotter, faster extraction and
  more evaporation; bottom cooler and slower.
- **Climate:** hot climates mature faster with higher **angels' share** (evaporation
  — commonly ~2% per year in Scotland, much higher in hot climates). In humid
  warehouses ABV tends to fall; in dry, hot ones ABV can rise as water evaporates
  faster.
- **Rotation:** some producers move casks between floors to even out batches.
- Fire, spill and security controls per regulations.

## 4. Sampling and judging the curve
- Sample with a thief on a schedule (e.g. 6 months, 1 year, then annually), from a
  consistent depth, into labelled bottles.
- Record ABV, colour, nose and palate at a reduced strength (~20%).
- Typical trajectory: early — raw new-make softening, vanilla and colour rise;
  middle — integration, fruit and spice; late — dry tannin, astringency, "woody".
  **Peak varies with cask, climate and spirit; use your own sample history.**
- Pull casks for bottling or blending (see blending-and-proofing) when they peak, not
  only when they reach a target age.

## 5. Finishing
Transfer matured spirit to a second cask (sherry, port, wine, rum) for weeks to
months. Taste frequently (monthly or more) — finishes can overpower quickly,
especially in active first-fill casks. Label claims about finishes may be regulated.

## 6. Records and cask management
Each cask: number, type, size, previous fill, char/toast, supplier, fill date,
entry ABV and volume, location, sample notes. Inspect for leaks; tighten hoops or
seal; log any losses for duty purposes.

## Gotchas
- Small casks marketed as "fast aging" — colour arrives before balance.
- Not recording entry ABV or location → can't explain batch variation.
- Finishing left untasted → sherry bomb.
- Bonded/duty records out of sync with physical stock.

## Verify
- [ ] Cask choice meets category rules (oak, new/used, size, minimum age).
- [ ] Entry ABV and volume recorded for every cask.
- [ ] Warehouse location logged; rotation policy decided.
- [ ] Sampling schedule followed; notes at reduced strength.
- [ ] Finishes tasted frequently and pulled on taste.
- [ ] Losses reconciled with duty/bond records.

*Uncertain:* angels' share, extraction behaviour and char effects are generalised
from industry practice; outcomes depend on your climate and casks.
