# Identifying beer off-flavours — taste, name, trace to the process

**Goal:** name what's wrong with a beer in a word a brewer can act on, link it to the step
that caused it, and catch drift before customers do.

## How to taste
- Quiet, odour-free room; no coffee, gum or perfume for 30 min before.
- Clear tasting glass, beer at ~8–12 °C (cold masks faults). Same pour volume each time.
- Order: **appearance → aroma (short sniffs, then swirl) → flavour → mouthfeel → aftertaste**.
  Many faults (DMS, diacetyl, sulfur) show first and best on the nose; let it warm and re-smell.
- Note intensity on a simple scale (0–5) and write descriptors before discussing — group
  talk biases panels.

## The core off-flavours
| Descriptor | Compound | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Butter, butterscotch, slick | Diacetyl | Early crash, weak yeast, *Pediococcus*, dirty lines | Diacetyl rest, forced test, clean lines |
| Green apple, emulsion paint | Acetaldehyde | Young beer, pulled off yeast early, oxidation | Time on yeast, healthy pitch |
| Cooked corn, tinned veg | DMS | Short/covered boil, slow chill (pilsner malt) | Vigorous open boil, fast chill |
| Wet cardboard, papery, sherry | Trans-2-nonenal etc. (oxidation) | Oxygen at packaging, warm storage, age | Low DO packaging, cold chain |
| Skunky | MBT (lightstruck) | Light on hop compounds — clear/green glass | Brown glass/cans, keep out of light |
| Medicinal, plastic, sticking-plaster | Chlorophenols | Chlorine/chloramine in water or sanitiser | Campden tablet, carbon filter, rinse |
| Clove, smoky (unwanted) | 4-vinyl guaiacol | Wild yeast or wrong strain (POF+) | Sanitation, check yeast |
| Vinegar | Acetic acid | *Acetobacter* with oxygen | Sanitation, air exclusion |
| Cheesy, sweaty | Isovaleric acid | Old, oxidised hops | Fresh, cold-stored hops |
| Rotten egg | H₂S | Yeast stress, some lager strains (often fades) | Healthy fermentation, time |
| Hot, solventy | Fusel alcohols | Fermentation too warm early | Temperature control |
| Metallic, blood | Iron | Water or unpassivated steel | Water treatment, passivation |
| Harsh, drying, tea-like | Tannins (astringency) | Over-sparge, high pH, too hot sparge | Stop runoff earlier |

Some descriptors are **style-appropriate**: low diacetyl in some British ales, clove in hefeweizen,
acetic in Flanders reds. Judge against the style or brand spec, not an absolute.

## Training the palate
Use commercial **spiking kits** (encapsulated reference compounds dosed into a neutral lager)
at threshold levels. Panellists differ widely in sensitivity — some people can't smell diacetyl
or DMS at all. Know each taster's blind spots and don't rely on one person.

## Quality tests
- **Triangle test:** three samples, two identical; taster picks the odd one. Used to check
  whether a process change (new malt lot, hop supplier) is detectable. Enough correct answers
  out of the panel size (look up a significance table) = a real difference.
- **True-to-brand (TTB):** each batch scored "true / not true" against the brand's written
  descriptor sheet and a fresh control, with comments. Log results by batch and date.
- **Taproom check:** taste the first pour from each line daily — line faults show up here first.

## Judging
For competition, follow the score-sheet structure (BJCP or similar): aroma, appearance, flavour,
mouthfeel, overall, with written feedback that names faults and suggests a likely cause.

## Verify
- [ ] Tasting notes written before discussion, with intensity scores.
- [ ] Every fault named with a likely cause and a process step to check.
- [ ] Panel trained on spiking kits; individual blind spots known.
- [ ] TTB results logged per batch; failed batches held, not released.
