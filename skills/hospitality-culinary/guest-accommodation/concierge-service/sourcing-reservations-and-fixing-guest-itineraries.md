# Sourcing reservations and fixing guest itineraries

**Goal:** turn "somewhere nice for dinner" or "tickets for tonight's sold-out show"
into a confirmed plan that fits *this* guest — and never send a guest somewhere
you wouldn't send your own family.

## 1. Qualify before you recommend
Ask four or five fast questions; the answer to "where should we eat?" depends on them:
- Occasion (anniversary, business, a quick bite before the theatre)?
- Budget per head and dress level?
- Dietary needs and cuisines they love or avoid?
- Time window and how they'll get there?
- Atmosphere: lively or quiet, view, outdoor?
Offer **two or three options** with a one-line reason each, not a list of ten.

## 2. Build and keep your knowledge
- Eat, visit and walk it. Recommendations from experience beat ones from review sites.
- Keep a living file (CRM, shared doc, or the desk's own system): venue, contact
  name and direct line, best tables, lead time, dress code, last visited, guest
  feedback. Update it when guests report back — that's your quality signal.
- Track closures, renovations, chef changes and seasonal openings.

## 3. Sourcing hard-to-get tables and tickets
- **Relationships first:** the restaurant manager or maître d' who knows your name
  and trusts that your guests show up and behave. Never no-show them; cancel
  promptly when plans change.
- Call rather than book online for difficult requests; ask about bar seating, the
  early or late slot, or a cancellation list.
- Tickets: official box office and its returns, then authorised resellers. Warn the
  guest about unofficial resale (fake tickets, inflated prices, name-checking at the
  door) — this varies by event and country.
- Be clear about price before committing the guest's card.

## 4. Itineraries
- Map it: travel times between stops at that time of day, opening hours, last
  entry times, and booking requirements (timed-entry museums).
- Leave slack — one fewer thing beats a plan that collapses at 3 pm.
- Put it in writing: a one-page plan with times, addresses, booking references,
  contact numbers, and the transport arranged.
- Fixing a broken itinerary (missed tour, closed venue, rain): offer a replacement
  of equal value and take the logistics off the guest — rebook, re-route, call ahead.

## 5. Confirmation and follow-up
- Confirm every booking in writing to the guest (card, message or email per house
  practice) with the name the booking is under.
- Log the request and outcome on the guest profile in the PMS — preferences you
  learn (window seat, no shellfish) make the next stay anticipatory.
- Next morning: "How was dinner at X?" Feedback is both service and quality control.

## 6. Commissions and ethics
Some venues and tour operators pay concierges commissions or incentives. If your
property permits them, **never let one decide a recommendation** and follow the
house disclosure policy. Guests lose trust fast when a recommendation was bought.

## Gotchas
- Recommending a place you haven't checked recently — it closed last month.
- A booking made under the wrong name or wrong date.
- Underestimating transfer times at rush hour.
- Promising a table you haven't confirmed ("I'm sure they'll fit you in").
- Booking with a guest's card without explicit approval of the amount.

## Verify
- [ ] Occasion, budget, dietary needs and timing asked before recommending.
- [ ] Two or three tailored options offered, each with a reason.
- [ ] Booking confirmed with the venue and in writing to the guest.
- [ ] Itinerary checked for travel time, opening hours and booking needs.
- [ ] Preferences and outcome logged on the guest profile.
- [ ] Follow-up asked the next day.

*Uncertain:* commission norms and ticket-resale legality vary by property and
jurisdiction.
