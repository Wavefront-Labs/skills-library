# Managing arrivals, transitions and departures at events

**Goal:** guests never queue long, never get lost, and never crush at a doorway
— because you calculated throughput and designed the flow before anyone arrived.

## 1. Arrivals: plan for the surge
Most guests arrive in a narrow window — often the majority within the first
20–30 minutes after doors. Plan capacity for that peak, not the average.
Throughput = service points × guests per minute per point.
- Name check from a printed alphabetical list: roughly 3–6 guests per minute per
  staffer (faster with QR scans) — **time a test run; these are estimates**.
- Example: 400 guests, 75% in 20 minutes = 300 / 20 = 15 per minute → about 3–5
  check-in points.
- Split lists alphabetically (A–G, H–M…) with clear signs; a separate "problems"
  desk so one issue doesn't block a line.
- Cloakroom is often the worst queue: numbered tickets, one rail-runner per ~100
  coats, a separate drop-off and collect line at departure.
- Something to do immediately after check-in (drinks, canapés) pulls people away
  from the doorway.

## 2. Seating plans people can read
- **Escort cards** (name → table number) or a large **table plan** board, sorted
  alphabetically by surname, not by table. Several boards for big events so people
  don't cluster at one.
- Table numbers visible from across the room (tall stands).
- Ushers near the plan to direct, especially for older or mobility-impaired guests.
- Accessibility: wheelchair spaces at tables with clear paths, near exits and toilets.

## 3. Room transitions (reception → dinner, plenary → breakouts)
- Announce once (MC or chime), then ushers physically walk the front of the crowd.
- Open doors **fully** and remove anything narrowing the route.
- Stagger if needed: call the far tables first, or by group.
- Allow realistic time — moving 300 seated is often 15–20 minutes.

## 4. Pinch points
Walk the route beforehand and look for:
- Single doors, stairs, lifts, corridor corners, toilets at interval time,
  bar positions next to entrances.
- Fix: relocate the bar, add a second exit route, hold people in the room with
  something to do, staff the bottleneck.
- Never let a room exceed its legal capacity; keep fire exits and routes clear —
  emergency evacuation planning is a separate, regulated discipline (follow venue
  and local requirements).

## 5. Departures
- Departures are slower than you think: coats, goodbyes, transport waits.
- **Stage the end**: last drinks call, then lights up gradually, then the DJ's last
  song; don't all-at-once.
- Transport: pre-booked shuttles with a posted timetable, a taxi rank or rideshare
  pick-up point that's lit and away from the road; staff with a list.
- Cloakroom collect line open early and separately staffed.
- Lost property collected and logged.

## 6. During the event
Assign one person to watch flow (not serve): radio contact with door, cloak, ushers.
Measure queue length at intervals; add staff or open another desk the moment one
exceeds your target (e.g. 5 minutes wait).

## Gotchas
- Planning check-in for average arrivals rather than the surge.
- Seating plan sorted by table number — 300 people scanning every table.
- Bar placed beside the entrance, blocking arrivals.
- Transitions announced once, then no one moves.
- No plan for coats at departure.

## Verify
- [ ] Peak arrival rate estimated; check-in points sized for it.
- [ ] Separate problems desk; cloakroom staffed for drop-off and collection.
- [ ] Seating plan alphabetical, multiple boards, readable table numbers.
- [ ] Route walked; pinch points fixed; capacity and exits respected.
- [ ] Transitions have ushers and realistic time.
- [ ] Departure and transport plan posted and staffed.

*Uncertain:* throughput and timing figures are planning estimates; time a test with
your own staff and tools.
