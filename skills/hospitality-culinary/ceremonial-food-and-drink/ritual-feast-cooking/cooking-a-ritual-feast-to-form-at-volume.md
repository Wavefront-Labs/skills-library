# Cooking a ritual feast to form, at volume

**Goal:** the dishes the occasion requires, in the form the community recognises as
right, ready at the moment the rite allows eating — for many more people than the
family recipe was written for — and nobody gets ill.

This is a method, not a catalogue. Ritual feasts (an iftar or Eid meal, a Passover seder,
a Sikh langar, an Onam sadhya, a harvest or wedding feast) each carry their own rules;
the specifics below are examples, and the community's authorities decide what is correct.

## 1. Establish the form before you cook
Ask the people who hold the tradition — clergy, elders, the family, the temple or
mosque committee — and write down:
- **Required dishes** and any that must be present symbolically (e.g. seder plate items)
  vs dishes that are customary but flexible.
- **Prohibited ingredients and methods** for the occasion (e.g. no leavened grain at
  Passover; langar is vegetarian; many Hindu festival meals exclude onion and garlic —
  practice varies by community).
- **Preparation rules:** who may cook, purity or separation requirements, blessing or
  prayer before cooking, fasting cooks, utensils reserved for the occasion.
- **Serving form and order:** e.g. a sadhya's dishes placed in a set order on a banana
  leaf; dates and water first at iftar; seated vs standing.
- **Timing:** sunset (iftar), after a service, after a procession. Get the exact time for
  the date and place.
Get sign-off on the menu in writing. If kashrut or halal standards apply, see
hospitality-culinary.ceremonial-food-and-drink.religious-dietary-preparation.

## 2. Document the recipe as it is actually made
Family and community recipes are often unwritten. Cook alongside the keeper of the
recipe once, **weighing everything** they add, timing steps, photographing stages and
the finished dish. Taste it with them and record their judgement words ("thick enough
that the spoon stands"). This becomes the standard recipe.

## 3. Scale it
- Convert to grams, then scale by a factor (see
  hospitality-culinary.culinary-arts.recipe-development-and-costing.yield-testing-and-plate-costing).
- **Portions:** at feasts people eat more and return; plan generous servings and
  ~10 % extra, more for open-door meals (langar, community iftars) where numbers are
  uncertain.
- Spices and salt don't scale linearly — scale at ~75–80 % and adjust by tasting with
  the recipe keeper.
- Split into batches your largest pots can handle evenly; a huge single pot scorches at
  the bottom and stays raw on top.

## 4. Build the timeline back from the moment of eating
1. Fixed point: the time eating may begin.
2. Dishes that improve with rest (stews, curries, many sweets) — the day before; cool
   and chill properly.
3. Dishes that need to be fresh (rice, fried items, breads like roti) — last, with
   enough hands.
4. Allow time for the rite itself: cooks may need to attend.
5. Equipment list: pots, burners, gas supply, hot-holding (chafers, insulated carriers),
   serving utensils per dish.

## 5. Food safety at volume
Large communal meals are a classic setting for outbreaks (often *Clostridium
perfringens* from big batches cooled slowly, or *Bacillus cereus* from rice held warm).
- **Cool large batches fast:** split into shallow trays, ice baths; never leave a big pot
  to cool overnight at room temperature.
- **Hot-hold ≥ 63 °C** (UK) / **57 °C** (US); reheat to ≥ 75 °C. Probe it.
- Rice: cook close to serving; if made ahead, cool within ~1 h and chill.
- Handwashing station with soap for every volunteer; no one cooks while ill.
- Allergen list for each dish, posted where people serve themselves.
- Check whether your venue or event needs a temporary food licence.

## 6. Running a volunteer kitchen
- One **lead cook** per dish who knows the standard; others are told exactly what to do.
- Brief everyone on both the ritual rules and the hygiene rules — they are equally
  non-negotiable.
- Taste before serving with the recipe keeper or an elder present.

## Gotchas
- "We've always done it this way" can hide unsafe cooling; respect the form, change the
  method of cooling.
- Substitutions (e.g. a different oil) may break a ritual rule even if they taste fine —
  ask first.

## Verify
- [ ] Menu and ritual rules signed off by the community authority.
- [ ] Each dish has a written, weighed recipe tasted by its keeper.
- [ ] Timeline lands food ready at the permitted time.
- [ ] Cooling, hot-holding and reheating temperatures probed and logged.
- [ ] Allergens labelled; volunteers briefed on handwashing and ritual rules.
