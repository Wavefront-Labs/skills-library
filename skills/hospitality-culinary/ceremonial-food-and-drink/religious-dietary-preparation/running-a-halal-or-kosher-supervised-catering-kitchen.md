# Running a halal- or kosher-supervised catering kitchen

**Goal:** food a religiously observant guest can trust, because the kitchen's sourcing,
separation and records satisfy the certifying authority — not because the menu says
"halal" or "kosher".

**The rule above all:** the **certifying body or supervising authority decides**.
Standards differ between certifiers (and, for kashrut, between Orthodox, Conservative
and other authorities; for halal, between schools and national bodies, e.g. on stunning
or machine slaughter). Never self-declare certification you don't hold. For a household
kosher kitchen see religion-philosophy.jewish-practice.kashrut-practice.setting-up-and-keeping-a-kosher-kitchen.

## 1. Before the first event
- Agree with the client **which certifier's standard** applies, in writing.
- Contact the certifier: what they require for a one-off event vs ongoing certification,
  supervisor fees, notice period, and whether your premises can be used at all.
- Kosher catering usually needs a **mashgiach** (supervisor) present; some require
  **bishul Yisrael** (an observant Jew lights the fire or takes part in cooking).
- Halal events may need a certified supplier list and an inspection; some certifiers
  require a halal supervisor for preparation.

## 2. Sourcing
- **Every ingredient** carries the accepted certification mark (hechsher or halal logo)
  on the certifier's approved list — not just meat. Keep the labels/invoices.
- **Meat and poultry:** certified slaughter and supply chain, sealed packaging intact on
  arrival. For kosher, also certified koshering (salting) and correct cuts.
- **Hidden problem ingredients:** gelatin (often pork or non-certified beef), animal
  rennet in cheese, L-cysteine, carmine/cochineal (an issue for some kosher standards),
  emulsifiers (E471 etc.) of uncertain origin, **alcohol** in vanilla extract, sauces,
  flavourings and wine reductions (halal), wine/grape products needing their own
  certification (kosher).
- For halal, items of doubtful status are **mashbooh** — avoid unless the certifier
  clears them.

## 3. Separation and equipment
- **Kosher:** complete meat/dairy separation — separate (often colour-coded) utensils,
  boards, pots, sinks or sink inserts, ovens or oven times, cloths. **Pareve** items stay
  neutral. Waiting times between meat and dairy are a guest matter, but menus usually
  avoid dairy entirely at a meat meal (no butter, cream or milk desserts — use pareve
  substitutes).
- **Kashering** non-kosher equipment (for an event in a non-kosher kitchen) is done only
  as the mashgiach directs: methods differ by material (boiling water, blowtorch,
  replacement); some items can't be kashered. Often a dedicated kosher kit is brought in.
- **Halal:** no pork, non-halal meat or alcohol in contact with halal food — dedicated or
  thoroughly cleaned equipment, separate storage, and preparation sequencing as the
  certifier requires. Fryer oil shared with non-halal items is a common failure.
- Label storage areas and keep certified stock physically apart.

## 4. Service
- Sealed or double-wrapped meals (common for kosher at non-kosher venues): sealed by
  the certifier, opened and reheated in front of the guest or as the seal instructions
  say. A broken seal = not certifiable.
- Serving staff briefed: no mixing of utensils, no outside food added to the buffet.
- Keep allergen and food-safety rules alongside — religious standards don't replace
  hygiene law.

## 5. Records
Supplier certificates (with expiry), delivery labels, supervisor visit logs, kashering or
cleaning records, staff briefings. The certifier can ask for them; customers may too.

## Sacramental food (briefly)
Communion bread/wafers, Orthodox prosphora and similar foods are made to the rules of
the church concerned (e.g. unleavened wheat for Latin-rite hosts; leavened bread with a
seal stamp for prosphora). Bakers usually work under the parish's or a religious order's
direction — confirm requirements with the clergy rather than general recipes.

## Gotchas
- "Halal-friendly" and "kosher-style" are not certifications; don't use them in a way
  that misleads.
- A single non-certified bottle of soy sauce or stock cube can break the whole event.
- Certifications expire; check dates on supplier certificates.

## Verify
- [ ] Certifier and standard named in the contract; supervisor booked if required.
- [ ] Every ingredient on site matches the approved list; labels kept.
- [ ] Equipment segregated or kashered/cleaned as directed and logged.
- [ ] Hidden-ingredient check done for gelatin, rennet, alcohol and emulsifiers.
- [ ] Seals intact at service; records filed.
