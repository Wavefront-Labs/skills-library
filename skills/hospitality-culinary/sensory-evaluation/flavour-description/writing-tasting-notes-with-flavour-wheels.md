# Writing tasting notes with flavour wheels

**Goal:** turn what you taste into words that another person can recognise in the
glass or cup — whether for a menu, a buyer, a QC log or your own memory.

## 1. Know what you're actually sensing
- **Taste** (tongue): sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami. That's all.
- **Aroma**: *orthonasal* (sniffing) and *retronasal* (from the mouth up the back of
  the nose while tasting/exhaling). Most of "flavour" is retronasal aroma.
- **Mouthfeel/texture**: body, astringency, carbonation, heat, creaminess, oiliness.
- **Finish**: what remains and how long.
Pinch your nose while tasting and release: what arrives on release is aroma.

## 2. Use a flavour wheel — centre to edge
Wheels exist for most products: e.g. the SCA/WCR Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel, the
Wine Aroma Wheel, beer flavour wheels, whisky wheels.
1. Start at the **centre**: broad category (fruity, floral, roasted, spicy, earthy).
2. Move one ring out: "fruity" → citrus / berry / stone fruit / dried fruit.
3. Move to the outer ring only if you can: "berry" → blackberry / raspberry.
**Stop at the level you're sure of.** "Red fruit" that's right beats "pomegranate" that
isn't. Precision grows with training.

## 3. Train your aroma memory
- **Aroma kits** (e.g. Le Nez du Vin, coffee kits): smell blind, name, check, repeat.
  Short daily sessions beat long weekly ones.
- **Real references** often teach better and cost less: zest a lemon vs lime, compare
  fresh and dried apricot, toasted nuts, cocoa nibs, black tea, cut grass, butter,
  vanilla pod. Smell them side by side with the product.
- **Spiked samples**: add a reference to a neutral base (e.g. a drop of vanilla in plain
  vodka/water) to learn the note in context.
- **Taste comparisons**: two coffees or wines side by side reveal contrasts you miss
  alone.
- Keep a notebook; vocabulary sticks when written.

## 4. Structure a tasting note
1. **Aroma** (dry/before tasting, then in the glass).
2. **Taste**: sweetness, acidity (and its type: citric, malic, tartaric/winey,
   acetic), bitterness, salt/umami if present.
3. **Flavour** descriptors (2–4 strongest, from the wheel).
4. **Mouthfeel**: light/medium/full, smooth/astringent, etc.
5. **Finish**: short/medium/long; what lingers.
6. **Overall**: balance and a one-line impression.
Add **intensity** to descriptors ("faint", "pronounced") — it's what lets two people
agree.

## 5. Notes that communicate
- Use **concrete, shared references**: "ripe peach, honey, black tea" over "joyful,
  luminous".
- Order by prominence; 3–5 descriptors is plenty for a menu or label.
- For guests: lead with the familiar ("chocolatey and nutty") and one hook ("with a
  cherry finish").
- For QC: be consistent — use your team's agreed lexicon so notes compare over time.
- Avoid negatives on guest-facing copy; keep faults in QC language.

## Gotchas
- Suggestion bias: reading the label's notes before tasting. Taste first, read later.
- Adaptation: smelling the same thing repeatedly dulls it — sniff short, then rest,
  or smell your own forearm to reset.
- Temperature changes everything; taste across a range.
- Colds, toothpaste, perfume and strong food ruin sessions.

## Verify
- [ ] You can identify ≥80% of a 12-aroma kit blind.
- [ ] Your notes separate taste, aroma, mouthfeel and finish.
- [ ] Descriptors move from wheel centre outward only as far as you're sure.
- [ ] Another taster can recognise at least two of your descriptors in the same sample.
