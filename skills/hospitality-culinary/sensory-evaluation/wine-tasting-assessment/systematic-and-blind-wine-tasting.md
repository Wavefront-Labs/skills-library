# Systematic and blind wine tasting

**Goal:** describe any wine consistently, judge its quality on evidence, spot faults
before a guest does, and — blind — reason toward grape, region and age. This follows
the shape of structured methods such as the WSET Systematic Approach to Tasting and
the Court of Master Sommeliers grid; use the exact grid your course or employer uses.

## 1. Conditions
ISO-style tasting glass (or good tulip glass), ~50 ml pour, white background, neutral
room, no perfume. Taste whites at cellar/cool temperature, reds slightly below room —
very cold wine hides aroma and faults.

## 2. Appearance
- **Clarity** (clear/hazy) — haze can signal a fault in non-natural styles.
- **Intensity** (pale → deep) and **colour**: whites lemon → gold → amber (age/
  oxidation/oak); reds purple (young) → ruby → garnet → tawny (age).
- Rim variation (watery or brick rim = maturity), legs (alcohol/sugar, weak evidence).

## 3. Nose
- **Condition**: clean or faulty — check *first*.
- **Intensity**, **development** (youthful / developing / fully developed / tired).
- **Aromas** in three clusters: **primary** (grape/fermentation: fruit, floral, herbal),
  **secondary** (winemaking: oak — vanilla, toast; malolactic — butter; lees — bread),
  **tertiary** (ageing: leather, earth, mushroom, dried fruit, nut, honey).

## 4. Palate
Sweetness, **acidity**, **tannin** (reds; level and texture — grippy, fine, coarse),
**alcohol** (warmth), **body**, flavour intensity and characteristics (confirm nose),
and **finish/length** — count the seconds the flavour persists after swallowing/
spitting.

## 5. Quality conclusion — BLIC
- **Balance**: fruit, acid, tannin, alcohol, sugar in proportion.
- **Length**: longer finish = higher quality, roughly.
- **Intensity** of flavour.
- **Complexity**: many distinct aromas/flavours, evolving in the glass.
Four present = outstanding; three = very good; and so on. State **readiness**: drink
now, can age, too old.

## 6. Faults to recognise
| Fault | Smells/tastes like | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cork taint (TCA)** | Damp cardboard, wet dog, musty cellar; flattened fruit | Any closure, but mostly natural cork; low levels just mute the wine |
| **Oxidation** | Bruised apple, sherry-like, flat; browning colour | Intentional in some styles (Sherry, Tawny) |
| **Volatile acidity (VA)** | Vinegar, nail-polish remover (ethyl acetate) | Low levels add lift; high = fault |
| **Brettanomyces** | Barnyard, band-aid, sweaty saddle, smoked meat | Some accept low levels |
| **Reduction** | Struck match, rotten egg, cabbage | Often blows off with air/decanting |
| **Heat damage** | Cooked/jammy fruit, pushed cork | Storage issue |
In service, open a suspect bottle for the sommelier/manager; replace without argument.

## 7. Deductive blind tasting
1. Assess structurally first: colour, acid, tannin, alcohol, oak, development.
2. Form a **shortlist** of grapes consistent with the structure (e.g. high acid, high
   tannin, pale garnet, red cherry, tar/roses → Nebbiolo).
3. Use climate cues: ripeness and alcohol (warm vs cool), acid retention.
4. Old World vs New World: earth/mineral/restraint vs riper fruit/oak (a weak heuristic).
5. Commit to grape → region → quality level → vintage range, stating the evidence.
Being wrong with sound reasoning is the training; being right by guessing isn't.

## Gotchas
- Calling a wine "corked" because you don't like it.
- Over-chilled reds tasting harsh and tannic.
- Palate fatigue after many reds — rinse, water, plain bread; spit.
- Leaning on sweetness of oak as "quality".
