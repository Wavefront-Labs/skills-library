# Concept, object selection and label writing

**Goal:** an exhibition that says one clear thing, with objects that carry the argument
and texts visitors actually read.

## 1. The big idea
Write it as **one sentence with a subject, an active verb and a consequence** (Serrell's
"big idea"): "Cheap printed cotton changed how ordinary people in 1800s Britain dressed
and saw themselves." Not a topic ("Textiles"). Every section, object and label must serve
it; anything that doesn't is cut, however beautiful. Test it on someone outside the field.

Also name: target audiences, what you want them to *feel/do/know* leaving, and two or
three key messages.

## 2. Object list
- Long-list from the CMS (search by vocabulary terms; see
  humanities.museum-and-archive-collections-management.cataloguing-and-documentation.object-records-numbering-and-controlled-vocabularies),
  then view in store. Records lie; condition and size surprise.
- For each object note: role in the story, size/weight, mount needs, **light
  sensitivity**, conservation status, any loan needed (start loans 12–18 months out),
  rights for image reproduction.
- Light-sensitive works (paper, textiles, photographs, natural dyes) often have annual
  lux-hour budgets (e.g. ~50 lux, limited months) — the conservator sets it. Plan
  rotations rather than dropping the object.
- Aim for "anchor" objects per section: one or two that show the point at a glance.

## 3. Interpretive hierarchy
Typical layers, each standing alone:
1. **Title / intro panel** — the big idea, ~100–150 words.
2. **Section panels** — one key message each, ~100–150 words.
3. **Object labels** — tombstone (maker, title, date, material, credit line, number)
   plus an interpretive label of roughly **50–75 words** that says what to *look at*.
4. Optional deeper layers: digital, catalogue, audio, tactile.
Word counts are common house norms (V&A and Serrell sit in this range); follow your
own house style if it differs.

## 4. Writing labels people read
- Start with what the visitor can see: "Look at the patched elbow..." Then why it
  matters to the big idea. Avoid starting with dates or biographies.
- One idea per label. Short sentences, active verbs, concrete nouns. Explain or remove
  jargon ("intaglio" → say how it was made).
- Write to the object, in front of the object — draft in the store with it.
- Voice: whose perspective? Name sources for contested history; consult the
  communities represented, especially for sacred, colonial or sensitive material.
- Accessibility: large print, sufficient contrast, type sizes legible at reading
  distance, label height for wheelchair users (check your national guidance), audio
  or easy-read versions.

## 5. Handover to design and install
Give designers: big idea and messages, object list with dimensions, weights,
environment and light needs, sightline priorities, all texts with word counts, and
mount requirements. Attend install; check every label against its object.

## Gotchas
- Text written to length at the end always overruns; set word budgets at layout stage.
- Credit lines are contractual for loans — copy them exactly.
- Evaluate: watch visitors (tracking and timing) and fix the worst labels after opening.

## Verify
- [ ] Big idea is one sentence and every section maps to it.
- [ ] Every object has a stated role, conservation sign-off and rights status.
- [ ] Labels hit word budgets and begin with something visible.
- [ ] Tombstones and credit lines checked against records and loan agreements.
- [ ] Accessibility formats exist; at least one outsider read-through done.
