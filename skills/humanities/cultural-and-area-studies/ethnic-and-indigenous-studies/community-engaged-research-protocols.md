# Community-engaged research protocols

**Goal:** carry out scholarship about an Indigenous or ethnic community in a way that the
community authorises, shapes and benefits from — and that won't be withdrawn, contested or
harmful after publication.

## 1. Before you have a question
- Learn the community's own governance: tribal council, land council, iwi authority,
  cultural committee, community association. Individual goodwill is not collective consent.
- Read what the community has already published and what earlier researchers did — some
  communities carry a long memory of extractive research and will ask about it.
- Find the applicable codes. Examples: Canada's TCPS2 Chapter 9; Australia's AIATSIS Code
  of Ethics; Aotearoa NZ Kaupapa Māori approaches and the Te Ara Tika guidelines; US tribal
  IRBs, which may run alongside your university IRB. *(Names current to my knowledge —
  check each for the latest edition.)*
- Approach with an open question and let the community shape it. Research that answers
  their priorities gets support; research that only serves your thesis often stalls.

## 2. Consent at two levels
- **Collective**: written approval from the governing body, often as a **research
  agreement** covering purpose, methods, data ownership, review rights, authorship,
  benefits, and what happens if the relationship ends.
- **Individual**: informed consent from each participant, in their language, with
  plain explanation of risks and of the right to withdraw.
- **FPIC** (free, prior and informed consent, from UNDRIP) is the reference standard:
  no pressure, before any work starts, with full information.
- Consent is ongoing — revisit it when the project changes scope.

## 3. Data governance
- **CARE** principles: Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics.
  Pair them with FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) — open data
  defaults can conflict with community control, and CARE takes priority.
- **OCAP®** (First Nations, Canada): Ownership, Control, Access, Possession.
- Agree where data lives, who can access it, and whether it is returned or destroyed.
- Use **TK (Traditional Knowledge) and BC (Biocultural) Labels** from Local Contexts to
  mark culturally restricted material in archives and publications.
- Some knowledge is restricted by gender, initiation status or season. If told something
  is not for publication, it stays out — including from appendices and data deposits.

## 4. Doing the work
- Employ and train community members as researchers, paid at fair rates.
- Use culturally appropriate methods (yarning circles, talanoa, story work) where the
  community prefers them, and learn their protocols before using them.
- Keep a log of decisions taken jointly — useful when people change roles.

## 5. Review, authorship and return
- Give the community draft findings with time to respond before submission; agree in
  advance whether this is a veto or consultation.
- Credit knowledge holders by name if they want it; co-authorship where contribution
  warrants it.
- Return results in usable forms: community report, plain-language summary, language
  materials, exhibition — not only a journal article behind a paywall.

## Gotchas
- "Ethnic studies" covers non-Indigenous minorities too (e.g. diaspora, racialised groups);
  the specific Indigenous codes may not apply, but the community-engagement logic does.
- University ethics approval does not substitute for community approval, and vice versa.
- Communities are not homogeneous; elders, youth and different families may disagree.
  Don't let one gatekeeper stand for everyone without checking.
- Funders' open-access mandates can collide with data restrictions — negotiate exceptions
  before the grant starts, not at deposit time.

## Verify
- [ ] Collective approval is documented in a research agreement.
- [ ] Individual consent is informed, in-language, and revisitable.
- [ ] Data ownership, storage and access follow CARE/OCAP, not just FAIR defaults.
- [ ] Restricted knowledge is excluded from all outputs and labelled in archives.
- [ ] The community reviewed findings before submission on agreed terms.
- [ ] Results were returned in a form the community can use.
