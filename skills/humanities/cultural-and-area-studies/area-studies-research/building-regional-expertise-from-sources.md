# Building regional expertise from sources

**Goal:** produce a piece of area-studies work (thesis chapter, briefing, article) that a
specialist from the region would recognise as accurate, and that draws on the region's
own voices rather than only on what outsiders wrote about it.

## 1. Frame the question narrowly enough to answer
"Politics in Central Asia" is a field, not a question. Pin down **place** (country,
province, city, community), **period** (with start/end years and why), and **disciplinary
lens** (historical, political, literary, anthropological). Area studies is
interdisciplinary, but each claim still has to meet the evidentiary standard of the
discipline it borrows from — an economic claim needs economic data, a literary one needs
close reading.

## 2. Map the literature in three layers
1. **Outside-in scholarship** — English/other metropolitan-language monographs and
   journals (e.g. the regional journals published by learned societies; the Journal of
   Asian Studies, International Journal of Middle East Studies, African Affairs are typical
   anchors). Use their bibliographies to snowball.
2. **In-region scholarship** — university presses, national academies and journals
   published in the region, often in the vernacular. These are routinely under-cited in
   anglophone work; a few of them in your bibliography is often what distinguishes real
   expertise.
3. **Primary and grey sources** — local press, government gazettes, NGO reports,
   statistical yearbooks, oral sources, social media, literature and film.
Keep a source log noting language, origin (inside/outside), and the author's
institutional position. Reviewers will check the balance.

## 3. Languages: be honest about competence
- State which languages you read and at what level; say when you relied on translation
  or a research assistant.
- Pick one **romanisation system** and apply it consistently (e.g. Hanyu Pinyin for
  Mandarin, a stated system such as ALA-LC or IJMES for Arabic/Persian/Turkish). Note
  common alternative spellings once so readers can search them.
- Machine translation is acceptable for triage, not for quoted evidence. Check every
  quotation you rely on with a competent reader. *(Exact field norms on MT use vary and
  are shifting — check your target journal's policy.)*

## 4. Triangulate every key claim
For each claim that carries your argument, aim for at least two independent kinds of
source (e.g. a statistic + a local press account + an interview). Watch for
**citation loops**: three Western reports that all trace back to one wire story are
one source, not three. Government statistics in some states are politically shaped —
say so, and compare with independent estimates where they exist.

## 5. Fieldwork and in-country access
- Get ethics approval from your institution **and** check host-country research permits
  or visa conditions; some states require formal affiliation.
- Assess risk to informants first — in authoritarian settings, anonymise people and
  sometimes places, encrypt notes, and avoid carrying identifiable data across borders.
- Build relationships with local scholars as collaborators, credit them, and consider
  co-authorship and publishing a version in the local language.

## 6. Writing without exoticising
- Avoid treating the region as timeless, uniform or defined by its difference from "the
  West" (Said's *Orientalism* critique is the standard reference point).
- Name internal diversity: ethnic, religious, class, urban/rural and regional splits.
- Use people's own categories and terms, glossed on first use.
- Compare with other regions only where the comparison is earned, not as a default
  measuring stick.

## Gotchas
- Country-level data hides sub-national variation that is often the real story.
- Diaspora sources can be well informed but have political stakes of their own.
- Place names change (colonial vs post-colonial, contested territories); give both and
  pick a stated convention.
- Outdated assumptions persist in old textbooks; check publication dates on anything
  framing "current" conditions.

## Verify
- [ ] The question names a place, a period and a disciplinary lens.
- [ ] Bibliography includes in-region and vernacular scholarship, not only outside-in work.
- [ ] One romanisation system is applied consistently; language competence is stated.
- [ ] Each load-bearing claim rests on two independent kinds of source.
- [ ] Informant risk and host-country permissions were assessed before fieldwork.
- [ ] A reader from the region would not find the framing exoticising or homogenising.
