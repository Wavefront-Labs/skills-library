# Researching repeat victimisation and victim impact

**Goal:** design and carry out victimology research — on who is at risk, why some people
and places are victimised again and again, what crime does to victims, and how they fare
in the justice process — in a way that produces usable evidence without re-harming
participants.

## 1. Frame the question
Victimology spans:
- **Risk** — who is victimised? (lifestyle-exposure theory, Hindelang et al. 1978;
  routine activity theory's target suitability and guardianship).
- **Concentration** — repeat and near-repeat patterns.
- **Impact** — financial, physical, psychological, social costs.
- **Process** — victims' experience of police, courts, support and restorative options.
- **Critical victimology** — who gets recognised as a victim at all (Christie's "ideal
  victim": weak, blameless, attacked by a stranger) and who is left out.
Avoid victim-blaming framings: studying risk factors is not assigning fault. Say so
explicitly in your write-up.

## 2. Repeat and near-repeat victimisation
A consistent finding is that a small share of victims, households and places experience a
large share of victimisations, and that risk of a repeat is highest soon after an
incident. *(Magnitudes vary by crime type and dataset — compute your own rather than
quoting a headline figure.)* To study it:
- **Survey data**: count incidents per respondent over the reference period; mind
  **series incidents** (surveys cap or specially code very frequent events like ongoing
  domestic abuse — capping undercounts concentration, so check the rule).
- **Recorded data**: link incidents by victim or address; measure time to repeat.
- **Near-repeat**: test whether nearby targets face elevated risk shortly after an
  incident (space-time clustering, Knox-type tests; near-repeat calculators exist). This is
  an aggregate research pattern; don't use it to make claims about individuals.
- **Poly-victimisation**: people (especially children) experiencing several types.

## 3. Measuring impact
Use validated instruments rather than ad hoc questions: PTSD and distress scales
(e.g. PCL-5), quality-of-life measures, costs of crime estimates. Compare against
appropriate non-victim groups and, ideally, pre-victimisation data (longitudinal panels)
to separate effects from prior differences.

## 4. Experience of the justice process
Measure satisfaction, perceived procedural justice (being informed, heard, treated with
respect), and **secondary victimisation** (harm from the process itself). Methods: surveys
of victims at case milestones, court observation, interviews; evaluations of victim
services, victim impact statements and restorative justice conferences.

## 5. Trauma-informed research ethics
- Full ethics review; recruit through support services where possible.
- Offer control: skip questions, pause, choose interview setting and interviewer gender.
- Distress protocol and referral information at every contact.
- Safety for domestic abuse participants: never send materials to shared addresses or
  devices; agree safe contact methods; consider an abuser discovering participation.
- Confidentiality limits stated up front (e.g. risk to children).
- Look after researchers: debriefing and supervision for vicarious trauma.

## Gotchas
- Police data under-represent victims who distrust or fear the police — often the most
  victimised groups.
- Hate crime and domestic abuse figures shift with definitions and awareness campaigns.
- Small samples of repeat victims can be dominated by a few extreme cases; report
  distributions, not just means.
- Victims' groups and advocacy organisations may have policy stakes; note them.
