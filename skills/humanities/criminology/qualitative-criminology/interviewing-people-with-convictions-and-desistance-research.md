# Interviewing people with convictions and desistance research

**Goal:** collect and analyse in-depth accounts from people in prison, on probation, or
with past convictions — about offending, punishment, and especially how people stop
(desistance) — in a way that is ethical, safe and analytically rigorous. This is academic
research, not investigative interviewing: you are not gathering evidence about crimes.

## 1. Approvals and access
- University ethics review, plus the justice system's own research approval (e.g. in
  England and Wales the HMPPS National Research Committee; US state departments of
  corrections have research review boards and federal rules add protections for prisoners
  as research subjects). *Body names correct to my knowledge — confirm current process.*
  Allow months; approval is often conditional on how you will handle disclosures.
- Gatekeepers (governors, probation managers, charities) shape who you meet. Ask how
  participants are selected and note the bias — staff tend to suggest "good" cases.

## 2. Consent when freedom is constrained
- Make it explicit that taking part or refusing has **no effect** on parole, sentence,
  privileges or probation — and make sure staff don't imply otherwise.
- Avoid inducements that are coercive in a deprived setting; check the institution's
  rules on payment or gifts.
- Check literacy and language; read the consent form aloud if needed. Assess capacity
  (substance use, mental health).
- Consent is ongoing; participants can stop or withdraw data up to a stated date.

## 3. Limits of confidentiality — say them up front
Typical limits agreed with ethics boards: disclosure of a risk of serious harm to self or
others, of undisclosed offences that are specific and identifiable, or of security
breaches (e.g. escape plans). Tell participants exactly what you'd have to pass on and to
whom, and design questions to avoid eliciting identifiable details of undetected crimes
("don't tell me names, dates or places"). *Legal duties vary by jurisdiction; get advice.*

## 4. The interview
- Use a semi-structured or life-history guide: early life, onset, turning points,
  relationships, work, identity, contact with the system, hopes.
- Use person-first language ("person with a conviction") — many find "offender" labelling.
- Expect testing and impression management; build rapport over repeated meetings if
  possible.
- Have a distress protocol and referral options; end on a neutral topic.
- Recording devices may be banned in prisons — plan for detailed handwritten notes.
- Researcher safety: follow the institution's security briefing; never carry items in or
  out for participants.

## 5. Analysis
- Transcribe, anonymise (names, places, offence details that identify), and code
  thematically or narratively.
- Desistance literature gives sensitising concepts: Maruna's "redemption script" vs
  "condemnation script"; Sampson & Laub's turning points (marriage, work, military);
  identity change; "primary/secondary/tertiary" desistance (relational recognition).
  Use them to sensitise, not force, your coding.
- Read accounts as narratives with purposes (justification, self-repair) — not simply
  true or false reports of events.
- Look for negative cases that complicate the pattern.

## 6. Writing and giving back
Protect identities in quotations (composite details if needed); remember prisons are small
worlds where staff can recognise people. Share findings with participants and
organisations; involve people with lived experience in design or analysis (convict
criminology and co-production traditions).

## Gotchas
- Released participants are hard to re-contact; collect safe contact routes at the start.
- Staff presence during interviews compromises candour; negotiate private space.
- Your sympathy or revulsion can shape questioning; keep a reflexive journal.
- Don't promise help with cases, appeals or parole letters.

## Verify
- [ ] University and justice-system approvals are in place.
- [ ] Consent explicitly separates participation from any system outcome.
- [ ] Confidentiality limits were stated before the interview began.
- [ ] Questions avoid eliciting identifiable details of undetected offences.
- [ ] Transcripts are anonymised, including indirect identifiers.
- [ ] Analysis treats accounts as narratives and includes negative cases.
