# Evaluating recidivism and rehabilitation evidence

**Goal:** judge whether a programme, sentence or prison regime actually changes
reoffending — whether you're reviewing the literature, evaluating a programme, or
critiquing a policy claim — and report the result with the right caveats.

## 1. Pin down what "recidivism" means in the study
Recidivism figures are not comparable unless the definition matches:
- **Event**: rearrest, reconviction, reincarceration, or technical violation of
  supervision. Rearrest rates run much higher than reincarceration for the same cohort.
- **Follow-up window**: 1, 2, 3, 5 years — and whether it starts at release or sentence.
  Reconviction data also need a lag for cases to reach court.
- **Measure**: binary (any reoffence), frequency, time to failure (survival analysis),
  or seriousness.
- **Data source**: official records only see detected crime.
Always quote rates with all four elements: "proven reoffending within 12 months of
release". *Specific national definitions (e.g. the UK's proven reoffending measure) change —
check the current methodology note.*

## 2. History: why this field is careful
Martinson's 1974 review was read as "nothing works", fuelling a shift away from
rehabilitation. Later meta-analyses found that some interventions reduce reoffending and
some (e.g. pure deterrence programmes like "Scared Straight") can increase it. The
**risk–need–responsivity (RNR)** model (Andrews & Bonta) summarises a large body of
findings: match intensity to risk, target criminogenic needs, deliver in ways people can
respond to (often cognitive-behavioural).

## 3. Rate the design
Use the **Maryland Scientific Methods Scale** (Sherman et al. 1997), levels 1–5:
1. correlation at one point in time;
2. before–after without comparison;
3. comparison group without strong matching;
4. well-matched comparison controlling for confounders (e.g. propensity scores);
5. random assignment.
Selection bias is the core threat: people who volunteer for or complete programmes differ
from those who don't. Watch especially for **completers-only** analyses — compare all
assigned (intention-to-treat) instead. Also check attrition, implementation fidelity, and
whether the evaluator developed the programme (developer-led evaluations tend to report
larger effects).

## 4. Synthesise across studies
Prefer systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Campbell Collaboration Crime and Justice
Group; national "what works" repositories such as CrimeSolutions in the US — *names
current to my knowledge*). Read effect sizes (odds ratios, Cohen's d) and heterogeneity,
not just the direction. Consider publication bias.

## 5. Imprisonment and sentencing effects
To estimate the effect of prison itself vs community sentences, designs exploit
as-good-as-random variation, such as random assignment of cases to judges of differing
severity ("judge stringency" instruments). Reviews (e.g. Nagin, Cullen & Jonson 2009)
found little evidence that custody reduces reoffending compared with non-custodial
sanctions, with some suggestion of criminogenic effects — though findings vary by
population and context. Distinguish **specific deterrence** (effect on the punished person)
from **general deterrence** and **incapacitation** (crime prevented while confined).

## 6. Report honestly
State the design level, recidivism definition, follow-up, sample, effect size with
confidence interval, cost where known, and generalisability limits. Separate "no evidence of
effect" (underpowered) from "evidence of no effect".

## Gotchas
- Raw comparisons of reoffending between prisons or programmes mostly reflect who they hold.
- Changes in policing or prosecution can move recidivism rates with no change in behaviour.
- Technical violations measure supervision intensity as much as conduct.
- Small pilot effects often shrink at scale.

## Verify
- [ ] Every recidivism figure states event, window, measure and data source.
- [ ] Each study is rated on the Maryland scale or equivalent.
- [ ] Completer-only and developer-led results are flagged.
- [ ] Conclusions draw on systematic reviews where they exist.
- [ ] Deterrence, incapacitation and rehabilitation effects are distinguished.
- [ ] Effect sizes and uncertainty are reported, not just significance.
