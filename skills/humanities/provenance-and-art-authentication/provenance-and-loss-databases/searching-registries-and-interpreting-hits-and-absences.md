# Searching registries and interpreting hits and absences

**Goal:** use the provenance and loss databases as one part of due diligence — searching
thoroughly, interpreting a match correctly, and never treating a clean search as clearance.

## 1. Know what each database is for
*Coverage and access change; check each site's current scope before relying on it.*
- **Getty Provenance Index** (free) — *historical* data: sale catalogues (mainly 1650–1945
  for some regions), dealer stock books (e.g. Goupil, Knoedler), archival inventories.
  Used to build provenance, not to check theft.
- **Art Loss Register** (commercial, fee per search) — large private registry of stolen,
  looted and disputed items; issues **search certificates**.
- **INTERPOL Stolen Works of Art** — police-reported thefts; public access via the
  **ID-Art** app and account registration.
- **FBI National Stolen Art File** (US), **Carabinieri "Leonardo" database** (Italy) and
  other national police databases.
- **German Lost Art Database (lostart.de)** — objects lost through Nazi persecution and
  wartime displacement, plus "found objects" reported by holders.
- **ERR Project database** and **Munich Central Collecting Point** records — Nazi
  confiscation and postwar recovery.
- **ICOM Red Lists** — not object databases but categories of at-risk objects by region;
  essential for antiquities and ethnographic material.
- **Price databases** (Artnet, Artprice, auction-house archives) — past sales, images and
  catalogue text that can help matching.

## 2. Search well
1. Prepare: images (front, back, details), dimensions, medium, inscriptions, and all
   known titles and owner names.
2. Search **artist name variants** (spellings, transliterations, "school of"), **title
   variants** in several languages, and **owner names** with variants.
3. Search **by object type and image** where supported; titles of stolen items are often
   generic ("Portrait of a Man").
4. Search **owners** too, not only the object: a past owner who reported a loss is a lead.
5. **Log every search**: database, date, terms, result, screenshot or certificate number.

## 3. Interpret a hit
A possible match is a **lead**, not a verdict.
- Compare dimensions, medium, details and marks; low-resolution or old photos may need
  expert comparison.
- Distinguish **stolen** (theft report), **looted / claim registered**, **"found object"**
  (reported by current holder), and **historical record** (Getty) entries.
- On a likely match: stop the transaction, don't alert the seller in a way that risks
  the object disappearing, and contact the registry/police and legal advice.

## 4. Interpret an absence
**No hit does not mean the object is clean.** Reasons:
- Most Nazi-era and colonial losses were never registered; families didn't know or
  couldn't report.
- Theft reports may lack images; titles differ; databases have regional gaps.
- Registries know only what was reported.
- A **search certificate** records that the item wasn't on that register at that date. It
  isn't title insurance and doesn't cover gaps in provenance. *Note:* read the issuing
  registry's own wording of what its certificate covers.
Pair database work with provenance research and gap analysis
(`humanities.provenance-and-art-authentication.provenance-research.reconstructing-an-ownership-chain-and-documenting-gaps`).

## 5. Due-diligence report
List: object description, images, sources and databases checked (with dates and result),
provenance summary, gaps and risk, and recommendation. Keep the record — it may be needed
to show good faith later, and some laws (e.g. UK and EU anti-money-laundering rules for
art-market participants) require proportionate checks. *Unconfirmed:* exact legal duties
depend on jurisdiction and transaction value.

## Gotchas
- Relying on the seller's certificate; run your own search.
- One search, once — repeat close to the transaction date.
- Searching only the current title.
- Treating a "found object" listing as a theft report (or ignoring it).

## Verify
- [ ] Each relevant database for the object type and region was searched.
- [ ] Name and title variants and owner names were searched.
- [ ] Every search is logged with date, terms and result.
- [ ] Any hit was compared on physical details before conclusions.
- [ ] The report states plainly that absence of hits is not clearance.
