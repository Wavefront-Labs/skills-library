# Comparative attribution and grading language

**Goal:** reach and express an attribution judgement — who made this, or how close to the
named artist it is — in a way that is reasoned, reproducible by another expert, and
honest about its limits.

## 1. Build the comparison corpus first
- Use only **securely attributed** works: documented commissions, signed and dated works
  with good provenance, works in the artist's own documented collection or sales. Mark
  the basis for each.
- Cover the **same period** (styles change across a career), **same medium and scale**,
  and similar subject. A late drawing is poor evidence for an early panel.
- Include works by **pupils, workshop assistants, followers and known copyists** — the
  realistic alternatives you need to exclude.
- Prefer seeing originals; if using images, use high-resolution, colour-calibrated,
  same-scale details (raking light helps for surface).

## 2. Compare like with like
Look at the object at the scale the artist worked, then at details:
- **Morellian details**: ears, hands, fingernails, drapery folds — features painted
  habitually, less consciously copied. Useful but not decisive.
- **Handling**: brush or stroke direction, pressure, how forms are built up (wet-in-wet,
  layered, hatched), how edges are resolved, how corrections were made.
- **Design logic**: composition, spatial construction, how the artist solves recurring
  problems.
- **Quality**: consistency of intelligence across the whole surface. Copyists tend to be
  strong in highlights and weak in transitions and unseen areas.
- **Condition**: abrasion and overpaint change what you see. Get a condition report;
  judge only original paint.

Write notes per feature: observation → comparison work → agreement/disagreement.

## 3. Weigh workshop practice
Many artists ran studios (Rubens, Rembrandt, Titian, Warhol). "Autograph" may be a
spectrum: designed by the master, executed partly or wholly by assistants, retouched by
the master. Research the artist's documented practice before deciding what "by" means.

## 4. Integrate other evidence
Eye-based judgement should be tested against **provenance** and **technical examination**
(`humanities.provenance-and-art-authentication.technical-examination.reading-imaging-and-materials-evidence-for-authenticity`).
If they disagree, say so: a confident attribution that technical evidence contradicts is a
red flag, not a triumph of connoisseurship.

## 5. State it in standard grading language
Auction houses publish their own glossary; terms are broadly (not exactly) shared:
- **[Artist's full name]** — in the house's opinion, a work by the artist.
- **Attributed to** — probably by the artist, in whole or part, but less certain.
- **Studio / workshop of** — by an unknown hand in the artist's studio, possibly under supervision.
- **Circle of** — by an unidentified hand closely associated with the artist, not necessarily a pupil.
- **Follower of / Style of** — working in the artist's style, contemporary or nearly so.
- **Manner of** — in the style, but of a later date.
- **After** — a copy of a known work.
*Note:* exact definitions vary by house and change over time; quote the glossary you're
using. Museums use similar terms in labels with less formal definition.

## Gotchas
- **Confirmation bias**: deciding first, then finding supporting details. Write down what
  would count against the attribution before you look.
- **Market pressure**: an upgraded attribution changes value dramatically; independent
  experts may face legal risk and some refuse to give opinions (e.g. some artist
  foundations stopped authenticating after litigation).
- **Authority isn't evidence**: catalogues raisonnés and named experts have been wrong.
- Images flatten texture and colour; never give a firm opinion from a phone photo.

## Verify
- [ ] The comparison corpus is securely attributed, period- and medium-matched, and includes workshop/follower works.
- [ ] Notes record observation → comparison → conclusion per feature.
- [ ] Condition was assessed and only original areas were judged.
- [ ] The judgement was tested against provenance and technical evidence.
- [ ] The result uses a named glossary's grading term, with the reason for that grade.
