# Nazi-era and colonial-loss due diligence

**Goal:** determine whether an object may have been looted, confiscated, sold under duress
or removed through colonial violence, and document the research so it can support an
acquisition decision, a claim or a proactive restitution.

*Legal outcomes depend on jurisdiction and are for lawyers. This page covers the research.*

## 1. Screen: which objects need this?
- **Nazi era**: any object that was, or could have been, in continental Europe between
  **1933 and 1945** (Germany from 1933; occupied territories from occupation) with a gap
  or a change of ownership in that window. The AAM standard asks museums to research all
  such objects.
- **Colonial era**: objects acquired in colonised territories, especially through
  military expeditions (e.g. the 1897 British expedition to Benin City), missionary
  collecting or administrative seizure; and **human remains**.
- **Archaeological/ethnographic**: objects without documented export before 1970.

## 2. Nazi-era method
1. Build the base provenance first (`humanities.provenance-and-art-authentication.provenance-research.reconstructing-an-ownership-chain-and-documenting-gaps`).
2. **Red-flag names**: check every owner and dealer against lists of dealers and agents
   known to have handled looted art (e.g. the 1946 OSS Art Looting Investigation Unit
   red-flag list). A name on it is a prompt to dig, not proof of looting.
3. **Databases**: German **Lost Art** database, **ERR Project** (Einsatzstab Reichsleiter
   Rosenberg records), the **Central Collecting Point** property cards (Munich), French
   **Rose-Valland / MNR** listings, **Art Loss Register** and national lists.
4. **Archives**: postwar restitution files (national archives in the US, Germany, France,
   Austria, Netherlands), auction-house records from the period (e.g. sales at Lepke,
   Dorotheum), compensation-claim files.
5. **Context of transfer**: a sale by a persecuted owner after 1933 may be a **forced sale**
   or **sale under duress** even if a price was paid. Look at: date relative to persecution,
   price vs market, whether the seller received the proceeds (blocked accounts, flight tax).
   Many restitution panels treat such sales as losses — criteria differ by country.

## 3. Colonial-era method
1. Trace acquisition **event and actor**: expedition, officer, missionary, trader, colonial
   official; dates and places.
2. Use **expedition records, military reports, collection registers, letters** and
   published accounts; many museums now publish colonial-provenance reports.
3. Assess **circumstances**: violent seizure, coerced exchange, purchase in an unequal
   context, or documented consent. Record the evidence for each.
4. Engage **source communities** and the government or community body now responsible.
   Their knowledge often fills gaps archives can't.
5. For US Native American items, **NAGPRA** (and its 2024 regulations) imposes legal
   duties; other countries have their own frameworks.
*Note:* policy is shifting quickly (e.g. returns of Benin bronzes from Germany and others);
check current national guidance.

## 4. Document and report
For each object: research question, sources consulted (including negative results), findings
per period, assessment (e.g. "no evidence of loss", "gap — cannot exclude", "evidence of
loss"), and recommended action. Publish where policy requires (many museums post Nazi-era
research and colonial provenance online).

## Gotchas
- **Absence of a database hit is not clearance.** Most losses were never registered.
- **Name changes**: aryanised firms changed names; owners emigrated and changed names.
- **Duress is contextual**: don't apply a market-price test alone.
- Acquisitions with unresolved 1933–45 gaps shouldn't proceed without documented
  justification; most codes of ethics say so.
- Be careful with publishing names of heirs and claimants.

## Verify
- [ ] Every 1933–45 and pre-1970 gap is identified.
- [ ] All owners/dealers checked against red-flag lists; all relevant databases searched and logged.
- [ ] Transfers by persecuted owners were assessed for duress, not just price.
- [ ] For colonial objects, the acquisition event and actor are documented and source communities contacted.
- [ ] The report records negative results and a clear assessment category.
