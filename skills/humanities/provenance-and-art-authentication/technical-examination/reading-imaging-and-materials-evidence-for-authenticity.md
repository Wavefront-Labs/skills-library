# Reading imaging and materials evidence for authenticity

**Goal:** as an art historian or provenance researcher, commission and interpret technical
examination well: ask the right question, understand what each method can establish, and
avoid over- or under-reading the results. The analysis itself is done by conservators and
scientists.

## 1. Ask a falsifiable question
"Is it authentic?" is not a technical question. Good ones:
- Are all materials consistent with a date before 1650? (anachronism test)
- Is there underdrawing, and does it resemble the artist's documented practice?
- Is the composition painted over another, and is that consistent with the studio?
- Does the canvas weave match another canvas from the same bolt?
Technical methods are far better at **disproving** an early date than proving authorship.

## 2. What each method shows
| Method | Shows | Typical inference | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Raking / transmitted light, microscopy** | surface texture, cracks, retouching | condition, artificial craquelure | interpretive |
| **UV fluorescence** | varnish, later retouching (dark patches) | restoration extent, recent surfaces | surface only; varnish masks |
| **Infrared reflectography (IRR)** | carbon-based underdrawing, changes | working process; copies often lack exploratory changes | needs carbon; some pigments opaque |
| **X-radiography** | lead-white density, structure, earlier compositions, tacking | pentimenti, reused supports, construction | superimposes all layers |
| **XRF / macro-XRF** | elemental composition, element maps | pigment identification (e.g. Ti, Zn, Cr, Cd) | elemental, not molecular; surface-weighted |
| **Raman / FTIR / cross-sections** | molecular identity, layer sequence | specific pigments/binders | needs sampling (micro) for cross-sections |
| **Dendrochronology** | felling date of panel wood | earliest possible use date (plus seasoning) | oak/some species, enough rings, reference chronology |
| **Radiocarbon** | age of organic material (canvas, binder) | 20th-c. "bomb peak" flags post-1950 material | wide ranges pre-1950; contamination |

## 3. Anachronism detection
Pigments with known introduction dates are the most powerful single test. Commonly cited
(approximate first commercial availability — *check a current pigment reference*):
- **Prussian blue** c. 1700s (1704–10)
- **Zinc white** (as artists' pigment) c. 1830s–1840s
- **Synthetic ultramarine** c. 1820s–1830s
- **Cadmium yellow** mid-19th c.
- **Titanium white** c. 1920s (common after c. 1920–1940)
- **Phthalocyanine blue/green** 1930s
A modern pigment in **original** paint (not retouching — check UV and cross-section) is
close to conclusive against an earlier date. The Beltracchi case was exposed partly by
titanium white in a purported 1914 painting.

## 4. Other forgery indicators
- Craquelure that is induced (baked, rolled, drawn on) — irregular, not following
  support movement; dirt rubbed into cracks.
- Old support with a new painting (scraped old panels/canvases) — X-ray shows remnants.
- Unusually "clean" underdrawing that matches a known composition exactly (a copy's tracing).
- Inconsistent ageing between paint and support.

## 5. Word the conclusion carefully
- "Materials are consistent with a date of c. X" ≠ "by artist Y".
- "Contains titanium white in original layers; inconsistent with the stated date."
- State sampling locations and whether retouching was excluded.
- Integrate with attribution (`humanities.provenance-and-art-authentication.connoisseurship-and-attribution.comparative-attribution-and-grading-language`)
  and provenance.

## Gotchas
- **Absence of anachronism proves little**: skilled forgers use period materials.
- **Restoration** can contain modern pigments; don't condemn a genuine work on retouching.
- **Lead-white priming** can blank out X-rays; IRR depth varies with pigment.
- Old forgeries (e.g. 19th-century copies) pass material dating tests.
- Ask for the **raw data and images**, not just a summary letter.

## Verify
- [ ] The commissioned question is specific and testable.
- [ ] You know each method's limit for this object.
- [ ] Any anachronistic material was confirmed to be in original paint, not retouching.
- [ ] The conclusion distinguishes "consistent with a date" from "by the artist".
- [ ] Technical findings were reconciled with provenance and stylistic evidence.
