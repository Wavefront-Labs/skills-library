# Mapping a historiographical debate

**Goal:** understand *how* the past you're studying has been interpreted — by whom, with
what evidence and assumptions — so your work joins a conversation rather than repeating it.

## 1. Find the literature efficiently
- Start from **recent review articles, handbook chapters and introductions** to edited volumes;
  they summarise the debate and cite its landmarks.
- Bibliographic databases: Historical Abstracts, America: History & Life, JSTOR, national
  bibliographies (e.g. Royal Historical Society Bibliography / IHR for British and Irish history).
  *Coverage and access depend on your institution.*
- **Citation chaining:** backward (who does this book cite?) and forward (who cites it? — Google
  Scholar "Cited by"). The works everyone cites are the landmarks.
- Read **book reviews** of the landmarks in major journals; reviewers state the stakes bluntly.

## 2. Read each work for its machinery, not just its conclusion
For each key work, note in a table:
| Field | Example question |
|---|---|
| Question | What is it actually trying to explain? |
| Argument | One sentence. |
| Evidence base | Which archives/sources? What scale (national, local, individual)? |
| Method/approach | Quantitative, biographical, cultural, comparative, microhistory? |
| Framework | Marxist, Annales, gender, postcolonial, rational-choice, none stated? |
| Against whom | Whose view is it correcting? |
| Context | When and where written — what was happening then? |

The last row matters: historiography is itself historical. Cold War, decolonisation, or a
new archive opening often explains a shift.

## 3. Group and sequence
- Cluster works by **position** on the core question, not by date or by author fame.
- Then lay clusters on a timeline: orthodox → revisionist → post-revisionist is a common
  but not universal pattern. Name what *changed*: new evidence, new method, new question.
- Note works that talk past each other (different questions dressed as disagreement) — that
  is often the real insight.

## 4. Position your own work
- Identify the gap precisely: an untested assumption, an unused source base, a region or
  group left out, a method not tried, a scale not examined.
- Phrase your contribution as a move in the debate: "X argued A using B; I use C to show
  that A holds only for D."
- Be fair: summarise opposing views in terms their authors would accept.

## 5. Writing it up
- Organise the historiographical section thematically (by position or question), not as
  a book-by-book list.
- Keep it proportionate: in an article, a few paragraphs; in a thesis, a chapter section.
- Every claim about a historian's view needs a page reference.

## Gotchas
- "Whig history" — reading the past as inevitable progress toward the present — is easy to
  slip into and easy for reviewers to spot.
- Labels ("revisionist") are contested and shift meaning between fields; define yours.
- Don't confuse popular history with the scholarly debate, or Wikipedia's summary with
  either.
- Newest is not automatically best; older works may rest on archives since lost.

## Verify
- [ ] Each landmark work summarised with question, evidence, method and target.
- [ ] Positions grouped thematically and sequenced with reasons for shifts.
- [ ] Your gap stated in one sentence relative to named works.
- [ ] Every attributed view has a page reference.
