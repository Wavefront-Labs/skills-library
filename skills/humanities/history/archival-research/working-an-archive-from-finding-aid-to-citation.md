# Working an archive: from finding aid to citation

**Goal:** get from a research question to the right documents, in the fewest visits, with
notes and images you can still cite exactly in five years.

## 1. Think in records, not topics
Archives are arranged by **who created the records** (provenance) and the **order they
kept them in**, not by subject. So ask: *which person, office or organisation would have
produced a document about this?* A strike might appear in employer board minutes, police
files, a union's correspondence, and a local newspaper — four different archives.

## 2. Read the finding aid
- Hierarchy (ISAD(G)): **fonds** (whole collection of one creator) › **series** › **file** ›
  **item**. The reference code encodes it, e.g. `HO 45/1234/5` = department, series, piece, item.
- Read the **administrative history** and **scope and content** notes at fonds and series
  level; they tell you what was kept, what was destroyed, and how it's arranged.
- Note **access conditions** (closure periods, data protection, depositor permission) and
  **physical condition** (fragile, on microfilm only, off-site storage needing days' notice).
- Search the aggregators (national catalogues, ArchiveGrid, Archives Hub, Archives Portal
  Europe, etc.) but always confirm in the holding archive's own catalogue.

## 3. Before the visit
- Email the archivist with your question and candidate references. They know uncatalogued
  material and quirks the catalogue doesn't show. Be specific and brief.
- Pre-order items if the archive allows it; check daily ordering limits and photography rules.
- Register for a reader's ticket in advance; bring ID as required.

## 4. In the reading room
- Pencil only; no pens, bags, food. Use supports/weights for bound volumes; don't lick fingers
  or lean on documents. Gloves only where the archive says (often for photos, not paper).
- Keep original order — return items in the folder exactly as they came.
- **Photograph systematically:** first shot of every file = its reference slip/cover.
  Then each page in order. Many archives allow phone/camera photography without flash;
  some charge a permit.
- Keep a **research log**: date, archive, reference, what you looked at, what you found,
  *and what you looked at and found nothing* (negative searches save repeat visits).

## 5. Managing images and notes
- Rename images or tag them immediately with the full reference (e.g. in Tropy or a folder
  per reference). Unlabelled camera roll photos are the main source of uncitable evidence.
- Transcribe key passages verbatim, with folio/page; mark your own comments clearly
  (e.g. in [square brackets]).
- Add each source to a reference manager (Zotero etc.) with the archival fields.

## 6. Citing
Cite from the smallest unit up, or as the house style requires. Chicago-style example:
`Letter, A to B, 3 March 1871, HO 45/1234/5, Home Office papers, The National Archives, Kew.`
Always include repository and full reference; page/folio where available.

## Gotchas
- Catalogue dates can be wrong; date from the document itself.
- Digitised copies may omit enclosures, versos, or blank-looking pages with pencil notes.
- Closed records: in some jurisdictions you can request review or use freedom-of-information
  routes. *Procedures vary by country.*
- Copyright and reproduction permission for publication are separate from permission to photograph.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name the record creator(s) likely to hold your evidence.
- [ ] Every image is linked to a full reference before you leave the room.
- [ ] The research log lists negative searches too.
- [ ] Each citation includes repository, collection, reference and item/folio.
- [ ] Publication permissions checked separately from reading-room rules.
