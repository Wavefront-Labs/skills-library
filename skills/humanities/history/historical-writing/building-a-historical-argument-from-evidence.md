# Building a historical argument from evidence

**Goal:** turn research notes into prose that makes a clear, defensible claim about the past,
shows the evidence for it, and is honest about what the evidence can't settle.

## 1. Find the argument before drafting
- Write the **thesis in two sentences**: what happened/why, and why it matters to the
  debate (see `humanities.history.historiography.mapping-a-historiographical-debate`).
  If you can't, you have a topic, not an argument — go back to the notes.
- List the 3–6 **sub-claims** the thesis needs. Each will be a section or chapter.
- For each sub-claim, list the strongest evidence and the strongest counter-evidence.

## 2. Structure: argument vs chronology
- Pure chronology ("then… then…") hides the argument. Pure thematic structure can lose
  the reader in time. Most good history uses **analytical narrative**: sections driven by
  claims, chronological within sections.
- Open each section with its claim; end it by tying back to the thesis.
- Give the reader a short orientation early: dates, places, key actors. Don't assume they
  know your period.

## 3. Using evidence
- **Quote** when the wording itself matters (tone, vocabulary, a striking phrase); otherwise
  **paraphrase** and cite. Long block quotes should earn their space — analyse them after.
- Use the "evidence sandwich": set up (who, when, why this source), the evidence, then
  your analysis of what it shows *and doesn't*.
- Name the source type in the prose when reliability matters: "In a petition to the
  magistrates — a genre that rewarded exaggeration — the weavers claimed…"
- One example is an anecdote; show pattern (several cases, numbers, or a reason the case is typical).

## 4. Calibrated certainty
- Match verbs to evidence: *shows / demonstrates* (strong), *suggests / indicates* (moderate),
  *may / possibly* (weak). Don't hedge everything; hedge what is uncertain.
- Causation: distinguish necessary from sufficient causes, long-term from triggers. Avoid
  monocausal claims and hindsight inevitability — note contingency (what could have gone otherwise).
- Avoid anachronistic language and categories unless you flag them.

## 5. Scholarly apparatus
- History usually uses **footnotes** (Chicago notes-bibliography). First citation in full,
  later ones short form. Archival citations down to item/folio
  (see `humanities.history.archival-research.working-an-archive-from-finding-aid-to-citation`).
- Discursive footnotes are acceptable but don't hide your main counter-argument in one.
- Check the target journal's or press's house style before formatting — many differ in detail.

## 6. Revision pass
1. Reverse outline: one line per paragraph. Does each advance a sub-claim?
2. Check every factual statement has a citation or is common knowledge.
3. Read only the first sentences of paragraphs — does the argument still flow?
4. Cut throat-clearing openings and summary-only conclusions; end with significance.
5. Verify quotations against your transcriptions/images, character by character.

## Gotchas
- "Data dumps": including every finding because it was hard to get. Cut what doesn't serve the argument.
- Present tense for historians' arguments ("Thompson argues"), past for past events.
- Over-quoting secondary historians makes their argument, not yours.

## Verify
- [ ] Thesis fits in two sentences and appears in the introduction.
- [ ] Each section opens with a claim and closes linked to the thesis.
- [ ] Every quotation is checked and every fact cited.
- [ ] Counter-evidence is addressed in the main text.
- [ ] Certainty language matches the evidence.
