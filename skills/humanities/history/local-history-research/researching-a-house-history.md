# Researching a house history

**Goal:** establish when a house (or the plot) was built, who owned and who lived in it,
and how it changed — working backwards from what you can see now. Examples are mostly UK
and US; the logic transfers, record names don't.

## 1. Start with the building and the present
- Look at the fabric: brick bond, window styles, roof, extensions, blocked openings. A
  building can be older than its façade, or a rebuild on an old plot. Note questions, not
  conclusions.
- Get the current title from the land registry (in England and Wales, HM Land Registry
  title register and plan for a small fee). Owners may also hold older **deeds** — ask.
- Check whether it is **listed/registered** (historic environment record, national
  heritage list, US National Register): the description often gives a date estimate.

## 2. Maps: bracket the construction date
- Line up dated maps of the area: in the UK, Ordnance Survey editions (1:2500 county series
  from the 1850s–90s onward), tithe maps (c.1836–50s) and enclosure maps; in US towns,
  **Sanborn fire-insurance maps**. Earlier town plans and estate maps where they exist.
- The house first appears between two editions = construction bracket. Watch for the plot
  outline changing, not just the presence of "a building".
- Georeference maps or overlay in an online viewer to compare plot boundaries.

## 3. Owners and occupiers are different people
- **Owners**: deeds, land registers, manorial records (copyhold), estate papers, wills and
  probate inventories, mortgages.
- **Occupiers**: census returns (every 10 years; UK 1841–1921 open, US to 1950),
  trade and street directories (often annual), electoral registers, rate books (local tax),
  utility records. Tenancy was normal — most 19th-century householders rented.
- Tabulate year × source × name; patterns show changes of tenancy and gaps.

## 4. Pin down the build
- **Building control/plans** (local council or city archives, from the late 19th century in
  many towns): plans and approval dates.
- **Rate books** — a new entry or a sudden jump in rateable value often marks construction
  or a major alteration.
- **Valuation Office (Lloyd George) survey 1910–15** in England and Wales describes almost
  every property. US equivalents: tax assessor records, building permits.
- Newspaper adverts (sales, lettings) often describe rooms and outbuildings.

## 5. Addresses change
Street numbering and even street names change — sometimes several times. Find renumbering
records or cross-reference neighbours in successive directories before assuming continuity.
House names move between houses too.

## Gotchas
- A date stone may commemorate a rebuild, a marriage, or nothing at all.
- "Built 1890" from an estate agent is a guess unless sourced.
- Census enumerators walked routes, not numbered sequences; locate by neighbours, not just by number.
- Online indexes miss or mis-transcribe; check the images.
- Recent occupants are living people — publish names with care
  (see `humanities.genealogy-and-family-history.family-history-writing.writing-a-sourced-family-history`).

## Verify
- [ ] Construction bracketed by at least two dated maps, refined by a documentary source.
- [ ] Owners and occupiers tracked in separate columns with sources.
- [ ] Address changes checked before linking records across decades.
- [ ] Every claim (date, name) traced to a specific record, not a summary.
