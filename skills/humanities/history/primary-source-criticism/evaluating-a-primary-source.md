# Evaluating a primary source

**Goal:** decide what a document can and cannot tell you — and how far to trust each part
of it — before you build an argument on it.

## 1. External criticism: is it what it seems?
Questions about the document as an object:
- **Provenance:** where has it been since it was made? A continuous custody chain (creating
  office → archive) supports authenticity; a document that "turned up" at auction needs more scrutiny.
- **Form:** material, script, layout, seals, letterhead — do they fit the claimed date and
  place? (See `humanities.history.palaeography-and-diplomatics.transcribing-historical-handwriting`.)
- **Dating:** explicit date, internal references (people in office, prices, events),
  calendar style (Julian/Gregorian, regnal years, Old Style year starting 25 March in England
  before 1752). Undated documents get a date range and the reasoning.
- **Anachronism check:** words, place names, titles or technology that didn't exist yet are
  the classic sign of forgery or later copying.
- **Original, copy, or draft?** A copy may introduce errors; a draft shows intention, not
  necessarily what was sent.

## 2. Internal criticism: what does it mean, and how reliable is it?
Use a fixed set of questions (some teach them as **who, what, when, where, why, for whom**):
- **Author:** who made it, what position, what did they know first-hand?
- **Proximity:** eyewitness, contemporary hearsay, or later recollection? Distance in time and
  space lowers reliability for detail but can add perspective.
- **Purpose and audience:** a report to a superior, a private diary, a legal deposition, a
  propaganda pamphlet — each pushes the content differently. Ask what the author *wanted to
  happen* as a result of writing.
- **Genre conventions:** petitions exaggerate hardship; obituaries flatter; court records
  are shaped by legal categories. Read through the formula.
- **Language:** meanings shift ("nice", "mob", currency names). Check period usage.
- **Incidental vs intended information:** details the author had no reason to distort (the
  weather, a price mentioned in passing) are often more reliable than the main claim.

## 3. Corroboration
- Look for **independent** sources: different authors without shared information. Two
  newspapers reprinting the same wire report are one source, not two.
- Where sources conflict, list exactly where; explain the conflict by author position and
  purpose rather than averaging.
- Grade claims: established / probable / possible / unsupported. Keep that grading visible
  in your writing.

## 4. Silences
Ask who is *absent*: whose voices weren't recorded, which records were destroyed or never
made. Absence in the record is evidence about record-making, not proof that nothing happened.

## Gotchas
- Bias doesn't make a source useless — a hostile source admitting something is strong evidence for it.
- Digitised transcriptions and translations are themselves sources; check the original image
  for key passages.
- Beware "presentism": judging motives by today's categories.
- Don't let one vivid source carry an argument the others don't support.

## Verify
- [ ] Provenance and dating reasoning written down.
- [ ] Author, purpose, audience and genre identified for each key source.
- [ ] Each key claim corroborated by an independent source, or flagged as single-source.
- [ ] Conflicts explained, not averaged.
- [ ] Silences in the record named.
