# Writing panels and labels people read

**Goal:** write text visitors actually read standing up, in a few seconds, outdoors or in a
gallery — accurate enough for specialists, clear enough for a twelve-year-old.

## 1. Know how text is read
Visitors **browse**: most read headlines and look at images; a minority read the body; few
read everything. Serrell's work found most visitors stop at a minority of labels and spend
seconds at each. Write for that: the headline and first sentence must carry the message.

## 2. One idea per panel
Before writing, state the panel's **big idea** in one sentence (Serrell's "big idea" for
exhibitions; Ham's "theme"). If you can't, the panel isn't ready. Everything on the panel
should support that sentence — the rest goes in the guidebook.

## 3. Hierarchy and word counts
Typical targets (adjust to house style — many institutions set their own limits):
| Element | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | 3–10 | Provoke or state the idea; not a topic label |
| **Intro / lead** | 30–60 | The big idea, in full |
| **Body** | 50–150 per panel (outdoor shorter) | Evidence, story, detail |
| **Object label** | 25–75 | Why this object matters, linked to idea |
| **Captions** | 10–30 | Say what the image shows and why it's there |
Outdoor panels: aim lower — people are standing in wind and rain.

## 4. Write
- **Plain language**: short sentences (aim ~15–20 words), active voice, everyday words; explain
  any essential technical term in the same sentence. Readability scores (e.g. aiming at a
  reading age of about 12) are a check, not a target.
- **Concrete and human**: people, actions, sensory detail. "Six-year-olds worked here 12 hours
  a day" not "Child labour was prevalent."
- **Point at the real thing**: "Look up — the soot marks show where the forge stood."
- **Address the reader** ("you") where it helps; ask a question only if you answer it.
- **Cut** dates and names that don't serve the idea; one or two anchor dates beat ten.
- Use names and plain terms for difficult histories; avoid euphemism.

## 5. Layout for legibility
- **Type size**: depends on viewing distance; outdoor and gallery panels typically use
  body text well above print sizes. *Unconfirmed precise figures* — follow your designer and
  accessibility guidance (e.g. many guidelines suggest ≥ 16–24 pt for labels read at arm's
  length and larger for panels).
- Strong **contrast**, sans-serif or clear serif, left-aligned, no text over busy images.
- Mount height for wheelchair users and children; avoid reflective surfaces.
- Provide **large-print**, audio or translated versions where audiences need them.

## 6. Accuracy review and testing
1. **Fact-check** every claim against sources; keep a reference sheet per panel.
2. **Specialist review** (curator/historian) for accuracy; **non-specialist review** for clarity.
   They will pull in opposite directions — the big idea decides.
3. **Prototype**: print at full size, stick it up in place, watch 10–20 visitors. Do they
   stop? Can they say the main point afterwards?
4. Proofread in final layout; errors appear in typesetting.

## Trail leaflets and guidebooks
Leaflets: a map first, numbered stops matching on-site markers, ~50–100 words per stop.
Guidebooks can carry depth, references and the full chronology — that's where the extra
material goes.

## Gotchas
- **Topic headlines** ("The Kitchen") waste the most-read words.
- **Committee text** — everyone adds a fact; length creeps. Fix word counts in the brief.
- **"Recent research suggests"** without saying what — state it.
- Outdoor text fades and gets vandalised; plan replacement and avoid content that dates fast.

## Verify
- [ ] Each panel has a one-sentence big idea that the headline and lead carry.
- [ ] Word counts are within the agreed limits for each element.
- [ ] Sentences are short and terms are explained; read-aloud test passed.
- [ ] Every fact is on a reference sheet and specialist-checked.
- [ ] A full-size prototype was tested with real visitors.
- [ ] Accessibility formats and mounting heights are addressed.
