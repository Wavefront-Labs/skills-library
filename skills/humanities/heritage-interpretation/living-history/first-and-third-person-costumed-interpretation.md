# First- and third-person costumed interpretation

**Goal:** bring a historic site to life through people in period dress, without sacrificing
accuracy, visitor comfort or safety.

## 1. Choose the mode
- **Third person**: period clothing, modern voice. "People here would have baked twice a
  week…" Easy for visitors to question, flexible, good for demonstrations and mixed
  audiences. Most common.
- **First person**: you *are* a historical (real or composite) person, in a fixed year;
  you speak only from that knowledge. Powerful and memorable; demanding; can confuse or
  unsettle some visitors.
- **Second person** (sometimes used): visitors are invited to take part — try the task,
  wear the apron.
Many sites mix modes, with a clear signal (a badge, a "modern" colleague nearby, a set
area) so visitors know which they're getting.

## 2. Research the character or role
- Fix **year, place, social position, occupation, family, religion, politics, literacy**.
  Real individuals: probate inventories, letters, court and parish records. Composites:
  base each attribute on documented people of that type.
- Learn the **daily round**: work, food, prices, news of the week. Visitors ask "what's
  for dinner" and "what's that cost" more than they ask about battles.
- Learn **what the character couldn't know** (later events, modern concepts) and how they'd
  react to unfamiliar questions.
- Keep a **character file with sources**; the site's research team should sign it off.

## 3. Authenticity standards
- Clothing, tools and food to a documented standard: fabric, cut, construction (hand-
  vs machine-sewn where visible), colours available, how they were worn.
- Decide the site's **"authenticity line"**: e.g. modern underwear, glasses, medication and
  safety equipment permitted; visible plastics not. Write it down.
- Record **known compromises** (fire-retardant treatments, food-safety rules) and be ready
  to explain them in third person.

## 4. Demonstrations
- Choose tasks visitors can see, smell and ask about: cooking, spinning, smithing, gardening.
- **Safety first**: risk assessment for fire, blades, hot metal, food hygiene (local food-
  safety law applies to anything visitors eat), barriers and distances. *Note:* follow your
  jurisdiction's health-and-safety and food rules — they override period practice.
- Narrate while working; stop work, not talking, when visitors arrive close to hazards.

## 5. Visitor interaction in role
- **Greet first** and invite in: "Good day — you're welcome to come and warm yourselves."
- Answer anachronistic questions **in character but helpfully**: "A telephone? I don't know
  that word — do you mean a messenger?" Then let the visitor in on the game.
- **Break character** (step out, signal clearly) when: a visitor is distressed or confused,
  a child is frightened, a safety issue arises, a visitor needs real information (toilets,
  first aid), or a sensitive topic would be harmful to play.
- Difficult histories (slavery, violence, prejudice of the period): first-person portrayal
  of oppression requires training, community consultation and debrief support. Many sites
  pair first-person scenes with a third-person facilitator.

## Gotchas
- **Playing period prejudice for laughs** — or making characters conveniently modern in
  their views. Both distort the past.
- **Visitors who try to break you** (teenagers, "gotcha" questions): stay friendly; a short
  in-role answer and a smile usually ends it.
- **Interpreter fatigue**: first person is tiring; rotate and schedule breaks out of sight.
- Overloading on facts; one good story beats ten details.

## Verify
- [ ] The mode is chosen and visibly signalled to visitors.
- [ ] Character/role file is researched, sourced and signed off.
- [ ] The site's authenticity line and known compromises are written down.
- [ ] Every demonstration has a risk assessment and complies with food and safety rules.
- [ ] Interpreters know when and how to break character.
- [ ] Difficult-history portrayals have consultation, training and debrief.
