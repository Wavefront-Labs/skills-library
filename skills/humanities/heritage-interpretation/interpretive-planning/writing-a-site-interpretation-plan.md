# Writing a site interpretation plan

**Goal:** produce a plan that decides, for a whole site, what visitors should come away
understanding, who they are, and which media carry which message where — before anyone
designs a panel or commissions an app.

## 1. Start from significance, not from stories you like
- Gather the **statement of significance** (from a conservation management plan if one
  exists): why the place matters, to whom, and which attributes carry that significance.
- Inventory **resources**: physical fabric, collections, archives, oral history, landscape,
  views, sounds, people (staff, volunteers, community).
- Note **contested and missing narratives**: whose stories aren't told (enslaved and
  servant labour, women, Indigenous peoples, migrants)? The ICOMOS Ename Charter asks for
  inclusive interpretation and community participation.

## 2. Know the audiences
- Use existing **visitor data**: numbers, seasons, dwell time, origin, repeat visits, group
  types (families, schools, coach tours, local residents, specialist visitors).
- Identify **target audiences** you want but don't yet get — and why (cost, access,
  relevance, language).
- Consider **access needs**: mobility, sensory, cognitive, language. Plan them in, not on.
- Do **front-end evaluation** (short interviews, surveys) to learn what visitors already
  know and believe; misconceptions are an interpretive opportunity.

## 3. Write the theme
Ham's **TORE**: interpretation should be **Thematic, Organised, Relevant, Enjoyable**.
- A **theme** is a complete sentence stating an idea, not a topic.
  Topic: "the mill". Theme: "The mill made this village rich — and the river paid the price."
- One **central theme** for the site, 3–5 **sub-themes**, each linked to specific places
  and resources.
- Tilden: interpretation should **provoke** and **relate** to the visitor, revealing meaning,
  not just information.

## 4. Choose media per message and place
For each sub-theme × location, choose media by:
- **Audience fit** (families → hands-on; specialists → depth on demand).
- **Site constraints**: listed building fabric (no fixings), weather, light, capacity,
  staffing.
- **Cost over lifetime**: content updates, maintenance, battery/tech replacement.
- **Message type**: atmosphere (sound, live interpretation) vs facts (labels) vs
  reconstruction (models, AR).
Typical mix: orientation (entrance), self-guided spine (panels/trail), depth layers
(guidebook, app, audio), live interpretation (tours, demonstration). See related:
`humanities.heritage-interpretation.interpretive-writing.writing-panels-and-labels-people-read`.

## 5. Plan structure (typical sections)
1. Purpose, scope, stakeholders.
2. Significance and resources.
3. Audiences (current, target, needs).
4. Central theme, sub-themes, visitor outcomes (know / feel / do).
5. Visitor journey and media plan (table: location, sub-theme, audience, media, content notes).
6. Implementation: phasing, costs, responsibilities.
7. Evaluation: formative (prototype testing) and summative (did outcomes happen?).

## Gotchas
- **Topic lists masquerading as themes** — the most common failure.
- **Too many messages**: visitors remember little; prioritise.
- **Media-led planning** ("we need an app") instead of message-led.
- **Ignoring staffing**: live interpretation and digital both need ongoing people and money.
- Consult heritage regulators early for physical interventions in protected places.

## Verify
- [ ] The plan links each theme to significance and to actual resources on site.
- [ ] Current and target audiences are described with data, plus access needs.
- [ ] The central theme and sub-themes are full-sentence ideas.
- [ ] A media table maps message × place × audience × medium, with lifetime cost.
- [ ] Underrepresented narratives and communities are addressed.
- [ ] Measurable visitor outcomes and an evaluation method are stated.
