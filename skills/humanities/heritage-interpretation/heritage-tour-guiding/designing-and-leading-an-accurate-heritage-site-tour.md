# Designing and leading an accurate heritage-site tour

**Goal:** lead 10–25 visitors through a historic site in a fixed time, leaving them with
one clear idea, well-sourced stories, and the building undamaged.

*For general tour delivery craft (voice, coach and city tours), see
`hospitality-culinary.tourism-and-travel-services.tour-guiding.commentary-structure-and-delivery-on-tour`.
This page focuses on historic sites: accuracy, fabric and difficult pasts.*

## 1. Pick a theme and build the route around it
- One **theme sentence** (an idea, not a topic): "This house was built to impress, and
  every room tells you who was meant to be impressed — and who did the work."
  If the site has an interpretation plan, use its themes.
- Choose **6–10 stops** for a 45–60 min tour. Each stop: one point that serves the theme,
  one object/feature to look at, one story.
- Route constraints: one-way systems, bottlenecks (staircases, narrow passages), stops
  where everyone can see and hear, other groups, rooms closed for conservation, step-free
  alternatives.
- Time each stop (typically 3–6 min) and walk the route with a stopwatch.

## 2. Source every story
Heritage sites are full of tradition that isn't true: secret tunnels, "Queen Elizabeth slept
here", "this is where the phrase X comes from".
- Keep a **tour script with sources** (archives, the site's research files, curator notes,
  published histories). Mark each claim: documented / probable / tradition.
- Present tradition as tradition: "The family story is that… there's no record of it, but…"
  That's often more interesting than pretending.
- Check dates, names and numbers with the curator or site historian; get updates when
  research changes.

## 3. Deliver at each stop
1. **Gather** — wait until the group is assembled and can see; position yourself with
   your back to the least interesting view, facing the group.
2. **Point** — "Look at the ceiling above the fireplace." Direct attention to something real.
3. **Tell** — the story, briefly, with sensory and human detail.
4. **Link** — connect back to the theme and onward to the next stop.
Ask open questions ("What do you notice about…?") sparingly and honestly; give time.

## 4. Handling the group in a fragile building
- Brief at the start: no touching, bags in front in narrow rooms, stay behind ropes, flash
  rules, where the toilets and exits are.
- Count at each move; designate a back marker for large groups if available.
- Watch for elbows and bags near objects, for heat and crowding (fragile textiles and
  humidity limits sometimes cap room capacity).
- Know the **emergency procedures** and your role.

## 5. Answering questions
- **Repeat the question** so everyone hears.
- If you don't know: "I don't know — I'll find out" (and do), or point to the site
  historian. Never invent.
- Hostile or loaded questions: acknowledge, give evidence, move on; don't debate at length.
- Note recurring questions — they show what the tour is missing.

## 6. Difficult histories
Slavery, colonial wealth, war, institutional abuse. Be **direct and evidence-based**; use
names of people where known; avoid euphemism ("servants" for enslaved people). Warn
visitors briefly when content is distressing. Expect some visitors to react strongly —
stay calm and factual. Follow the site's agreed approach and community consultation.

## Gotchas
- Too much content: guides cut least, visitors remember least. Each stop has one point.
- Reading the script aloud — memorise structure, not sentences.
- Drifting into personal opinion on contested present-day politics.
- Mixing dates/eras confusingly — use a simple timeline at the start.

## Verify
- [ ] The tour has one theme sentence, and every stop serves it.
- [ ] Route was walked and timed, with accessible alternatives.
- [ ] Every story in the script has a source and a certainty grade.
- [ ] Group briefing covers object safety and emergencies.
- [ ] Unknown answers are followed up, not invented.
- [ ] Difficult histories are told plainly, with names and evidence.
