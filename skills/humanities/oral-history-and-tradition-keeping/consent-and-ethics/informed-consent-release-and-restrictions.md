# Informed consent, release and restrictions

**Goal:** make every recording legally usable and ethically sound: the narrator understands
and agrees to what will happen with their story, the project holds the rights it needs, and
the narrator's limits are recorded and honoured.

*Not legal advice. Law differs by country (copyright in recordings and in spoken words;
data protection; defamation). Have your release reviewed for your jurisdiction.*

## 1. Consent is a process, not a form
1. **Before**: explain in plain language the project's purpose, who will hear or read the
   interview, where it will be archived, online publication plans, and the narrator's
   right to skip questions, stop, restrict or (within limits) withdraw.
2. **During**: remind at the start (on tape) and when sensitive topics come up.
3. **After**: the narrator signs the **release** once they know what they said; offer to
   send the recording or transcript first.
4. **Ongoing**: keep contact details; notify narrators of significant new uses where
   feasible.
Check **capacity** (e.g. narrators with dementia) and use supported decision-making or
appropriate proxies only per your ethics framework.

## 2. What the release (recording agreement) should cover
- **Copyright**: in many jurisdictions the narrator owns copyright in their words and the
  interviewer (or employer) in the recording/questions. The release usually **assigns** or
  **licenses** copyright to the repository. A licence (narrator keeps copyright) is often
  more respectful; it must be broad enough for intended uses.
- **Uses**: archive access, research, education, exhibitions, broadcast, online, publication,
  derivative works; commercial use stated explicitly.
- **Online/open licensing** (e.g. Creative Commons) if planned.
- **Restrictions** (section 3).
- **Credit**: name, pseudonym or anonymous.
- **Signatures** of narrator and interviewer, date; repository details.
- In the UK/EU, a **data-protection** notice (lawful basis, retention, rights).
Archives often have templates; use the receiving archive's before you start.

## 3. Restrictions and embargoes
Offer, and record precisely:
- **Closure** of the whole interview or parts (time-coded) until a date or for N years, or
  until the narrator's death.
- **Access conditions**: e.g. "researchers only", "not online", "with permission".
- **Redaction** of names or passages in public versions.
- **Anonymisation**: hard in oral history — voice, places and details identify. Say
  honestly what anonymity can and can't guarantee.
Record restrictions in the catalogue so the archive enforces them.

## 4. Risks to third parties and to the project
- **Defamation**: narrators may make damaging allegations about named living people.
  Review before publication; redact or restrict. (The dead generally can't be defamed, but
  privacy and family harm still matter.)
- **Privacy/sensitive data**: health, sexuality, criminal allegations, immigration
  status. Consider risk to narrator and others; GDPR "special category" data needs care.
- **Illegal activity disclosed**: know your project's policy and any legal obligations
  (e.g. safeguarding) before interviewing.

## 5. Shared authority and narrator rights
Frisch's **"shared authority"**: narrators are co-creators of meaning. Practice:
involve narrators in reviewing transcripts and outputs, credit them, return copies, and
consult communities on how stories are framed. Respect community protocols over cultural
knowledge (see `humanities.oral-history-and-tradition-keeping.tradition-keeping.memorisation-recitation-and-custodial-transmission`).

## 6. Withdrawal
State the realistic limit: withdrawal is possible until publication/online release; after
that it can be limited to removing access. Act promptly on requests.

## Gotchas
- **Interviewing first, paperwork later** — narrators die or lose touch; get at least
  recorded oral consent at the start.
- Release too narrow for later uses (e.g. "for this exhibition only").
- Restrictions noted on paper but not in the catalogue.
- Institutional ethics review (IRB/REC): in the US, many oral history projects are not
  "research" under the 2018 Common Rule definition, but universities differ — check.

## Verify
- [ ] Narrator received plain-language information before the interview.
- [ ] Release covers copyright, uses, online, credit, and data protection where needed.
- [ ] Restrictions are time-coded, dated and entered in the catalogue.
- [ ] Defamation and privacy review done before any publication.
- [ ] Narrator got copies and a route to raise concerns or withdraw.
