# Scoping, staffing and returning a community oral-history project

**Goal:** plan and deliver a project that records a community's memories well, is owned in
part by that community, ends with a durable archive, and gives something back people value.

## 1. Scope it
- **Question and boundary**: one clear focus (a closed factory, a migration, a
  neighbourhood before redevelopment, a sports club's history). Write it as a question:
  "How did the closure of the docks change family life in X?"
- **Who decides**: set up a **steering group** that includes community members, not just
  the organisation running it. Agree decision-making and credit early.
- **Scale**: be realistic. A common small project is 15–30 interviews over 12–18 months.
  Each interview costs far more than the recording time: pre-interview, travel,
  recording, backup, summary/index, transcript, audit, paperwork, deposit.
- **Archive partner**: agree with a local record office, library, museum or university
  archive *before* recording: formats, release template, metadata, restrictions handling.
- **Outputs**: decide early what the community wants (exhibition, booklet, podcast,
  website, school resource, listening event) — it shapes who you interview.

## 2. Budget lines people forget
Recorders, mics and memory cards; storage and backups; transcription (or staff time);
volunteer expenses (travel, refreshments); training; accessibility (interpreters, large
print, venues); archive deposit costs; output production; evaluation; contingency.
Grant funders (e.g. national heritage funds) often require an evaluation and a legacy plan.

## 3. Recruit narrators deliberately
- Map the **range of perspectives** relevant to the question: different roles, ages,
  genders, ethnicities, classes, sides of a dispute, people who left.
- Recruit through **multiple routes**: community groups, faith centres, clubs, local
  newspapers and radio, social media, word of mouth, existing narrators ("who else should
  I talk to?").
- Watch **gatekeepers**: a single contact can steer the project toward their network.
- Prioritise **oldest and most at-risk** voices early.
- Keep a **narrator tracker** (contact, status, consent stage, restrictions) — store it
  securely; it contains personal data.

## 4. Train interviewers
A typical one-day volunteer training covers:
1. What oral history is; memory and subjectivity.
2. Ethics, consent and release (see
   `humanities.oral-history-and-tradition-keeping.consent-and-ethics.informed-consent-release-and-restrictions`).
3. Question design and listening (see
   `humanities.oral-history-and-tradition-keeping.oral-history-interviewing.preparing-and-conducting-a-life-story-interview`).
4. **Hands-on recorder practice** — everyone records and plays back a practice interview.
5. File handling, backup and paperwork.
6. Looking after yourself and narrators; who to call if something goes wrong.
Follow up with **mentored first interviews** and listen-back feedback. Consider pairing
volunteers.

## 5. Process and quality
- Central log of every recording and where its copies are.
- Weekly or monthly check that paperwork, backups and summaries are complete.
- Agree a single style guide for summaries and transcripts
  (`humanities.oral-history-and-tradition-keeping.transcription-and-archiving.transcribing-indexing-and-depositing-interviews`).

## 6. Return results to the community
- **Narrators first**: copies of their own recording/transcript, invitations to events,
  first look at excerpts used.
- **Public outputs** in places people go (library, community centre, school), at times and
  in languages they can attend.
- **Listening events** where narrators hear each other — often the most valued output.
- Respect restrictions; get specific approval for sensitive excerpts.
- Leave a **legacy**: archived collection, training notes, equipment, and community members
  able to continue.

## Gotchas
- **Over-recording**: interviews pile up unprocessed. Process as you go.
- **Single-voice history**: only the confident, well-connected or official story.
- **Volunteer drop-off**: build in social time and recognition.
- **Ownership disputes** at the end — settle rights and credit at the start.

## Verify
- [ ] Project question, steering group and archive partner are agreed before recording.
- [ ] Budget covers processing, deposit, access and evaluation, not just recording.
- [ ] Narrator recruitment covers the range of perspectives, tracked securely.
- [ ] Interviewers trained hands-on and mentored on first interviews.
- [ ] Every recording is logged, backed up, summarised and has paperwork.
- [ ] Narrators received copies and the community had accessible outputs.
