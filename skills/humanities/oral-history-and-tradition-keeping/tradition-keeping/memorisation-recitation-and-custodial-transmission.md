# Memorisation, recitation and custodial transmission

**Goal:** understand and support how a spoken record — genealogies, histories, law,
epics, ritual knowledge — is held accurately across generations, whether you are learning
within a tradition, documenting it, or helping a community keep it going.

*Important:* every tradition has its own methods, rules and authority. Griot (jali/jeli)
lineages in West Africa, Māori whakapapa experts, Aboriginal Australian song custodians,
Pacific navigators and many others differ greatly. This page draws on scholarship
(Vansina, Lord, Hale, Kelly) and generalises; **the community's knowledge-holders are the
authority**, not this page. *Uncertainty:* generalisations across traditions are
approximate.

## 1. How accuracy is achieved (documented techniques)
- **Structure carries memory.** Genealogies are recited in fixed order and rhythm; epics are
  built from **formulas** and **type-scenes** (Parry–Lord oral-formulaic theory) that let a
  performer recompose fluently rather than recall word for word.
- **Fixed vs fluid text**: some genres demand verbatim accuracy (ritual formulas, some
  genealogies, sacred songs); others are recomposed in performance around a stable core
  (narratives, praise). Know which you're dealing with — "accuracy" means different things.
- **Place and object mnemonics**: knowledge linked to landscape (songlines), to journeys,
  to carved or woven objects, string figures or memory boards. Kelly links these to the
  **method of loci**.
- **Music and metre**: melody, drum patterns and metre constrain wording and make errors
  audible.
- **Repetition with audience**: regular public performance means errors are noticed and
  corrected by listeners and peers.

## 2. Learning and practising (apprentice model)
Where communities teach openly, transmission typically involves:
1. **Long apprenticeship**, often from childhood, within a family or lineage.
2. **Listening before speaking**: years of attending performances.
3. **Segmented learning**: learn a section (one generation, one episode) until perfect, then
   chain it; rehearse daily.
4. **Performance under supervision**, with correction by a master.
5. **Gradual authorisation**: the right to perform certain parts, or to perform in public,
   is granted, not assumed.
For your own memorisation of a recitation you are entitled to learn: break it into
units, anchor each to a place or object, recite aloud daily, practise with music/rhythm if
the tradition uses it, and recite to a qualified listener for correction.

## 3. Protocols for custodianship
- **Rights to speak**: some knowledge belongs to particular lineages, genders, initiation
  levels or clans. Ask who may hold, perform and hear it.
- **Restricted knowledge**: some material must not be recorded, published or shared outside
  defined groups. Recording it can cause real harm. Ask before recording; respect "no".
- **Seasonal and contextual rules**: some stories are told only at certain times or places.
- **Succession**: who will hold the knowledge next? Communities often want help supporting
  learners, not just archives.

## 4. Documenting a tradition as an outsider
- Work through the community's **authorities and protocols**; get collective as well as
  individual consent where the knowledge is collective.
- Use the consent and archive practice in
  `humanities.oral-history-and-tradition-keeping.consent-and-ethics.informed-consent-release-and-restrictions`,
  extended with **community access protocols** (e.g. Mukurtu CMS supports
  culturally-defined access levels; **Traditional Knowledge (TK) Labels** from Local
  Contexts flag cultural conditions on use).
- **Recording supports, it doesn't replace**, living transmission. Avoid freezing one
  performance as "the authoritative version" if the tradition is fluid.
- Vansina's cautions for historians: traditions are shaped by present interests (e.g.
  legitimising authority), compress or telescope chronology, and may merge figures. Use
  them as history with source criticism, alongside other evidence.
- The **UNESCO 2003 Convention** frames safeguarding as supporting communities' own
  transmission.

## Gotchas
- Treating variation as error — or treating all variation as acceptable in a fixed genre.
- Publishing restricted knowledge because a single individual agreed.
- Recording elders while ignoring the need for young learners.
- Romanticising: traditions are maintained by skilled specialists with status and
  obligations, and they change.
