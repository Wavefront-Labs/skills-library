# Reading a building's construction history

**Goal:** turn a standing building into a dated sequence — what was built first, what was
added, removed or rebuilt, and when — with each claim tied to physical or documentary
evidence. This underpins historical writing, conservation plans and listing/designation
cases.

## 1. Desk research first
- **Maps**: successive editions of large-scale maps (in Britain, Ordnance Survey 25-inch
  and town plans; in the US, Sanborn fire-insurance maps) show footprint changes between
  dates — a cheap way to bracket extensions.
- **Documents**: title deeds, estate surveys, tax and rate books, building-control and
  permit files, architects' drawings, sale particulars, insurance records, trade directories.
- **Images**: old photographs, postcards, prints, aerial photographs.
- **Existing records**: designation (listing) descriptions, previous surveys, local
  historic environment records. Treat listing descriptions as starting hypotheses — many
  were written quickly from the street.

## 2. Look at the fabric systematically
Walk outside then inside, every room, roof space and cellar if safe and permitted. Note:
- **Straight joints and breaks** — a vertical seam where masonry doesn't bond means one
  part was built against another, which came first. Bonded corners mean same build.
- **Materials and bonds** — brick sizes and bonds (English, Flemish, stretcher), stone
  dressing, mortar type (lime vs cement). Changes signal phases. *Brick-size dating is
  regional and unreliable on its own.*
- **Blocked openings** — filled doors and windows, changed sill heights, relieving arches
  over nothing.
- **Roof structure** — truss type, carpenters' assembly marks, reused timbers (empty
  mortices), smoke blackening (evidence of an open hearth before a chimney was inserted).
- **Mouldings and joinery** — profiles of cornices, architraves, window glazing bars;
  datable within regional ranges using pattern books and type series.
- **Fixtures** — fireplaces, staircases, nails (hand-forged vs machine-cut vs wire) and
  screws.
- **Alignment** — walls off-square to each other, changes in floor level.

## 3. Scientific dating
- **Dendrochronology** for timbers with enough rings (commonly cited as 50+) and ideally
  surviving bark edge, which gives a felling season; without sapwood you only get a
  terminus post quem. Commission from an accredited lab; sampling is destructive and
  needs consent.
- Others (radiocarbon, mortar dating, thermoluminescence of brick) are less precise —
  use as supporting evidence. *Confirm suitability with a specialist; this summary is
  general.*

## 4. Record and phase
- Measured plans and sections (or annotate existing drawings / a laser-scan survey).
- Photograph systematically with a scale and a numbered photo register keyed to plans.
- Draw a **phase plan**: colour-code each phase, with a key giving date range and evidence.
- Recording levels (Historic England Levels 1–4; HABS levels in the US) define how much
  detail is expected — agree the level in advance.

## 5. Write the history
Narrate phase by phase. For each date, give the evidence and its strength: "documented"
(deed of 1782), "dated" (dendro felling winter 1611/12), "stylistic" (moulding profile,
c. 1720–50), "inferred". Note what is unresolved.

## Gotchas
- Façades are often refronted: a Georgian front can hide a timber-framed medieval core.
- Reused material (spolia, salvaged timbers) gives dates older than the building.
- Styles lag in provincial and vernacular building; a style date is a range, not a year.
- Map surveys may be years older than their publication date.
- Never remove fabric or take samples without the owner's and (for designated buildings)
  the authority's consent.

- [ ] Date claims are graded by strength (documented/scientific/stylistic/inferred).
- [ ] Photographs are scaled, numbered and keyed to plans.
