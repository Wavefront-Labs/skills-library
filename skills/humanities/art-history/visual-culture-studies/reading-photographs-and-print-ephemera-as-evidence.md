# Reading photographs and print ephemera as evidence

**Goal:** treat everyday images — family photos, press photographs, posters, adverts,
postcards, packaging — as sources you can date, situate and interpret, rather than as
transparent windows on the past or illustrations bolted onto a text.

## 1. The image is an object
Start with the physical thing (or its best surrogate): size, support, process, mounts,
stamps, handwriting on the back, album placement, trims and damage. Edwards and Hart's
point is that photographs are handled, captioned, exchanged and stored — and those uses
are evidence too. A scan stripped of its back and album page loses half its information.

## 2. Date and identify the process
For photographs, process and format give date ranges (*broad ranges, from general
reference knowledge — confirm with a process guide or conservator*):
- daguerreotype (unique, mirror-like, cased): roughly 1840s–1850s;
- ambrotype and tintype: mid-1850s onward, tintypes into the early 20th century;
- albumen prints on card mounts — **carte de visite** (small) and **cabinet card**
  (larger): roughly 1860s–1890s, with mount styles, colours and studio imprints narrowing
  the date;
- gelatin silver prints: dominant from the 1890s through most of the 20th century;
- chromogenic colour prints and later digital.
Also use clothing, hairstyles, vehicles, signage, and studio addresses in trade
directories. For print ephemera: printing method (letterpress, lithography, offset —
check with a loupe for halftone dots), typefaces, prices, phone-number formats, postal
codes, printer's imprint.

## 3. Production: who made it and why
- Who commissioned and who produced it (studio, agency, ad agency, state ministry)?
- Conventions and constraints: long exposures forced stiff poses; studio props and
  backdrops were shared; press photos were cropped and retouched; propaganda was planned.
- Was the scene staged or reconstructed? Many famous "documentary" images were posed or
  composited — check the photographer's other frames (contact sheets) if they survive.

## 4. Circulation and reception
Trace where the image went: newspaper, album, shop window, wall, postcard rack, social
media. Same image, different captions, different meanings. Look for reprints and
adaptations; a reverse-image search (Google Lens, TinEye) helps for modern circulation.

## 5. Read it critically
Burke's cautions apply: images show what makers wanted seen and what viewers expected.
Ask what's outside the frame, who's absent, who is looking at whom, how class, gender,
race and colonial relations are staged. Read with other sources — never let one image
carry a claim alone.

## 6. Archive and cite
- Record repository, collection, item number, photographer/maker, date (with basis),
  process, dimensions, inscriptions.
- Note where the caption came from — archive captions are often added later and can be
  wrong.
- Check copyright and orphan-works status before reproduction.

## Gotchas
- Digitised collections often lose the backs, mounts and album context; ask for them.
- Hand-colouring, retouching and composite printing predate digital manipulation by a
  century.
- Postcard postmarks date the sending, not the photograph.
- Family attributions ("Grandma's sister") are memory, not fact; corroborate.
- Mass-produced ephemera survive unevenly — the archive over-represents what someone
  thought worth keeping.
