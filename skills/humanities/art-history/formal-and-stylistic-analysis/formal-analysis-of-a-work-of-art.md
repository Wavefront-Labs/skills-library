# Formal analysis of a work of art

**Goal:** describe what you see precisely enough that a reader who has never seen the work
could picture it, then argue what those formal choices *do* — and use them, by
comparison, to place the work in time, place or hand.

## 1. Look first, read later
Spend at least 15–20 minutes with the object before reading about it — in person if at
all possible. Reproductions lie about **scale, colour, surface and texture**; a work you
thought was monumental may be the size of a postcard. Note your viewing conditions
(lighting, glazing, hang height, which reproduction).

## 2. Gather the basics (the "tombstone")
Artist (or "unknown"/workshop), title, date, medium and support, dimensions (H × W × D,
cm), current location and inventory number. Medium matters: egg tempera, oil, fresco,
bronze cast vs carved marble each impose different possibilities.

## 3. Work through the formal elements
Make notes under each heading, observing before interpreting:
- **Composition** — overall shape (pyramid, diagonal, grid), balance and symmetry,
  where the eye enters and moves, focal point, framing and cropping.
- **Line** — contour vs implied lines, sharp or soft edges, direction and rhythm.
- **Colour and value** — palette, warm/cool, saturation, contrast of light and dark
  (chiaroscuro, tenebrism), where the lightest and darkest areas sit.
- **Space** — linear perspective and vanishing point, atmospheric perspective,
  overlapping, flatness; for sculpture/architecture, how it occupies real space and
  invites movement around it.
- **Light** — source, consistency, whether it models form or flattens it.
- **Facture/surface** — brushwork visible or blended, impasto, glazes, chisel marks,
  finish.
- **Scale** — relation to the viewer's body; intended viewing distance.
- **Figures** — pose, gesture, gaze (where they look, whether they address us).

## 4. Turn description into argument
Pick the two or three formal features doing the most work and connect them: "The steep
diagonal and the cut-off figures at the edge make the scene feel like a fragment of a
larger, ongoing event." A formal analysis needs a thesis, not just a checklist.

## 5. Stylistic analysis: comparison
Style is visible only by contrast. Set the work beside one or two others (same subject,
different period; same artist, different date; same school, different hand). Wölfflin's
paired concepts — **linear/painterly, plane/recession, closed/open form,
multiplicity/unity, absolute/relative clarity** — are a classic tool for contrasting
Renaissance and Baroque, though they're now treated as heuristics, not laws.
For attribution, connoisseurs (Morelli's method) compare habitual details an artist paints
without thinking — ears, hands, drapery folds. Pair that with technical evidence
(see catalogue-raisonné and provenance skills); eye alone is no longer sufficient.

## 6. Writing it up
- Describe from general to specific, in an order the reader can follow (left to right,
  foreground to background).
- Use present tense for what the work shows.
- Precise vocabulary beats adjectives: "a cool, desaturated blue-grey" not "a sad colour".
- Include a reproduction with caption and credit; refer to it by figure number.

## Gotchas
- Colour in digital images varies with camera, file and screen; never argue fine colour
  points from a web JPEG alone.
- Condition changes appearance: varnish yellows, pigments fade (some reds and blues are
  known to shift), sculptures lose original paint. Check conservation reports.
- Don't confuse the subject (what's depicted — iconography) with form (how). Formal
  analysis can come first, but both are needed for a full reading.
- Period labels (Mannerist, Rococo) are later constructions; use them, but don't let a
  label substitute for looking.

## Verify
- [ ] You looked at the object (or the best reproduction) before reading about it.
- [ ] Tombstone data is complete, including dimensions.
- [ ] Each formal element was observed and noted.
- [ ] The essay has a thesis linking form to effect.
- [ ] Any stylistic claim rests on an explicit comparison.
- [ ] Condition and reproduction limits are acknowledged where they matter.
