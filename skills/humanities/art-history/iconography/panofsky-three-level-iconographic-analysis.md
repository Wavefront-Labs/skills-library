# Panofsky three-level iconographic analysis

**Goal:** work out *what* an image shows (which story, which saint, which god) and then
*why* it was shown that way, in that place, for those viewers — without over-reading
symbols that may be mere furniture.

## 1. Level one — pre-iconographic description
Describe what anyone could see without specialised knowledge: "a young woman holding a
wheel with spikes, a crowned figure lying at her feet, a palm branch." Resist naming yet.
Panofsky called this primary or natural subject matter; its control is familiarity with
how things look and how styles render them (a figure floating may be a vision, not a
falling body — style tells you which).

## 2. Level two — iconographic analysis
Identify the conventional subject:
- **Attributes** — objects that identify figures. Spiked wheel → St Catherine of
  Alexandria; keys → St Peter; lion → St Mark or St Jerome (context decides); club and
  lion skin → Hercules; in Buddhist art, mudras and objects identify bodhisattvas.
- **Textual sources** — the image usually follows a text: the Bible, Apocrypha, Jacobus de
  Voragine's *Golden Legend* for saints, Ovid's *Metamorphoses* for myths, sutras and
  Puranas for Buddhist and Hindu imagery, Cesare Ripa's *Iconologia* for personifications.
  Find the specific passage and check the image against it detail by detail; divergences
  are evidence too.
- **Reference tools** — Iconclass (a hierarchical classification of subjects with
  alphanumeric codes, e.g. 73 for New Testament; *specific deeper codes should be looked
  up, not recalled*), subject dictionaries (Hall's *Dictionary of Subjects and Symbols in
  Art* is a standard starter), and photo archives searchable by subject.
- **Visual precedents** — earlier images of the same subject; artists copied compositions
  as often as they read texts.
The control here is the history of **types**: how a given theme was conventionally shown in
that period and place.

## 3. Level three — iconological interpretation
Ask what the choice of subject and its treatment reveal about the culture, patron and
moment: why this saint in this town's chapel (local cult, plague, guild patronage)? Why
this myth in a marriage chest? Evidence: contracts and commissions, patron's biography,
the building's liturgy or function, contemporary writings. Panofsky called the control
here the history of "cultural symptoms" — it's the most speculative level and needs the
most documentation.

## 4. Programmes
In a cycle (chapel frescoes, a ceiling, a manuscript, a temple facade) read the parts as a
designed whole: order, pairings (Old/New Testament typology), hierarchy by position and
scale. Find out who devised the programme — often a theologian or humanist adviser, not
the artist.

## Gotchas
- Not every object is a symbol. The "disguised symbolism" approach to Early Netherlandish
  painting has been criticised for finding meaning in every lily and candle; demand
  textual or contextual support.
- Attributes are shared and shift over time and region; one attribute rarely settles an
  identification — look for combinations.
- Titles are often later inventions by dealers or cataloguers; don't treat them as evidence
  of the artist's intended subject.
- Panofsky's scheme was built on European art; applying it to other traditions works
  only if you use those traditions' own texts and categories.
- Restorations and overpainting can add or remove attributes; check condition reports.

## Verify
- [ ] Level one description was written before any identification.
- [ ] Identification rests on a combination of attributes, not one.
- [ ] The specific textual source passage is cited and compared with the image.
- [ ] Iconclass or a subject reference was consulted, codes looked up not guessed.
- [ ] Iconological claims are backed by documents about patron, place or function.
- [ ] Symbolic readings of incidental objects have independent support.
