# Designing a cross-tradition comparison

**Goal:** compare concepts, practices or institutions across religious traditions in a
way that teaches something about each — not a list of surface likenesses, and not an
argument that "all religions really say the same thing".

## 1. Stance: scholarship, not theology
Academic comparison is **methodologically agnostic**: you describe and explain what
people believe and do, without ruling on whether it's true. Keep evaluative or
apologetic language out ("authentic", "higher", "primitive"). If you are an insider to
one tradition, declare it.

## 2. Choose the point of comparison precisely
J. Z. Smith's warning: comparison is something the scholar *constructs* for a purpose, so
state the purpose and the respect in which things are compared (the *tertium
comparationis*). Weak: "Prayer in Islam and Christianity." Better: "How obligatory
fixed-time prayer structures the day — salat in Sunni Islam and the monastic Liturgy of
the Hours in Benedictine Christianity." Narrow by:
- **tradition and sub-tradition** (Theravada, not "Buddhism");
- **period and place** (12th-century Cairo, not "Judaism");
- **level** — official doctrine, elite practice, or popular practice.

## 3. Build the description in each tradition's own terms first
For each case, describe it from primary sources and specialist scholarship before
comparing, using its own vocabulary (emic terms) with glosses. Only then map onto your
comparative (etic) category. Check whether your category even exists in the tradition —
"religion", "faith", "belief", "scripture" and "mysticism" are themselves Western/Christian-
shaped categories, and Masuzawa shows how the "world religions" list was constructed in
the 19th century.

## 4. Compare in both directions
Set up a table: rows = features relevant to your purpose (who performs it, when, required
or optional, textual basis, function, meaning to participants); columns = cases. Then
write up:
- **Similarities** — and whether they come from shared history (borrowing, common
  origin) or independent parallel development (analogy). This matters: one is a
  genealogical claim, the other a typological one.
- **Differences** — Smith insisted difference is what makes comparison informative.
- **What the comparison reveals** about each case that a single-case study would miss.

## 5. Typologies and grand theories, carefully
Classic typologies (Eliade's sacred/profane, Otto's numinous, Weber's ideal types of
religious leadership) are useful as prompts, not as conclusions. Eliade in particular is
criticised for selecting evidence to fit universal patterns. If you use one, test it
against your cases and report where it fails.

## Gotchas
- **Protestant default**: treating belief and text as the core of religion and ritual as
  secondary distorts traditions centred on practice, lineage or law.
- **Perennialism**: "the mystics of all traditions agree" claims usually ignore what the
  mystics themselves said about each other.
- **Unequal evidence**: comparing one tradition's scripture to another's village practice
  is not like with like.
- **Translation drift**: "God", "soul", "sin", "heaven" import Christian content; gloss or
  keep the original term.
- **Insider sensitivities**: some traditions reject comparison with others; describe
  respectfully without letting that set your method.

## Verify
- [ ] The comparison's purpose and the precise respect compared are stated.
- [ ] Each case is narrowed by sub-tradition, period, place and level.
- [ ] Each case is described in its own terms before mapping to a shared category.
- [ ] Differences are analysed as seriously as similarities.
- [ ] Genealogical vs analogical similarities are distinguished.
- [ ] No evaluative or apologetic judgements about truth or superiority.
