# Researching the history of a religious movement

**Goal:** write an evidence-based history of a religious movement — an ancient sect, a
medieval order, a modern revival, a new religious movement — that explains its rise,
spread and change, using the same critical standards as any other history and without
taking sides on its truth claims.

## 1. Define the movement and the period
Name the movement, its self-designation and outsiders' labels (often hostile: "cult",
"heresy", "sect"), and choose neutral terms. Set start and end dates and the geographical
frame. Decide your focus: founder and origins, institutional development, spread, doctrine,
or social composition.

## 2. Classify your sources by standpoint
- **Insider** — scriptures and founding texts, sermons, letters, internal histories,
  hagiography (saints' and founders' lives), membership publications. Rich but shaped by
  edification: miracles, persecution narratives and conversion stories follow genre
  templates.
- **Outsider-hostile** — heresiologies, polemics, inquisition and court records, anti-cult
  literature, critical press. Detailed but distorting; still often our only source for
  suppressed groups.
- **Outsider-administrative** — tax lists, censuses, police and colonial reports, property
  records, newspapers. Less interested, so often more reliable for numbers and dates.
- **Ex-member** accounts — valuable, but shaped by the circumstances of leaving.
Read each against the grain and cross-check across standpoints. A founder's life written
decades later by followers is a source for the later community's self-image first.

## 3. Archives
Denominational archives, mission society archives (rich for 19th–20th century global
history), monastic and diocesan records, government archives. Access to private religious
archives may need permission and can be restricted; ask early, and record any conditions
on use.

## 4. Explaining rise and spread
Test explanations against evidence rather than adopting one:
- **Charisma and routinisation** (Weber): how authority passed from founder to institution;
  look at succession crises and schisms.
- **Social networks**: conversion usually runs along existing family and friendship ties
  (Stark and others); reconstruct networks from letters, marriage records, membership lists.
- **Supply-side / rational choice** theories of religious markets — debated; use with care.
- **Political and economic context**: patronage, state sponsorship or persecution, trade
  routes, migration, colonialism.
- **Ideas and practices**: what the movement offered that others did not.
Quantify where you can (membership counts, foundations of houses or congregations per
decade), stating source and reliability of each figure.

## 5. Change over time
Track doctrine, ritual, organisation and membership by period. Look for schisms and
reform movements as points where internal tensions become visible.

## 6. Ethics with living movements
Members and ex-members may be in conflict; anonymise where needed, avoid becoming a
partisan resource in disputes or litigation, and allow right of reply on contested facts
where practical.

## Gotchas
- Self-reported membership numbers are usually inflated; hostile estimates often deflated.
- Hagiographic chronology frequently compresses or reorders events.
- "Origins" accounts are often rewritten after schisms to legitimise one faction.
- Labels like "cult" carry legal and social weight; avoid them as analytic terms.
- Avoid teleology — the movement's later success was not inevitable at its start.

## Verify
- [ ] Neutral terminology is used, with self- and outsider-labels noted.
- [ ] Sources are classified by standpoint and cross-checked.
- [ ] Founder narratives are dated and read as later community texts where applicable.
- [ ] Explanations for spread are tested against network, context and numerical evidence.
- [ ] Every figure cites its source and reliability.
- [ ] Ethical risks with living communities were addressed.
