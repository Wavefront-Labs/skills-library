# Ethnographic observation of ritual and practice

**Goal:** study religion as people actually practise it — in homes, streets, shrines,
congregations and pilgrimages — through careful observation and conversation, as a
researcher, without pretending to belong or quietly converting your study into advocacy.

## 1. Frame the study
Lived-religion research (McGuire, Orsi, Ammerman) looks at practice, bodies, objects,
emotions and improvisation, not only official doctrine. Pick a bounded setting (one
congregation, one festival, one shrine, one pilgrimage route) and a question about
practice: *How do Mexican-American households maintain home altars? What do pilgrims do
at the shrine that the clergy don't prescribe?*

## 2. Ethics approval and access
- Institutional ethics review is required for human-subjects research; religious settings
  often need extra care because participants may be vulnerable (grieving, ill, minority
  faiths under pressure).
- Seek permission from whoever governs the space (clergy, temple committee, shrine
  custodians) *and* tell participants who you are. Covert research in religious groups is
  rarely justifiable.
- Ask about restrictions: spaces closed to non-members, gendered spaces, times when
  observation or recording is forbidden, dress codes.

## 3. Participation without pretence
- Decide in advance what you will and will not do. Standing, sitting and observing the
  etiquette (removing shoes, covering head) is respectful; taking communion, reciting
  creeds or performing initiations as a non-believer usually is not, and may offend.
  Ask a trusted contact.
- If asked about your own beliefs, answer honestly and briefly.
- Practise **methodological agnosticism**: record what participants experience and
  claim (healing, visions, blessings) in their terms, without endorsing or debunking.
- Insiders researching their own community need to work at making the familiar strange;
  outsiders at not exoticising.

## 4. Field notes
- **Jottings** during or right after (if writing during ritual is inappropriate, step out
  or use memory cues).
- **Full notes** the same day: sequence of events, who does what, spatial layout (sketch
  a plan), objects, sounds, smells, clothing, emotion, talk (verbatim where possible,
  marked as such).
- Keep observation, participants' explanations and your own interpretations in separate
  columns or tagged sections.
- Add a **reflexive log**: your reactions, mistakes, how people responded to you.

## 5. Material religion and media
Document objects (amulets, rosaries, altars, votive offerings, clothing, food) and their
handling. Photograph and audio-record only with permission, never during prohibited
moments, and avoid identifying faces without consent. Some objects or images are not to be
photographed at all.

## 6. Interviews and analysis
Follow observation with semi-structured interviews about what people did and why. Code
notes and transcripts (manually or in NVivo/ATLAS.ti/Taguette) inductively; compare
prescribed vs actual practice; look for variation by gender, age, class and generation.
Write "thick description" (Geertz) — enough context that the meaning of an act is clear.

## Gotchas
- Official spokespeople present the normative version; the lived version is in the
  margins — the kitchen, the car park, the after-party.
- One festival is one year's performance; return across cycles if possible.
- Your presence changes behaviour — note when.
- Don't publish details that could expose participants to persecution, family conflict or
  legal risk (e.g. converts in hostile settings).

## Verify
- [ ] Ethics approval and gatekeeper permission are documented.
- [ ] Participants know who you are; no covert observation.
- [ ] Participation limits were set and respected.
- [ ] Notes separate observation, emic explanation and your interpretation.
- [ ] Recording and photography followed consent and sacred restrictions.
- [ ] Analysis contrasts prescribed and practised religion and shows internal variation.
