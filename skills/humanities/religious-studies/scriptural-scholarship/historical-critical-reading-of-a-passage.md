# Historical-critical reading of a passage

**Goal:** produce an academic exegesis of a passage — what it meant in its time of
composition and editing, how it came to its present form, and how it was later read —
distinct from devotional or doctrinal interpretation.

The method was developed mainly on the Hebrew Bible and New Testament; analogous work on
the Qur'an, Pali Canon and other corpora adapts it. Examples below are biblical; the
steps carry over.

## 1. Establish the text
Work from the original language with a critical edition (for the Bible: BHS/BHQ for the
Hebrew Bible, Nestle–Aland NA28 or UBS5 for the New Testament; *edition numbers current to
my knowledge — check for newer*). Check the apparatus for variants that affect meaning;
see the sacred-language-philology skill for weighing them. Make your own working
translation; compare two or three scholarly translations.

## 2. Delimit the unit (pericope)
Find where it starts and ends: changes of scene, speaker, time, genre, or formulae
("After these things…"). Justify the boundaries — they shape interpretation.

## 3. Literary analysis
Structure (chiasm, parallelism, repetition), key terms and their range of meaning
(concordance and lexicon work: BDB/HALOT for Hebrew, BDAG for Greek), narrative point of
view, rhetoric. Note tensions: doublets, contradictions, abrupt shifts — these are the
data the next steps explain.

## 4. The critical methods
- **Source criticism** — does the passage combine earlier written sources? Classic cases:
  the Pentateuchal sources (the Documentary Hypothesis, now heavily revised and contested)
  and the Synoptic Problem (most scholars hold Markan priority plus a sayings source "Q"
  for Matthew and Luke; minority alternatives exist). State which model you assume.
- **Form criticism** — identify the genre/form (lament psalm, miracle story,
  pronouncement story, prophetic oracle, legal code) and its typical social setting
  (**Sitz im Leben**).
- **Tradition history** — how material developed in oral/written transmission.
- **Redaction criticism** — what the final editor changed or emphasised. With a synopsis
  (e.g. Aland's *Synopsis Quattuor Evangeliorum*), compare parallel passages line by line
  and colour-code differences; consistent edits reveal the editor's theology.

## 5. Historical context
Date and provenance of composition (with the scholarly range, not one confident year),
political and social setting, comparative material (Ancient Near Eastern texts, Second
Temple literature, Greco-Roman parallels, archaeology). Separate what the text claims
happened from what can be said historically.

## 6. Reception history
Trace how the passage was read later: early translations (Septuagint, Targums), Jewish
and Christian commentary, art, liturgy, politics. Reception is now a major field in its
own right and often explains why a passage matters today.

## 7. Write up
Thesis, then evidence step by step; engage two or three major critical commentaries
(e.g. the Hermeneia, Anchor Yale, or Word series) and recent journal literature; separate
clearly what is consensus, majority view and your proposal.

## Gotchas
- Hypothetical sources (Q, J/E/D/P) are scholarly reconstructions; don't cite them as if
  they were extant documents.
- Confessional commentaries mix exegesis with doctrine; know a series' stance.
- English concordances mislead — the same English word can translate different originals.
- Dating arguments are often circular; flag where yours depends on another hypothesis.
- Treat sacred texts and communities respectfully, but don't let that blunt the method.

## Verify
- [ ] The passage is read in the original language from a critical edition.
- [ ] Boundaries of the unit are justified.
- [ ] Source/form/redaction claims state the model they assume.
- [ ] Dates and provenance are given as scholarly ranges with evidence.
- [ ] At least one reception stage is traced.
- [ ] Consensus, majority and personal views are clearly distinguished.
