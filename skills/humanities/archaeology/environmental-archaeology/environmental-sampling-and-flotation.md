# Environmental sampling and flotation

**Goal:** recover the seeds, charcoal, small bones, insects and sediments that tell you
about diet, economy and landscape — evidence that hand-excavation alone misses almost
entirely.

## 1. Sampling strategy (agree it before digging)
- Talk to the environmental specialist at project design stage. Their questions decide
  what is sampled.
- Common approach: **systematic** bulk sampling of all well-sealed, datable deposits of
  certain types (hearths, pit fills, floors, ditch bases), plus **judgement** samples of
  anything unusual. Avoid sampling only "the interesting-looking black layer".
- Standard bulk sample sizes are often around 40 litres, or 100% of small features;
  confirm with the specialist and local guidance (*figures vary by guidance document*).
- Record per sample: number, context, volume, purpose, position on plan/section, and
  sampler. Use double-bagged sealed containers with labels inside and out.

## 2. Sample types
| Type | For | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk | Charred plants, charcoal, small bones, finds | Process by flotation/wet-sieving |
| Monolith tin | Pollen, micromorphology, sediment sequence | Hammer metal tin into cleaned section, record top/bottom heights, wrap airtight, keep cool |
| Spot/specific | Radiocarbon, a single seed, residue | Clean tools, foil, no handling |
| Waterlogged | Wood, insects, uncharred seeds | **Never let dry.** Keep wet, dark and cool; specialists may need it within weeks |
| Column/kubiena | Micromorphology | Orientation arrow marked on the box |

## 3. Flotation
- Machine (e.g. Siraf-type tank) or bucket flotation. Water agitates the sample; charred
  material floats and is caught in a fine mesh (typically **250–300 µm**); the **heavy
  residue** sinks onto a coarser mesh (typically **1 mm**).
- Pre-soak clays (or add a dispersant such as sodium hexametaphosphate — *check with the
  specialist*); dry the sample first if very clay-rich, then rewet.
- Dry the flot slowly in the shade on paper; label. Sort the residue for small bones, fish
  scales, beads, microdebitage.
- Clean meshes between samples — cross-contamination is the commonest flotation error.
  Record sample volume processed.

## 4. Animal bone basics
- Hand-collected bone is biased toward large species and large fragments; sieved samples
  correct for this. Report recovery method alongside species lists.
- Quantify as **NISP** (fragments identified) and **MNI**; both have biases. Record
  butchery marks, burning, gnawing, age (fusion, tooth wear), sex where possible.

## Gotchas
- Dried-out waterlogged material shrinks and cracks irreversibly — the most common
  avoidable loss.
- Modern seeds (uncharred, in dry sites) are usually intrusive; report but don't interpret
  as ancient.
- Charcoal from a single context can contain old wood; short-lived taxa (cereal grains, twigs)
  are better for radiocarbon (see `humanities.archaeology.archaeological-dating.radiocarbon-sampling-and-calibration`).
- Don't sample contaminated contexts (root-disturbed, modern intrusions) and pretend otherwise.

## Verify
- [ ] Sampling strategy agreed with specialist and written into the project design.
- [ ] Each sample has context, volume, purpose, location and label inside and out.
- [ ] Mesh sizes, sample volume and processing method recorded per sample.
- [ ] Waterlogged samples kept wet, dark and cool; monoliths oriented.
- [ ] Bone reports state recovery method and quantification measure.
