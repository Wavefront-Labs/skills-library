# Identification, die studies and hoard analysis

**Goal:** identify a coin to a standard catalogue type, and use groups of coins (die
series, hoards, site finds) as historical evidence without overreaching.

## 1. Record the coin
- **Metal** (AV gold, AR silver, AE bronze/copper alloy), **weight** to 0.01 g, maximum
  **diameter** in mm, **die axis** as clock hours (turn the coin on its vertical axis:
  12h = both sides upright, 6h = reverse upside down).
- Photograph both faces with even, angled light, with scale; and the edge if relevant.
- **Obverse**: usually the portrait/head; **reverse**: the type (deity, scene) and any
  mint marks or control marks in the exergue (below the ground line) or field.
- Transcribe legends exactly as visible, with breaks marked (`IMP CAES TRAIAN / AVG`)
  and missing letters in brackets.

## 2. Identify
- Narrow by region and period (style, portrait, script, denomination standard),
  then use the reference corpora:
  - Roman Republic: **RRC** (Crawford) — searchable via **CRRO**.
  - Roman Empire: **RIC** — via **OCRE**; late Roman bronzes often via LRBC.
  - Provincial (Greek Imperial): **RPC** online.
  - Greek: **SNG** volumes, **BMC** catalogues, regional corpora; Nomisma-linked
    databases increasingly cover them.
- Match **every** element (legend, type, marks) — similar types differ only by a
  control mark or legend break. Cite fully: `RIC II Trajan 147` or `RRC 494/23`.
- Worn coins: identify to a range ("Constantinian, AE3, AD 330–40, GLORIA EXERCITVS
  type") rather than forcing a number.

## 3. Die studies
Each coin was struck between an obverse and reverse die; die identity shows in tiny
details (letter positions, flaws).
- Photograph all available specimens at the same scale; compare details side by side
  (overlay in image software) and assign die numbers (O1, O2... R1, R2...).
- Map **die links** (which obverses pair with which reverses) to order the issue and
  identify mints and sequence.
- Estimate the original number of dies with a statistical estimator (e.g. methods
  reviewed by Esty) — report the confidence interval; coins-per-die figures used to
  turn dies into output volume are disputed.

## 4. Hoards and site finds
- A hoard's **closing date** (terminus post quem for deposition) is set by its *latest*
  coin — and the hoard may have been buried much later. Check that the record is
  complete (hoards are often dispersed before recording).
- Composition tells about circulation (age profile, denominations, mints). Compare with
  catalogues: IGCH (Greek), CHRR (Roman Republic), national hoard databases.
- Site finds (single losses) reflect low-value coin use; don't compare them directly
  with hoards.

## 5. Authenticity and ethics
- Warning signs of fakes: cast seams and pitting, soapy surfaces, wrong weight for the
  denomination, identical "die" across many coins with casting bubbles, style
  anachronisms. Specialist examination and metal analysis settle hard cases.
- Many coins on the market lack provenance and may come from looting. Follow your
  institution's acquisition policy; in England and Wales, finds are recorded via the
  **Portable Antiquities Scheme** (and hoards fall under the Treasure Act).

## Gotchas
- Weights vary within a type; use ranges, not single values, to judge denomination.
- Die axis is reported differently in older literature (arrows or degrees).
- Status: draft; references and database coverage evolve — check current online corpora.

- [ ] Hoard date given as a terminus post quem with completeness noted.
