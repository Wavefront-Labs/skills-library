# Fieldwork, recording and transcription of oral traditions

**Goal:** come back with recordings, notes and transcriptions that are ethically
obtained, technically usable and properly documented — and that the community can
access and control. For general language-documentation recording see
languages-linguistics.linguistics.language-documentation.elicitation-field-recording-and-archiving.

## 1. Ethics and permissions first
- Institutional review (IRB/ethics committee) where required — plan months ahead.
- **Community-level** permission as well as individual consent: who has the right to
  perform, record or share a song? Some repertoire is restricted by gender, initiation,
  season or lineage. Ask before you press record, not after.
- Agree in writing (or recorded oral consent where writing is inappropriate):
  what's recorded, where it goes, who can hear it, attribution, commercial use, how to
  withdraw. Consider **Local Contexts TK labels** to carry access rules with the files.
- Plan reciprocity: copies to performers, fees where customary, co-authorship.

## 2. Participant observation
Learn to perform where invited (bimusicality); it reveals what listening misses —
how a cycle is counted, what counts as a mistake. Keep **field notes daily**: who, where,
what event, your role, what people said about the music, your own uncertainties. Date
everything; cross-reference recording IDs.

## 3. Recording that survives
- Format: uncompressed **WAV**, 24-bit, 48 kHz (or 96 kHz) — never MP3 masters.
- Stereo pair (XY or ORTF) for ensemble; close mic for solo detail; a second recorder
  as backup at events that won't recur.
- Set levels peaking around −12 dBFS to leave headroom for sudden drums; use a
  windshield outdoors. Record 10 s of room tone.
- Slate each file aloud: date, place, performers (as permitted), occasion.
- Video adds dance, gesture and instrument technique — get separate consent.
- Back up nightly to two drives; keep one with you.

## 4. Metadata
Per file: ID, date, place (as precise as consent allows), performers and roles, genre
or local term, occasion, equipment, language, consent and access conditions, recordist.
Use local terms with your gloss — "*ngoma* (drum-dance event)", not just "dance".

## 5. Transcription
- Decide **descriptive vs prescriptive** (Seeger's distinction): describing what was
  sounded for analysis vs instructing performance. Most field transcription is
  descriptive.
- Western staff notation distorts non-equal-tempered pitch and non-metric time. Add
  cent deviations, contour graphs, or spectrograms (Sonic Visualiser, Praat) and say
  what the notation leaves out.
- Time-align text and music in **ELAN** for sung texts; check transcriptions with
  performers where possible — their correction is data.

## 6. Archive and return
Deposit with a sound archive (national archive, university, or community archive)
following IASA guidance; attach consent records and access conditions. Return copies
in formats the community can use.

## Gotchas
- "Public performance" doesn't mean "free to publish" — confirm explicitly.
- Your presence changes events; note it.
- Status: legal consent and copyright rules differ by country; check locally.

## Verify
- [ ] Community and individual consent documented, including access rules.
- [ ] Masters are WAV 24-bit, backed up twice, slated and named consistently.
- [ ] Every file has full metadata and links to dated field notes.
- [ ] Transcriptions state their type and limits; at least some were checked by performers.
- [ ] Deposit and return copies are arranged.
