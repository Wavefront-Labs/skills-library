# Archival research and source criticism for music history

**Goal:** answer a music-historical question (who performed this, when was it written,
how was it heard) from evidence, not from the last textbook that repeated it.

## 1. Frame a question you can answer
"Mozart's reception" is a topic. "How was Mozart's music programmed in Leipzig's
Gewandhaus concerts, 1781–1840?" is a question: bounded in place, time and source type.
Write what evidence would answer it (programmes, reviews, payroll, publisher catalogues).

## 2. Map what's known (secondary sources)
- **Grove Music Online** (Oxford Music Online): article plus bibliography — your
  starting map, not your source of facts. Check the article's date; some are decades old.
- **RILM Abstracts** (and RIPM for historical music periodicals): the scholarly
  literature since 1967 in many languages. Search in the languages of the topic.
- **JSTOR**, dissertations (ProQuest), thematic catalogues (Köchel, BWV, Hoboken).
- Read the most recent monograph's footnotes: they point to the archives.

## 3. Find primary sources
- **RISM** (Répertoire International des Sources Musicales): manuscripts and early
  prints with library sigla (e.g. `D-B` = Berlin, Staatsbibliothek). Search by composer,
  title, incipit. Many entries link to digitised copies.
- Library catalogues (WorldCat, national libraries), **IMSLP** for scans of early
  editions (check edition details; IMSLP metadata is user-supplied).
- Institutional archives: court and church account books, payroll lists, minutes of
  concert societies, theatre playbills, publishers' archives, letters.
- Digitised newspapers for concert notices and reviews.
Before visiting: email the archivist with your question and shelfmarks; many archives
need appointments and have photography rules.

## 4. Read sources critically
For every document ask: who made it, when, why, for whom? What could they know?
- A **programme** shows what was planned, not necessarily played.
- A **review** reflects the critic and the paper's politics.
- **Anecdotes** in early biographies (deathbed scenes, prodigy stories) are frequently
  embellished; look for contemporary corroboration.
- **Dates on manuscripts** may be added later by owners or librarians — check ink and hand.
- Payroll records give real ensemble sizes, often smaller than modern assumptions —
  a key performance-practice source.
Record uncertainty in your notes: *certain / probable / possible / legend.*

## 5. Record as you go
Note full citation at capture: archive, collection, shelfmark/call number, folio, date
consulted, photo filename. Use Zotero or similar; music-specific Chicago style is
standard (check the journal's house style). Transcribe with original spelling and mark
your expansions in brackets.

## Gotchas
- Grove-sourced "facts" recycled across websites aren't independent confirmation.
- Old-style vs new-style dates (Julian/Gregorian; the switch varied by country) can
  shift events by 10–13 days or put them in a different year.
- Currency and pay comparisons across eras need a stated method; avoid casual
  "worth X today".
