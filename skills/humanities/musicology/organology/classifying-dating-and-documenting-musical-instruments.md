# Classifying, dating and documenting musical instruments

**Goal:** a record of an instrument precise enough to classify it, compare it with
others, support its dating and attribution, and guide its care. Making and repair are
covered under handcrafts.musical-instrument-making.

## 1. Classify
- Use **Hornbostel–Sachs** (H-S), ideally the **MIMO 2011 revision**, which adds
  electrophones (5). Top classes: 1 idiophones, 2 membranophones, 3 chordophones,
  4 aerophones, 5 electrophones.
- Codes are decimal and hierarchical: e.g. **321.322** = necked box lute (violin, guitar).
  Suffixes like `-71` (bowed) and `-6` (with keys) refine it. Look up the code in the
  published table rather than reconstructing it from memory; misclassification is common.
- Add the local/emic name and the maker's own name for the type; H-S describes
  sound production, not cultural category.

## 2. Measure and photograph
- Measurements by type (common examples): bowed strings — total length, body length,
  upper/middle/lower bout widths, rib heights, string length (nut to bridge);
  woodwinds — sounding length, bore at each joint end, tone-hole positions and
  diameters; keyboards — compass, three-octave span, string scaling.
- Use calipers and a flexible tape; record units and method. Never force joints apart.
- Photograph front, back, sides, details of marks, wear, repairs, with scale bar and
  colour card.

## 3. Read marks and construction
- **Maker's marks**: stamps (woodwinds, brass), printed or handwritten labels (violins —
  labels are frequently false or replaced; treat as a claim), serial numbers (pianos,
  brass: makers' serial lists date them to the year).
- **Construction evidence**: tool marks, joinery, materials (key metal, pad type,
  string material), alterations (neck reset, added keys, re-bushed tone holes).
  Changes are part of the history — record them, don't call them "damage".
- **Scientific dating**: dendrochronology of spruce tops gives a terminus post quem
  (the tree's latest ring, not the making date); CT scanning and X-radiography reveal
  internal construction non-invasively. Results need a specialist; cite them.
- Pitch evidence (sounding length, original fingerholes) can hint at the pitch standard
  the instrument was built for — interpret cautiously.

## 4. Materials and law
Note ivory, tortoiseshell, whalebone, Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*), and other
regulated materials. Cross-border loans or sales may need **CITES** permits or
exemptions; rules change — check before any movement.

## 5. Playing and conservation
Decide policy before anyone plays it: playing strains historic instruments (string
tension, moisture in wind bores). CIMCIM recommends a documented assessment; many
collections make playing copies instead. Record environment needs (RH stability for wood).

## 6. Catalogue entry
Object number, name (local and standard), H-S code, maker and place (with confidence),
date (with evidence), materials, dimensions, inscriptions, condition, alterations,
provenance, literature, images. See
humanities.museum-and-archive-collections-management.cataloguing-and-documentation.object-records-numbering-and-controlled-vocabularies.

## Gotchas
- A label inside a violin is the weakest evidence for its maker.
- "Original" rarely means unchanged; most surviving instruments were modernised.
- Status: draft; H-S examples should be checked against the current MIMO table.
