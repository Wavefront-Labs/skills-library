# Cross-tradition comparison and translation analysis

**Goal:** a comparison that is justified, linguistically grounded and fair to both
traditions — not two summaries side by side. For translating literature yourself, see
languages-linguistics.translation.literary-translation.voice-register-and-cultural-reference.

## 1. Justify the comparison
State which kind it is, because each needs different evidence:
- **Influence / contact**: A read B (or a translation of B). Evidence: letters,
  library records, translations available at the time, explicit allusion.
- **Reception / circulation**: how a text travelled (editions, translations, reviews,
  syllabi) — the "world literature" model of texts gaining meaning in circulation
  (Damrosch).
- **Typological analogy**: no contact, but comparable forms or problems (the ghazal
  and the sonnet; picaresque traditions). Name the **tertium comparationis** — the third
  term (genre, theme, social structure) that makes them comparable — and define it
  so it doesn't smuggle in one tradition's categories.
Without one of these, reviewers will ask "why these two?"

## 2. Work with the originals
- Read in the original language where you can; where you can't, say so and work
  with more than one translation plus a language consultant.
- Quote original and translation together (original first, translation in brackets
  or a following line), and say **whose translation** — yours, or a named published one.
- Check key words in a historical dictionary of that language; semantic fields don't
  map one-to-one.

## 3. Analysing a translation
Set source and target texts in parallel columns and mark:
- **Shifts**: additions, omissions, explanation (glossing), changes in register,
  syntax, imagery, metre/rhyme strategy.
- **Names and culture-bound items** (food, kinship terms, honorifics, religious terms):
  kept, explained, replaced?
- **Strategy**: Venuti's **domestication** (fluent, reads like an original in the
  target culture) vs **foreignisation** (keeps the strangeness visible). Most real
  translations mix them; describe the pattern with examples.
- **Context**: who commissioned it, for which market, what paratexts (introduction,
  notes, cover) frame it. Compare several translations over time to show changing norms.

## 4. Avoid the common failures
- **False universals**: treating "the novel", "lyric" or "tragedy" as categories every
  culture must have. Check the tradition's own genre terms and theory (e.g. Sanskrit
  *rasa* theory, Arabic *adab*, Japanese *monogatari*).
- **Centre-periphery bias**: using the European text as the norm and the other as
  deviation. Read each in its own critical tradition first.
- **Untranslatability**: some terms resist equivalence (Apter); discuss them, don't
  paper over them.
- **Asymmetrical expertise**: deep on one side, Wikipedia-deep on the other. Get the
  second tradition's scholarship into your bibliography, ideally in its language.

## 5. Structure
Alternate or interleave rather than "first A, then B": organise sections around the
comparison's questions, with both texts in each section. Close by saying what each
text reveals about the other that a single-tradition reading would miss.

## Gotchas
- Transliteration: pick a system (e.g. ALA-LC, Hepburn, Pinyin) and use it consistently.
- Dates and calendars differ; give both where relevant.
- Status: draft; the strategy terms are standard but contested in translation studies.

## Verify
- [ ] The kind of comparison is named and supported with matching evidence.
- [ ] Originals are quoted with attributed translations.
- [ ] Translation analysis cites specific shifts, not general impressions.
- [ ] Both traditions' own critical vocabularies and scholarship are used.
- [ ] Sections compare throughout rather than in two blocks.
