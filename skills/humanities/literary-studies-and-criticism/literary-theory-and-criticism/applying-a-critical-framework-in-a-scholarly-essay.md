# Applying a critical framework in a scholarly essay

**Goal:** an essay where theory does work — it lets you see something in the text
you couldn't otherwise, and the text in turn tests or refines the theory.

## 1. Start from the text and a problem, not from the theory
Find something puzzling in the text first (a silence, a contradiction, a strange ending,
an odd reception history). Then ask which framework is built to explain *that kind*
of problem. Theory picked first tends to produce readings that only confirm it.

## 2. Match framework to question (rough guide)
| If your question is about... | Frameworks that address it |
|---|---|
| how form makes meaning | formalism, New Criticism, narratology |
| unstable meaning, binaries | deconstruction, poststructuralism |
| desire, repression, the unconscious | psychoanalytic (Freud, Lacan, Kristeva) |
| class, labour, ideology, the market | Marxist criticism, cultural materialism |
| the text's historical moment and power | New Historicism |
| gender and sexuality | feminist criticism, gender studies, queer theory |
| empire, race, nation, language | postcolonial criticism, critical race theory |
| nature, environment, the non-human | ecocriticism, new materialism |
| readers and reception | reader-response, reception theory, book history |
Frameworks combine (e.g. feminist narratology); say so explicitly.

## 3. Use concepts precisely
- Read the **primary theorist** for the concepts you use, not only summaries: if you
  use "the uncanny", cite Freud's essay and say which aspect you need.
- Define each concept in a sentence in your own words and show how it applies, once.
- One or two well-used concepts beat a paragraph of names. "Theory-dropping"
  (Foucault, Butler, Derrida in one sentence) is a common reviewer complaint.
- Note where the text **resists** the theory — that is often your most original point.

## 4. Enter the critical conversation
- Search **MLA International Bibliography** (and JSTOR, Project MUSE) for the text and
  author; read the last 10–15 years of criticism and the classic older pieces.
- Map positions: who reads it which way? Then locate your intervention in the
  "they say / I say" move: *Critics have read X as Y; this overlooks Z; reading Z through
  [concept] shows...*
- Cite fairly: summarise opponents' best version.

## 5. Structure (typical journal article, ~7,000–10,000 words in many humanities
journals — check the journal)
1. Opening: the textual puzzle, the critical consensus, your claim.
2. Framework: the concept(s) and why they fit — short.
3. Readings: 3–5 sections of close reading through the framework (see
   humanities.literary-studies-and-criticism.close-reading.close-reading-a-passage-into-an-argument).
4. Stakes: what this changes for reading the text, the author, or the theory itself.
Cite in the journal's style (MLA 9th is common in English studies; Chicago in others).

## Gotchas
- Avoid anachronism traps: a historical frame needs period evidence (archives,
  contemporary texts), not just theory.
- Don't make the text a mere illustration of the theorist.
- Respect identity-based frameworks' origins and current debates; cite scholars from
  the communities discussed.
