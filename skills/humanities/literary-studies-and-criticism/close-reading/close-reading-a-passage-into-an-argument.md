# Close-reading a passage into an argument

**Goal:** move from "I noticed things" to a claim about how the passage makes its
meaning, supported by the precise words on the page.

## 1. Read three times, differently
1. **Straight through**, for sense: who is speaking, to whom, about what, where?
   Paraphrase it in two sentences. If you can't, look up words (use the *OED* for
   historical meanings — "nice" in 1600 is not "nice" now).
2. **Aloud**, for sound and pace: where do you stumble, speed up, pause?
3. **With a pencil**, annotating the categories below.

## 2. What to annotate
- **Diction**: register (Latinate vs Anglo-Saxon, formal vs colloquial), repeated words,
  odd word choices, words with double meanings.
- **Syntax**: sentence length, where the main verb falls, inversions, lists, questions,
  sentences that run over line or stanza breaks.
- **Imagery and figures**: metaphor, simile, metonymy, personification — and *patterns*
  of imagery across the passage (light/dark, enclosure, water).
- **Voice**: speaker or narrator, distance, irony, shifts in tense or person; in prose,
  **free indirect discourse** (narration taking on a character's idiom).
- **Form**: stanza shape, rhyme scheme, line length, sonnet volta, paragraphing.
- **Sound and metre** (poetry; see section 3).
- **Tension**: the moment something doesn't fit — a shift in tone, an image that cuts
  against the rest, a contradiction. This is usually where your argument is.

## 3. Scanning metre
Mark stressed (/) and unstressed (x) syllables by natural speech stress first, then see
which metre fits.
```
x   /    x    /     x   /    x    /   x    /
Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
```
Is it regular **iambic pentameter**, or does "Shall I" invite a trochaic opening?
Scansion is interpretive; argue the effect of a variation (an inverted first foot,
a spondee, a feminine ending, a caesura mid-line), don't just label it.
Check pronunciation history (e.g. "-ed" endings once sounded as syllables).

## 4. From notes to thesis
- Group annotations into 2–3 patterns. Ask: *how do these patterns relate to the
  tension?*
- Write a thesis that is **arguable and specific**: not "the poem uses imagery of
  light" but "the poem's light imagery, which promises clarity, repeatedly fails at the
  line break, so that the speaker's confidence is undone by the form itself."
- Test: could a thoughtful reader disagree? If not, it's an observation, not a thesis.

## 5. Write it
- Each paragraph: claim → short quotation (a word or phrase, not blocks) → analysis of
  *how* those words do it → link to the thesis.
- Quote precisely: line numbers for poetry (`ll. 5–6`), slashes for line breaks
  ("compare thee / to a summer's day"), page numbers for prose, citing a named edition.
- Analysis should be longer than the quotation. Don't retell the plot.
- Address the strongest counter-reading in one paragraph.

## Gotchas
- Don't attribute the speaker's views to the author without argument.
- Intention claims ("the poet wanted...") are weaker than effect claims ("the line
  makes the reader...").
- Sound effects need meaning: "alliteration creates emphasis" is empty unless you say
  what is emphasised and why it matters.
- Status: draft; method is standard practice, details vary by school and examiner.

## Verify
- [ ] You can paraphrase the passage accurately.
- [ ] Annotations cover diction, syntax, imagery, voice, form and sound.
- [ ] The thesis is arguable and names a tension.
- [ ] Every paragraph quotes precisely and analyses more than it quotes.
- [ ] Citations point to a specific edition and line/page.
