# Applying the Genealogical Proof Standard

**Goal:** reach conclusions about identity and relationships ("Mary Smith b. 1842 was the
daughter of John Smith") that hold up — instead of copying trees that merely agree with
each other.

## The five elements of the GPS (BCG)
1. **Reasonably exhaustive research** in sources that might answer the question.
2. **Complete and accurate source citations.**
3. **Analysis and correlation** of sources, information and evidence.
4. **Resolution of conflicting evidence.**
5. **A soundly reasoned, coherently written conclusion.**

## 1. Ask a focused question
Not "research the Smiths" but "Who were the parents of Mary Smith, who married Thomas Jones in
Leeds in 1863?" Identify her uniquely first: name, approximate birth, place, spouse, children.

## 2. Reasonably exhaustive search
Plan by record type and period, e.g. for 19th-century England: civil registration
(from July 1837), parish registers and bishop's transcripts, census (1841–1921),
nonconformist registers, probate, newspapers, poor-law and settlement records, land/rate
records. For the US: vital records (dates vary by state), federal and state census, church,
probate, land, military, naturalisation and passenger lists.
"Exhaustive" means you looked where the answer could be and stopped when further sources were
unlikely to change the conclusion — not "everything online". Keep a **research log** of
negative searches.

## 3. Classify what you have (Mills's model)
- **Source:** *original* (first recording), *derivative* (transcript, index, abstract), or
  *authored* (a compiled tree, a family history).
- **Information:** *primary* (from someone with first-hand knowledge — the officiant at a
  marriage) or *secondary* (hearsay — a death certificate's birth date given by a grandchild).
  One document can hold both.
- **Evidence** (relative to your question): *direct* (states the answer), *indirect* (answers
  only when combined with other evidence), *negative* (an expected record or fact is absent).
An index entry is a pointer, not evidence: always get the image or certificate.

## 4. Correlate and resolve conflicts
- Put every relevant fact in a table: source, date of record, informant, what it says.
  Patterns emerge (ages drifting by census, consistent birthplace).
- Conflicts are normal: ages, spellings, even names vary. Prefer primary information from
  original sources made close to the event, but **explain** the choice — don't just pick.
- Watch for **same-name** people in the same area: build a profile of each candidate
  (spouse, occupation, residence over time) and exclude the others explicitly.
- Indirect-evidence cases (no single document states the answer) need a written proof argument.

## 5. Write the conclusion
- Short questions: a proof statement with citations. Harder ones: a proof summary or full
  proof argument that walks through the evidence and dismisses alternatives.
- Cite every source fully (Evidence Explained style, including the image/film/database you used).
- A conclusion is always open to revision if new evidence appears.

## Gotchas
- Online trees copied from one another are one (often unsourced) source repeated, not consensus.
- "Ancestry hints" and transcriptions contain errors; verify against images.
- Registration districts, parishes and borders change — know the jurisdiction at the date.
- Pre-1752 England: dates between 1 Jan and 24 Mar use double-dating (1720/1).

## Verify
- [ ] The question names one uniquely identified person and one relationship.
- [ ] Research log shows all relevant record types searched, including negatives.
- [ ] Each source classified (original/derivative/authored, primary/secondary, direct/indirect).
- [ ] Every conflict addressed with reasoning; same-name candidates ruled out.
- [ ] Written conclusion cites every source fully.
