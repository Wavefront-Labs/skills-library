# Writing a sourced family history

**Goal:** a family history people actually read — and that a future researcher can trust,
because every fact can be traced to its source.

## 1. Choose scope and format
- **Scope:** one ancestor's life, one couple and their children, one surname line, all
  descendants of an immigrant, or the ancestors of one person. Smaller scopes get finished.
- **Format options:**
  - *Narrative biography* — chapters on lives in context; best for general family readers.
  - *Genealogical summary* in **Register** (NEHGS) or **NGSQ** format — structured generations
    with numbered individuals; standard for journals and reference.
  - A hybrid: narrative chapters with a genealogical summary appendix.
- **Numbering:** Ahnentafel for ancestor books (subject = 1, father = 2, mother = 3, father of
  n = 2n); Register/NGSQ for descendant books. Pick one and explain it in the introduction.

## 2. Build the story from evidence
- Start each chapter from what the records actually show; arrange the facts as a timeline.
- **Context fills the gaps honestly:** what the parish, the trade, the war, the voyage were
  like, from local histories and contemporary accounts. Phrase it as context: "Labourers in the
  parish at that time typically…", not "John felt…".
- Mark speculation clearly: "Perhaps", "It is likely, given…", with the reasoning in a note.
  Never invent dialogue or feelings for real people in a non-fiction history.
- Include the difficult material (illegitimacy, crime, the workhouse, enslavers or enslaved
  ancestors) with sensitivity; omitting it distorts the record. Decide with care when it touches living people.

## 3. Charts and illustrations
- Pedigree charts (ancestors), descendant charts, and family-group sheets keep readers oriented.
  Put a small chart at the start of each chapter.
- Use images of documents, places and maps with captions and source credits; check copyright and
  archive reproduction permissions.

## 4. Citation
- Use **endnotes or footnotes**, not a bare bibliography: each fact traces to a specific record.
  Evidence Explained formats work for almost every record type.
- Cite what you used (the image on a website, the microfilm, the certificate) — not the
  original if you never saw it.
- Proof arguments for tricky conclusions can sit in an appendix (see
  `humanities.genealogy-and-family-history.records-research.applying-the-genealogical-proof-standard`).

## 5. Living people and privacy
- Common practice: omit or minimise details of living people (often defined as anyone born in
  the last ~100 years without a death record) unless they consent. Many writers give names only.
- Be cautious with adoption, parentage surprises (DNA), health, and criminal history of recently
  deceased people with living children.
- Data-protection law may apply when publishing about living people, especially in the EU/UK.
  *Not legal advice; check your jurisdiction.*

## 6. Finishing and sharing
- Draft one generation or chapter at a time; ask a relative to read for clarity.
- Publish as PDF, print-on-demand, or a website; deposit copies in relevant libraries,
  genealogical societies and record offices so it survives.

## Gotchas
- Family legends ("descended from a duke", "Cherokee princess") go in as legends with the
  evidence for and against, not as fact.
- Don't let software-generated reports stand in for writing; they produce lists, not history.
- Endless research is the usual reason books never appear — set a cutoff date.

## Verify
- [ ] Scope, format and numbering system stated in the introduction.
- [ ] Every fact has a note pointing to a specific source.
- [ ] Context and speculation are clearly marked as such; no invented thoughts or dialogue.
- [ ] Living people's details are minimised or consented.
- [ ] Copies deposited where future researchers will find them.
