# Reading a blazon and researching a surname

**Goal:** interpret coats of arms correctly as evidence, and study a surname's origin and
spread without falling for the myths that surround both.

## Part A — Heraldry

### Arms belong to individuals, not surnames
In English and Scottish heraldry, arms are granted to **a person** and pass to their
legitimate descendants (in Scotland only one person at a time bears the undifferenced arms).
There is no "Smith family crest". A mug with "your family coat of arms" is marketing. Arms
are evidence only if you can prove descent from the grantee. Continental practices differ
(burgher arms, more informal use) — *check the country's tradition*.

### Reading a blazon
A blazon describes arms in a fixed order: **field**, then **principal charge(s)**, then
secondary charges.
- **Tinctures:** metals — *Or* (gold/yellow), *Argent* (silver/white); colours — *Gules*
  (red), *Azure* (blue), *Vert* (green), *Sable* (black), *Purpure* (purple); furs — *ermine*,
  *vair*. Rule of tincture: no metal on metal, no colour on colour (with exceptions).
- **Ordinaries:** chief, pale, fess, bend, chevron, cross, saltire.
- **Directions** are from the bearer's viewpoint: *dexter* (viewer's left), *sinister* (viewer's right).
- Example: "*Azure, a chevron between three mullets Or*" = blue field, gold chevron, three gold
  five-pointed stars.
- **Cadency marks** (label, crescent, mullet…) identified sons in English practice; useful but
  inconsistently used.
- The **crest** is the thing on top of the helm, not the shield. "Family crest" usually means arms.

### Identifying arms
- Have a blazon? Look it up in an **ordinary of arms** (arranged by design) such as
  Papworth's Ordinary for British arms. Have a name? Use an **armorial** (by name) such as
  Burke's General Armory — but these are 19th-century compilations with errors.
- Authoritative registers: College of Arms (England, Wales, NI), Court of the Lord Lyon
  (Scotland), Chief Herald of Ireland. Arms on a seal, tomb or bookplate can date or identify a person.

## Part B — Surnames

### Origin
- Main types: **patronymic** (Johnson, Davies), **locative/toponymic** (from a place: Bradford),
  **topographic** (Hill, Atwood), **occupational** (Smith, Baker), **nickname** (Brown, Little).
- Use a scholarly dictionary (e.g. the Oxford Dictionary of Family Names), which gives early
  bearers and evidence, rather than websites that offer one confident meaning.
- A surname may have **several independent origins**. Bearers aren't necessarily related.

### Distribution
- Map the name in a census year (e.g. 1881 for Britain) and over time; concentration often
  points to a locative origin or a single founding family.
- Variants and mis-spellings must be grouped (Soundex-like matching helps, judgement decides).

### One-name and one-place studies
A **one-name study** collects *all* occurrences of a surname worldwide (registered with the
Guild of One-Name Studies, if wanted); a **one-place study** does the same for a place. Plan
data collection by record set, keep a source-linked database, combine with Y-DNA projects to
test which bearers share a paternal line.

## Gotchas
- "The name was changed at Ellis Island" is a myth — passenger lists were made at departure;
  changes usually happened later, by the family.
- Spelling was fluid until the 19th–20th centuries; don't exclude variants too early.

## Verify
- [ ] Any arms claimed are tied to a proven ancestor or labelled as unproven.
- [ ] Blazon read in the correct order with tinctures and dexter/sinister right.
- [ ] Surname origins cite a scholarly dictionary, allowing multiple origins.
- [ ] Distribution work groups variants explicitly.
