# Tracing a birth family with DNA and records

**Goal:** identify an adoptee's (or donor-conceived or foundling person's) birth parents and
reach out in a way that protects everyone involved. This is emotionally heavy work — for the
searcher and for the people found.

## 1. Records routes first
- **Non-identifying information** from the agency/court: ages, occupations, health,
  circumstances, sometimes religion and birthplace. Every detail is a later filter.
- **Original birth certificate (OBC):** access varies widely — e.g. adult adoptees in England
  and Wales can apply (with counselling required for adoptions before 12 Nov 1975); many US
  states now allow adult adoptees access, others still seal records. *Laws change; check the
  current rules for the jurisdiction of the adoption.*
- **Agency or court files** and **adoption contact registers** (national/state mutual-consent
  registers); an **intermediary** service can search and make contact on your behalf.
- Note the date and place of birth, the agency, the hospital — and any name you're given.

## 2. DNA unknown-parentage method
1. Test at **Ancestry** first (largest database), then **23andMe**, **MyHeritage** and upload
   to **FTDNA** and **GEDmatch** where allowed.
2. List the closest matches (above ~90 cM). Separate maternal and paternal sides using shared
   matches (Leeds method — see
   `humanities.genealogy-and-family-history.genetic-genealogy.interpreting-shared-cm-and-triangulating-matches`).
3. For the best matches, build **quick-and-dirty trees** back to their great-grandparents
   using records, not just their posted trees.
4. Find the **common couple** shared by several matches on one side — that couple is likely
   your ancestors too.
5. **Reverse genealogy:** trace all descendants of that couple forward; filter by age, place
   and the non-identifying details. Use WATO to score where you fit.
6. **Mirror tree** (optional technique): build a tree for a match, attach your DNA test to it
   privately, and let the platform's hints point at shared ancestors. *Check current
   platform terms; some changed how private/unlinked trees work.*
7. Confirm candidates by targeted testing (with consent) or with a close match's cM fitting
   the predicted relationship.

## 3. Before contact
- Verify with at least two independent lines of evidence. A wrong contact causes real harm.
- Consider the birth parent's likely situation: they may never have told a spouse or
  children. Research publicly (obituaries, social media) quietly — don't message relatives first.
- Decide what you want: information, medical history, a relationship. Have support lined up
  (adoptee support groups, counselling).

## 4. First contact
- Use a private channel addressed to the person only: a letter or an intermediary is
  often gentler than a social-media message or phone call.
- Keep it short, warm, specific enough to be believed, and give them control: "I'd welcome
  hearing from you whenever you feel ready; if you'd prefer not, I'll respect that."
- Don't include accusations or ask for anything in the first message. Expect silence to be
  possible and not final.

## Gotchas
- Half-siblings, grandparents and aunts/uncles share similar cM; don't jump to "parent" from one match.
- Search angels and online groups help, but never post identifying details of living
  candidates publicly.
- Donor conception: donor anonymity promises may conflict with DNA reality; tread carefully.
- Pre-adoption names given to adoptees are sometimes placeholders, not family names.

## Verify
- [ ] Legal records routes exhausted or ruled out for the jurisdiction.
- [ ] Candidate identified by DNA + records with two independent supports.
- [ ] Maternal/paternal sides separated before the tree work.
- [ ] Support in place; contact plan private, short and non-demanding.
- [ ] No living person's details shared publicly during the search.
