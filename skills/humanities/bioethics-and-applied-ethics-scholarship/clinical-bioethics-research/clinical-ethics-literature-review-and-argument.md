# Clinical-ethics literature review and argument

**Goal:** write a clinical-ethics paper (end-of-life, resource allocation, reproductive
ethics, etc.) that shows command of the existing debate and advances a clear normative
claim — not a tour of opinions.

## 1. Search where the literature actually lives
Bioethics is split across medicine, philosophy and law, so one database is never enough.
- **PubMed/MEDLINE** — use MeSH (`Bioethics`, `Ethics, Medical`, `Withholding Treatment`,
  `Resource Allocation`, `Reproductive Techniques, Assisted`) plus free-text synonyms.
- **PhilPapers** — philosophy journals and books PubMed misses.
- Law databases (Westlaw/LexisNexis/BAILII or your jurisdiction's) for cases and statutes.
- Key journals to hand-search recent issues: *Journal of Medical Ethics*, *Hastings Center
  Report*, *American Journal of Bioethics*, *Bioethics*, *Journal of Clinical Ethics*,
  *Kennedy Institute of Ethics Journal*.
- *Note:* the Georgetown ETHXWeb/Bioethics Research Library database has changed form
  over the years; check current availability before relying on it.
Record your search strings and dates — a "systematic review of reasons" needs them.

## 2. Choose a review type
- **Narrative/critical review**: you select and synthesise; acceptable for a position paper
  but say how you selected.
- **Systematic review of reasons** (Strech & Sofaer): systematic search, then extract the
  *reasons* (arguments, considerations) offered for and against a position, not empirical
  effect sizes. Report a PRISMA-style flow. Useful when a debate is large and repetitive.
State which you're doing; reviewers punish narrative reviews dressed as systematic.

## 3. Map the debate
Build a table: **position → key argument → main proponents → main objection → reply**. This
exposes where the debate is genuinely stuck versus where people talk past each other —
often because they use terms differently.

Key terminology traps:
- **Futility** — physiological vs quantitative vs qualitative; many societies now prefer
  "potentially inappropriate treatment" (2016 multisociety statement). Say which.
- **Withholding vs withdrawing** — widely held to be ethically equivalent; clinicians often
  feel otherwise. Don't assume your reader agrees.
- **Assisted dying / MAID / euthanasia / physician-assisted suicide** — terms are politically
  loaded and legally defined differently by jurisdiction. Define yours.
- **Allocation**: macro (budgets, QALY thresholds), meso (hospital policy), micro (bedside
  triage). Arguments that work at one level often fail at another.

## 4. Build the argument
1. Make the **thesis one sentence** and contestable.
2. Use a valid structure — set out premises so a reader can see which one they reject.
3. Address the **best** counter-argument from your map, not the most common.
4. Connect to practice: what policy, guideline or clinical behaviour would change if you're
   right? Clinical-ethics journals want this.
5. If you rely on empirical claims (e.g. how often clinicians override surrogates), cite
   empirical studies — not other ethicists' assertions.

## Gotchas
- **Jurisdictional drift.** Law on assisted dying, embryo research limits (e.g. the 14-day
  rule) and surrogacy differs widely and changes; date-stamp legal claims.
- **Reproducing slogans** ("slippery slope", "playing God") without stating the argument in
  premise form.
- **Ignoring disability and lay critiques** in end-of-life and reproductive debates — these
  are central literatures, not footnotes.
- **Case examples** from real patients need consent or de-identification.
- *Unconfirmed:* journal word limits and reference styles vary; check the author guide.

## Verify
- [ ] At least two databases from different disciplines were searched; strings are recorded.
- [ ] The review type is declared and reported accordingly.
- [ ] A debate map exists and key terms are defined.
- [ ] The thesis is one contestable sentence with visible premises.
- [ ] The strongest objection is answered and the practical upshot stated.
- [ ] Legal claims are jurisdiction-specific and dated.
