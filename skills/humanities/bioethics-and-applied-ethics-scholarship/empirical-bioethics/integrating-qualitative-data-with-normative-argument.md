# Integrating qualitative data with normative argument

**Goal:** run an empirical-bioethics project where the data (interviews, focus groups,
surveys) do real work in reaching an ethical conclusion — not a qualitative study with an
ethics paragraph bolted on, nor an ethics paper with decorative quotes.

## 1. Decide what the data are *for*
Write down, before design, which of these roles the empirical work plays:
- **Descriptive** — showing what people think or do (useful, but not yet normative).
- **Testing feasibility** of a norm ("ought implies can").
- **Revealing considerations** philosophers missed (new reasons, morally relevant features).
- **Evaluating** a practice against a stated norm.
- **Input to justification** — stakeholder intuitions as data in a reflective-equilibrium process.
Only the last two produce normative conclusions directly; be explicit which you claim.

## 2. Choose and name an integration method
Davies, Ives & Dunn's review found dozens of methodologies; reviewers now expect you to
name one. Common choices:
- **(Wide) reflective equilibrium** — move between principles, theories and considered
  judgements (including participants') until coherent; document each revision.
- **Symbiotic empirical ethics** (Frith) — theory and data inform each other iteratively.
- **Reflexive balancing / dialogical approaches** — conclusions reached *with* participants
  (e.g. deliberative workshops).
The 2018 consensus standards (Ives et al.) ask you to state your aims, integration method
and the **normative status** you give empirical findings. Follow them.

## 3. Design the empirical part properly
- **Sampling**: purposive, aimed at the range of morally relevant perspectives (patients,
  families, clinicians, the excluded) — not convenience.
- **Interview guide**: open questions about experiences and reasons ("Tell me about a time…",
  "What made that feel right or wrong?"). Avoid leading with ethical jargon; participants
  will perform for the ethicist.
- **Surveys**: vignette designs work well for attitudes; pilot for comprehension. Report
  response rates — attitude surveys are notoriously self-selecting.
- **Analysis**: reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke) is common; framework analysis
  suits pre-specified ethical concepts. NVivo / ATLAS.ti / MAXQDA help manage codes but
  don't do analysis. Keep an audit trail and a reflexive memo on your own moral views.
- Get **ethics approval** for the empirical work itself; ethicists aren't exempt.

## 4. Do the integration in writing
A good results/discussion section:
1. Presents themes descriptively first, with quotes that show variation (not just
   agreement).
2. Identifies which findings are **normatively relevant** and why.
3. Shows the **move**: "Participants' reason R was not in the existing literature; it
   bears on premise P because…; therefore we revise P to P′."
4. States what the data **cannot** settle.

## Gotchas
- **Is-ought slip**: "most participants support X, therefore X is permissible." Majority
  views are data, not justification; say what extra premise links them.
- **Expert-voice dominance**: clinicians' views are easier to recruit and articulate;
  balance or acknowledge.
- **Over-claiming from small qualitative samples** to population attitudes. Saturation
  claims are themselves debated; Braun & Clarke now advise against them for reflexive TA.
- **Disciplinary double jeopardy**: ethicists will critique the normative step, social
  scientists the method. Have both read the draft.
- Report against **COREQ** (interviews/focus groups) or **SRQR**; many journals require it.

## Verify
- [ ] The role of the empirical data is stated (descriptive / feasibility / evaluative / justificatory).
- [ ] An integration methodology is named and its steps are visible in the paper.
- [ ] Sampling targets morally relevant diversity; ethics approval obtained.
- [ ] Every normative conclusion has an explicit bridging premise, not just a majority view.
- [ ] Limits of what the data show are stated; COREQ/SRQR checklist attached.
