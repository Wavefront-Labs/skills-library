# Analysing consent, risk-benefit and oversight in research-ethics scholarship

**Goal:** produce research-ethics scholarship (articles, commentaries, review chapters) that
engages the governing documents accurately, analyses a concrete question with established
tools, and says something new — rather than re-stating Belmont.

*This page is about writing the scholarship. For running consent in an actual study, see
`health-medicine.clinical-research-operations.informed-consent-and-participant-protection.informed-consent-process-and-documentation`.*

## 1. Know the canon and cite the right version
- **Nuremberg Code (1947)**, **Declaration of Helsinki** (WMA; revised repeatedly, latest
  2024 — always cite the paragraph *and* the revision year, paragraph numbers change),
  **Belmont Report (1979)**: respect for persons, beneficence, justice.
- **US Common Rule** (45 CFR 46, revised rule effective 2019) — introduced broad consent and
  the "key information" summary. **CIOMS 2016** for international and low-resource settings.
- Your jurisdiction's equivalents (e.g. UK HRA/REC system, Australia's National Statement,
  Canada's TCPS 2). *Note:* check the current edition before submitting; these are revised.
- Reference framework: Emanuel, Wendler & Grady's **seven requirements** (social value,
  scientific validity, fair subject selection, favourable risk-benefit, independent review,
  informed consent, respect for enrolled subjects) is the common checklist reviewers expect.

## 2. Consent: be precise about which element fails
Break consent into **capacity, disclosure, understanding, voluntariness, authorisation**.
Most papers that say "consent was inadequate" don't say which element. Then ask:
- Is the problem **therapeutic misconception** (participants believing research is care)?
- Is it **undue inducement** vs fair payment? Distinguish payment that clouds judgement of
  risk from payment that is merely attractive.
- For data and biobanks: **broad, tiered, dynamic** consent or waiver — what does each
  actually protect against?
Use the empirical literature on what participants understand where it exists; armchair
claims about comprehension are weak.

## 3. Risk-benefit: use component analysis
Weijer's **component analysis** separates *therapeutic* procedures (judged by clinical
equipoise) from *non-therapeutic* ones (risks minimised and justified by knowledge gained;
capped for those unable to consent). It stops benefits from one arm being used to justify
risks from a research-only biopsy. Name the **minimal-risk** standard you're applying —
"risks of daily life" vs "routine examination" give different answers.

## 4. Vulnerability: analyse, don't list
Lists of "vulnerable groups" (children, prisoners, pregnant women) are criticised as
stigmatising and over-protective. Prefer an **analytic account** — what specific
susceptibility (cognitive, institutional, deferential, medical, economic, social) is at
stake, and what safeguard addresses *that*? Also discuss the cost of exclusion: under-
representation means less evidence for those groups.

## 5. Critiquing oversight
IRB/REC critique is a large literature (inconsistency between committees, mission creep,
burden on social science, low evidence of benefit). Strong critique:
- cites **empirical** studies of committee variation, not anecdotes;
- distinguishes **design flaws** from **implementation failures**;
- proposes something testable (single IRB review, reliance agreements, proportionate review).

## 6. Historical cases
Tuskegee, Willowbrook, the Guatemala STI experiments and the Havasupai case are standard
anchors. Use primary histories (e.g. Reverby on Tuskegee), get the facts right, and avoid
using them as decoration. Misconduct scholarship uses **FFP** (fabrication, falsification,
plagiarism) as the US federal definition — broader "questionable research practices" are a
separate category.

## Gotchas
- Citing Helsinki without a revision year — reviewers will catch it.
- Treating legal compliance as ethical sufficiency (or vice versa).
- US-centrism: say which regulatory system you analyse and whether it generalises.
- Conflating research ethics (protecting participants) with research integrity (honesty of
  the science). Related but different literatures.

## Verify
- [ ] Every governing document is cited with version/year and the right paragraph.
- [ ] Consent critique names the failing element(s).
- [ ] Risk-benefit separates therapeutic from research-only procedures.
- [ ] Vulnerability is analysed by type of susceptibility, with the exclusion cost noted.
- [ ] Any critique of oversight rests on empirical evidence and proposes a testable change.
