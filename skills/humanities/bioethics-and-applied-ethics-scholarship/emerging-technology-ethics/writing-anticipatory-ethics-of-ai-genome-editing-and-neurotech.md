# Writing anticipatory ethics of AI, genome editing and neurotech

**Goal:** produce scholarship on a technology that is still changing — so that it is
accurate about what the technology does, honest about uncertainty, and still useful in
two years' time.

## 1. Get the technology right first
The commonest reason emerging-tech ethics papers are rejected (or ignored) is a technical
misunderstanding.
- Describe the **actual current capability** with a primary technical source (peer-reviewed
  paper, technical report, regulatory filing), not press coverage.
- Separate **demonstrated**, **plausible near-term** and **speculative** capabilities in a
  table. Ethics of each tier is different work.
- Have a domain scientist read the technical section. For genome editing: somatic vs
  germline; base and prime editing vs nuclease editing; mosaicism and off-target effects.
  For neurotech: invasive vs non-invasive BCI, read vs write, clinical vs consumer. For AI:
  which model class, trained on what, deployed where.

## 2. Avoid the speculation trap
Nordmann's critique of "if-and-then" ethics: arguing about remote possibilities as if
they're imminent diverts attention from present issues and lends hype credibility.
- For each harm, state **likelihood, timescale and who bears it**.
- Give present-day harms (bias in deployed systems, consent for neural data, access and
  cost of approved gene therapies) at least as much space as speculative ones.
- If you write about speculative scenarios, say so and say *why* thinking ahead is useful
  here (e.g. lock-in, irreversibility).

## 3. Use an anticipatory method
- **Anticipatory technology ethics** (Brey): analyse at technology, artefact and
  application levels; identify ethical issues per level.
- **Scenario / vignette methods** for structured "what if" without prediction claims.
- **Responsible Research and Innovation** (anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion,
  responsiveness).
- **Collingridge dilemma** framing: early on, a technology is easy to shape but its effects
  are unknown; later the effects are known but it's entrenched. Use this to argue for *what*
  to decide now.

## 4. Track governance — and date-stamp it
Governance moves fast; a paper citing a superseded rule looks dated on arrival.
- AI: EU AI Act (in force 2024 with staged application dates), UNESCO 2021 Recommendation,
  OECD AI Principles, national frameworks. *Note:* check which obligations apply at your
  date of writing — they phase in.
- Genome editing: the 2020 National Academies/Royal Society report; WHO 2021 governance
  framework; many jurisdictions prohibit heritable editing (e.g. via the Oviedo Convention
  for ratifying states). After the 2018 He Jiankui case, China tightened rules.
- Neurotech: neurorights debates; Chile's 2021 constitutional amendment is the usual
  example; OECD 2019 Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology.
Write "as of <month year>" for every governance claim.

## 5. Make it normatively specific
Generic lists (transparency, fairness, accountability) are saturated. Contribute by:
- choosing one **concrete use** and one **specific question**;
- showing where principles conflict (e.g. explainability vs accuracy, privacy vs safety
  monitoring) and how to trade them off;
- naming **who should do what** (developer, regulator, clinician, funder).

## Gotchas
- **Ethics-washing**: writing that serves as cover for industry. Disclose funding and ties.
- **Shelf life**: preprint early if the area moves fast; keep technical claims minimal and
  sourced so they age well.
- **Hype-mirroring**: adopting company language ("understands", "reads minds") uncritically.
- **Global blind spots**: most literature is US/EU-centred; harms often fall elsewhere
  (data labour, trial sites, access).

## Verify
- [ ] Capability claims cite primary technical sources and were checked by a domain expert.
- [ ] Demonstrated / near-term / speculative are separated, with likelihood and who bears harm.
- [ ] An anticipatory method is named.
- [ ] Every governance claim is dated and current at submission.
- [ ] The paper answers one specific question and says who should act.
- [ ] Funding and conflicts are disclosed.
