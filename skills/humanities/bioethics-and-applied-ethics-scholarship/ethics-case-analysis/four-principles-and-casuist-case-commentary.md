# Four-principles and casuist case commentary

**Goal:** turn a messy case into a commentary a journal editor (or ethics committee) can
use: the dilemma stated precisely, two complementary methods applied honestly, and a
conclusion defended against its strongest objection.

## 1. Fix the facts before the ethics
Most weak commentaries argue about a case that isn't the one described.
- Separate **stated facts**, **reasonable inferences** and **unknowns**. Write them as three
  lists. Many apparent dilemmas dissolve once a missing fact (capacity, prognosis, what
  the patient actually said) is named.
- Note **who the decision-maker is** and **what options are really on the table**. "Respect
  autonomy vs protect life" is not an option list; "discharge with services / hold under
  mental-health law / continue negotiating" is.
- If the case is anonymised or composite, say so and avoid adding embellishing detail.

## 2. Frame the dilemma as a question with stakes
Good shape: *"May clinician X do A, given B, when C objects?"* One sentence, one agent, one
action. A real dilemma means each option has a serious moral cost; if one option is merely
inconvenient, it's a problem of practice, not ethics — say so.

## 3. Principlist pass (Beauchamp & Childress)
1. List how each of **autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice** bears on each
   option. Not every principle bites in every case; say which don't.
2. **Specify**: turn the abstract principle into a case-level norm ("respect autonomy" →
   "respect a capacitous refusal of a specific treatment after disclosure of risks").
   Specification is where most of the argumentative work happens — show it.
3. **Balance** with stated justification: why does one specified norm outweigh another *here*?
   Beauchamp & Childress list conditions for justified infringement (realistic prospect of
   achieving the goal, no morally preferable alternative, least infringement, minimise
   effects). Use them explicitly.

## 4. Casuist pass (Jonsen & Toulmin)
1. Find **paradigm cases** where the answer is widely agreed (a clear capacitous refusal; a
   clear emergency without known wishes).
2. Place the case on the spectrum between them and argue by **analogy and disanalogy**: which
   morally relevant features does it share with each paradigm?
3. Note relevant **maxims** and established precedent (professional guidance, landmark cases).
   *Note:* legal precedent varies by jurisdiction; flag which you are relying on.

Where the two methods converge, the conclusion is stronger. Where they diverge, that's
usually the most interesting part of the commentary — explain why.

## 5. Optional clinical scaffold
For clinical cases, the **four topics / four-box** method (medical indications, patient
preferences, quality of life, contextual features) is a good way to make sure the facts
section is complete. It organises facts; it doesn't decide the case.

## 6. Write the commentary
Typical structure for a 1,500–3,000-word commentary (check the target journal's limits):
1. Case summary (or reference to it) and the framed question.
2. Ethically relevant facts and unknowns.
3. Analysis — principlist and casuist, or another declared framework.
4. **Strongest objection** and reply.
5. Conclusion: what should be done, under what conditions it would change, and what
   process (e.g. second opinion, ethics consult) would improve the decision.

## Gotchas
- **Principle-listing without balancing** is the classic failure: four paragraphs naming
  principles and then an unargued verdict.
- **Straw-man options.** State the other side at its best, ideally as its proponent would.
- **Hindsight.** Judge the decision on what was knowable at the time.
- **Law ≠ ethics.** Lawful and ethical can diverge; say which question you're answering.
- **Cultural framing.** Principlism is criticised as individualist and Anglo-American. If the
  case involves relational or family-centred decision-making, engage that critique rather
  than ignoring it.
- **Confidentiality.** Published cases need consent or robust de-identification; check the
  journal's policy (many follow ICMJE/COPE guidance). *Unconfirmed:* exact requirements
  differ by journal.

## Verify
- [ ] Facts, inferences and unknowns are separated, and the decisive unknowns are named.
- [ ] The dilemma is one sentence with one agent and one action.
- [ ] Each principle is *specified* for this case, and the balancing is justified.
- [ ] At least one paradigm case is used for analogy, with disanalogies stated.
- [ ] The strongest objection appears and is answered.
- [ ] The conclusion says what would change it.
