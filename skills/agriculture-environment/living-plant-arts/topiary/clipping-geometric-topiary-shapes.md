# Clipping geometric topiary shapes

**Goal:** crisp, dense geometric shapes — balls, cones, cubes — built gradually and kept
healthy, using guides rather than eye alone.

## 1. Choose the right plant
Small-leaved, dense evergreens that regrow from older wood:
- **Yew (*Taxus baccata*):** the classic for large shapes; regrows from old wood; slow.
- **Box (*Buxus sempervirens*):** traditional small shapes; but **box blight** and the
  **box tree caterpillar** have made it risky in many regions.
- Box alternatives: ***Ilex crenata*** (Japanese holly), ***Lonicera nitida***,
  ***Euonymus japonicus*** cultivars, small-leaved privet, Portuguese laurel for larger forms.
- Avoid most cypresses (×*Cuprocyparis leylandii*) for topiary: they don't regrow from
  bare brown wood.

## 2. Tools
Sharp **one-handed topiary shears or sheep shears** for detail; long-bladed hand shears
for flat faces; a battery hedge trimmer for large yew forms. Clean and sharpen before
work. Canes, string, a spirit level, and templates.

## 3. Using guides (geometry beats eyesight)
- **Cone/pyramid:** tie three or four canes in a wigwam around the plant at the target
  angle, tips meeting over the centre; clip to the canes.
- **Ball:** cut a **semicircular template** from plywood/cardboard with radius = target
  ball radius; rotate it around the plant from above, or around a central cane.
- **Cube/box:** stretch string lines at the top and sides; check the top with a spirit
  level; sight along the edges.
- **Spiral:** wind a ribbon or rope around the cone in an even spiral, mark it, then
  cut the groove deeper over successive seasons.
Step back every few minutes and look from several sides.

## 4. Building a shape from a young plant
- Start with a plant larger than the eventual shape's core, or grow it into the shape:
  clip lightly and often so it bushes out; shape gets **denser through repeated light
  cuts**, not one hard cut.
- Let the plant grow beyond the outline by a few centimetres each year until it fills
  the form, then clip back to the outline.
- Complex figures (animals, birds) are often grown through a **wire frame**: tie shoots in
  to fill the frame and clip anything outside it.

## 5. When to clip
- **Box:** once or twice a year — commonly late spring/early summer after the first
  flush hardens, and optionally again late summer. Clip on **dry, overcast days** to
  limit scorch and blight spread.
- **Yew:** once a year, late summer (or a second tidy in early summer for sharp formal pieces).
- Fast plants (*Lonicera nitida*, privet): several times in the growing season.
- Avoid clipping in frost, heat waves or late autumn (soft regrowth gets frosted).
- Hard renovation of overgrown yew: cut one side back hard one year, the other side the
  next (late winter/early spring); box tolerates renovation less reliably.

## 6. Health and hygiene
- **Box blight** (*Cylindrocladium/Calonectria* and *Volutella*): brown patches, leaf drop,
  black streaks on stems. Clip in dry weather, disinfect tools between plants, collect all
  clippings, improve air flow, avoid overhead watering.
- **Box tree caterpillar:** webbing and stripped leaves inside the plant; check regularly
  in season; treat early (local regulations on products vary — check).
- Feed in spring with a balanced fertiliser and mulch to keep regrowth strong; topiary in
  pots needs regular watering and feeding.

## Gotchas
- Cutting only from one side makes shapes lopsided — work all the way round.
- Over-clipping into old wood on box or most conifers leaves permanent holes.
- Timings are typical for a temperate northern-hemisphere climate, **not confirmed for
  yours**; species tolerance of hard cutting varies.

## Verify
- [ ] Plant species regrows from old wood (or you know it doesn't and clip accordingly).
- [ ] Guides/templates used; shape checked from several sides.
- [ ] Clip done in the right season and in dry conditions; clippings cleared.
- [ ] Tools disinfected between box plants.
- [ ] Shape is dense with no brown patches after regrowth.
