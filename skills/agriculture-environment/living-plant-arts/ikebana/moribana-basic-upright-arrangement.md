# Moribana: the basic upright arrangement

**Goal:** build a balanced, asymmetric three-line arrangement in a shallow container —
the foundation form taught in most modern schools — and keep it fresh for days.

*Note:* each school (Ikenobo, Ohara, Sogetsu and others) names, measures and angles the
main lines differently. The figures below follow the commonly taught **Sogetsu-style
basic upright** proportions; a teacher of another school will adjust them.

## 1. Materials and tools
- **Suiban** (shallow container, ~25–35 cm across) and a **kenzan** (pin frog) placed
  in it, covered with water.
- Ikebana scissors (strong blades that cut branches), a bowl of water for cutting.
- Material: **two or three branches** with clear line (e.g. willow, quince, pine, cherry,
  forsythia — whatever is in season) and **flowers** (iris, chrysanthemum, tulips,
  camellia, etc.). Choose for the current season; seasonality is central to ikebana.

## 2. The three main stems (Sogetsu basic upright, common proportions)
Let **A** = container diameter + depth.
| Stem | Name | Length | Angle from vertical | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main, longest | **Shin** | ~1 to 1.5 × A | ~10–15° | leaning to the front-left |
| Second | **Soe** | ~¾ of Shin | ~45° | to the front-left (further forward than Shin) |
| Third | **Hikae** | ~¾ of Soe | ~75° | to the front-right |
Usually Shin and Soe are branches; Hikae is often a flower. Lengths are measured from the
kenzan to the tip (above the water line). Other stems added later are **jushi** (fillers),
each shorter than the main stem it supports.

## 3. Building it
1. Place the kenzan in one corner of the container (e.g. front-left or back-left quarter,
   not dead centre) — the empty water surface is part of the design.
2. **Cut stems under water** (*mizugiri*): hold the stem end under water and cut on a
   slant — water is drawn up instead of an air bubble.
3. For thick branches, cut a vertical split (or a cross) in the base so it grips the
   pins. To tilt a branch, push it straight down onto the pins first, then angle it.
4. Insert **Shin** at the back of the kenzan, set its angle. Then **Soe** to its left-front,
   then **Hikae** to the right-front. Check from the front at eye level — the three tips
   form an asymmetric triangle with depth (not flat).
5. Add a few **jushi** to give depth and hide the kenzan, but keep spaces — less is more.
   Each jushi supports one main line and is shorter than it.
6. Trim leaves and twigs that cross, clutter or hide the line. The branch's best side
   faces the viewer.
7. Top up water to cover the kenzan fully.

## 4. Bending branches
Warm the branch with your hands, hold with both thumbs together on the inside of the
curve and bend in small increments along its length. Soft branches (willow, broom) bend
easily; brittle ones (cherry) crack — make small angled cuts on the outer side if needed.

## 5. Keeping it fresh
- Condition flowers before arranging: recut under water and let them drink deep for an
  hour or more. Woody stems: split or crush bases.
- Change or top up water daily; recut stems under water every couple of days.
- Remove spent blooms promptly. Out of direct sun and heating draughts.

## 6. Variations to try next
- **Slanting style:** Shin and Soe swap roles (Shin leaning ~45°).
- **Nageire:** tall vase, no kenzan; stems held by a cross-bar (kubari) or split stems.

## Gotchas
- Too many flowers is the commonest beginner fault — ikebana values line and space.
- Kenzan pins rust and break; dry it after use.
- Proportions above are **approximations of school curricula, not an official text**;
  learn from a qualified teacher of your chosen school for formal study.

## Verify
- [ ] Shin, Soe, Hikae lengths roughly in the ratio 1 : ¾ : ~½ and at distinct angles.
- [ ] Triangle has depth (tips at different distances from the viewer).
- [ ] Kenzan hidden, water surface visible.
- [ ] Material is in season; stems cut under water.
- [ ] Arrangement still fresh after 3–5 days with daily water care.
