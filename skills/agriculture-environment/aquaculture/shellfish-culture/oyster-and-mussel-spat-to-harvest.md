# Oyster and mussel culture — spat to harvest

**Goal:** turn spat into market shellfish over ~1.5–3 years (oysters) or ~1–2 years
(mussels), and sell them safely. Shellfish are filter feeders: they eat the
phytoplankton in your water and **also concentrate pathogens and toxins**, so food
safety controls are central, not optional. You need a site licence/lease from the
relevant authority; growing in unclassified waters is illegal in most places.

## 1. Seed
- **Wild spat collection**: put out collectors (ropes, tiles, shells, plastic collectors)
  just before the local spatfall season (monitor larvae counts if available).
  Mussels settle on ropes readily; oysters on cultch.
- **Hatchery seed**: oyster seed (diploid or **triploid** — sterile, so they keep condition in
  summer instead of spawning) at a few mm. Check health certificate/movement permit —
  oyster herpesvirus (OsHV-1) and other diseases are controlled in many regions.

## 2. Grow-out systems
- **Oysters, intertidal**: mesh bags on **trestles** (rack-and-bag), exposed at low tide —
  exposure hardens shells and controls fouling. Start with fine mesh (~2–4 mm), move to
  coarser (~9–14 mm) as they grow.
- **Oysters, subtidal/floating**: floating bags or baskets on lines; flip bags weekly-ish
  for drying (fouling control).
- **Mussels**: **longlines** (a backbone rope on buoys, anchored) with droppers or
  continuous socking; seed stuffed into **cotton/mesh socking** that rots as mussels attach.
  Bottom culture: seed relaid on leased seabed.

## 3. Husbandry calendar (in season, check every 2–6 weeks)
- **Grade and thin**: tip bags into a grader (or hand-sort), split into sizes, restock at
  lower density as they grow — e.g. a bag that held 2,000 seed may hold 100–200 at
  market size. Uncrowded oysters grow faster and more evenly.
- **Tumbling** (mechanical or wave action in tumble baskets) chips the growing edge,
  giving deep-cupped, uniform oysters.
- **Mussels**: re-sock as clumps get heavy and fall off; check buoyancy — add floats as
  weight builds.
- **Fouling and predators**: crabs, starfish, oyster drills, birds (eider ducks on mussels).
  Mesh, drying cycles and predator nets help.
- Check trestles/longlines after storms; lost gear is marine litter and money gone.

## 4. Harvest and food safety
- Harvest area **classification** (e.g. A/B/C by E. coli levels in shellfish flesh in EU/UK
  systems; approved/conditional/restricted in the US) decides whether you can sell
  directly or must **depurate** or relay first.
- **Biotoxin** monitoring (PSP, DSP, ASP from algal blooms): official closures override
  everything — don't harvest from a closed area.
- **Vibrio** controls (harvest-to-cooling time limits in warm months) where they apply.
- **Depuration**: clean UV-treated seawater in tanks for a set minimum time (often ~42 h in
  UK practice) — but depuration does not remove biotoxins and isn't fully reliable for norovirus.
- Keep **traceability tags**: harvest area, date, lot, harvester, on every bag.
- Store live shellfish cool (often ~2–10 °C), humid, not in fresh water, cup-side down for oysters.

## Gotchas
- Moving seed or gear between sites can spread disease and invasive species — follow
  movement rules.
- Summer mortality events in oysters (heat + herpesvirus) — triploids and lower-density
  culture may help; diversify seed batches.
- Heavy rainfall events often trigger temporary closures (sewage overflow).

## Verify
- [ ] Site licence and harvest area classification known and current.
- [ ] Seed source and health certification documented.
- [ ] Grading log: density per bag/rope by size class.
- [ ] Every harvested lot tagged; biotoxin status checked on harvest day.
