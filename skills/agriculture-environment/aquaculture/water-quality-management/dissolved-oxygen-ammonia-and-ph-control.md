# Dissolved oxygen, ammonia and pH control

**Goal:** catch the water problems that kill fish, usually low oxygen at dawn and toxic
ammonia, before they happen. Guidance here is for pond fish such as largemouth bass and
bluegill; other species (trout, salmon, marine and tank-culture species) have different
tolerances, so take limits from your species' extension or vet guidance.

## 1. Dissolved oxygen (DO)
- **Targets (Penn State Extension, pond fish)**: largemouth bass and bluegill prefer DO
  above 5.0 mg/L but tolerate lower levels for limited periods; fish may suffocate rapidly
  at 2.0 mg/L or lower. Consistent weekly readings above 5 mg/L over several midsummer
  weeks suggest DO is adequate.
- **Daily cycle**: surface DO is lowest just before sunrise (most likely in late July or
  through August) and peaks just before sunset in midsummer. Measure at dawn, not
  afternoon.
- **Measuring**: sample right after collecting, about an arm's length underwater for the
  surface; use a pump or Van Dorn sampler for bottom water. Hand-held meters are accurate
  but need regular calibration; Winkler kits are cheaper and accurate enough to flag low
  levels.
- **Bloom die-off**: a mass die-off of algae or aquatic plants (including after
  poorly timed pesticide applications) can use up so much oxygen decomposing that fish
  die.
- **Aeration**: wind and diffusion add oxygen naturally; subsurface diffusers circulate
  DO through the full depth; fountains mostly add DO to surface layers.
- Fish gasping at the surface indicates critically low DO.

## 2. Ammonia and pH
- Ammonia is extremely toxic to fish; Penn State's pond table lists ammonia-N below
  0.1 mg/L. Read test-kit TAN together with pH and temperature using your kit's table,
  because the toxic unionised fraction depends on them.
- pH: Penn State's table gives 6.5 to 8.4 as the fishing criterion; most fish do better
  near 7.0; below 6.0 may result in stunted or reduced fish populations.
- For acidic ponds, Penn State suggests one to two tons of pulverised limestone broadcast
  over winter ice; repeated applications are often needed. Take a pond-specific lime
  rate from a local test.

## 3. Testing routine
- Keep a log with time of day for DO, temperature, pH and ammonia. Calibrate meters per
  the maker's instructions; check test-kit expiry dates.

## 4. Fish kill response
1. Start all available aeration and stop feeding.
2. Measure DO, temperature, pH and ammonia right away (the cause may vanish later).
3. Collect a few dying fish for a vet or fish-health lab.
4. Record numbers dead, time, weather, recent actions (fertiliser, treatment, feeding).
5. Report as required by permits (discharge or pollution events).

## Gotchas
- Afternoon DO readings are misleading; the dawn low is what matters.
- Treatments that change pH fast can change how toxic ammonia is.
- Algae crashes after cloudy spells and pesticide treatments drive the worst sags.
