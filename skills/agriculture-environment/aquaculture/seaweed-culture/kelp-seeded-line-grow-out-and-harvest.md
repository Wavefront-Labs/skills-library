# Kelp farming: seeded line, grow-out and harvest

**Goal:** a winter kelp crop (e.g. sugar kelp *Saccharina latissima*, winged kelp
*Alaria*) grown on longlines in a temperate sea. Tropical red seaweeds (Eucheuma,
Gracilaria) are farmed vegetatively from cuttings tied to lines — a different
method outlined at the end. You need a lease/licence and often a permit to collect
wild reproductive material.

## 1. Nursery: from sorus to seeded spool (late summer–autumn)
1. **Collect sori** — the dark, thickened reproductive patches on mature wild blades
   (seasonal; typically late summer/autumn for sugar kelp).
2. Clean: trim, wipe, brief dip in dilute iodine or bleach solution and rinse in
   sterile seawater (method varies), dry in a cool dark place overnight (wrapped in paper towel).
3. **Release spores**: immerse in cold (~10 °C) filtered, sterilised seawater; spores
   release within ~30–60 min. Check density under a microscope.
4. **Seed**: pour spore solution over **spools of twine** (a few mm nylon or similar) in tanks.
5. Grow 4–8 weeks at ~10 °C, low light, with nutrient medium (e.g. f/2-type), changing water
   weekly. Gametophytes develop, then young sporophytes ~1–5 mm long.
   Alternative: **gametophyte culture** kept vegetative under red light, then sprayed onto
   twine — allows year-round seed and strain selection.

## 2. Outplanting (autumn, when water cools)
- Site: good water flow, enough nutrients, shelter from extreme swell, depth for lines at
  ~1–3 m below surface. Water usually needs to be below ~15–17 °C for sugar kelp.
- **Longline**: anchored backbone rope with buoys; grow-out lines often 50–200 m. Wrap
  the seed twine around the grow-out rope (spiral) or tie in lengths.
- Lower lines in turbid water? Deeper lines get less light; shallower lines get wave
  stress and more fouling. Adjust buoyancy as the crop gains weight — a mature line can
  carry several kg per metre.

## 3. Grow-out management (winter)
- Inspect monthly and after storms: buoys, anchors, chafe points, tangles.
- Add floats as kelp weight increases.
- Record growth (blade length on a few marked plants), water temperature and nutrients if possible.

## 4. Harvest (spring)
- Harvest when blades are long (often ~1–3 m) and **before biofouling** (bryozoans,
  hydroids, epiphytic algae) builds as water warms in late spring — fouled blades lose value.
- Cut lines or strip blades into totes/bags on the boat. Keep cool, shaded, and move fast —
  fresh kelp deteriorates within hours in heat.
- **Primary processing**: air- or oven-dry (to <~15% moisture for stable storage), or
  **blanch** (brief hot-water dip, turning bright green) and freeze for food markets.

## 5. Food safety and quality
Food-grade seaweed may require testing for **iodine** (kelp is very high), heavy metals
(arsenic, cadmium, lead), and microbes, plus traceable lots. Check the local food
authority's rules.

## 6. Tropical red seaweeds (outline)
Eucheuma/Kappaphycus: ~50–100 g cuttings tied to lines (off-bottom or floating rafts),
~45-day crop cycles, harvest by pulling the lines, sun-dry on platforms. Main threats are
"ice-ice" disease and grazers — keep some stock as seed.

## Gotchas
- Sorus collection out of season gives few or no spores.
- Overheating the nursery tank kills gametophytes.
- Long lines without enough floats sink when the crop is heavy.
- Entanglement risk for marine mammals: keep lines taut, follow local gear rules.

## Verify
- [ ] Nursery spools show visible sporophytes (~1–5 mm) before outplanting.
- [ ] Lines at target depth with float plan for crop weight.
- [ ] Harvest scheduled before the epiphyte season.
- [ ] Dried/blanched product meets moisture target and labelled by lot.
