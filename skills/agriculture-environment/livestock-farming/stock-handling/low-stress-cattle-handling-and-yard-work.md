# Low-stress cattle handling and yard work

**Goal:** cattle that move calmly where you want them, fewer injuries to stock and people,
less bruising and weight loss — by using cattle behaviour instead of noise and force.

## 1. Two concepts that do most of the work
- **Flight zone:** the animal's personal space. Step into it and it moves away; step
  out and it stops. Its size varies: quiet dairy cows maybe 1–2 m, rangeland cattle
  20 m+. Work at its **edge** — deep inside it causes panic, outside it does nothing.
- **Point of balance:** roughly at the animal's **shoulder**. Stand behind the shoulder
  (in the flight zone) and it moves forward; in front of the shoulder and it moves back
  or turns.
Cattle have wide-angle vision with a **blind spot directly behind** — never stand
there; they'll turn to see you.

## 2. Pressure and release
Apply pressure (move into the flight zone edge), and **release the instant the animal
does what you want** (step back, stop). The release is the reward and the lesson.
Constant pressure (shouting, waving, chasing) just makes cattle flighty.

## 3. Moving a mob
- Approach calmly and let them see you; work from the side, zig-zagging behind the mob
  at the edge of the flight zone to keep the leaders moving.
- **Let the leaders lead** — cattle follow one another; walk the pace of the mob.
- Move cattle in groups the facility can handle — fill forcing pens only about **half to
  two-thirds full** so animals have room to turn and follow.
- Dogs and horses: well-trained, used quietly. A barking, biting dog at the heels raises
  stress and injury risk.

## 4. In the yards
- **Walk-through first:** look for things that make cattle balk — shadows and bright
  patches, reflections on water or metal, chains hanging, a coat on the fence, people
  ahead in their line of sight, air blowing toward them, dark entrances (cattle move
  from dark to light). Remove distractions.
- **Alley/race work:** walk **back past the point of balance** (in the opposite direction
  to the way you want them to go) — as you pass each animal's shoulder, it moves forward.
  This is more effective than pushing from behind.
- **Bud box / forcing pen:** let cattle enter, then position yourself so they turn and
  flow back past you into the race. Don't crowd them.
- **Crush/chute:** catch the head gate cleanly; use the squeeze only enough to hold.
  Don't leave animals restrained longer than needed.
- **Electric prodders:** last resort only, never on sensitive areas, never on animals
  with nowhere to go. A well-run yard needs one rarely. Some quality-assurance schemes
  limit their use to a small percentage of animals.

## 5. Loading for transport
- Ramp slope ideally ≤ ~20° (some codes allow up to ~25° for cattle); non-slip, with
  cleats or steps; solid sides; no gap between ramp and truck.
- Load in small groups at the deck's correct density (follow your transport code);
  overloading causes bruising, underloading lets animals fall.
- Fit-to-load check: lame, weak, sick or heavily pregnant animals near term may not be
  legally fit to travel — check your national code.

## 6. Safety
- Plan escape routes before entering any pen; never get between a cow and her calf or in
  a confined space with a bull.
- Work in pairs where possible; tell someone where you are.
- Watch the ears, head and tail — an animal looking straight at you with head lowered,
  pawing or blowing is a warning.
- Keep hands and arms out of gates that can swing and of the head bail when it's operating.

## Gotchas
- Numbers (flight-zone sizes, ramp angles) are commonly cited ranges, **unconfirmed for
  your animals or local transport law**.
- One rough handling session makes cattle harder to handle next time — they remember.
- Yelling as a habit: try a silent session and compare times.

## Verify
- [ ] Yard walk-through done; distractions removed before cattle arrive.
- [ ] Forcing pen no more than about 2/3 full.
- [ ] Few or no prodder uses; record if any.
- [ ] No slips, falls, or animals going over gates; balking points noted for fixing.
- [ ] Animals loaded fit-to-load and at correct density.
