# Lambing and kidding assistance

**Goal:** know when to leave a ewe or doe alone, when to help, how to correct the common
problems, and how to get every newborn up and fed within its first hours.

## 1. Kit (ready before the first birth)
Disposable arm-length gloves, obstetric lubricant, lambing ropes/snare, iodine (strong,
for navels), stomach tube + 60 mL syringe, colostrum (stored ewe/doe colostrum frozen,
or a commercial replacer), heat box or lamp, thermometer, glucose solution (vet
supplied, for injection by trained staff), marker spray, record book, vet's number.

## 2. Normal birth and when to intervene
Normal: restlessness, pawing, then the **water bag** appears; front feet (soles down)
and nose follow, and the lamb/kid is born within about **30–60 minutes of the water bag
appearing**. Second twin often within ~30 min of the first.

**Examine (clean hands, lube, gloves) if:**
- straining hard for **30+ minutes** with no progress;
- the water bag has been out **~1 hour** with nothing more;
- you see a head with no feet, a single foot, a tail, or feet soles-up;
- the ewe stopped straining and is dull (may be exhausted, or ringwomb — cervix not dilated).

## 3. Correcting common presentations
Always: restrain gently (ewe on her side or held standing), be clean and gentle, push the
lamb **back** into the uterus to make room before repositioning — never pull against a
problem. Pull only with the ewe's contractions, downward toward her hocks.
- **One leg back:** push lamb back slightly, cup the hoof of the missing leg in your hand
  and bring it forward. Many small lambs will deliver with one leg back, but large singles won't.
- **Both legs back (head only):** push the head back, find and bring both legs forward,
  then deliver. If the head is very swollen, it may need vet help.
- **Head back (legs only):** push back, cup the nose and bring it round into the pelvis.
  Can be hard — a head snare helps.
- **Backwards (hind legs, soles up, hocks felt):** deliver promptly once started — the
  cord gets pinched and the lamb may breathe fluid. Pull steadily and quickly.
- **Breech (tail only):** push back, bring each hind leg forward by cupping the hoof
  (flex the hock first), then deliver backwards.
- **Twins tangled:** follow each leg to its body before pulling; deliver the one
  nearest the exit first.
**Call the vet** if you can't correct it in ~15–20 minutes, the cervix won't open, there's
fresh blood, or you're not sure. Caesarean in time saves both.

## 4. First hours: the newborn
1. Clear mucus from nose/mouth; rub vigorously with straw; a straw tickle in the nostril
   can start breathing. Swinging is discouraged now (can harm organs) — rub and stimulate.
2. **Dip navels** in strong iodine immediately (and again a few hours later).
3. **Colostrum:** aim for about **50 mL/kg bodyweight within the first 6 hours** and
   ~200 mL/kg in the first 24 h (common guide). A 4 kg lamb → 200 mL in 6 h, 800 mL in 24 h.
   Strip each teat of the ewe to check milk flows; watch the lamb suck.
4. Weak lamb that can't suck: **stomach tube** — measure tube from mouth to last rib,
   pass gently down the left side of the mouth; the lamb should swallow; if it coughs or
   the tube meets resistance, remove. Only tube lambs that can lift their head.
5. **Hypothermia:** take rectal temperature. Normal ~39–40 °C. 37–39 °C → dry, feed,
   warm. Below 37 °C → if under ~5 h old, warm and feed; if older and unable to hold
   its head up, it likely needs glucose (intraperitoneal injection by trained staff/vet)
   **before** warming, or it can die in the warmer. Get trained in this in advance.

## 5. Jug (pen) routine
House ewe and lambs in an individual pen for 24–48 h: bonding, checking colostrum intake,
spotting mismothering. Mark ewe and lambs with matching numbers before turning out.

## Gotchas
- Pregnancy toxaemia (twin-lamb disease) in late pregnancy — dull, isolated, blind-acting
  ewes; often fatal; vet and drench quickly; prevent with late-pregnancy nutrition.
- Zoonoses: pregnant women should not handle lambing ewes (enzootic abortion, toxoplasma,
  Q fever risks). Gloves always.
