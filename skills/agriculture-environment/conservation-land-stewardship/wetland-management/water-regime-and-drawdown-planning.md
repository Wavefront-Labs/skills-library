# Wetland water regime and drawdown planning

**Goal:** the right depth, at the right time, for the right length, so the plants and
animals you are managing for can complete their cycles. Most managed wetlands decline
because they're held permanently full (or permanently dry) when they evolved with
wetting and drying.

## 1. Know the wetland's natural regime
- Classify: permanent, seasonal (fills most years, dries most years), intermittent or
  ephemeral. Look for evidence: aerial photo time series, old records, vegetation
  zonation (deep-water trees, sedge/rush fringe), local knowledge.
- Record the **hydroperiod** (months wet per year), typical depth, filling season and
  how it fills (river flood, local run-off, groundwater, pumped/environmental water).

## 2. Objectives drive the regime
Write them as outcomes with the water needed, e.g.:
- "Maintain river red gum health: flood roots for 2–4 months every 2–3 years" (example
  of a commonly cited pattern for floodplain eucalypts — verify for your species).
- "Waterbird breeding event: hold water ≥ 4–6 weeks beyond chick fledging" —
  colonial waterbirds abandon nests if water drops too soon.
- "Migratory shorebird habitat: shallow water < ~10–15 cm and exposed mud during
  migration months."
- "Moist-soil seed plants for waterfowl: slow spring/summer drawdown to expose mud
  progressively, reflood in autumn."

## 3. Measure
- **Staff gauge** at the deepest stable point, surveyed to a datum, read on a fixed
  schedule (weekly while managing water). Add a depth/level logger for continuous data.
- Know the **stage–area–volume** relationship (from survey or LiDAR): how many ML to
  reach each level, how much area is wet at each level.
- Water quality during fills and drawdowns: dissolved oxygen, temperature, salinity
  (EC), pH where acid sulfate soils are possible.

## 4. Structures and operation
- Stoplog/flashboard risers: add or remove boards **one at a time**, a few cm per step,
  for gradual change. Drawdowns over weeks give successive mudflat bands for birds and
  germination fronts for plants; a sudden drop strands fish and nests.
- Inlet screens to keep carp out where possible; carp ruin water clarity and
  submerged plants. A full drawdown in late summer is one of the few effective carp
  controls.
- Log every operation: date, board count, gauge reading, reason.

## 5. Vegetation management through the regime
- Prolonged deep flooding drowns emergent plants; permanent shallow water favours
  dense **cumbungi/Typha** and **Phragmites** monocultures. A drying phase, then a
  deeper fill, sets them back; combine with slashing below water or grazing on drydown.
- Germination of many sedges, rushes and annual herbs is triggered on drawdown mud —
  time drawdowns for the growing season.
- Watch for weed invasion on exposed beds; follow up early.

## 6. Risks to plan for
- **Blackwater:** first flush over heavy leaf litter in warm weather can strip oxygen
  and kill fish. Fill in cooler months or in pulses where possible; monitor DO.
- **Acid sulfate soils:** drying sulfidic sediments can produce acid and release
  metals when rewetted. If the site has a history (coastal, saline-affected inland),
  test before drying deeper than usual.
- **Avian botulism:** warm, stagnant, shallow, decaying-organic conditions; remove
  carcasses promptly and avoid drawdowns into hot still weather where outbreaks occur.
- **Mosquitoes:** shallow warm water near towns may need a management plan.

## 7. Approvals and water rights
Moving water usually needs a licence, an environmental water allocation or approval
from the river operator; structures may need works approvals. Coordinate with the
environmental water holder and downstream users.

## Gotchas
- One "good" year of full water isn't success — judge across the wet-dry cycle.
- Gauge datums drift when posts are bumped; resurvey periodically.
