# Positive identification protocol for wild mushrooms

**Goal:** never eat a mushroom you haven't identified to species with certainty,
using every feature — and never by app, photo match or a single "rule". There is **no
general test** for edibility: peeling, silver spoons, animals eating it, pleasant smell,
cooking — all myths. Several deadly species taste good. **This skill is a method, not
an identification guide**: learn species in person with an experienced forager or
mycological society and cross-check with at least two regional field guides.

## 1. Collect properly
- Take the **whole** mushroom including the base: dig gently around the stem. The
  volva (cup) at the base of Amanitas is often buried — cutting at ground level hides
  the most important feature of the deadliest group.
- Note habitat: on wood or soil? Which trees nearby (many fungi are partners of
  specific trees)? Growing singly, in clusters, in rings?
- Carry in a basket or paper bags, **separated by species** — fragments of a toxic
  species in a mixed bag contaminate the rest. Never plastic (they sweat and rot).
- Take young, mature and old specimens if you can; features change with age.

## 2. Record every feature
- **Cap:** size, colour, texture, shape, margin, any patches/warts (veil remnants).
- **Underside:** gills (attachment to stem — free, attached, decurrent; spacing;
  colour; do they fork?), pores (colour, bruising), teeth, ridges (chanterelle "false
  gills" are blunt, forked, run down the stem), or smooth.
- **Stem:** ring (skirt) present? texture, colour, hollow/solid, base (bulb, volva,
  cobwebby veil/cortina).
- **Flesh:** colour change when cut or bruised, smell, latex (milk caps).
- **Spore print:** cap gill-side down on half white, half black paper, under a bowl,
  for 2–12 hours. Colour (white, cream, pink, brown, rusty-brown, purple-brown, black)
  is a key separator — e.g. rusty-brown prints point to *Galerina* and *Cortinarius*.

## 3. Key against the deadly groups (wherever you are, learn these first)
- **Amanitas** (death cap *A. phalloides*, destroying angels): white gills, white spore
  print, ring on stem, **volva at the base**. Cause most fatal mushroom poisonings
  worldwide; symptoms are delayed 6–24 h, then apparent recovery while the liver fails.
  Many foragers avoid **all** white-gilled, white-spored mushrooms with a ring and
  bulbous base as beginners.
- **Galerina** (e.g. *G. marginata*): small brown mushrooms on wood with rusty-brown
  print and a ring zone; same toxins as death cap; confused with honey fungus and
  "magic mushrooms".
- **Cortinarius** (deadly webcaps such as *C. rubellus*, *C. orellanus*): rusty-brown
  print, cobweb veil when young; kidney damage appearing days to weeks later.
- **Gyromitra** (false morels): brain-like, wrinkled, chambered or cottony inside —
  true morels are **hollow** from cap to stem when sliced lengthwise.
- Regionally important: jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus*) vs chanterelle (true gills,
  grows in clusters on wood), *Chlorophyllum molybdites* (green spore print) vs parasol
  mushrooms, and others in your guides.

## 4. Decide
Eat only when **every** feature matches the species description, the spore print
matches, the habitat and season fit, and you have positively excluded every listed
lookalike. If any point is uncertain, it's a "no". First time with a new species:
have an expert confirm in person, eat a small cooked portion, and keep an uncooked
specimen in the fridge for identification if you react.
Cook wild mushrooms thoroughly; many edible species are mildly toxic raw (e.g. morels).
Avoid alcohol with ink caps (*Coprinopsis atramentaria*) — disulfiram-like reaction.

## 5. If someone may be poisoned
Call poison control or emergency services immediately — don't wait for symptoms.
Bring remaining specimens, cooking leftovers and photos. Delayed onset (6+ hours)
after a meal of wild mushrooms is a red flag for amatoxins.

## 6. Harvest responsibly
- Know the law: some parks and reserves ban collecting; commercial picking often needs
  permits; landowner permission everywhere else.
- Cut or twist? Both are argued; for edibles you've already identified with certainty
  either is fine — minimise soil disturbance, leave small and old specimens.
- Don't collect from roadsides, industrial land or treated lawns (heavy metals,
  pesticides).

## Verify
- [ ] Whole specimen collected, base included, kept separate from others.
- [ ] Every feature recorded and a spore print taken.
- [ ] All deadly lookalikes for the region explicitly ruled out.
- [ ] Identification confirmed by an expert for any species new to you.
- [ ] Emergency plan known: poison control number, keep a specimen.
