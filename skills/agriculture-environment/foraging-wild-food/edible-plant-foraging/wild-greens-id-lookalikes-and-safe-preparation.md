# Wild greens: identification, lookalikes and safe preparation

**Goal:** add wild plants to the table without poisoning anyone or stripping a
patch. The method matters more than any list of edibles: identify by several
independent features, know the dangerous lookalikes in your region first, and treat
anything uncertain as inedible. **This is a method, not a field guide** — use two
regional guides and learn plants in person before eating them.

## 1. Identify by multiple features
For each plant, check and write down:
- **Leaf:** arrangement (opposite, alternate, whorled, basal rosette), shape, margin,
  hairiness, venation, compound or simple.
- **Stem:** round, square (mint family), hollow, ridged, hairy, spotted/blotched, sap
  colour (milky sap in dandelion and lettuces, but also in toxic spurges).
- **Smell** when crushed (garlic, carrot, mint) — useful, but crushing hands can carry
  smell between plants.
- **Flowers/seeds:** family-level clues (umbels in the carrot family, 4 petals in a
  cross in brassicas, square stems + opposite leaves in mints).
- **Root/bulb** for root crops.
- **Habitat and season.**
Return to the same plant through the year (shoot → flower → seed) — knowing a plant in
one stage only is how misidentifications happen. Plant ID apps are good for
suggestions, not decisions.

## 2. Learn these lookalike pairs first (temperate examples)
- **Carrot family (Apiaceae)** — contains wild carrot, parsnip, fennel, cow parsley
  *and* some of the most poisonous plants in the temperate world: poison hemlock
  (*Conium maculatum*: smooth stem with purple blotches, musty "mousy" smell), water
  hemlock (*Cicuta* spp.) and hemlock water-dropwort (*Oenanthe crocata*: tuberous
  roots near water, very toxic). Beginners should **avoid the whole family** until expert.
- **Wild garlic / ramsons** vs **lily of the valley**, **lords-and-ladies** (*Arum*) and
  autumn crocus leaves — pick leaf by leaf and crush each for the garlic smell; don't
  grab handfuls.
- **Wild onions** vs **death camas** (*Toxicoscordion/Zigadenus*, North America): no
  onion smell → not an onion.
- **Comfrey/borage** vs **foxglove** rosettes (foxglove contains cardiac glycosides;
  comfrey itself has liver-toxic alkaloids, so it's a poor choice for regular eating anyway).
- **Elder** (berries cooked only) vs **water hemlock**-type umbels (leaves differ).
Learn your region's equivalents from local guides.

## 3. Timing
- Greens are best young: nettle tops before flowering, dandelion leaves before buds
  (bitterness rises), chickweed while lush.
- Roots in autumn to early spring when energy is stored (dandelion, burdock, wild
  parsnip — parsnip sap causes phytophotodermatitis: gloves and long sleeves).
- Flowers on dry mornings.

## 4. Plants that need preparation
- **Stinging nettle:** cook or dry to neutralise stings; harvest with gloves.
- **High-oxalate plants** (sorrels, docks, fat hen/lamb's quarters in quantity,
  purslane): fine in moderation; cooking and discarding water lowers soluble oxalate;
  caution with kidney stone history.
- **Pokeweed** (*Phytolacca americana*): eaten traditionally as young shoots only after
  boiling in changes of water; roots, mature stems and berries are toxic. Many guides
  advise beginners to avoid it.
- **Bracken**: carcinogenic compounds; avoid despite traditional use.
- **Acorns** (see wild-food-processing skill) need tannin leaching.
- Raw elderberries, unripe and leaves/stems: cause nausea — cook ripe berries only.

## 5. Where not to pick
- Roadsides (lead and other pollutants, de-icing salt), field edges that may have been
  sprayed, railway lines, industrial sites, dog-walking verges below knee height,
  water downstream of sewage or livestock (liver fluke risk with wild watercress in
  livestock areas).
- Know the law: many protected areas ban picking; uprooting wild plants without
  landowner permission is illegal in some countries (e.g. UK Wildlife and Countryside
  Act); protected species must never be taken.

## 6. Sustainable take
Common forager rules: take no more than ~10% of a patch (less for slow-growing or
uncommon plants), never the only plant in sight, pick leaves rather than uprooting where
the leaf is what you want, spread picking across patches, avoid rare species entirely.

## 7. First time eating a new plant
Positively identified, a small amount, cooked if appropriate, one new species at a time,
and keep a sample. Allergies and individual reactions happen even with true edibles.

## Verify
- [ ] Each plant identified by several independent features, confirmed in two guides.
- [ ] Region's deadly lookalikes known and ruled out (Apiaceae avoided unless expert).
- [ ] Preparation requirements met for plants that need it.
- [ ] Picking site free of spray, traffic and dog contamination; picking legal there.
- [ ] Take kept to ~10% of a patch.
