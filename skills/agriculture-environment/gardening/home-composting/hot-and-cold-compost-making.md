# Hot and cold compost making

**Goal:** turn kitchen and garden waste into crumbly, sweet-smelling compost, fast
(hot) or slow (cold), without a slimy, smelly or rat-infested heap.

> Times and temperatures are typical; small heaps and cold climates run slower.
> Local rules on composting food waste (rats, meat) vary.

## 1. The two inputs
- **Greens** (nitrogen-rich, wet): grass clippings, fruit/veg scraps, coffee grounds,
  young weeds, manure from herbivores.
- **Browns** (carbon-rich, dry): cardboard, paper, straw, dry leaves, chipped woody
  prunings, sawdust (untreated wood). Shredding or chipping speeds breakdown.
- Aim for roughly **2-3 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume**, which lands near the
  ~30:1 carbon:nitrogen ratio the microbes need (UC Master Gardeners). Too much nitrogen
  can overheat the pile and give ammonia odours; too little slows decomposition.
- **Keep out:** meat, fish, dairy and bones in kitchen scraps, excessive fat, stove or
  fireplace ashes, soil, and manure from meat-eating animals (dogs, cats).

## 2. Moisture and air
- **Squeeze test:** target roughly 40-60% moisture. A squeezed handful should give a few
  drops and hold together; if it falls apart it is too dry, if it oozes water it is too wet.
- Keep it aerobic: turning or aerating the pile supplies oxygen.

## 3. Hot composting
1. Build in one go with a pile or bin of at least **3 ft x 3 ft x 3 ft** (about 1 m
   each way); smaller piles take longer. Chop woody material to small bits.
2. Mix greens and browns, watering as you go.
3. With the right balance, size and moisture a pile reaches working temperature in about
   1-2 days. Use a compost thermometer; the active band is about **131-160 °F (55-71 °C)**.
4. If it does not heat, it is too wet or too dry (add water, or green material if short
   of nitrogen). Turn the pile to keep it aerobic and to cool it if it gets hot.
5. When it no longer reheats after turning it is cured. Cure for further weeks to months.
Hot composting is the method that kills most pathogens and weed seeds. UGA gives the
active phase as about 3-8 weeks plus 1-3 months curing.

## 4. Cold composting (low effort)
Add material as it comes. It takes roughly 6 months to 2 years, and weed seeds, pests and
pathogens may survive, so keep seeding weeds out. Do not apply the product to edible crops
unless it has aged many months or gone through a hot phase.

## 5. Worm bins (vermicomposting)
Use composting worms (*Eisenia fetida*), not garden earthworms. Keep them cool and moist,
at ambient temperatures of about 55-77 °F (13-25 °C); not compatible with a hot heap.

## 6. Using compost
- **Finished:** dark, crumbly, earthy smell, with little or no recognisable food scraps
  or leaves, and it no longer heats after turning.
- Screening out large pieces is optional (UC).

## Gotchas
- Grass clippings alone mat down: mix with browns.
- A heap that is "not doing anything" is usually too dry, too wet, or too small.

## Verify
- [ ] Mix is roughly 2-3 browns : 1 green by volume, damp but not oozing.
- [ ] Hot heap is at least 3 ft cubed and reaches the working band within days.
- [ ] No meat, dairy, bones or cat/dog manure in the bin.
- [ ] Finished compost is dark, crumbly and earthy before use.
