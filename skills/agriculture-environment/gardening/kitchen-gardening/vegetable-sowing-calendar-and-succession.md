# Vegetable sowing calendar and succession

**Goal:** a one-page calendar for *your* garden that says what to sow, when, indoors
or out, and how often, so you get a steady supply instead of thirty lettuces at once
and nothing in August.

> Anchor on your own local frost data and adjust after your first season. Seed packets
> list minimum soil and air temperatures (Penn State).

## 1. Find your anchor dates
- **Average last spring frost** and **first autumn frost** for your area (extension office
  or national weather service). Cornell Cooperative Extension gives county dates, for
  example, and notes its fall schedule uses a date with a 50% chance of a first killing frost.
- Season length is the span between them; it decides which long warm-season crops you can
  grow outdoors.

## 2. Group crops by season
- **Cool-season** crops (for example beets, broccoli, cabbage, carrots, kale, lettuce, peas,
  radishes, spinach, turnips) are planted anytime from several weeks to a couple of months
  before the last frost date, and are generally seeded direct (Penn State).
- **Warm-season** crops (beans, corn, cucumbers, eggplant, melons, peppers, squash, tomato)
  are always planted after the last frost date and are usually started indoors and
  transplanted once soil and air are warm enough (Penn State).
- Cornell Albany lists beans, peppers, potatoes, sweet corn and tomatoes after the last
  average frost, and basil, cucumber, melons, squash and watermelon after the soil has warmed.
- Tomatoes: start indoors about 6 to 8 weeks before the last frost date (Cornell).
For autumn crops, check the days to maturity on the packet, **count back that number of
days from the first frost date and add the days the seed needs to germinate** (Cornell Warren).

## 3. Succession, small and often
- Many cool-season crops can be sown in early spring and again in autumn, giving two
  cycles; warm-season crops have one cycle (Penn State).
- Crops with a short harvest period last longer with several smaller sequential
  plantings. Set the interval from what you eat per week and how long the crop stays good.
- **Catch crops:** quick crops between slow ones. **Follow-on crops:** sow a new crop where an
  early one came out.

## 4. Rotation and season extension
- Keep a simple bed map each year and avoid planting the same family in the same bed
  year after year; use a local extension rotation guide for the cycle.
- Fleece, cloches, low tunnels and cold frames let you sow or harvest beyond the frost
  dates (Penn State describes season extenders for this). Ventilate on sunny days.

## Gotchas
- Sowing into cold, wet soil can rot seed; check the packet's minimum soil temperature.
- Harden off indoor-raised plants before planting out.
- Flowers for pollinators are a reliable addition; treat other companion pairings as
  experiments.

## Verify
- [ ] Your frost dates are written at the top of the calendar.
- [ ] Every crop has a sow date, method (indoors/direct) and repeat interval.
- [ ] Autumn sowings counted back from first frost with maturity days plus germination days.
- [ ] Rotation map exists.
- [ ] After the season, note what was early/late and shift dates next year.
