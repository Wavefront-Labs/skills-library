# Shelterbelt and windbreak design

**Goal:** a line of trees and shrubs that cuts wind speed over crops, stock or
buildings across a useful distance, without creating frost pockets or turbulence.

> Draft. Rules of thumb (protection distance in multiples of height, ~40–50% porosity)
> are well established in extension literature; species and spacing are region-specific —
> use your local agroforestry/extension species list.

## 1. Define the job
Write what you are sheltering and from what:
- Crop yield / wind erosion on light soils → long belts across the prevailing wind.
- Livestock (lambing, calving, winter stress) → denser belts, often L- or U-shaped
  around a paddock or yard.
- Buildings / snow → dense belts set back far enough that snow drifts land short of the yard.
Get the **damaging wind direction** (not just the prevailing one): local wind rose,
farm experience, where trees lean and snow drifts.

## 2. Geometry
- **Orientation:** perpendicular to the damaging wind. For variable winds, use L-shapes or
  a grid of belts.
- **Height (H)** determines reach. Wind is reduced roughly to **10–20 H downwind**
  (greatest reduction at ~3–6 H) and ~2–5 H upwind. A 10 m belt shelters roughly
  100–200 m downwind.
- **Length:** at least ~10–12× height, otherwise wind wraps around the ends.
- **Gaps** (gateways, tracks) funnel wind and create jets; avoid them or angle and offset them.
- **Porosity** (proportion of open area seen side-on):
  - **~40–50%** for crops and erosion control: longest sheltered zone, low turbulence.
  - **~25–35% or denser** for livestock and buildings: stronger shelter close in, shorter reach.
  - A solid wall (0%) gives intense turbulence just behind it — avoid for crops.

## 3. Structure and rows
- 1 row for field-edge crop shelter (e.g. a tall deciduous row with shrubs) up to 3–5 rows
  for livestock/farmstead.
- Row spacing roughly 3–5 m (wide enough for mowers/weed control); in-row spacing 1–3 m
  for shrubs, 2–4 m for trees. Stagger plants between rows.
- Combine: a shrub or low-branching row on the windward edge (fills the bottom, prevents
  under-blow), taller trees in the middle/leeward rows.
- Mix species for resilience (one disease shouldn't open a gap); include evergreens where
  winter shelter matters.
- Leave the bottom closed: belts that lose their lower branches let wind jet underneath.

## 4. Siting details
- Keep ≥ the mature canopy width from fences, drains, power lines; 30–60 m from
  farmyards so snow drops before it.
- Don't put a dense belt across the bottom of a slope — it dams cold air into a frost pocket.
- Allow for root competition: crops within ~1–2 H of the belt yield less; often offset by
  gains further out. Root-pruning (ripping) along the crop side limits it.

## 5. Establishment
Follow tree-planting basics (see planting-bare-root-and-cell-grown-trees): weed-free strip
1–2 m wide for at least 3 years (mulch film or herbicide), guards against rabbits/deer,
stock-proof fencing on livestock land, beat up losses in year 2 — gaps are hard to fill later.

## Gotchas
- Gateways cut through a belt become wind tunnels; place them at the end.
- Shelterbelts shade southern-side crops (northern hemisphere) — orient and space accordingly.
- Grazing animals destroy unfenced belts in a few weeks.

## Verify
- [ ] Belt is perpendicular to the damaging wind; length ≥ ~10× mature height.
- [ ] Target porosity stated for the purpose; windward low row included.
- [ ] Gaps and frost-pocket risks checked on the map.
- [ ] Weed control, guards and fencing planned for ≥ 3 years.
- [ ] Losses counted after year 1 and replaced.
