# Fuel load assessment and treatment

**Goal:** turn a stand that would carry a crown fire into one where a fire stays on
the ground at moderate intensity — by breaking the vertical and horizontal continuity
of fuel, not by removing all vegetation.

> Draft. Principles follow widely taught fire-resilience logic ("reduce surface fuels,
> raise canopy base height, reduce canopy density, keep big fire-resistant trees").
> Fuel models, burn permits and seasons are regional — follow your fire agency.

## 1. Assess three fuel layers
**Surface fuels** (litter, duff, grass, shrubs, down woody material)
- Down woody fuel by time-lag class: 1-hr (<0.6 cm), 10-hr (0.6–2.5 cm), 100-hr
  (2.5–7.6 cm), 1000-hr (>7.6 cm). Measure with **planar-intersect (Brown's) transects**:
  count pieces crossing a vertical plane along a measured line, by class; convert with
  published equations to t/ha.
- Quick method: compare with a regional **photo series** of known fuel loads.
- Note shrub height and cover, and litter/duff depth.
**Ladder fuels**
- Small trees, low live and dead branches, shrubs that link the ground to the canopy.
- Measure **canopy base height (CBH)**: height to the lowest live/dead branches that
  would carry fire upward. Under ~2–3 m CBH, crown fire initiation is easy.
**Canopy fuels**
- **Canopy bulk density (CBD)**, approximated from BA and species. Values above roughly
  0.10–0.15 kg/m³ support active crown fire under severe weather (commonly cited range).
Map fuels by stand; assign each a fuel model if your agency uses one (e.g. Scott & Burgan 40).

## 2. Choose treatments (usual order)
1. **Thin from below:** remove small and suppressed trees first; keep the largest,
   thickest-barked, fire-resistant individuals (species-dependent: e.g. ponderosa pine,
   larch, cork oak). This raises CBH and lowers CBD at once.
2. **Prune** retained trees to ~2–3 m (not more than ~1/3 of live crown).
3. **Treat the slash you just created** — the thinning itself increases surface fuel:
   - Lop-and-scatter to < ~45–60 cm depth (low continuity, fast decay; OK in some climates).
   - **Chip** or **masticate** (machine-shredded mulch; reduces flame length but can
     smoulder long).
   - **Pile and burn** in wet/winter season; small, compact, clean piles away from
     retained trees; under permit.
   - Remove as biomass where markets exist.
4. **Prescribed burning** of surface fuels where trained crews, permits and burn windows
   allow; often the most effective maintenance once thinned.
5. **Shrub control** (cutting, grazing, burning) on a repeat cycle — shrubs come back in
   3–10 years.

## 3. Fuel breaks and firebreaks
- **Shaded fuel break:** thinned and pruned forest strip (often 50–300 m wide) along
  ridges/roads where crews can work — canopy kept for shade to slow regrowth.
- **Bare-earth firebreak:** narrow strip down to mineral soil; maintain yearly; watch erosion.
- Maintain before every fire season; an overgrown break is a false comfort.

## Gotchas
- Thinning without treating slash can raise fire hazard for several years.
- Mastication on hot days has started fires (blade strikes on rock) — follow fire-season
  restrictions on machinery.
- Over-opening a stand increases wind speed and grass/shrub growth on the floor.

## Verify
- [ ] Surface, ladder and canopy fuels recorded per stand, with method named.
- [ ] Target CBH and residual density written before marking.
- [ ] Every thinning has a planned slash treatment and date.
- [ ] Burn permits/windows and machinery restrictions checked.
- [ ] Post-treatment re-measurement shows lower surface load, higher CBH.
- [ ] Maintenance cycle for shrubs and fuel breaks scheduled.
