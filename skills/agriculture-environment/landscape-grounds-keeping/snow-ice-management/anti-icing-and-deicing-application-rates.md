# Anti-icing and de-icing: materials and rates

**Goal:** stop ice bonding to pavement with the least salt, so sites stay open and
safe without killing turf, corroding concrete and loading streams with chloride.

## 1. The principle
- **Anti-icing** (before the storm): put down a thin layer of liquid brine so snow and
  ice can't bond. Then a plough scrapes clean. Uses a fraction of the salt of de-icing.
- **De-icing** (after ice has bonded): melt through ice to break the bond. Takes far
  more material and time.
- **Plough first, then treat:** salt on 10 cm of snow just makes slush; scrape to bare
  pavement, then apply.

## 2. Pick material by pavement temperature
Read **pavement surface temperature** (infrared thermometer or vehicle sensor), not air
temperature — pavement can be several degrees different. Common practical working limits
(lower than these, the material works too slowly to be useful):

| Material | Practical lowest pavement temp (approx.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rock salt (NaCl) | about −9 °C (15 °F) | cheapest; slow when colder |
| Salt brine 23.3% | anti-icing above ~ −9 °C | the standard anti-icing liquid |
| Magnesium chloride | about −15 °C (5 °F) | liquid or flake; sticky residue |
| Calcium chloride | about −25 °C (−13 °F) | exothermic, fast; corrosive, attracts moisture |
| Potassium acetate / CMA | about −10 °C to −25 °C | less corrosive, pricier; near sensitive structures |
| Sand / grit | any | traction only, doesn't melt; clogs drains |

Figures vary by source — treat as **approximate, not confirmed**.

## 3. Rates (starting points — calibrate and adjust)
- **Anti-icing brine:** car-park guides commonly quote roughly 0.5–1.5 US gal per
  1,000 ft² (≈ 0.02–0.06 L/m²; 1 gal/1,000 ft² ≈ 0.04 L/m²). Apply 1–24 h ahead on dry pavement; don't apply when rain is
  forecast first (washes off) or on pavement colder than ~ −9 °C.
- **Granular salt de-icing on parking areas:** often ~1.5–4 lb per 1,000 ft²
  (≈ 7–20 g/m²) for light conditions, rising for colder/heavier events. Much municipal
  practice is far heavier than needed.
- **Pre-wet** granular salt with brine (~5–10% by weight) at the spinner: it sticks
  instead of bouncing off, and starts working faster.
A coffee mug (~350 g) of rock salt is enough for roughly a 20 ft driveway or ten
pavement squares in many smart-salting guides — useful as a sanity check for hand work.

## 4. Calibrate the spreader
1. Mark gate settings. With the spreader stationary, run it at each setting for a
   timed interval and catch output in a tarp/bucket; weigh it (kg/min).
2. Measure spread width and travel speed. `Rate (g/m²) = output (g/min) ÷ (speed m/min × width m)`.
3. Make a chart: setting vs g/m² at your normal speed. Tape it in the cab.
Without calibration, operators typically apply 2–4× what's needed.

## 5. Storm routine
1. **Before:** check forecast, pavement temp; brine the priority routes (entrances,
   ramps, steps, accessible routes, loading docks) if conditions suit.
2. **During:** plough in a set order (priority first), push snow downhill and away from
   drains, not onto pedestrian routes, accessible parking or sight lines; spot-treat.
3. **After:** final scrape, treat to bare pavement, watch for **refreeze** at dusk and from
   melt off snow piles — check again in the evening and early morning.
4. **Log** every visit: time, pavement temp, conditions, material, amount, areas.
   Logs are your defence in slip-and-fall claims.

## 6. Environment and property
- Salt kills turf and roadside plants, corrodes metal, spalls young concrete
  (< 1 year old) — avoid salting new concrete; use sand or acetates.
- Sweep up visible leftover granules after the event and reuse them.
- Store salt covered on an impermeable pad.

## Gotchas
- Brine applied on a heavy rain-to-snow forecast just washes off.
- Calcium chloride on pavement slicks if over-applied in humid conditions.
- Unit conversions are messy (US units dominate the literature): 1 lb/1,000 ft² ≈
  4.9 g/m². The rates above are commonly quoted starting points, **not confirmed for any
  site** — use your calibrated chart and local agency guidance.

- [ ] Spreader calibrated; chart in the cab.
- [ ] Priority routes ploughed and treated first.
