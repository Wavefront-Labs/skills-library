# Blood trailing and sign reading

**Goal:** find game by its sign, and — more importantly — recover every animal you hit,
by reading what the sign says and not rushing it.

> Draft. Hit-sign interpretation and waiting times are widely taught rules of thumb, not
> certainties. Tracking-dog use and night tracking (lights, firearms) are regulated in many
> places — check before you need it.

## 1. Reading sign
- **Tracks:** identify by shape and size (e.g. deer: two cloven toes, heart-shaped, pointed end
  forward; dewclaws print in soft ground or when running). Measure length/width; note
  stride and gait (walking prints overlap; bounding prints spaced and splayed).
- **Aging tracks:** crisp edges, no debris inside, moist exposed soil = fresh (hours). Rain
  pits inside the print, leaves on top, crumbled edges = older. Compare with a print you make
  yourself next to it, then check again later to calibrate for the day's conditions.
- **Scat:** shiny, moist, warm/soft = fresh; dull, dry crust = older. Shape changes with diet.
- **Beds:** flattened oval in vegetation/snow; melted snow or warm ground = just vacated.
- **Rubs and scrapes** (deer): rubbed bark/saplings and pawed ground under overhanging
  branches — show travel routes and breeding-season activity.
- **Trails:** used paths through cover; look for fresh hoof cuts, disturbed leaves, broken twigs.

## 2. At the shot: collect evidence
- Mark where you shot from and where the animal stood (look through the scope/at landmarks
  again before moving). Note direction of last sight and sound.
- Find the arrow or the hit site: hair (colour/length tells area — e.g. long hollow brown
  back hair vs white belly hair), blood, bone fragments, stomach content.
- **Blood clues** (rules of thumb):
  - Bright pink/red, frothy with bubbles → **lung**. Wait ~30 min (gun) to ~1 h (bow), then follow.
  - Dark red, no bubbles, plenty → **liver** (or muscle/major vessel). Wait ~2–4 h.
  - Green/brown matter, gut smell, sparse blood → **paunch/gut**. Wait many hours (often 6–12,
    overnight in cool weather).
  - Little blood, bright red, animal ran strongly → possible **muscle/leg** hit: may need a dog
    soon or a long follow-up.
- If it's raining and the trail will wash out, weigh the risk of bumping the animal against
  losing sign — many hunters mark the trail start and wait anyway for gut hits.

## 3. Following the trail
1. Go quietly with one or two people; one follows sign, one watches ahead for the animal.
2. **Flag the last blood** with tape at each find; look back to see the line of travel.
3. Check **both sides of the trail** and higher up on vegetation (wiping height shows the wound
   height and side).
4. Blood often increases after the animal runs; it can stop when a wound plugs with fat.
5. Animals hit hard often head downhill, toward water or thick cover.
6. Approach any downed animal from behind/uphill and confirm death (eye test).

## 4. When the trail ends
- Go back to the last blood and mark it boldly. **Grid search** outward in widening loops or
  parallel lines a few metres apart, checking thick cover, creek banks and blowdowns.
- At night, a bright white or specialised red-blood-enhancing light helps; peroxide fizzes on
  blood (useful to confirm specks).
- **Call a leash-tracking dog** handler early (where legal) — dogs recover many animals that
  humans give up on, and work best when the trail hasn't been trampled.

## Gotchas
- Following too soon is the single biggest cause of lost game.
- Trampling the trail with a group destroys sign for a dog.
- "No blood" doesn't mean a miss — check the shot site thoroughly and grid-search anyway.

## Verify
- [ ] Shot site, animal position and direction of travel marked before moving.
- [ ] Hit sign collected (hair, blood type, arrow) and waiting time chosen from it.
- [ ] Trail flagged at each blood find; last blood marked bold.
- [ ] Grid search done when trail ended; dog handler called where legal.
- [ ] Outcome recorded (found / lost) for honest feedback on shot choice.
