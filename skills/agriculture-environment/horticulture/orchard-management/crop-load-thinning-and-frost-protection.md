# Crop-load thinning and frost protection

**Goal:** the right number of fruit per tree — big enough to sell, not so many the tree
skips next year — and blossom that survives spring frosts.

> Draft. Spacing and fruit numbers are for apples on dwarfing rootstocks; stone fruit
> differs. Chemical thinners are product- and country-registered: use only labelled products
> and local extension timing models.

## 1. Pollination first
- Most apples, pears, sweet cherries (many), plums need a **compatible pollinizer variety**
  flowering at the same time, within ~15–30 m. Check pollination groups/S-allele charts.
- Common provision: 1 pollinizer per ~8–10 trees, or crab apples in rows.
- Honey bees: ~2–5 hives/ha for apples, placed at ~5–10% bloom. Don't spray insecticides
  in bloom; mow flowering weeds in the row before introducing bees (or they forage there).

## 2. Set a target crop load
- Decide target fruit size class from the market, then the fruit number per tree:
  e.g. a mature tall-spindle apple tree might carry ~80–150 fruit; express also as
  **fruit per cm² trunk cross-sectional area (TCSA)** — commonly ~4–8 fruit/cm² for apples.
- Count blossom clusters on sample trees before bloom to know how much to remove.

## 3. Thinning — earlier is better
Fruit size is set largely by cell division in the first ~4–6 weeks after bloom: excess
fruit competing then costs size even if removed later.
- **Blossom thinning** (bloom stage): fastest effect; risky if frost later reduces set.
- **Chemical fruitlet thinning** (apples): commonly applied at ~10–20 mm king-fruit
  diameter, using registered products (e.g. based on NAA, 6-BA, carbaryl where still legal,
  metamitron in Europe). Warm, cloudy weather increases thinner activity; use a
  carbohydrate model where available.
- **Hand thinning** after June drop for the final adjustment: leave **one fruit per
  cluster** (usually the king fruit if well formed), ~15–20 cm apart along the branch;
  remove damaged, misshapen and small fruit first. Twist off, don't pull the spur.

## 4. Biennial bearing
Heavy crops suppress next year's flower bud initiation (in apples, within ~4–6 weeks of
bloom). Thinning early and hard in the "on" year is the main fix; varieties like
'Honeycrisp', 'Fuji', 'Elstar' are prone.

## 5. Frost protection
**Critical temperatures** fall as buds open: roughly for apple at full bloom ~−2.2 °C
kills ~10%, ~−3.9 °C ~90%; tight cluster can tolerate a few degrees colder. Use extension
tables per crop and bud stage — and measure at bud height, not at a weather station.
Methods:
- **Site selection:** slopes with cold-air drainage, never in a frost hollow.
- **Wind machines (frost fans):** mix the warmer inversion layer (several degrees warmer
  ~10–15 m up) down into the orchard; work only on clear, calm **radiation frosts** with a
  strong inversion — useless in windy advective freezes.
- **Overhead sprinkling:** latent heat of freezing keeps tissue near 0 °C. Must start before
  bud temperature reaches critical (allow for evaporative cooling at low dew points: start
  when wet-bulb nears ~0 to +1 °C) and run **until ice melts** after sunrise. Stopping early
  causes more damage than doing nothing. Needs ~2.5–4 mm/h of water.
- Orchard floor: mown short, firm and moist bare soil stores and releases more heat than tall grass.
- Frost alarm thermometers in the coldest blocks.

## Gotchas
- Thinning late (after ~30 days past bloom) mostly saves next year's bloom, not this year's size.
- Overhead irrigation on weak limbs: ice load breaks branches.
- Hives brought in too early forage on dandelions; too late miss king bloom.

## Verify
- [ ] Pollinizer and hives in place at the right density and bloom stage.
- [ ] Target fruit number per tree/TCSA written before thinning.
- [ ] Thinning done by ~30 days after bloom; final hand-thin to single fruit per cluster.
- [ ] Critical-temperature table for your crop at hand; bud-height thermometers in cold spots.
- [ ] Frost method matches frost type; sprinklers run until ice is melting.
