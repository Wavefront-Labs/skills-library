# Marker training, shaping and reinforcement schedules

**Goal:** be able to teach *any* behaviour to *any* animal that will work for a reward,
with a method that gives clear information and no fallout. The same mechanics are used
from pet dogs to dolphins.

## 1. The marker (bridge)
- A **marker** — click, a short word ("yes"), a whistle — means "that exact moment
  earned a reward". It bridges the gap between the behaviour and the food.
- **Charge it**: mark → reward, ~15–30 repetitions, no behaviour required. Test: the
  animal orients toward you when it hears it.
- Mark **during** the behaviour, not after; delivery can come a second or two later.
- One mark = one reward, always. Don't use the marker to get attention.

## 2. Three ways to get the behaviour
- **Capturing**: wait for it to happen (a dog lying down), mark, reward. Great for
  natural behaviours.
- **Luring**: food guides the body (nose follows the treat into a down). Fast, but
  **fade the lure within ~3–6 reps** (move to an empty hand gesture), or it becomes a
  bribe the animal waits for.
- **Shaping** (successive approximations): reward small steps toward the final
  behaviour — look at the box → step toward → touch → put a foot in → two feet.
- **Targeting**: teach the animal to touch a target (hand, stick, ball) with nose or
  foot; then move the target to position the animal. Central to zoo and husbandry work.

## 3. Shaping rules
1. Write the final behaviour and the steps before you start.
2. **Raise criteria one at a time** (e.g. first duration, then distance).
3. Move up when the animal gets ~**80% success** at the current step; drop back if it
   fails 2–3 times in a row.
4. Keep the **rate of reinforcement high** — 10–20 rewards per minute early on.
   Long gaps = frustration.
5. Sessions short: ~2–5 minutes; end on a success.
6. **Reward placement** sets up the next repetition: toss food away to reset a
   go-to-mat behaviour; feed in position to build duration.

## 4. Adding the cue
- Add the cue **only once the behaviour is fluent** and you'd bet money on the next rep.
  Say the cue just before the behaviour happens.
- **Cue transfer**: new cue → old cue → behaviour → reward; drop the old cue gradually.
- **Stimulus control** = the behaviour happens when cued, doesn't happen when not cued,
  doesn't happen to another cue, and no other behaviour occurs to this cue. Test all four.

## 5. Schedules — from learning to maintenance
- **Continuous** (every correct rep rewarded) while learning.
- Then a **variable** schedule — reward some reps, choosing the better ones (differential
  reinforcement). Variable schedules keep behaviour resistant to extinction.
- Never thin the schedule and raise difficulty at the same time.
- In real-life use, reinforce with life rewards (door opens, release to play).

## 6. Chaining
Long sequences are taught as chains: each behaviour's cue is reinforced by the next
behaviour. **Back-chain** (train the last link first) so the animal is always working
toward the most familiar part.

## Gotchas
- Poor timing is the #1 failure — record yourself on video and watch marker timing.
- "Poisoned" cues: if a cue predicts something unpleasant, the animal hesitates — rename.
- Reward value must match difficulty; low-value food for hard tasks = refusals.
- Satiation and fatigue look like "stubbornness".
- An animal that offers random behaviours is not misbehaving — it's learning to shape;
  stay calm and keep criteria clear.

## Verify
- [ ] The animal orients to the marker sound in a new room.
- [ ] A written shaping plan exists with criteria per step.
- [ ] Your rate of reinforcement was ≥10/min in the last session (count on video).
- [ ] The finished behaviour passes all four stimulus-control tests.
