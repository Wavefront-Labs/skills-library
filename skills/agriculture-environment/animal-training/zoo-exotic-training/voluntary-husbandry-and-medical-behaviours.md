# Voluntary husbandry and medical behaviours in zoo animals

**Goal:** an animal that walks into a crate, presents a foot, opens its mouth or holds
still for an injection — voluntarily — so the vet doesn't have to anaesthetise or
restrain it. Assumes marker training basics (see
marker-shaping-and-reinforcement-schedules). Work under your institution's safety
protocols; dangerous species are trained only through **protected contact**.

## 1. Safety and setup
- **Protected contact**: a barrier (mesh, bars, training wall with ports) is always
  between keeper and animal. Free contact with dangerous species is now uncommon and
  usually against policy.
- Two people for high-risk species: the trainer and a spotter/safety person.
- Design the training wall: ports sized to present one body part, sleeve ports for
  blood draws, a stable stationing target.
- Approval: the behaviour plan is signed off by curators/vets before starting.

## 2. Foundation behaviours
- **Station**: animal goes to and stays at a specific spot (target on the mesh, a mat).
  This is the base for everything else and lets several animals be trained at once.
- **Target**: nose or body part to a target pole; used to position the animal.
- **Shift**: move between enclosures on cue — essential for cleaning and safety.
- **Recall** to the training area.
- **Start-button behaviour**: the animal offers a behaviour (e.g. chin on a rest) that
  means "I'm ready, go ahead". If it removes the chin, you **stop**. This gives the animal
  control and increases cooperation.

## 3. Medical behaviours — shaping plan example: voluntary injection
1. Station, present the hip/shoulder against the mesh.
2. Touch with a finger → touch with the blunt syringe cap → hold pressure longer.
3. Pinch the skin; mimic the injection with a blunt object or a fingernail flick.
4. Use a real needle hub cap pressed firmly, then a fine needle with no drug.
5. Real injection, during a routine session, with high-value rewards.
Each step must be calm and repeated many times before progressing. Same logic for:
mouth open (dental checks), eye drops, ultrasound gel and probe, foot presentation for
trimming (elephants, rhino), **voluntary blood draw** from tail or arm via a sleeve.

## 4. Crate and transport training
Crate in the enclosure for weeks → food near → inside → door moves → door closes briefly
→ crate lifted slightly. Desensitise to noise of the forklift/truck. Saves a sedation event
and stress during moves.

## 5. Documentation
- Behaviour plans per animal with steps and criteria.
- Session records: date, trainer, step, responses, reinforcement, issues.
- Keep a **behaviour inventory** of what each animal can do; vets use it to plan procedures.
- Hand-over training: behaviours must transfer across keepers, not depend on one person.

## Gotchas
- Never "sneak" an unpleasant procedure after the animal stations — break that trust and
  the behaviour is lost.
- Social animals: separate training to avoid competition, or use station training for groups.
- Hormonal seasons (musth, breeding) change motivation and safety; adjust or pause.
- Food rewards come from the daily diet allowance — coordinate with nutrition staff to avoid
  overfeeding.
- Species specifics (e.g. elephant foot work, cetacean husbandry) need specialist mentors.

## Verify
- [ ] Animal stations and shifts on cue reliably, with different keepers.
- [ ] Medical behaviour plan approved by vet/curator, steps written.
- [ ] Animal can withdraw (start-button released) and session stops.
- [ ] Vet completed a real procedure without restraint or sedation.
