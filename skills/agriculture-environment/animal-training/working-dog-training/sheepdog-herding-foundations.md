# Sheepdog herding foundations — first sheep to a useful farm dog

**Goal:** turn a keen young border collie (or kelpie, etc.) into a dog that gathers,
fetches and stops on command without gripping or scattering stock. Herding is a
working-dog discipline; detection, protection and gun-dog work follow different
pathways and are not covered here. Learn with an experienced handler if you can — the
timing is physical and hard to get from text.

## 1. Before sheep
- Age: most start seeing sheep at **~6–12 months**, when the dog has the physical
  maturity and some steadiness; individual dogs vary.
- Pre-teach on the lawn: name, a **recall**, "lie down"/stand, and "that'll do" (stop
  work, come to me). These are what you'll lean on when instinct is in full flow.
- **Stock**: a small group (~5–10) of quiet, **dog-broke** sheep that flock together.
  Wild or flighty stock, or a single sheep, produce panic and gripping.

## 2. Round pen: find balance
- Use a round pen ~15–25 m across, sheep in the middle, you on the far side.
- Instinct puts the dog at the **balance point** — opposite you, holding the sheep to
  you. Walk around; the dog should move to keep the sheep between you.
- Hold a long stick, crook or a flag to block the dog's path and widen it; use it as an
  extension of your arm, never to hit.
- Reward the dog by letting it keep working; the biggest correction is "that'll do" and
  leaving the sheep.
- If the dog dives in or grips, step in, block, slow everything down, shorten sessions.
  Some gripping in very young dogs is normal; persistent grip needs an experienced eye.

## 3. Core commands (traditional, UK/US convention)
| Command | Meaning | Common whistle |
|---|---|---|
| **Come-bye** | flank clockwise around the stock | usually a rising phrase |
| **Away (to me)** | flank anticlockwise | usually a falling phrase |
| **Lie down / stand** | stop | single long blast |
| **Walk up** | approach the stock straight | short pips |
| **That'll do** | quit, come to handler | — |
Teach flanks by stepping the way you want the dog to go *the other side* of the sheep,
saying the word as it moves the correct way. Whistles vary per handler — pick one set
and never change it. Teach whistles by pairing: voice then whistle, later whistle alone.

## 4. Building the work (in open field)
1. **Short outrun**: leave the dog at your feet, sheep 30–50 m away. Send on a flank; the
   dog should run a wide **pear shape**, not straight at the sheep. If it cuts in, walk
   toward the dog's line, or shorten the distance.
2. **Lift**: the dog stops (or slows) at the balance point behind the sheep before
   moving them — gentle, so they don't bolt.
3. **Fetch**: dog walks up to bring sheep straight to you; you walk backwards. Stop the
   dog if pressure gets too high.
4. **Drive**: the dog pushes stock *away* from you. This is against its instinct; start
   by walking alongside the dog, then gradually drop back.
5. Increase distance in small steps over months.

## Gotchas
- Too much repetition burns out keen young dogs; 10–15 minutes is plenty.
- Overusing "lie down" makes a sticky dog that won't move; underusing it makes a
  dog that runs the stock.
- Sheep's welfare comes first: heat, heavily pregnant ewes and lambs are off-limits for
  training.
- Commands and whistle phrasing here are conventions, not standards; trial rules
  (ISDS, USBCHA and others) define course work.

## Verify
- [ ] Dog stops reliably on voice and whistle with sheep moving.
- [ ] Dog flanks wide both directions on the correct words.
- [ ] Outrun is pear-shaped at your current distance with a quiet lift.
- [ ] "That'll do" ends the session with the dog returning to you.
