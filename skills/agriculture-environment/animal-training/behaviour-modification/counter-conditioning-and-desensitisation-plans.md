# Counter-conditioning and desensitisation (DS/CC) plans

**Goal:** change how an animal *feels* about a trigger — other dogs, strangers,
being alone, handling — so the problem behaviour stops being needed. Written for dogs;
the method is the same for cats and horses. **Safety first:** bites to people, serious
fights, or severe separation distress → involve a vet and a qualified behaviour
professional (veterinary behaviourist or certified behaviour consultant).

## 1. Rule out medical causes
Pain (joints, teeth, ears, gut), thyroid disease and sensory loss commonly underlie
sudden aggression or fear. **Vet check first**, especially in older animals or sudden
changes. Some cases need medication alongside training — that's a vet's call.

## 2. Functional assessment (ABC)
For each incident, write:
- **A**ntecedent — what happened just before (where, who, distance, time of day)?
- **B**ehaviour — described objectively ("stiffens, stares, barks, lunges"), not "is dominant".
- **C**onsequence — what happened next (trigger left, owner pulled away, got the bone)?
The consequence usually reveals the **function**: increase distance from a scary thing,
keep a resource, get attention. Log a week of incidents. Note **body language** before
the explosion: freezing, hard stare, lip lick, yawning, whale eye, tail tucked.

## 3. Management — stop practising
Every rehearsal strengthens the behaviour. Before training:
- Change walk times/routes, use visual barriers, gates, a muzzle (muzzle-trained
  positively) where bite risk exists.
- Separation cases: arrange so the dog is not left alone beyond its tolerance while training.
- Remove the triggering resource situations (feed in separate rooms for guarding).

## 4. Find the threshold
**Under threshold** = the animal notices the trigger but can still eat, respond to its
name and move loosely. Find the distance/intensity where that's true (e.g. another dog
at 30 m). That's where training happens. Above threshold, no learning occurs.

## 5. Desensitisation + counter-conditioning
- **DS**: present the trigger at a very low intensity (far, quiet, brief), raising it in
  tiny steps over sessions.
- **CC**: pair trigger with something great: **trigger appears → food flows; trigger
  goes → food stops**. Order matters — trigger first, then food.
- After repetitions, look for the **conditioned emotional response**: the dog sees the
  trigger and looks to you expectantly. Then (and only then) shorten distance slightly.
- Useful structured protocols: "Look at that" (mark looking at the trigger),
  "engage–disengage", BAT-style set-ups with a decoy dog at a controlled distance.
- Separation anxiety: graded absences starting below the time the dog shows stress
  (could be seconds), using a camera to watch; never leave longer than the dog can handle.

## 6. Plan and log
A one-page plan: diagnosis, triggers, management rules, current threshold, the next
step, criteria to move on, emergency U-turn routine. A log per session: trigger, distance,
dog's response (0–3 stress scale), notes. Progress graphs make plateaus obvious.

## Gotchas
- **Trigger stacking**: several mild stressors (vet visit, noisy street, poor sleep)
  add up; the dog explodes at something it "usually tolerates". Plan rest days after hard days.
- Punishment (prong collars, shock, scruffing) suppresses warnings without changing the
  emotion — you get a dog that bites "without warning".
- Feeding the dog while it is over threshold doesn't reward fear — but it won't work either;
  move further away.
- Progress is not linear; expect setbacks.

## Verify
- [ ] Vet check done and documented before starting.
- [ ] ABC log of at least a week with function identified.
- [ ] Management in place so the behaviour is no longer being rehearsed.
- [ ] Session log shows threshold distance shrinking over weeks.
