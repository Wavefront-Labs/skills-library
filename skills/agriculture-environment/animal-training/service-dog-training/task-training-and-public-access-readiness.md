# Service-dog task training and public-access readiness

**Goal:** a dog that performs specific tasks that mitigate a person's disability, and
behaves invisibly in public. Guide-dog work (route training, intelligent
disobedience) is usually done by specialist schools and is outlined only. **Legal
definitions differ by country** — e.g. under the US ADA a service dog must be trained to
perform tasks for a person with a disability, with no certification required; the UK and
EU rely more on accredited organisations (ADI/ADUK). Check your jurisdiction.

## 1. Selection — most candidates wash out
- Programmes typically lose a large share of dogs (figures of 50% or more are often
  quoted) — mostly for temperament or health.
- Screen for: quick recovery from startle (< a few seconds), social but not
  over-excited with strangers, low prey/dog reactivity, food- and toy-motivated, physically
  sound (hip/elbow scores for mobility work).
- Re-assess at ~6, 12 and 18 months; owner-trainers should have a plan B for a dog that
  doesn't make it.

## 2. Foundation (puppy to ~12 months)
- Marker training basics (see marker-shaping-and-reinforcement-schedules): settle on a mat,
  loose-lead walking, recall, leave it, wait at doors, tuck under a chair/table.
- **Socialisation in public** with a training/in-training vest where allowed: shops
  (pet-friendly first), transport, lifts, crowds, children, floors of every type,
  restaurants. Short, successful visits; leave before the dog is overwhelmed.
- Toilet on cue, on a range of surfaces, before entering buildings.

## 3. Task training
Choose tasks from the handler's actual needs, then break each into steps.
- **Retrieve** (dropped items, phone, medication): shape hold → take from hand →
  pick up from floor → carry → deliver to hand. Train the **end first** (backchaining):
  delivery-to-hand is the most rehearsed piece.
- **Alerts** (hearing dogs, medical alert): teach a clear, unmistakable **alert
  behaviour** first (nose-nudge, paw, or chin rest) on a cue, then attach it to the
  trigger (doorbell, alarm, a scent sample for medical alert). Scent work for diabetic
  or seizure alert has variable evidence; programmes using it collect samples under
  medical guidance — treat home claims cautiously.
- **Deep pressure therapy (DPT)**: "lap" or "over" — dog lies across the handler on cue,
  stays until released.
- **Mobility**: bracing/counterbalance requires a suitable size, health clearances and a
  rigid brace harness fitted by a professional; **never** train bracing on a young dog
  or by pulling on a collar/vest.
- **Interrupt** (psychiatric tasks): dog trained to nudge/paw when the handler shows a
  cued self-harm or dissociation behaviour; practise on cue first.

## 4. Proofing to public-access standard
Use a published test as the target — e.g. the ADI public access test or a CGC-type
evaluation as a minimum. Typical criteria:
- Calm, controlled entry through doors; no pulling.
- Ignores food on the floor, other dogs, people trying to pet.
- Down-stay under a table for a meal (~30–60 min) without fuss.
- No reaction to dropped objects, trolleys, loud noise; recovers fast.
- Toilet only on cue; no sniffing shelves.
- Performs at least one task on the spot, in public, first time.

## Gotchas
- A dog that's cute and friendly in public but solicits attention fails access standards.
- Vests don't make a service dog; behaviour does. Misrepresenting a pet as a service dog
  is an offence in many places.
- Over-working: build rest days, play and off-duty time.
- Task behaviours must be maintained: re-practise weekly, or they fade.

## Verify
- [ ] Dog passes a mock public-access test in three new venues.
- [ ] Each task is performed on cue, first time, ≥90% across a week's log.
- [ ] The handler (if not the trainer) can cue and reinforce every task themselves.
- [ ] You know your jurisdiction's legal definition and access rules.
