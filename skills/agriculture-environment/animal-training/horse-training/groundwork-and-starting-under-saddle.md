# Groundwork and starting a horse under saddle

**Goal:** a young horse that leads, yields, accepts tack and carries a rider calmly
at walk, trot and canter — without fights. Starting horses is risky; do it with an
experienced helper, a hat, gloves and boots, and in a safe enclosed area. If you've
never started one, ride horses started by others first.

## 1. Readiness
- Physical maturity: many trainers back at **~3–4 years**, with light work only; growth
  plates close late. Breed and individual vary.
- Teeth checked (wolf teeth?), feet trimmed, back and saddle fit checked by a fitter.
- The horse already leads, ties, picks up feet and loads — if not, start there.

## 2. The principle: pressure and release
The horse learns from the **release** — the moment pressure stops is the reward.
- Apply the lightest pressure; increase gradually until the horse tries the right
  answer; **release within ~1–2 seconds** of the try.
- Late release teaches the wrong thing. Constant pressure teaches the horse to ignore it.
- Equitation-science principle: one signal = one response; don't use the same cue for
  two things.

## 3. Groundwork set
- **Lead**: walk on, halt, back up from a light rope signal; horse stays a step behind
  your shoulder.
- **Yield hindquarters / forequarters**: pressure on flank/shoulder → horse steps across.
- **Head down** from light poll pressure (calming, useful for bridling).
- **Desensitise**: rope over body and legs, saddle pad, plastic bag on a stick — approach
  and retreat, stopping before the horse is alarmed (*approach-retreat* builds tolerance).

## 4. Tack and longe
1. Roller/surcingle, then the saddle with stirrups tied up. Girth snugly in stages;
   walk a step between tightening.
2. **Lungeing** in a round pen or on a 15–20 m circle: walk/trot/halt on voice. Keep
   sessions short (~10–20 min), both directions. Then let stirrups down so they touch the sides.
3. Bridle with a mild snaffle; let the horse mouth it while doing groundwork.
4. **Long-reining**: driving from behind teaches stop, go and turn from rein pressure
   before a rider is on board. Mind the hind legs — stay out of kicking range.

## 5. Backing
1. Helper at the head, you on a mounting block. Lean over the saddle, weight on and off,
   stroking both sides. Repeat until the horse is relaxed and dozy.
2. Swing the leg over, sit lightly, keep low, dismount. Repeat over days.
3. Helper leads at walk with you on board; you start to add rein and leg cues the horse
   learned in long-reining.
4. Off the lead: walk, halt and turns in the enclosed area. Then trot. First canter often
   comes in week 2–4 on a confident horse, sometimes later.
5. Hack out with a calm escort horse early — straight lines help forward impulsion.

## Typical timescale
Many trainers spend ~4–8 weeks from groundwork to steady walk-trot-canter. Rushing the
horse that is tense costs more time later.

## Gotchas
- Fear signs: high head, white eye, tight mouth, tail clamped, snorting, freezing — back
  off a step before it becomes a bolt or buck.
- Pain causes many "behaviour" problems (saddle fit, teeth, ulcers, back) — rule it out
  first when a horse resists.
- Never tie a horse up by the bridle, and never tie in a way you can't release fast.
- Problem horses (rearing, napping) need an experienced professional.

## Verify
- [ ] The horse leads, halts, backs and yields from a light cue with no rope tension.
- [ ] Tacks up quietly, lunges both ways at walk and trot on voice.
- [ ] Stops and turns from long-reins before the first sit.
- [ ] Walks, trots and halts under rider in the arena, unled, relaxed.
