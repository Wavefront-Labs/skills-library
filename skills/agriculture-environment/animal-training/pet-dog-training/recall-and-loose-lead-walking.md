# Recall and loose-lead walking — the two cues that matter most

**Goal:** a dog that turns and runs to you when called, and walks on a slack lead —
built with rewards, practised in small steps, kept reliable for years.

## 0. Set up once
- **Marker word** ("yes") or a clicker: say it the instant the dog does the right
  thing, then deliver a treat within ~1–2 s. Charge it: 20 × "yes" → treat, no
  behaviour asked, in a quiet room.
- **Reward ladder**: kibble (easy indoors) → cheese/sausage/chicken (outdoors, distractions)
  → a toy or the chance to go sniff (real-world reward). Tiny pieces, pea-sized.
- Equipment: a flat collar or a well-fitting Y-front harness; a 5–10 m **long line**
  (attach to the harness, never a collar, for recall work). Avoid retractable leads for
  training — they teach pulling.

## 1. Recall
1. **Pick a fresh cue** ("here!", or a whistle — two pips) if "come" has been used and
   ignored for months; a poisoned cue is easier to replace than repair.
2. Indoors, dog a metre away: say the cue once in a happy voice, step back, mark as the
   dog reaches you, treat *at your body*, touch the collar briefly (so collar-grab
   isn't a punisher later), then release: "go play".
3. Grow **one variable at a time** — distance, then distraction, then duration of
   ignoring something. Get ~8–9 successes in 10 before moving on.
4. Outside on the long line: call when the dog is *not* busy first. If they don't come,
   don't repeat the cue — reel gently or move away, then reward when they arrive.
5. Recall then release ~4 times out of 5, so coming back doesn't always mean the fun ends.
6. **Emergency recall**: a separate cue used rarely, always paid with something huge
   (a handful of chicken, a game). Practise it a few times a week in easy settings.

## 2. Loose-lead walking
1. Decide where the reward zone is (your left or right side).
2. Indoors: walk; every time the dog is next to you with a slack lead, mark and feed at
   your seam. Dozens of rewards per minute at first.
3. **Lead goes tight → you stop.** Wait still until the lead slackens (or lure back),
   mark, feed at your side, carry on. Or turn and walk the other way, rewarding as the
   dog catches up. Pulling must never get the dog where it's going.
4. Gradually space out rewards: every step → every 3–5 steps → occasional.
5. Use a **"go sniff"** release as a reward for a few good metres of walking.
6. When you can't train (late for work), use a front-clip harness — a management tool
   — rather than letting pulling pay.

## Common mistakes
- Calling a dog to end fun, bathe it or tell it off → recall gets slower each time.
- Repeating the cue ("come, come, COME") teaches that the first three don't count.
- Chasing the dog — they think it's a game. Run *away* instead.
- Training too long: 3–5 minute sessions, several a day, beat one 30-minute slog.
- Expecting adolescent dogs (~6–18 months) not to regress — they do; go back a step.
- Punishing a dog that eventually comes back.

## Gotchas
- Reliability isn't proven until the cue works with the dog's top distraction
  (squirrel, other dog). Until then, long line in unfenced areas.
- Leash laws and protected wildlife areas still apply.
- If pulling or lunging is fear- or reactivity-driven (barking at dogs), that is a
  behaviour-modification case — see counter-conditioning-and-desensitisation-plans.

## Verify
- [ ] The dog responds to the recall cue first time, 9/10, at 10 m in a park on the long line.
- [ ] The dog can walk 50 m on a slack lead past a mild distraction.
- [ ] You've not used the recall cue for anything unpleasant this week.
- [ ] A training log shows distance/distraction steps progressed one at a time.
