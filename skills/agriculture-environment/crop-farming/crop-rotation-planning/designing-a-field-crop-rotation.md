# Designing a field-crop rotation

**Goal:** a multi-year sequence per field that keeps disease and weeds below the
level where they cost yield, builds fertility, spreads workload and market risk —
and still pays. A rotation is a set of constraints solved together, not a habit.

## 1. Gather field history (per paddock, 4–6 years back)
Crop and variety, yield, herbicides used (by **mode-of-action group**), fertiliser,
disease and weed problems seen, soil tests (pH, P, K, organic carbon), and any
herbicide plant-back restrictions still running. Put it in a spreadsheet: one row per
field, one column per year.

## 2. Constraints to encode
**Disease breaks** (illustrative intervals commonly advised — confirm with local
agronomy, they vary by region and pathogen pressure):
- Cereal root/crown diseases (take-all, crown rot): one non-host year (oilseed or
  pulse, or grass-free fallow) clears much of take-all; crown rot often needs longer.
- Canola/oilseed rape: commonly ≥ 3–4 years between canola crops (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) on the same field
  for blackleg/phoma and clubroot management; clubroot fields often much longer.
- Pulses (peas, lentils, chickpeas, faba beans): commonly 3–5+ years between the same (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
  pulse for root rots and ascochyta.
- Sclerotinia hosts span canola, pulses, sunflower and soybeans — count them together.
- Corn–soybean systems: soybean cyst nematode and corn rootworm pressure drive breaks.

**Weeds:**
- Alternate crop types (winter/spring, broadleaf/grass, competitive/non-competitive)
  so the same weeds don't get the same window every year.
- Rotate **herbicide groups**, and include at least one non-chemical tactic year
  (hay or silage cut before weed seed set, crop topping, harvest weed seed control).
- Note plant-back periods (e.g. some residual sulfonylureas and imidazolinones restrict
  following crops for months to years, worse on high-pH or dry soils) — read labels.

**Nutrition:**
- Legumes add nitrogen for the following crop (a commonly used rough credit is tens of
  kg N/ha; it varies widely with biomass and whether residues are removed). Put the
  most N-hungry crop after them.
- Deep-rooted crops after shallow-rooted ones can use subsoil water and N.

**Practical:**
- Workload and machinery: avoid every field needing sowing in the same week.
- Market and storage: on-farm storage, contracts, delivery distance.
- Soil: acid-sensitive crops (lucerne, many pulses) need limed fields; schedule lime ahead.

## 3. Build the sequence
1. Start with the most constrained crop (often canola or a pulse). Place it where its
   break interval is met and soil suits it.
2. Place the high-value/high-N cereal after the legume/break crop.
3. Fill with cereals while keeping the cereal run short (often ≤ 2 years in a row (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked) in
   disease-prone areas).
4. Check herbicide group sequence per field; flag repeats.
5. Balance areas so each crop's whole-farm area is similar each year (smooths workload
   and income). A **"rotation wheel"**: if the rotation is 4 years, split the farm into 4
   similar-sized blocks, each one a year offset.

Example 4-year winter-cropping sequence (one of many): canola → wheat → pulse (lentil
or field pea) → barley. Example temperate mixed: winter wheat → spring beans → winter
wheat → oilseed rape → spring barley (+ cover crop).

## 4. Test it
- **Gross margin per field per year** (yield × price − variable costs) using
  conservative yields; compare to the continuous-cereal alternative over the full cycle,
  including the yield bounce the cereal gets after a break (often 10–20%+ in trials, (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)
  site-dependent).
- Stress test: a dry year, a price collapse in the break crop, a disease flare.
- Keep a fallback (e.g. switch canola to barley if the sowing break comes too late).

## Gotchas
- Volunteer plants (volunteer canola in wheat, cereal volunteers in pulses) break your
  disease break — control them.
- Grass weeds in pulses carry cereal disease through the "break".
- A rotation plan written once and never reviewed against what happened isn't one;
  update yearly.

## Verify
- [ ] Every field has a history row with herbicide groups.
- [ ] Break intervals for canola, pulses and cereal diseases met per field.
- [ ] No field gets the same herbicide group on the same weeds in consecutive years without a non-chemical tactic.
- [ ] Plant-back periods checked for every planned crop.
- [ ] Gross margin over the whole cycle compares favourably and a fallback exists.
