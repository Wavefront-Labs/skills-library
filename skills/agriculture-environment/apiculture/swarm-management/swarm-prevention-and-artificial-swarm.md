# Swarm prevention and control

**Goal:** don't lose half your bees (and your honey crop) to a swarm. Swarming is how
colonies reproduce; you can delay it with space and young queens, and control it with a
planned split.

## 1. Prevention: reduce the urge
- **Room**: give colonies extra space by adding hive bodies or supers in spring or early
  summer before they need it. Southern US beekeepers often add a super when six combs of
  a 10-frame honey super are full or nearly full (Mississippi State Extension).
- **Queen age**: colonies with queens more than a year old are more likely to swarm than
  those with young queens; the same guide recommends requeening every 1 or 2 years.
- **Inspect weekly** in swarm season (April and May in Mississippi; adjust to your
  climate).

## 2. Read the queen cells
- **Swarm cells** are commonly on or near the bottom bars of the combs in the upper brood
  chamber(s). To check quickly, tip back the top brood chamber(s) and look up between the
  frames.
- **Supersedure cells** generally occur on the face of the comb.
- Once the bees cap a queen cell, they are committed to swarming.
- Location alone is a guide, not proof; look at the whole colony (queen, eggs, space).

## 3. Control
- **Cutting out queen cells alone seldom prevents swarming**; it only delays it because the
  bees usually build more cells within a few days. If you destroy cells, re-check in 7 to
  10 days for new ones (the Demaree method is described with this 7 to 10 day return).
- Splits are done in late March or early April in the Mississippi guide. Move a split
  with a new queen at least 3 miles away so the foragers do not drift back; nucleus
  colonies can be made by removing four to six frames of brood with adhering bees from
  strong colonies.
- A double screen board lets you place the old queen with three to five frames of
  unsealed brood in the bottom brood chamber; reunite the two units after the swarming
  season by removing the screen.
- For the Pagden artificial swarm, walk-away split and other methods, follow a full
  method write-up from your beekeeping association or extension service; this skill does
  not give their steps.

## 4. Catching a swarm
- Wear a veil anyway. Treat swarms as unknown disease status and keep them apart from
  your other colonies until you have checked them.
- Swarms on buildings or in chimneys need a professional removal.

## Gotchas
- Destroying all queen cells without making a split often fails: bees build more, or you
  leave the colony queenless after the queen has already left.
- Casts (secondary swarms with virgin queens) can follow the prime swarm.

## Verify
- [ ] Inspections every week through swarm season, cells recorded.
- [ ] You can name each queen cell type you found and why.
- [ ] After a split, both units have a laying queen a few weeks later.
- [ ] No swarms lost from the apiary this season (or causes noted).
