# Gillnet rigging, setting and soak management

**Goal:** catch the target size of fish in a static net, keep them fresh, and avoid
marine mammal and seabird bycatch and lost gear. Covers bottom-set gillnets and
trammels at small-boat and inland scale. Mesh sizes, soak limits, net lengths,
pingers and marking are set by **local regulations** — check before rigging.

## 1. Choosing the net
- **Mesh size** selects fish size: fish are caught by the gills just behind the head,
  so smaller fish pass through and much bigger ones bounce off. A common approach is to
  test a few mesh sizes and keep the one whose catch matches your legal/market size.
- **Twine**: monofilament (low visibility, more catch, cuts hands); multifilament (more
  durable, easier to handle). Thinner twine catches more but tears more.
- **Trammel net**: a fine inner net between two coarse outer walls — fish push the inner
  net through the outer to form a pocket. Good for flatfish and shrimp; more bycatch.
- **Depth**: wall height to suit water depth and species (bottom-dwellers vs mid-water).

## 2. Hanging the net (rigging)
- **Hanging ratio** E = length of rope ÷ stretched length of netting. Common gillnet
  ratio ~0.5 (net is "hung in" by half): meshes open in a diamond. Higher E (tighter)
  gives a stiffer net; lower E (baggier) entangles more.
- Hang the netting on the floatline and leadline with twine at regular intervals
  ("hanging twine"), a fixed number of meshes per tie. At E = 0.5, each mesh takes up half
  its stretched length of rope — e.g. 3 meshes of 100 mm stretched mesh per 150 mm of rope.
- Floats and leads sized so the net stands upright but sits on the bottom (for bottom-set nets).

## 3. Setting a fleet
1. Plan the set: depth, bottom type (avoid wrecks and rough ground), tide direction,
   other gear nearby, closed areas.
2. **Anchors** at both ends, each with a downline to a **marked buoy** (dan/highflyer flag,
   radar reflector, light) carrying the legally required markings (vessel ID).
3. Shoot downtide/downwind at low speed so the net runs straight off the bin or drum.
4. Record GPS position of both ends.
5. Attach **pingers** (acoustic deterrents) where required or effective for porpoise bycatch.

## 4. Soak time
- Longer soaks = more fish but **lower quality** (dead fish degrade, scavengers like
  sea lice/amphipods and seals eat them) and more bycatch.
- Many coastal fisheries soak **overnight to ~24 h**; high-value markets often prefer
  shorter soaks (a few hours).
- Warm water shortens the acceptable soak; check fish condition and adjust.
- Never leave nets unattended longer than legal limits; storms force earlier hauling.

## 5. Hauling and clearing
1. Pick up the end buoy, haul with a **net hauler** at steady speed, standing clear of the
   hauler sheaves.
2. **Clear fish** as they come aboard: back them out through the mesh the way they entered
   (head first), or cut a mesh if needed. Bleed and ice immediately (see
   icing-chilling-and-hold-stowage).
3. Release live bycatch carefully and quickly — seabirds and mammals cut out with minimal
   handling; report as required.
4. Flake the net back into the bin clean for the next shot; note damage for repair
   (see net-mending-and-rope-splicing).

## Gotchas
- **Ghost nets**: lost gillnets keep fishing for years. Mark well, use sufficient
  anchoring, record positions, report and recover lost gear.
- Wind against tide rolls nets into "ropes".
- Gloves and a knife in reach; monofilament around a wrist during shooting is a man-overboard risk.
- Seals raiding nets: shorter soaks; check local seal deterrent rules.

## Verify
- [ ] Mesh size, net length and marking comply with current local regulation.
- [ ] Both ends anchored, marked and logged by GPS.
- [ ] Soak time recorded per fleet and matched to catch quality.
- [ ] All bycatch released and logged; no gear left unaccounted for.
