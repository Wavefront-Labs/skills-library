# Net mending and rope splicing

**Goal:** fix a torn net so its meshes match the surrounding net, and make strong,
neat splices in rope — the two most-used gear skills on deck. Learn on scrap net
first; mending is easier to *see* than read, so pair this with a demonstration.

## 1. Kit
- **Net needles** loaded with twine matching the net (same material and thickness).
  Load: twine round the tongue, down to the heel, back up the other side, repeat; not
  so full it won't pass through a mesh.
- **Mesh gauge** (flat stick) the width of half a stretched mesh (bar length) to keep
  meshes even.
- Sharp knife, gloves for wire/mono, a hook or nail to hang the net at working height.

## 2. Reading the hole (diamond mesh)
- **Knot** = where four bars meet. **Bar** = one side of a mesh.
- After damage you'll see loose ends. Every hole must be trimmed to a shape that has
  exactly **two "three-leggers"** (knots with one broken bar = start and finish points),
  and otherwise only **points** (knots with two bars — top/bottom of the hole) and
  **sides** (a half-mesh step down the edge).
- **Trim**: cut away broken bars close to the knot (leave ~1 cm of tail) until the edge
  is clean. A badly trimmed hole can't be mended to shape.

## 3. Mending (sheet-bend mesh knot)
1. Start at one three-legger: tie the twine onto it with a sheet bend.
2. Make the mesh: pass the needle around the gauge, then through the next knot point
   from behind, and tie a **sheet bend** (mesh knot): the needle goes round behind
   both legs of the existing mesh and through the loop you formed, pulled tight against the gauge.
3. Work across the row: down the sides by half-meshes, across the points.
4. Each new row: reverse direction (mend left to right, then right to left).
5. Finish at the other three-legger with a sheet bend and a locking half-hitch; trim.
6. Check: all meshes the same size as the surrounding net (stretch and compare).
**Big tears**: cut a patch from spare matching netting, trim both to fit, and mend the
patch in round its edges. Faster than mending row by row.

## 4. Eye splice (three-strand rope)
1. Unlay roughly 15 rope diameters, enough for 5+ tucks (≈ 15 cm on 10 mm rope); whip
   or tape strand ends.
2. Form the eye. Tuck the **middle strand** under one strand of the standing part, against
   the lay.
3. Tuck the **left strand** under the next strand to the left (over the one just used).
4. Turn the splice over; tuck the **last strand** under the remaining standing strand.
   Each standing strand now has one working strand under it.
5. Continue tucking **over one, under one**, against the lay; minimum **5 full tucks** for
   synthetic rope (4 for natural fibre).
6. Taper (optional): cut a few yarns from each strand for the final tuck. Roll under foot to
   set; trim ends leaving short tails (heat-seal synthetics).
**Back splice** (stops unlaying at an end): crown knot, then tuck strands back down against the lay 5 times.
**Short splice** joins two ropes (it thickens the rope — won't pass through blocks).
Braided ropes (double-braid, 12-strand) use different splices — follow the manufacturer's splicing guide with a fid.

## 5. Hanging nets (outline)
Netting is attached to ropes (headline, footrope) with **hanging twine** at a set
**hanging ratio** (see gillnet-rigging-setting-and-soak-management). Measure, mark the
rope at equal intervals, tie the same number of meshes in each interval.

## Gotchas
- Using thinner twine than the net creates a weak spot that tears again.
- Mismatched mesh size in the mend changes selectivity — legally relevant in codends.
- Splicing with too few tucks → the splice pulls out under load.
- Wire splicing and heavy trawl gear repairs need specialist tools and training.

## Verify
- [ ] The trimmed hole had exactly two three-leggers before you started.
- [ ] Mended meshes measure the same as the surrounding net with a gauge.
- [ ] Eye splice has ≥5 tucks on every strand and holds a hard pull.
- [ ] Repair twine matches the net's material and thickness.
