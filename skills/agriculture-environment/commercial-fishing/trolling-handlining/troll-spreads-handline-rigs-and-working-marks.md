# Troll spreads, handline rigs and working marks

**Goal:** catch fish one at a time with hooks — towed lures (trolling) or hand/jig
lines on the drift — and sell them as top-quality "line-caught". This is small-boat
and artisanal fishing worldwide: tuna and salmon trollers, mackerel and bass
handliners, snapper and grouper handliners in the tropics. Licences, size limits,
hook rules and closed seasons apply — check them.

## 1. Trolling — a spread that doesn't tangle
- **Gear**: troll poles/outriggers to spread lines wide, plus stern lines;
  commercial trollers use **gurdies** (small winches) and wire mainlines with leaders
  ("spreads" of lures on each line at depth).
- **Depth**: weights (cannonballs/leads), **downriggers**, planers/diving boards put lures at
  the depth fish are marking (see echo-sounder-and-sonar-interpretation).
- **Speed** sets lure action — e.g. ~2–3 knots for salmon, ~5–8 knots for tunas with
  skirted lures; check each lure swims right alongside the boat before letting it back.
- **Spread layout**: shortest lines in the middle/inside, longest and deepest outside on
  the poles — so turns don't cross lines. Stagger distances behind the boat.
- **Turns**: wide; inside lines slow and sink, outside lines speed up — fish often bite on
  turns.
- **Working**: troll along temperature breaks, current lines, bait balls, birds, and
  around marks; zig-zag edges.

## 2. Handlines and jigs
- **Handline rigs**: mainline (mono or braid), a sinker, and one to several hooks on
  snoods (paternoster/flasher rig), baited or with lures/feathers. Weight just enough to
  hold bottom in the current.
- **Jigging**: metal or soft-plastic jigs worked up and down; **mechanised jigging
  machines** on many small boats (squid, cod, mackerel) — set the depth band and jerk
  speed from the marks.
- **Squid jigging**: lights at night attract squid; jig colours/sizes per area.
- Hook size and type matched to target; **barbless or circle hooks** reduce damage to
  released fish.

## 3. Working marks (on the drift)
1. Find a mark: reef, wreck, pinnacle, ledge edge, or a fish mark on the sounder.
2. Read the drift: wind and tide direction; set up **uptide** so the drift carries you over
   the mark. Note your drift line on the plotter.
3. Drop when you're just before the mark, line near the bottom, lift a turn of line or
   two off as you approach rough ground to avoid snagging.
4. After passing over, motor back uptide and redo the same drift line; small adjustments
   (a few boat lengths) change catches.
5. On a tight wreck, consider anchoring uptide and fishing back (if allowed and safe).

## 4. Landing and quality — the line-caught advantage
- Unhook fast with minimal handling; return undersized fish quickly.
- **Bleed** immediately (cut gill arches or the artery behind the pectoral fin, bleed in seawater
  for a few minutes), **ikejime** for premium markets, then **chill** in slurry ice right away
  (see icing-chilling-and-hold-stowage).
- Handle fish by the head or with wet hands; don't drop on deck or stand on them.
- Tag/log fish with date, area and gear; some markets pay a premium for traceable line-caught fish.

## Gotchas
- Too many lines for the crew = tangles and lost fish.
- Wrong speed: lures spin or roll, nothing bites.
- Handlines under load cut hands — gloves or finger stalls.
- Small open boats far offshore: weather, VHF, PLB/EPIRB, lifejackets — non-negotiable.

## Verify
- [ ] Every lure checked for action at troll speed before letting back.
- [ ] Spread arranged so lines clear on turns (shortest inside, longest outside).
- [ ] Drift lines saved on the plotter for each productive mark.
- [ ] Every fish bled and on ice within minutes of landing.
