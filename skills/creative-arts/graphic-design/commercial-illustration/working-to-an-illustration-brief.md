# Working to an illustration brief — concept, roughs, delivery

**Goal:** turn a commission (editorial, advertising, packaging, icons) into an image
that solves the client's communication problem, delivered on time, with rights and
revisions agreed up front.

## 1. Clarify the brief before you sketch
Get these in writing (email is fine):
- **Purpose and audience:** what must the image communicate? For editorial, read
  the article (or a summary) yourself.
- **Format:** dimensions or aspect ratio, bleed, where text/headline/logo will sit,
  print or screen, colour limits (spot colours, house palette).
- **Style reference:** usually your own portfolio pieces they liked.
- **Deadlines:** roughs, final, and any intermediate check.
- **Fee, usage and rights:** what media, territory, duration, exclusivity. Commercial
  advertising usage costs much more than one-time editorial. Licence usage rather than
  assigning copyright unless paid for it.
- **Revisions:** number of rounds included (commonly two at rough stage, one minor at
  final) and the fee for extras; a **kill fee** if cancelled (often a percentage of the
  fee, higher after roughs). Norms vary by market — check a current pricing guide or
  illustrators' association for your country.

## 2. Find the concept
Especially for editorial work, the idea matters more than rendering.
- List the article's key nouns and verbs; brainstorm **visual metaphors** — combine
  two ideas into one image (a ladder made of pencils for "education and career").
- Avoid the first cliché (lightbulb, puzzle pieces, handshake) unless twisted.
- Thumbnail 10–20 tiny ideas fast; choose 2–3 that read instantly.

## 3. Roughs
- Send **2–3 roughs** that are clearly different concepts, drawn at the correct aspect
  ratio, with the text-safe area shown. Clean enough to read the idea; not finished.
- Add a one-line explanation for each. Recommend one.
- Get explicit approval of **one** rough before going to final. Changes after
  approval may count as additional revisions — say so politely if needed.

## 4. Final artwork
- Work at final size or larger: print commonly 300 ppi at final dimensions; screen
  art per the platform's specs. Ask for exact sizes; don't guess.
- Keep layered working files (PSD/Procreate/AI) so late changes are possible; flatten
  only the delivery copy.
- Colour: work RGB, convert to the printer's CMYK profile at the end if required, and
  check that saturated colours (bright greens, blues) survive conversion.
- For **icon sets**: define a grid (e.g. 24×24 with 2 px stroke), keyline shapes,
  consistent stroke, corner radius and perspective across every icon; deliver SVG.

## 5. Deliver and wrap up
- Name files clearly (`client_project_v2_final.tif`), deliver in the agreed formats
  through a link, and include the agreed credit line.
- Invoice promptly with the usage licence written on the invoice.
- Ask for printed copies or links for your portfolio (check whether you may share
  before publication — embargoes are common).

## Gotchas
- **Vague briefs** produce endless revisions; questions at the start are cheaper.
- **Text areas forgotten** — the headline lands on the focal point. Ask for the layout.
- **Scope creep** ("could you also do social versions?") is new usage and new work; quote it.
- **Style drift:** clients hire you for your portfolio style; jumping styles risks
  rejection unless agreed.
- **AI-generated content** clauses are increasingly common in contracts; read them.
- Fee and rights norms differ widely by country and sector; specific percentages
  are deliberately omitted.

## Verify
- [ ] Brief, format, deadlines, fee, usage and revision limits confirmed in writing.
- [ ] Multiple distinct concepts explored; roughs at correct ratio with text-safe area.
- [ ] One rough explicitly approved before finals.
- [ ] Final at specified size/resolution and colour mode; layered master kept.
- [ ] Delivery and invoice state the licensed usage.
