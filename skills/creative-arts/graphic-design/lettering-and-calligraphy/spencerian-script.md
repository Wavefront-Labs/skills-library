# Spencerian script

**Goal:** fluent, light, rhythmic writing in the nineteenth-century American business hand named after Platt Rogers Spencer.

## Background
Spencerian script developed in the United States in the 1800s and was the standard business penmanship before the typewriter. It is a pointed-pen style related to roundhand but lighter, more oval, and with less contrast. Authentic study uses period manuals and modern instruction from groups such as IAMPETH (the International Association of Master Penmen, Engrossers and Teachers of Handwriting).

## Tools
- Flexible pointed nib (many students begin with a Nikko G, Hunt 101 or Leonardt Principal or Hiro 41 for fine lines), oblique holder if right-handed (see `creative-arts.graphic-design.lettering-and-calligraphy.oblique-holder-and-nib-setup`), and smooth paper.
- Ink: sumi, walnut or iron-gall style inks that flow; test each paper for feathering.

## Slant and proportions
- Slant is commonly cited as about 52 degrees from the baseline in many manuals (copperplate is steeper at 55); check the model you follow.
- Proportions are built on a small unit (the x-height space): small letters one space, taller letters several spaces; follow a manual's chart rather than approximations.

## Movement
- Spencerian relies on **whole-arm and muscular movement** for large forms and finger or wrist movement for small ones, which gives speed and fluency; the writing surface is kept angled.
- Practise **oval and push-pull drills**, then compound curves, **before** letters; form follows motion.

## Shading
Shading is **lighter and more selective** than copperplate: used on capitals and select letters, not on every downstroke.

## Letters
- Small letters are built from shared strokes: the oval, the straight stem, the compound curve.
- Capitals are more ornate with flowing loops; practise them as large exercises first.

## Practice
15 minutes of drills, then words and a line of connected text. Compare with the model for slant and spacing; mark slant lines on the paper.
