# Broad-pen calligraphy basics — the edged pen does the work

**Goal:** write even, rhythmic letters in a basic broad-edge hand (foundational or
italic), understanding why thick and thin strokes appear, so the skill transfers to
other scripts and to drawn lettering.

## 1. Kit
- **Pen:** a beginner-friendly option is a **Pilot Parallel Pen** (3.8 mm is a common
  starting size) — no dipping, consistent ink flow. Alternatives: dip pen holder with
  broad nibs (e.g. Speedball C-series, Brause bandzug) or broad calligraphy markers.
- **Ink:** for dip pens, non-waterproof calligraphy ink or gouache thinned to a
  milky consistency. Avoid India ink with shellac in fountain-style pens (it clogs).
- **Paper:** smooth, heavy enough not to bleed — layout/marker pads or 90–120 gsm
  smooth paper. Test bleed with your ink.
- A ruler, pencil, and a sloped board (~30–45°) if possible for comfort and ink flow.

## 2. Rule guidelines in nib widths
Letter heights are measured in **nib widths**: hold the pen at 90° to the baseline and
make little square steps, one on top of the next (a "ladder").
- Foundational hand: x-height commonly ~4–5 nib widths; ascenders/descenders ~2–3 more.
- Italic: x-height ~5 nib widths.
Mark x-height, ascender and descender lines lightly in pencil, or print guide sheets.
(Proportions differ between teachers — pick one model alphabet and follow it.)

## 3. Hold a constant pen angle
The thick/thin contrast comes from the angle of the nib edge, not from pressure.
- Foundational: about **30°** to the baseline. Italic: about **40–45°**. Some
  strokes (e.g. diagonals in some hands) change angle — follow your model.
- Keep the whole nib edge in contact; tilt it to one corner and strokes go ragged.
- Move the arm and hand together; fingers stay relatively still.
- Check: a stroke pulled straight down at 30° is thick; a diagonal along the nib edge
  makes a hairline.

## 4. Ductus (stroke order and direction)
Every letter is built from a few strokes in a fixed order and direction, mostly pulled
**down or sideways**, rarely pushed up (pushing the broad pen catches the paper).
Learn letters in families that share strokes:
- Foundational: *o, c, e* (round, based on the circle), then *a, d, g, q* (o plus stem),
  *b, p*, then *n, m, h, r* (arches), *i, l, t, u*, diagonals *v, w, x, y, k, z*, then *s, f, j*.
- Italic: *i, l, u, t*; *n, m, h, r, b, p* (branching arches); *a, d, g, q* (based on a
  slanted oval); diagonals; *s, f*; then capitals.

## 5. Practice routine (20–30 minutes)
1. **Drills:** rows of vertical strokes, diagonals, circles and zig-zags at constant angle.
2. **Letter family** of the day, several lines each.
3. **Words** with even spacing: the area of space between letters should look equal,
   not the distance. Round letters sit closer; straight stems need more space.
   Common practice words: *minimum*, *banana*, *ono*.
4. **Review:** compare against the model; circle your best letter and repeat it.

## Gotchas
- **Ink skipping**: clean the nib (new dip nibs have an oily coating — wipe with
  soapy water or a quick pass through a flame, carefully), refill, check paper isn't too rough.
- **Wobbly strokes** usually mean drawing too slowly or with fingers; move steadily with the arm.
- **Changing pen angle** mid-word is the commonest fault; recheck after every letter at first.
- **Left-handed writers** often use left-oblique nibs, angle the paper, or write with
  an underwriting hand position — the Parallel Pen helps here.
- Angles and proportions given are typical teaching values; exemplars vary, so follow one
  reputable model alphabet consistently.

## Verify
- [ ] Guidelines ruled from a nib-width ladder for your pen.
- [ ] Vertical downstrokes are thick and nib-direction diagonals are hairlines (angle correct).
- [ ] Letters built with the model's stroke order, pulled not pushed.
- [ ] Spacing in practice words looks even in area.
- [ ] Daily drill + family + words routine followed for at least two weeks.
