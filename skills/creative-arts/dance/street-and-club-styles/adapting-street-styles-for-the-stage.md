# Adapting street styles for the stage

**Goal:** breaking, popping, locking, house and related styles grew in cyphers, clubs and
battles — circles where dancers face each other and respond in the moment. A stage turns
that into one-directional performance. This skill is about keeping what makes the dance
alive (groove, foundation, freestyle energy, community) while making it read for an audience.

> **Status: draft.** Drawn from general theatre-hip-hop practice and the scene's own
> commentary. Approaches differ widely between companies and communities; this is a
> starting framework, not a rulebook.

## 1. Start from foundations, not tricks
Each style has its own base the scene expects to see:
- **Breaking:** toprock, go-downs, footwork (six-step and variations), freezes, power moves.
- **Popping:** the pop (muscle contraction on the beat), with styles like waving, tutting,
  boogaloo, robot.
- **Locking:** locks, points, wrist rolls, Scooby-doo/Skeeter rabbit-type steps, the funk
  character and audience interaction.
- **Hip hop (party/social dances):** bounce and rock as the core groove, with named
  social dances from different eras.
- **House:** footwork, jacking, lofting, driven by the house groove.

A staged piece that's all tricks with no groove reads as gymnastics; dancers from the
scene in the audience notice instantly. Keep the groove visible even under the big moments.

## 2. Open the circle
- **Sightlines:** in a cypher, the "front" is wherever the circle is. On a proscenium,
  floor moves upstage vanish behind front rows — put footwork and freezes **downstage**
  or on a raised level; use diagonals so bodies don't block each other.
- **Turning out:** reangle confrontational call-and-response so both dancers are
  three-quarter to the audience — the exchange still reads, the faces are visible.
- **Lighting:** side light flatters popping isolations and freezes; top light flattens them.
- **Floor:** breaking needs a smooth, firm, non-slip surface — vinyl dance floor on a
  sprung stage; marley can be grippy for spins (dancers often test and prefer specific
  surfaces; ask). Pad backstage spaces for warm-ups.

## 3. Structure set and freestyle
Common structure: **set choreography** (unison, formations, story beats) alternating with
**freestyle windows** — a mini-cypher on stage where dancers improvise within a fixed
time and space. Freestyle keeps the work honest to the culture and keeps dancers sharp
night to night.
- Define the freestyle window by music cue and count (e.g. "16 bars, then the drop").
- Agree an exit cue so freestyle doesn't drift into the next set section.
- Balance energy: battles peak early; a theatre piece needs an arc — build, release, peak.

## 4. Music
Original or licensed music matters: breaking is built around breakbeats; locking around
funk; house around house. Tempo choices change what's dance-able (power moves need
space; popping wants clear hits). Clear the rights for touring.

## 5. Context and credit
These are Black and Latino-originated cultures with named pioneers and living
communities. Credit styles and originators in programmes, hire dancers from the scene,
pay them as artists and consult them about representation. Using the vocabulary without
the people is the commonest criticism of staged hip hop.

## Gotchas
- Choreographing street styles "in counts" like jazz can kill the bounce — rehearse
  to music, feel the groove, then count.
- Overlong unison: street dancers' individual flavour is the asset. Let it show.
- Repeating the same freezes every night as freestyle — it stops being freestyle.
- Injury: power moves on an unfamiliar stage — always floor test before tech.

## Verify
- [ ] Foundations of each style visible, groove present even in set sections.
- [ ] Floor moves placed where the whole house can see them.
- [ ] Freestyle windows defined by cue, length and exit.
- [ ] Floor surface tested by the dancers before tech.
- [ ] Styles and originators credited; scene dancers paid and consulted.
