# Translating battle energy to stage

**Goal:** a battle is electric because it is live: two dancers or crews answer each other,
the crowd reacts, the DJ can change the record, and nobody knows who wins. Staged versions
often lose this and become a showcase of tricks. This skill keeps the stakes.

> **Status: draft.** Built from theatre hip hop practice (Breakin' Convention-style
> programmes) and battle formats such as Red Bull BC One. Check with the dancers which
> conventions of their own scene they want kept.

## 1. Name what makes a battle work
List these with the cast before staging anything:
- **Exchange:** a round is an answer to the last one. Burns, call-outs and "biting"
  (copying) all only mean something because the opponent is watching.
- **Uncertainty:** the music, the opponent's choice and the result are unknown.
- **Crowd as participant:** the cypher shouts, leans in, reacts to a crash or a burn.
- **Roles:** host/MC, DJ, judges, the crew behind the dancer hyping them.
- **Time pressure:** a round is short (often around 45-60 seconds or a fixed number of
  bars at a jam), then the other side goes.

## 2. Stage the exchange, not just the moves
- Face-off reangled to three-quarter so both faces and the reply are visible; keep the
  reacting dancer lit, not in the dark while the other goes.
- Choreograph the **response logic**: dancer B starts by quoting dancer A's freeze, then
  flips it. The audience reads it as a conversation even without battle knowledge.
- Give the waiting crew active business (hype, mimic, call-outs) planned per round.

## 3. Keep something genuinely live
- Fixed structure, open content: the order of rounds, bar counts and exit cues are set;
  what happens inside a round is freestyle.
- A live DJ, or at least alternative tracks per night, keeps dancers listening.
- Optional: let the audience decide a round by noise. Plan both outcomes so the show
  continues either way.

## 4. Build an arc
A real battle can peak in round one. A show needs build and release:
introduce characters and rivalry, escalate (power, then burns, then crew rounds), one
moment of breakdown or respect (a shared round, a handshake), then the final.
Vary the texture: a quiet round with only footwork after a power round lands harder.

## 5. Safety and floor
Battles on an untested stage are where injuries happen: test floor grip and spin surface
in the venue, keep risky power moves downstage centre with clear space, and set a stop
word for crashes.

## Gotchas
- Every round at maximum energy: the audience tires and nothing reads as a peak.
- Rehearsing the "freestyle" into a fixed routine by week three.
- Judges or host played as cartoon roles; scene audiences notice disrespect.
- Burn gestures that would be insulting out of context without the audience knowing it is play.

## Verify
- [ ] Each round has a planned response logic and an exit cue.
- [ ] At least one element is genuinely live each night (freestyle, DJ or audience).
- [ ] The evening has an arc with a clear low point and final peak.
- [ ] Floor tested in venue; crash protocol agreed.
