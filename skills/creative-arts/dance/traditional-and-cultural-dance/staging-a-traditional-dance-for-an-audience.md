# Staging a traditional dance for an audience

**Goal:** many traditional dances live in village squares, temples, cèilidhs, weddings or
social nights — circular, participatory, long, embedded in occasion. Putting one on a
stage for spectators means choices about space, length, costume and meaning. Done well, it
honours the form; done carelessly, it becomes a costume parade or causes offence.

> **Status: draft.** This is a general framework across forms; every tradition (flamenco,
> Bharatanatyam, Irish step, Māori haka, folklórico…) has its own codes, teachers and
> protocols. Defer to tradition-bearers of the specific form over anything here.

## 1. Research before rehearsing
- **What is it for at home?** Social, ceremonial, devotional, courtship, competitive,
  storytelling. This sets what may be adapted.
- **Is it performable outside its context?** Some dances are **sacred or restricted** —
  by gender, lineage, season, initiation — and are not for public staging. Ask; don't assume.
- **Who holds it?** Find tradition-bearers, recognised teachers or community organisations.
  Their involvement is the main safeguard of quality and respect (the "nothing about us
  without us" principle echoed in UNESCO's intangible heritage convention).
- **Gather primary material:** films of the dance in its setting, notes from teachers,
  music recordings, costume references by region and era.

## 2. Decide what must stay and what can change
Make an explicit list with the tradition-bearers:
- **Fixed:** core steps and their technique, rhythm patterns, gestures with meaning
  (mudras, hand signals), order where it carries meaning, who may dance which roles.
- **Adaptable:** length, floor pattern, number of dancers, entrances and exits, lighting,
  stage costume construction (lighter fabrics, quick-change closures).
Write it down; it settles arguments later in tech.

## 3. Adapt space and time
- **Circles and lines become arcs and diagonals** so the audience sees faces and footwork.
  A full closed circle on a proscenium hides half the dancers' fronts.
- **Footwork-heavy forms** (flamenco zapateado, Irish step, kathak tatkar) need an
  amplifying floor (wooden riser or tap board) and sometimes miking — work out with sound.
- **Length:** a 40-minute social dance may become a 6-minute suite. Keep the structural
  arc (opening, development, climax) rather than cutting at random.
- **Live music** where possible: many forms are a dialogue between dancer and musician
  (flamenco's cante and toque, kathak's tabla). Rehearse with them, not to a recording.

## 4. Context for the audience
Programme notes or a short spoken introduction: origin, occasion, meaning of key gestures,
names of teachers and communities. Avoid exoticising language ("ancient", "primitive",
"exotic") and blending different regional forms into one generic "national" dance without
saying so.

## 5. Costume and objects
Regional dress is often specific (fabric, embroidery, jewellery, colour by marital status
or region). Build stage versions that keep the signifiers and are dance-safe: secure
headdresses, hem lengths that don't catch, jewellery that won't fly off, shoes appropriate
to the floor (and any footwear rules — some forms are danced barefoot by tradition).

## Gotchas
- **Smoothing it into ballet lines** — over-theatricalising erases style (a long-running
  critique of state folk companies).
- Performers from outside the tradition without training from its teachers.
- Sacred items used as props.
- No pay or credit for consultants and musicians.

## Verify
- [ ] Function and performability confirmed with tradition-bearers; no restricted material.
- [ ] Written list of fixed vs adaptable elements, agreed.
- [ ] Floor pattern visible to the audience; footwork amplified if needed.
- [ ] Programme notes credit sources and explain meaning without exoticising.
- [ ] Costumes keep regional signifiers and are safe to dance in.
