# Act dramaturgy and rigging-aware staging

**Goal:** turn a list of tricks into an act that has shape and meaning, then stage it
so the venue, the rigging and the costume all support it rather than endanger it.

## 1. From trick list to act arc
1. **List every skill** you can do reliably (performance-ready = clean nearly every time
   while tired). Mark each: difficulty, "wow" level, recovery time needed.
2. **Pick a through-line:** a character, a relationship, an image or a question.
   Contemporary circus leans on this; classical acts can simply escalate. Either way
   decide it consciously.
3. **Order by escalation with breathing room:** opener that establishes skill fast
   (in the first 30 s), development, a quieter moment, build, **climax trick**,
   short ending image. Put the riskiest element where you are warm but not exhausted
   — often two-thirds through, not the final seconds.
4. **Transitions are choreography.** Rehearse the moves between tricks as carefully as
   the tricks; that's where amateur acts look amateur.
5. **Length:** a single variety/cabaret act is commonly 4–7 minutes; cut until every
   moment earns its place. *Booking norms vary; ask the presenter.*

## 2. Music
- Choose for dynamics: the music's build should match your build. Edit tracks to your
  timing rather than rushing a trick to hit a drop.
- Mark **cue points** (where the drop happens, where the lights change) in a cue sheet.
- Rights: public performance usually needs the venue/event's licence; recorded or
  streamed shows may need more. Check before you film. *Licensing differs by country.*
- Have a plan if music fails (count in silence; agreed restart cue).

## 3. Costume that doesn't injure you
- Aerial/trapeze: no loose fabric to tangle, no hooks/sequins at contact points,
  grippy material where you hold with skin, skin exposed where you need friction
  (backs of knees on silks).
- Acrobatics: flyers' costume gives bases a grippable surface; no slippery fabrics on
  grip points.
- Rehearse in full costume at least a week before the show — and in show makeup
  (sweat + makeup changes grip).

## 4. Staging around rigging and sightlines
- **Get the venue's tech spec early:** ceiling height, rated rigging points
  (location and working load limit), floor type, wing space, power, haze rules.
- Write a **rigging plot**: your apparatus, its point(s), hardware list, required trim
  height, swing clearance, mat footprint. The venue's rigger approves it — you never rig
  to an unassessed beam.
- **Clearance:** check swing paths, spinning radius and drop paths against lighting
  bars, set and audience. Spin + 2 m diameter hoop needs much more space than you think.
- **Sightlines:** walk the auditorium. What's high may be lost under a proscenium;
  what's low on a flat floor vanishes for the back rows.
- **Lighting:** front light blinds aerialists looking out; agree no strobes during
  catches and releases.

## 5. Show creation (ensemble)
- Running order alternates apparatus types, energy and rigging changes; place rig
  changes behind a floor act or clown entrée.
- Build a **cue-to-cue** and a **rigging changeover** rehearsal.
- Every act has a written **risk assessment**: hazards, controls (mats, spotters,
  lines), who checks rigging and when, what happens if a performer is hurt mid-show
  (stop cue, who goes on, who calls).

## Gotchas
- Building the act on a perfect day's skills; on tour you will be jet-lagged and cold.
- Picking music you love that's 30 s too long.
- Forgetting the warm-up space and time before the slot.
- Arriving at a venue with a 5 m rig and a 4.2 m ceiling.

## Verify
- [ ] You can describe the act's arc in two sentences.
- [ ] The riskiest trick is placed where you are warm and fresh enough.
- [ ] A rigging plot and tech rider exist and the venue's rigger has signed off.
- [ ] Rehearsed in full costume, makeup and show lighting.
- [ ] A risk assessment with stop procedure exists for every act.
