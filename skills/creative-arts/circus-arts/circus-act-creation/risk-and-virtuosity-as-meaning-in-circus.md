# Risk and virtuosity as meaning

Circus audiences read risk and skill directly. The dramaturgy of the act decides how those readings are used.

## Reading risk
- The audience judges risk from height, speed, closeness to the ground, and the performer's body language.
- A trick looks more dangerous when it is prepared (the pause, the breath, a look) and less dangerous when it is hidden in a sequence.

## Pacing the big trick
1. Prepare the audience: show smaller versions earlier.
2. Create the pause: a moment where the audience can feel the risk.
3. Make the trick clear: the audience needs to see it.
4. Let the audience respond: do not rush into the next movement.

## Virtuosity
- Skill that appears effortless shows mastery; skill that shows effort shows struggle. Choose which one the act needs.
- Repeating a difficult trick can be a way to build meaning (the performer insists), but repeated risk raises the injury risk.

## Honesty about risk
- Do not suggest that a staged risk is real if it is not; decide what the audience should believe.
- Do not add risk to an act for effect: the real risk is the performer's. Any staged danger must pass a written risk assessment.
- Rig safety, spotting and fall protection are not up for artistic negotiation.

## Rehearsal
- Rehearse the pause and the trick separately, then together.
- Do not rehearse high-risk elements while fatigued.
- Record the full act with the safety setup visible so that the team can review.

## Verify
- [ ] The act has one clear high-point trick with a prepared build-up.
- [ ] Any staged danger is covered by the act's risk assessment.
