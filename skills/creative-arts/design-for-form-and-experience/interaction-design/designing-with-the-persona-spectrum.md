# Persona spectrum

The Microsoft Inclusive Design toolkit introduces the **persona spectrum**, which shows that a mismatch between a person and their environment can be permanent, temporary or situational, and that solutions for one group help many.

## Example (touch, see, hear, speak)
| Ability | Permanent | Temporary | Situational |
|---|---|---|---|
| Touch | One arm | Arm injury | Holding a baby |
| See | Blind | Cataract | Bright sunlight |
| Hear | Deaf | Ear infection | Noisy bar |
| Speak | Non-verbal | Laryngitis | Heavy accent in a new country |

## Steps
1. **Take a design problem** and a persona (see `...ux-design.building-research-based-personas`).
2. **Choose a dimension** (vision, hearing, mobility, cognition, speech) and add the three variants to the persona.
3. **List the barriers** for each variant in your flow (for example one-handed use of a map, captions in a silent office).
4. **Brainstorm solutions** that work across the spectrum (captions, voice and touch alternatives, high contrast, large targets).
5. **Recruit people with disabilities** for research (see `...ux-design.recruiting-participants-with-a-screener`); personas are not a replacement for real participants.
6. **Add requirements** to the backlog and check them in the WCAG audit (see `...interaction-design.auditing-a-design-against-wcag`).

## Gotchas
- Do not equate temporary or situational limitation with permanent disability; the spectrum shows shared design benefits, not identical experience.
- Avoid tokenism in persona names and photos.

## Verify
- [ ] Each flow was checked against at least one permanent, one temporary and one situational case.
- [ ] People with disabilities participated in testing.
- [ ] Resulting requirements are tracked.
