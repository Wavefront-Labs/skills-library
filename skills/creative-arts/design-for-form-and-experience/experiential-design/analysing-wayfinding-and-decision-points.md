# Wayfinding analysis and decision points

Wayfinding is how people orient and find routes. Arthur and Passini's book treats it as spatial problem-solving in which signs support (not replace) legible architecture. Kevin Lynch's The Image of the City (1960) gave a vocabulary of paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks.

## Steps
1. **Define users:** first-time and frequent, staff, patients, children, people with disabilities, drivers and pedestrians; and the languages they use.
2. **List destinations** and rank by importance and frequency (primary: entrances, reception; secondary: services; tertiary: rooms).
3. **Map routes** between origins and destinations on plans: main paths, vertical circulation, entrances and exits.
4. **Mark decision points:** any place where a choice of direction occurs: entrances, junctions, lifts, stairs, forks, thresholds between zones.
5. **Audit legibility:** sightlines, landmarks, colour or material cues, lighting, visual clutter, and where people get lost (observe, ask staff, map complaints).
6. **Set the information strategy per point:** orientation (where am I; maps), direction (arrows to destinations), identification (name the destination at arrival), and reassurance (confirm along long stretches). Give only the information needed at that point to avoid overload.
7. **Name consistently:** one name per destination across signs, maps, website and staff speech.
8. **Output:** a wayfinding plan with decision points, messages and sign types, feeding the message schedule and location plan (see `...experiential-design.writing-a-sign-message-schedule`, `...experiential-design.drawing-a-sign-location-plan`).

## Verify
- [ ] Every decision point has an assigned information need.
- [ ] Destination names are unique and consistent.
- [ ] Test users can reach three destinations without help.
