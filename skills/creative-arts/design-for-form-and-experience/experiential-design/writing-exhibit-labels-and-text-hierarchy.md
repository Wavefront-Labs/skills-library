# Exhibit label writing and hierarchy

Serrell's book is the standard reference on writing labels that visitors actually read; it describes a hierarchy of label types and a visitor-centred approach.

## Hierarchy (typical)
1. **Exhibition title and introduction:** the big idea and orientation.
2. **Section or group labels:** introduce a theme and connect the items nearby.
3. **Object or caption labels:** identify an object and say one interesting thing.
4. **Interactive and media labels:** instructions and questions.
Visitors read little; each level should work on its own and in order.

## Writing
- **Lead with what the visitor sees or feels;** put the main point first.
- **Short and plain:** many museums keep object labels to a few short paragraphs and limit total length (guidance often cited is around 50 words for object labels, but follow your institution's standard); use active voice and everyday words; explain terms.
- **Ask or tell a story,** mention people and connections instead of listing facts.
- **Match the reading level** to the audience (many aim for a general adult level that families can share).
- **Provide translation or large-print and audio options** (see `...experiential-design.planning-accessible-routes-and-clearances` for the physical access side).

## Design
- **Hierarchy by size and weight,** consistent colour and placement; high contrast; matte non-glare surfaces.
- **Type size for the viewing distance;** sit labels near the object, at a height and angle that works for seated and standing visitors (check the current ADA Standards and your accessibility guidelines).
- Avoid text over busy images; keep line lengths comfortable.

## Test
Mock up labels on foam board in the gallery and watch whether visitors read them; revise (formative evaluation).

## Verify
- [ ] Each label states one idea.
- [ ] Hierarchy is clear from five metres.
- [ ] A visitor test showed people read and understood them.
