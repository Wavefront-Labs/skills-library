# Designing an exhibition visitor journey

**Goal:** turn a story (a collection, a brand, a theme) into a physical sequence that
visitors move through — what they see first, where they slow down, what they remember
leaving — while keeping the space navigable, safe and accessible.

> **Status: draft.** Practice from museum and brand-experience design. Accessibility
> numbers below are typical guidance values (e.g. Smithsonian guidelines); local building
> codes and accessibility law take precedence and must be checked for each venue.

## 1. Storyline before space
- Write the **big idea** in one sentence (what a visitor should feel or know after).
- Break it into **3–7 chapters/zones**, each with a key message and a key object or moment.
- Mark the **emotional arc**: introduction, build, climax/"wow" moment, reflection, exit.
- Decide the mode: **linear** (fixed order — chronological stories), **hub and spoke**
  (central space with optional rooms), or **open/free-flow** (themed islands).

## 2. Bubble diagram to floor plan
1. Draw zones as bubbles sized roughly by importance and content, linked by arrows for
   the intended sequence.
2. Overlay on the venue plan: entrances, exits, fire exits, columns, ceiling heights,
   power, daylight. Fixed constraints often decide the order.
3. Refine to a plan with walls, cases, plinths, AV and seating (SketchUp, Vectorworks,
   AutoCAD, or paper at 1:50 / 1:100).

## 3. Pacing and attention
- **Decompression zone** at the entrance: visitors need a few metres to adjust from street
  to exhibition; don't put the key text there.
- **Museum fatigue** is real: attention drops after the first rooms (a consistent
  visitor-studies finding). Put the strongest content early-to-middle, vary density, and
  provide **seating and rest** points.
- **Rhythm:** alternate dense information with open, visual, or interactive moments.
- **Text hierarchy:** title, introduction panel, section panels, object labels — most
  visitors read headlines and look; keep labels short (see
  `humanities.heritage-interpretation.interpretive-writing.writing-panels-and-labels-people-read`).

## 4. Sightlines and wayfinding
- From each doorway, what's the **first view**? Use a "magnet" (striking object, light,
  colour) visible at the far end to pull people through.
- Avoid dead ends in linear routes; avoid visual clutter blocking orientation.
- People often tend to turn right on entering (a commonly cited tendency — treat as a hint
  to test, not a law).
- Wayfinding: zone colours, numbers, floor graphics, maps at decision points.

## 5. Capacity, flow and safety
- Estimate peak visitors and **dwell time** per zone; bottlenecks form at AV screens,
  popular objects and narrow doorways. Give queues somewhere to go.
- Keep fire exit routes and widths clear per local code; get early sign-off from the venue.
- Separate entry and exit flows for timed or immersive experiences.

## 6. Accessibility (design in, not on)
- Step-free routes and clear widths for wheelchairs (commonly ~90 cm+ for routes, wider
  for passing), turning spaces, gentle ramps.
- Labels and interactives at seated-reachable heights; text size and contrast readable
  at distance; large-print guides.
- Audio description, captions on AV, tactile objects, quiet spaces, lighting avoiding glare.

## 7. Test
Build a **scale model** or 3D walkthrough; walk it at eye height (seated and standing).
Mock up key graphics at full size on site. Watch early visitors (soft opening) and adjust.

## Gotchas
- Designing from the plan view only — visitors experience elevation and sightlines.
- Everything equally important → nothing is.
- AV bottleneck at the entrance.
- Forgetting maintenance: interactives break; plan access and spares.

## Verify
- [ ] Big idea, zones and emotional arc written.
- [ ] Bubble diagram fitted to venue constraints and exits.
- [ ] Decompression zone, rest points and pacing variation planned.
- [ ] Sightlines from each entrance checked; wayfinding at decision points.
- [ ] Capacity, exits and accessibility reviewed against local code; model walkthrough done.
