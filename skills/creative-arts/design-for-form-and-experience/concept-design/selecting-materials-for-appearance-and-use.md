# Selecting materials for appearance and use

Goal: choose a material and finish that delivers the intended look and feel and survives real use, within cost and process limits. Ashby and Johnson frame the task as balancing technical function with the perceptual and emotional qualities of materials.

## Method
1. **List requirements per touch point:** what the user sees, touches, holds, cleans, drops; temperature, UV, chemicals, abrasion, sweat, food contact, flammability or regulatory limits.
2. **Shortlist by process and volume:** injection moulding, sheet metal, die cast, extrusion, thermoforming, 3D print, textile, wood. Process drives achievable form and finish.
3. **Add perceptual criteria:** surface (gloss, grain, texture), colour stability, warmth, weight, sound when tapped, how it ages (patina vs wear).
4. **Get physical samples:** hold, scratch, drop, wipe, light them in daylight and store-like light. Do not decide from a screen swatch.
5. **Compare in a table:** rows = candidates; columns = function, appearance, durability, process fit, cost, sustainability, supply risk.
6. **Plan finishing:** moulded-in texture, paint, anodise, PVD, laser etching, overmoulding; check the finish suits the geometry (see `...concept-design.specifying-mould-textures-and-gloss`).
7. **Test:** abrasion, light ageing, chemical wipe, colour match under different lights, using methods agreed with the supplier.

## Gotchas
- Metals and plastics of the "same" colour rarely match; plan tolerance bands.
- Soft-touch coatings can wear shiny or tacky; ask the supplier for field data.
- Recycled-content materials can vary in colour batch to batch.

## Verify
- [ ] Every candidate was judged from a physical sample.
- [ ] Requirements, not preference, eliminated candidates.
- [ ] The supplier confirmed process feasibility and minimum order.
