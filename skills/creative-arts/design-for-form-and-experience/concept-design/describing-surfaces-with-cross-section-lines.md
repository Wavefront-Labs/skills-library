# Cross-section lines and line weight

**Goal:** a line drawing that tells a modeller exactly how the surface curves between the
silhouette edges, without shading.

## Placing cross-sections
1. Draw the outer silhouette and the **centreline** (a section through the plane of
   symmetry) first. The centreline is the most informative single line on a product.
2. Add sections perpendicular to the main flow of the surface at places where the shape
   changes: the highest point, where a crown flattens, where a feature starts.
3. Sections wrap: a section line must hit the silhouette tangent to it (it turns away from
   you there). A section that hits the edge at an angle reads as a sharp corner.
4. Keep sections parallel to one plane set (e.g. all perpendicular to the length axis)
   so they read as slices through a loaf; mixing directions creates a mesh that is hard to read.
5. Draw sections lighter than the outline; they are explanation, not structure.

## Line-weight hierarchy
| Line | Weight | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Outer silhouette (against background) | Heaviest | Separates object from page. |
| Edges where a surface turns away (horizon of a form) | Heavy-medium | Shows overlap. |
| Edges against the light / underside contact | Heavy | Grounds the object. |
| Parting lines, panel gaps | Medium, crisp | Manufacturing detail. |
| Cross-sections, construction | Lightest | Explanation. |
Heavier near the viewer, lighter further away adds depth.

## Common errors
- Sections that are straight lines on a curved part (implies flat).
- Too many sections: 3-5 per major surface is usually enough.
- Erasing construction lines entirely; light construction adds credibility.

## Verify
Hand the sketch to someone else and ask them to describe the shape in words (where it is
flat, crowned, sharp). If they hesitate about a region, it needs one more section.
