# Building a mood board and style tiles

**Goal:** agree on how something should *feel* before anyone designs it. A mood board
shows a direction through curated images; a style tile translates the chosen direction
into concrete design ingredients (colour, type, texture, UI flavour) without designing
real screens or products yet. Both save rounds of "it's not quite what I imagined".

> **Status: draft.** The style-tile method is from Samantha Warren's widely used
> approach; mood-board practice is general studio habit. Tools named are examples.

## 1. Start from words, not images
From the brief, write:
- **Audience and context** (who sees it, where).
- **3–5 adjectives** ("warm, tactile, confident, unhurried") and **3 anti-adjectives**
  ("not luxury, not techy, not childish"). Anti-words prevent drift.
- Competitors or existing work to differ from.

## 2. Gather widely (1–2 h)
- Pull from **outside the category**: architecture, fashion, film stills, packaging,
  nature, interiors, historic print. Boards built only from competitor screenshots
  produce the same thing again.
- Tools: Pinterest, Are.na, Milanote, PureRef (desktop canvas), a Figma/FigJam board,
  your own photographs.
- Capture **why** each image is there — a note like "colour", "light", "type", "texture",
  "composition". Many images are only useful for one quality.
- Save the source/link of every image (for rights and credit).

## 3. Curate into 2–3 directions
- Cluster images into distinct directions — e.g. "Soft Modernist", "Archive Print",
  "Bright Utility". Each direction gets a name and one-line description using the adjectives.
- **8–15 images per board.** Remove anything that pulls in another direction, even if
  it's beautiful. A coherent board beats a big one.
- Arrange with hierarchy: one or two hero images large, supporting images smaller.
  Add a strip of extracted colours and a type sample.
- Make the directions genuinely different so the choice is meaningful.

## 4. Present and choose
- Show each board full-screen, say the name and adjectives, and ask which **parts** feel
  right, not just which board. Clients often pick "this palette with that photography".
- Write down the decision and any combinations.

## 5. Turn the choice into a style tile
A single page (or Figma frame) with:
- **Colour palette** (primary, secondary, accents, neutrals) with hex values.
- **Typography**: heading and body typefaces with sizes and a sample paragraph.
- **Textures, patterns, imagery treatment** (photo style, illustration style, grain).
- **UI or product flavour** samples: a button, a form field, an icon style, a card, a
  material swatch — small pieces, not whole layouts.
- The adjectives at the top.
Make 1–3 tile variations if the direction still has open questions. Approval of the
tile becomes the reference for design (and later the design system tokens — see
`creative-arts.design-for-form-and-experience.design-systems.design-tokens-and-a-modular-type-scale`).

## Gotchas
- **Boards that are just pretty pictures** with no stated reasons — impossible to apply.
- **Too many images**: directions blur; clients pick everything.
- **Image rights:** mood boards are internal references. Don't reuse found images in
  final deliverables or public posts without permission or licence; credit sources when sharing.
- **Accessibility:** check palette contrast at the tile stage before it gets approved.
- Showing the board without the brief's words — the conversation becomes taste-only.

## Verify
- [ ] Adjectives and anti-adjectives written from the brief.
- [ ] References come from beyond the category; each has a reason and source.
- [ ] 2–3 named, distinct directions with 8–15 images each.
- [ ] Decision recorded, including mixed picks.
- [ ] Style tile with colours (hex), type, textures and sample elements; contrast checked.
