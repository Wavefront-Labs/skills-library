# Straight ahead for chaotic action

Goal: use straight-ahead animation (drawing 1, then 2, then 3...) where unpredictable,
flowing shapes matter more than hitting precise poses. Thomas and Johnston recommend it for
"wild, scrambling action"; effects animators use it for fire, splashes and smoke.

## Method
1. **Set guardrails first**: thumbnail the start, the rough mass at the end, the general path,
   and a frame budget. Straight ahead without a destination drifts.
2. **Track volume**: draw the overall silhouette mass of the effect lightly on each frame
   before details, so the total size stays under control.
3. **Draw forward**: each frame is derived from the last - shapes split, stretch, curl, and
   dissipate. Flame tongues rise and taper; water splashes climb, slow at the apex, fall
   with gravity spacing.
4. **Check on ones, every 6-8 drawings**: flip or play. Correct drift before continuing.
5. **Timing**: fire usually on twos for 2D (on ones it can look nervous); splashes on ones at
   the fast start, twos at the hang.

## Fire specifics
- Flames: a base that stays put, tongues that rise, detach, shrink and vanish.
- Cycle lengths of 8-12 drawings are typical; break the loop with irregular detachments.

## Water specifics
- Crown splash: rises fast (wide spacing), slows, breaks into droplets that fall on arcs.
- Keep the droplets' spacing consistent with gravity on the same scale as the characters.

## CG equivalent
Straight-ahead in CG is simulation or keying frame by frame; for stylized 2D-look FX, many
studios hand-draw straight ahead over a 3D layout (Blender Grease Pencil, Toon Boom Harmony).

## Failure modes
- Growing or shrinking volume without intent.
- Missing the hit point the action had to reach (match points with the character animation).
- Everything evenly timed: no accents.

## Verify
- [ ] Silhouette mass tracked frame to frame (no unintentional growth).
- [ ] The effect reaches its planned end shape on the planned frame.
- [ ] Played at speed it feels spontaneous, with clear accents.
