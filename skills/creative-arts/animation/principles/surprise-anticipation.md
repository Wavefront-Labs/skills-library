# Surprise anticipation

Goal: use the audience's expectation of anticipation against them. Thomas and Johnston note
that the audience should know what is coming unless you want to surprise them - this is the
"unless" case.

## Three patterns
1. **No anticipation** - the action happens with zero warning (an anvil lands, a character
   is yanked off-screen). Works only if the audience is looking at the spot already; stage
   the area first, then hit it.
2. **False anticipation** - a big wind-up that results in a tiny action (huge pitcher wind-up,
   ball dribbles out). The gag is the gap between expected and actual size.
3. **Reverse anticipation** - the wind-up goes one way, the action goes another (the character
   readies to dodge left, gets hit from the right).

## Making it read
- The *result* must be held long enough for the audience to register it, because they had no
  warning: hold 12-24 frames on the aftermath, longer than a normal settle.
- Keep the camera still. Surprise needs a stable frame of reference.
- Precede the surprise with a calm, steady beat so the change is maximal.
- For false anticipation, make the wind-up clearly over-sized (2-3x normal) so the audience
  commits to the expectation.

## Failure modes
- Surprising where the audience is not looking: they miss it, it is not a surprise.
- Short hold after an un-anticipated action: confusion instead of a laugh.
- Using it everywhere: the audience stops trusting any wind-up.

## Verify
- [ ] Test screening or a fresh viewer: they react at the intended frame.
- [ ] The aftermath hold is long enough to read at playback speed.
- [ ] Only the intended gags break the anticipation rule.
