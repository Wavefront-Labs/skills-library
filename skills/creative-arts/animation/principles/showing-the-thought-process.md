# Showing the thought process

Goal: the audience believes the character is thinking. Thomas and Johnston single out showing
the thought behind the action as what made Disney characters come alive; Ed Hooks' Acting for
Animators applies Stanislavski: thinking leads to emotion, emotion leads to action.

## Plan before animating
For the shot, write:
1. **Objective** - what the character wants in this scene.
2. **Obstacle** - what is in the way.
3. **Beats** - each change of tactic or thought (usually 1-4 per shot).
4. **For each beat**: what the character thinks, what they feel, what they do.

## Animating thought
- **Thought precedes action**: the face/eyes change before the body (see anticipating a thought change).
- **Eyes are the window**: eye direction changes, focus, darts and blinks show processing.
- **Stillness** can be thought - a held pose with eye movement says "thinking" more than gestures.
- **Each beat looks different**: a new thought means a new pose, rhythm or tempo.
- **Contrast**: thinking vs doing - slow, inward beats versus fast, outward beats.

## Shot-planning tools
- Act it out and film yourself (several takes, pick the honest one).
- Thumbnail one drawing per beat with the thought written underneath.
- Check against the line: is the character reacting, planning, hiding something?

## Failure modes
- Choreography: the character performs gestures with no thought between them.
- Illustrating each word with a gesture (pantomiming the dialogue).
- Same attitude throughout a shot with several beats.

## Verify
- [ ] Beat list written with thought per beat.
- [ ] Each beat has a distinct physical state.
- [ ] Muted playback: viewer can describe what the character is thinking at each beat.
