# Pantomime vs illustrative gesture

Goal: clear acting through body language - strong pantomime when there are no words, and
restraint (no "sign language") when there are.

## Pantomime
Animation's roots are silent pantomime (Chaplin, Keaton; Disney's early shorts). When a scene
has no dialogue, the body must carry the story:
- Clear, staged actions with readable silhouettes.
- Larger, deliberate gestures; one idea at a time.
- Props as storytelling (checking a watch = waiting).
- Strong contrast between beats.

## Illustrative (literal) gesture - the trap
Literal gestures mimic the words: "big" with arms spread, "me" pointing to chest, "over there"
pointing. Williams and most supervisors flag it as amateur when it accompanies every phrase.
- It tells the audience what the voice already told them.
- It hides the character's feelings (what they *mean*) behind what they *say*.

## Rules with dialogue
1. Gesture the **subtext or attitude**, not the text.
2. **Budget**: one main gesture per phrase or per thought, not per word.
3. Allow literal gestures only when the character would naturally use them (a showman, a
   child, a sarcastic beat), and then make them specific.
4. Consider no gesture: a still body with good eyes is often stronger.

## Failure modes
- Hands constantly moving during dialogue.
- Gestures that start and end exactly with words.
- Pantomime scenes with tiny, subtle acting that cannot be read in a wide shot.

## Verify
- [ ] Muted playback: dialogue shots show attitude, not word illustrations.
- [ ] Silent shots tell the story without audio.
- [ ] Gesture count per phrase is low and deliberate.
