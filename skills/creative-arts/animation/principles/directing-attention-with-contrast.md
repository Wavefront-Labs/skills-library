# Directing attention with contrast

Goal: control where the audience looks, frame by frame. The eye goes to the point of greatest
contrast; Bruce Block's contrast-and-affinity principle and Mateu-Mestre's focal-point rules
both reduce to this.

## Attention magnets, strongest first (for moving images)
1. **Movement** - the one thing moving in a still frame wins; the one still thing in a
   busy frame also wins.
2. **Faces and eyes** - especially a face turning toward camera.
3. **Value contrast** - the lightest light against the darkest dark.
4. **Saturation/color contrast** - one saturated color in a muted field.
5. **Size and position** - larger, closer to frame centre or on a thirds point.
6. **Eyelines and pointing** - characters looking or gesturing at something send the eye there.

## Method
1. Mark where the eye must be at each beat of the shot (a dot on each key frame).
2. At each beat, make that spot win on at least two magnets (e.g. movement + value).
3. Quiet the rest: background characters on moving holds, lower contrast away from the focus.
4. **Hand-offs**: move attention with a motion that travels (a look, a throw, a pan) rather
   than a jump. Across cuts, put the focus of the incoming shot near where the outgoing
   shot's focus was ("eye trace").

## Failure modes
- Competing movement: background loops or cloth sims pulling the eye from the acting.
- Brightest value on an unimportant prop.
- Eye trace jump at a cut: focus left of frame, then far right - the audience misses the first
  half-second of the new shot.

## Verify
- [ ] Watch the shot once with a fresh viewer and ask where they looked; compare with the dots.
- [ ] Blur the frame heavily: the focal spot still stands out.
- [ ] Across cuts, focus positions are within about a third of the frame of each other unless
      a jolt is intended.
