# Reaction shots and cutting for performance

**Goal:** the cut shows the audience the face that carries the emotion at that moment, which is often the
listener's, not the speaker's.

## When to cut to the listener

- The line's meaning is in how it lands: an insult, a reveal, a joke whose punchline is the other character's face.
- The speaker's next line is known to the audience; staying on the speaker adds nothing.
- To compress dialogue: over a reaction you can tighten the off-screen line's pauses, which you cannot do on-screen
  without breaking lip-sync.

## How long

- A clear expression change needs about **12-16 frames** at 24 fps to read; a slow burn or take in comedy may hold
  1-3 seconds, and the hold is the joke.
- Cut **in** on the reaction a few frames **before** it starts (the audience sees the face change); cutting after
  the change hides the acting.
- Out of a reaction, cut on the next line's first syllable or a look (cutting on the look motivates the cut).

## Animation-specific cost

Every reaction shot is a shot to board, lay out and animate. In animatic stage, a reaction may be a held board; ask
whether it needs full acting or can be a held pose with a blink (cheap) and note it for the producer
(see `creative-arts.animation.pipeline.producing-scheduling`). Reusing a reaction from another sequence is a common
saving, but watch continuity (lighting, props).

## Comedy rules of thumb

- Rule of three, and the reaction comes after the third beat.
- A cutaway reaction gets a bigger laugh than the same reaction in a two-shot because the cut itself is a beat.
- Do not step on the laugh: leave the reaction long enough for an audience response in a screening, then trim.

## Verify

In a test screening (see `creative-arts.animation.editorial.test-screenings`), the reaction beats get the response;
in the lock cut, every reaction shot has a documented purpose in the cutting notes.
