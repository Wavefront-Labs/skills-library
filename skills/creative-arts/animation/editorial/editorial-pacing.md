# Editorial pacing — rhythm, shot length, and when to cut

Pacing is the manipulation of *time* in the cut: how long each shot holds, when the cut
lands, and how the rhythm of cuts makes an audience feel tension, ease, dread, or delight.
It is the editor's primary emotional instrument and it is decided first in the story reel
(`story-reel-editorial`), where it is cheap to change. This skill is the specific craft of
pacing a cut well — the decisions a feature editor makes shot by shot. It assumes shots
assembled to scratch audio; it feeds `continuity-supervision` (which guards the cuts you
make) and inherits beats from `shot-episode-structure`.

## 1. The governing rule: pace serves emotion, not the clock

There is no correct shot length — only a length correct *for the feeling*. Before timing a
sequence, name its emotional target in one word (dread, panic, calm, joy, grief). The
target dictates the rhythm:

- **Tension/panic:** shorter shots, accelerating cut rate, cuts that arrive slightly before
  the eye is done — the audience feels hurried.
- **Calm/intimacy/grief:** longer holds, slow cut rate, letting shots breathe past
  comfortable so the audience sits in the moment.
- **A sequence's pace should *change*.** A flat cut rate anaesthetizes. Build rhythm:
  accelerate into a climax, then cut to a long held shot for the release. Contrast is what
  the audience feels.

## 2. Shot length and the hold-vs-cut decision

Cut when the shot has delivered its information and emotion; hold when it is still
delivering. Practical decision for each shot:

1. **What is this shot's job** (one piece of info or one beat of feeling)? When that job is
   done, the shot is spent.
2. **Cut on the beat after it lands**, not in the middle of it — but not long after, or the
   shot dies and the audience's attention leaks.
3. **Hold longer than comfortable, deliberately, for weight.** A held shot after a fast
   sequence is a punctuation mark; a reaction held an extra beat makes the audience *lean
   in*. The uncomfortable hold is a tool, used sparingly.
4. **The 3-second instinct is a myth** — dialogue reaction shots can be 12 frames, an
   establishing vista 6 seconds. Length follows job and feeling, measured in frames at the
   show rate.

## 3. Cutting on action and on motivation

Cuts hide where the eye is already moving and land where the audience *wants* to look next.

- **Cut on action.** Match-cut on a movement (a hand reaching, a turn, a sit-down): start
  the action in shot A, complete it in shot B, overlapping a few frames. The motion carries
  the eye across the cut and the edit becomes invisible. This is the default for coverage.
- **Cut on motivation (eyeline / reaction).** When a character looks off-screen, the
  audience wants to see what they see — cut there. Motivated cuts feel inevitable;
  unmotivated cuts feel like editing.
- **Avoid the jump on a static hold.** Cutting between two near-identical static framings
  reads as a glitch; either change the size/angle enough (the ~30° / size-step rule) or cut
  on a movement.

## 4. J-cuts and L-cuts (split edits) — overlapping sound and picture

Sound and picture need not cut together; staggering them is what makes a cut feel natural
and a scene flow.

- **J-cut:** the *audio* of the next shot starts *before* its picture (you hear the next
  scene/line while still seeing the current image). Pulls the audience forward; great for
  scene transitions and leading into a reaction.
- **L-cut:** the audio of the current shot *continues over* the picture of the next (you've
  cut to the listener while the speaker's line finishes). The staple of dialogue editing —
  cut to reactions while the line plays so you watch *listening*, not just talking.
- **Rule of thumb:** in a dialogue scene, cut picture to the person *reacting* as often as
  to the person *talking*; the L-cut is how. Hard sync-cuts (sound and picture together) on
  every line make a scene feel mechanical.

## 5. Comedic vs. dramatic timing

The same beat cut two ways lands as a joke or as a gut-punch.

- **Comedy wants the cut to be a beat *snappier* than feels safe.** Cut *out* of a gag the
  instant it lands — lingering kills it. The reaction shot (the deadpan, the take) is often
  the joke; time it precisely: a held pause *then* the reaction (the "double-take beat") vs.
  an instant cut gives completely different laughs. Comedic timing is often about the
  **hold before the punchline** and the **quick cut after it**.
- **Drama wants the cut *later*.** Let the reaction breathe; hold on the face past comfort so
  the emotion registers. Cutting away too soon from a dramatic beat feels like you flinched.
- **Rhythm of threes.** Comedy and montage both exploit the three-beat pattern (set-up,
  repeat, break); pace the third beat differently (longer hold, or snap cut) to land it.

## 6. Breathing room and the overall shape

- **Give the audience rest.** After an intense or information-dense sequence, a quiet held
  shot (a landscape, a slow reaction, silence) lets the audience absorb it. Relentless pace
  exhausts; the quiet beats are what make the loud ones hit.
- **Front-load clarity, not speed.** Establish a scene's geography with enough hold that the
  audience is oriented before you accelerate; cutting fast before orientation reads as
  confusion, not energy.
- **Shape the whole, not just the scene.** An episode/film has a macro-rhythm — vary
  sequence pacing so the viewer rides waves, not a flat line. Map the cut rate across the
  whole and look for a long stretch at one tempo; that's where attention dies.

## 7. Working the cut in the reel

Because pacing is set in the reel, iterate there cheaply:

1. Rough the sequence to scratch audio at first-guess timing.
2. Watch it for *feeling* first (does it tense/land/breathe?), not for technical cuts.
3. Tighten: remove frames at the head/tail of shots (most cuts improve by trimming a few
   frames off the front — the "trim the fat" pass); add holds where weight is needed.
4. Pass J/L cuts through the dialogue; move picture cuts off the sync points.
5. Re-watch whole for macro-rhythm; flag any flat tempo stretch and vary it.
6. Lock timing and push frame counts to the floor only once the *feeling* is right.

## 8. Worked example — pacing a two-hander confrontation

A dialogue confrontation between two characters, cut for rising tension then release:

1. **Open wide and hold.** Establish both characters and the room in a wide, held ~4–6
   seconds — the audience must own the geography before the cutting starts. Pace here is
   calm; the slow open makes the acceleration felt.
2. **Settle into shot/reverse with L-cuts.** As the argument builds, cut to reactions on the
   L-cut — picture to the listener while the speaker's line finishes — so we watch the barb
   land, not just the barb thrown. Shots run 2–4 seconds, cutting on the line beats.
3. **Accelerate into the peak.** As it heats, shorten shots (down to ~12–24 frames on the
   sharpest exchanges), tighten the framings (push to close-ups), and cut on the overlaps of
   their movements. The accelerating cut rate *is* the rising temperature.
4. **The snap or the hold at the climax.** The decisive beat — a slap, a confession, a door
   slam — lands on a cut-on-action. Then make the choice: snap-cut straight out for shock, or
   hold on the aftermath face past comfort for weight. Drama almost always wants the hold.
5. **Release to a long quiet shot.** Cut to a wide or an empty hold — the room after, a
   landscape, silence — held long. This is the breathing room; the length of the quiet is
   proportional to how loud the peak was.

The whole scene rides one wave: slow → accelerate → peak → long release. The audience feels
the shape even though they never count a cut. Every length here is a *feeling* decision made
in the reel against scratch audio, then handed to the floor as frame counts.

## Verify

- [ ] Each sequence has a **named emotional target** and its cut rate/shot length serves it;
  the pace **changes** across the sequence rather than staying flat.
- [ ] Each shot's length matches its **job and feeling** (measured in frames at show rate),
  cut just after the beat lands or held deliberately for weight.
- [ ] Coverage cuts mostly **cut on action** (overlapping movement) or **on motivation**
  (eyeline/reaction); no unmotivated jumps on static near-identical framings.
- [ ] Dialogue uses **J/L cuts** so picture cuts to reactions, not only to talkers; cuts
  aren't all hard-synced to lines.
- [ ] Comedic beats are **snapped out of after the gag** (and use the pause-then-reaction
  where needed); dramatic beats are **held past comfort**.
- [ ] The cut has **breathing-room** beats after intense stretches, establishes geography
  before accelerating, and has **no long flat-tempo stretch** across the whole.
- [ ] Pacing was **worked in the reel** and judged by feeling before frame counts were
  locked to the floor.
