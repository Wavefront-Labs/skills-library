# Chase sequences

Goal: chases with tension the audience can follow - who is ahead, by how much, and what is in
the way.

## Rules
1. **Screen direction**: both pursuer and pursued move the same direction on screen (e.g. left
   to right). Changing direction requires a visible turn or neutral shot.
2. **The gap**: the core of a chase is the distance between them - show it shrinking and
   growing with shots that contain both, and with cross-cutting.
3. **Obstacles and set-pieces**: each section of the chase has a distinct obstacle (a market,
   a bridge, traffic) that creates a new problem and a gag or thrill.
4. **Geography**: the audience should know where the goal is (escape point) - establish it.
5. **Escalation**: speed, danger and stakes increase.

## Shot patterns
- Tracking shots alongside (speed).
- Head-on shots of the pursued with the pursuer behind (gap).
- POV shots from the pursuer (threat).
- Overhead shots for complex routes.
- Cross-cutting between them, shortening shots as the gap closes.

## Method
1. Map the route and obstacles on a plan.
2. Beat list: start, obstacles, near-misses, reversals, end.
3. Board with screen direction arrows on each panel.

## Failure modes
- Pursuer and pursued running opposite directions on screen.
- Repetitive obstacles.
- Gap never shown - no tension.

## Verify
- [ ] Screen direction consistent across the chase.
- [ ] Gap visible and changing.
- [ ] Each section has a distinct obstacle and escalation.
