# Bird's-eye and worm's-eye views

Goal: use extreme vertical angles for clarity of layout, graphic impact, awe or menace.

## Bird's-eye (directly overhead or near)
- **Clarifies geography**: maze, chase routes, positions in a fight, a table of characters.
- **Graphic**: turns a scene into pattern and shape (Busby Berkeley, Wes Anderson top-downs).
- **Detachment / fate**: characters seen as small, observed from above.
- Draw: one-point perspective looking down; verticals converge to a point below (the ground).

## Worm's-eye (from ground looking up)
- **Awe or menace**: towering buildings, giants, a hero standing over the camera.
- **Comedy**: something about to step on the camera.
- Draw: three-point perspective with verticals converging upward; horizon very low or below
  frame.

## Cutting in and out
- Use a bird's-eye as a re-establishing shot in complex action, then cut back to eye level.
- Avoid consecutive extreme angles of different kinds without a bridge - it disorients.
- Keep screen direction consistent: in a top view, left-right still matches the eye-level coverage.

## Failure modes
- Overhead shots that lose the characters' faces when emotion matters.
- Worm's-eye on characters who should feel vulnerable.
- Perspective errors - verticals not converging in extreme angles.

## Verify
- [ ] Each extreme angle has a clear purpose (geography, awe, menace, graphic).
- [ ] Screen direction consistent with adjacent shots.
- [ ] Perspective verticals converge correctly.
