# Over-the-shoulder coverage

Goal: cover a two-person conversation so the audience feels the relationship and always knows
who is where.

## The OTS shot
- Camera behind one character's shoulder, looking at the other's face.
- Foreground shoulder/head occupies about 1/4-1/3 of frame, out of focus or darker, cropped at
  the frame edge.
- The facing character is placed on the thirds line opposite the foreground shoulder.

## Shot / reverse shot
1. Set the line between the two characters in an establishing two-shot.
2. OTS on A (over B's shoulder), reverse OTS on B (over A's shoulder) - both cameras on the same
   side of the line.
3. **Match** the two: same shot size, similar lens/angle, mirrored composition. Mismatched sizes
   suggest a power difference - use that deliberately.

## OTS vs clean single
- **OTS**: keeps the characters connected; good for intimate or confrontational talk.
- **Clean single** (no shoulder): isolates; used when a character is emotionally alone, or to
  intensify.
- Moving from OTS to clean singles as a conversation grows hostile is a classic progression.

## Boarding tips
- Draw the foreground shoulder simply - silhouette or a dark shape.
- Keep eyelines: A looks frame right, B looks frame left (or vice versa) consistently.
- Number setups (A, B) so editorial can reuse panels for repeated angles.

## Failure modes
- Crossing the line on the reverse: both characters look the same direction.
- Foreground shoulder too big, blocking the face.
- Unmatched shot sizes without intent.

## Verify
- [ ] Eyelines oppose each other across the cut.
- [ ] Reverse shots matched in size unless a power shift is intended.
- [ ] The pattern changes at the scene's turning point.
