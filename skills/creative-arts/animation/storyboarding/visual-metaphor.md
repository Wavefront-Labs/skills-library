# Visual metaphor

Goal: images that show what the scene means, not only what happens - Glebas' "visual
storytelling" emphasises showing the inner state through the picture.

## Sources of metaphor
- **Staging**: characters separated by a wall, a pillar, a gap = emotional distance; one
  character in light, the other in shadow.
- **Framing**: a character boxed in by door frames = trapped; tiny in a vast space = lost.
- **Environment/weather**: storm building as conflict grows (use sparingly - can be cliché).
- **Objects**: a wilting plant, a broken toy, an empty chair at a table.
- **Height/level**: the dominant character higher in frame.
- **Composition lines**: diagonal, unstable compositions for turmoil; horizontals for peace.

## Method
1. State the scene's emotional idea in one sentence ("They have grown apart").
2. Brainstorm 5-10 visual ways to express it; discard the first obvious ones.
3. Choose one that fits the world and the tone; build it into staging and layout.
4. Repeat or evolve it across the film for a motif (e.g. separation by a barrier, then the barrier removed).

## Failure modes
- Heavy-handed symbolism that the characters seem to notice.
- Metaphors contradicting the scene's tone.
- Too many metaphors at once.

## Verify
- [ ] Viewers sense the emotional idea from a still frame.
- [ ] Metaphor grounded in the scene's world (not pasted on).
- [ ] Used consistently if part of a motif.
