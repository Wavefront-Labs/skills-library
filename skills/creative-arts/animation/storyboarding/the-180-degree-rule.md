# The 180-degree rule

Goal: keep the audience oriented. If character A is on the left and B on the right in one
shot, they stay that way in every shot of the scene unless the camera visibly moves across.

## The line
- The **line of action** (axis) runs through the two characters (or along a moving
  character's direction of travel).
- All camera positions stay within the 180-degree half-circle on one side of it.
- Result: A always looks frame right, B frame left; a runner always moves the same screen direction.

## Method
1. In the scene's establishing panel, mark the line (draw it on the overhead sketch).
2. Draw a quick overhead floor plan with camera positions as arrows for each panel (Glebas
   recommends this for every complicated scene).
3. Check every camera is on the same side.
4. When the line must change (character moves, someone new enters, a turn in the scene),
   re-establish it - see crossing the line legitimately.

## Multiple characters
- Each pair in conversation has its own line; in groups, choose the line between the two
  characters speaking at that moment and keep consistency within each exchange.
- Around a table, a central axis often works for most coverage.

## Failure modes
- Reverse shot taken from the opposite side: both characters appear to face the same direction.
- Chase where the pursuer is shown running the opposite direction from the pursued.
- Line jumps hidden in a sequence of close-ups (still disorienting).

## Verify
- [ ] Overhead diagram shows all cameras on one side of the line per exchange.
- [ ] Eyelines oppose across shot/reverse shots.
- [ ] Line changes are motivated and shown.
