# Style frames for the pitch — selling the show, not directing the build

A **style frame** and a **keyframe** (`concept-art` section 5) look similar — both are
finished paintings — but they serve different audiences and fail in different ways. A
keyframe is for the crew: it's a lighting/comp north star, allowed to assume context
(the viewer already knows the story). A style frame is for a buyer, exec, or investor
who has never seen the project before and will judge it in the **three seconds** it's
on screen in a deck or sizzle reel. This skill is that audience-facing deliverable; it
specializes `concept-art` for pitch packaging and is distinct from both
`character-visdev-exploration` (internal option sheets, never shown to a buyer) and
`prop-and-environment-visdev` (build-target scale/material sheets). Unconfirmed where
noted — pitch conventions vary by buyer/market (streamer vs. network vs. festival) and
this reflects commonly-observed practice, not a universal standard.

## 1. A style frame must stand alone with zero context
Nobody reading it has seen the script, the bible, or the character bios. Every frame
must answer, unaided: what genre is this, what's the tone (comedic/dramatic/dark/warm),
and who is this about — in the time it takes to glance at a deck slide. A frame that
needs a caption to explain its mood has failed; fix the frame, don't add the caption as
a crutch (a one-line title under it is fine, a paragraph of explanation is not).

## 2. Pick a small, deliberately varied set — not everything you made
Internal vis-dev produces dozens of thumbnails and sheets; a pitch deck shows roughly
4-8 style frames, each earning its slot by doing a DIFFERENT job (unconfirmed exact
count — varies by deck length and buyer, but packing more than ~8 dilutes attention).
A workable spread:
1. **The hero/world frame** — the single image that IS the show if someone only sees
   one picture (this is often reused as the deck cover and later as key art).
2. **A character-defining frame** — the lead in a pose/moment that shows who they are,
   not a neutral turnaround.
3. **A tone-range frame or two** — if the show swings between comedy and heart, or
   cozy and scary, show BOTH registers; a buyer judging tonal range from one frame
   alone will guess wrong.
4. **An action/energy frame** — proves the show can deliver spectacle or momentum, not
   just stillness.
5. **A relationship/emotional-beat frame** — two characters, a quiet moment; proves the
   show has heart, not just design.
Order them to tell a mini-story across the deck (establish world → meet the lead → show
the range → land the feeling), since buyers read a deck's frame sequence like a trailer.

## 3. Never show a fidelity you cannot build
The single most damaging failure mode: a buyer greenlights against a style frame's
promised fidelity (character detail, lighting complexity, FX density), then the
delivered pipeline can't hit it at episode budget/schedule. Paint the style frame at
roughly the finish level production can actually sustain per episode, not a one-off
painting-quality best-case. If a frame uses a rendering trick (hand-painted light
rays, a matte-painted background) that the real pipeline can't reproduce at volume,
either prove the pipeline can fake it convincingly first, or don't put it in the deck.
This is a business risk, not just an art one — flag it to the showrunner/producer if a
frame is aspirational past what's budgeted.

## 4. Style consistency across frames beats individual polish
Buyers notice when frames look like they came from different projects — inconsistent
line weight, palette logic, or render finish reads as "the show doesn't have a style
yet," even if each frame alone is beautiful. Lock a shared finish (same brush/render
pass, same palette discipline per `concept-art` section 3, same edge/line treatment)
across every frame in the set before finishing any one of them individually.

## 5. Format for the actual deck/sizzle
Match the delivery: full-bleed 16:9 for a slide deck or video sizzle (safe margins for
any overlaid title/logo), leave negative space where a title card or exec's pointer
will land, and export at a resolution that survives a screen-share without compression
artifacting. A gorgeous frame that pixelates on a shared screen loses the room.

## Gotchas
- Don't reuse an internal keyframe as-is for the deck without checking it reads
  context-free — keyframes often assume story knowledge a buyer doesn't have.
- Overpromising fidelity is the costliest mistake in this skill; when in doubt, show
  the pipeline-achievable version and say so, rather than the aspirational one.
- Too many frames of the same register (five moody frames, no range) under-sells a
  show that actually has range; deliberately diversify tone across the set.
- A style frame set with no consistent finish undercuts buyer confidence even when
  individual frames are strong — fix this before adding more frames, not after.

## Verify
- [ ] Each frame reads genre + tone + who-it's-about with zero outside context, in a
      few seconds.
- [ ] The set (4-8 frames) deliberately covers a hero/world shot, a character-defining
      moment, tonal range, energy, and an emotional beat — not five versions of one mood.
- [ ] Every frame's promised fidelity is one production can sustain per episode at
      budget, or is explicitly flagged to the producer as aspirational.
- [ ] All frames in the set share one finish/palette discipline — a buyer can tell
      they're the same show.
- [ ] Exported format/resolution matches the actual deck or sizzle delivery and
      survives a screen-share.
