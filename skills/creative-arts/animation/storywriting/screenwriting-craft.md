# SKILL: Screenwriting craft (animated teleplay) — expert

The *talent* of writing episodes people can't stop watching. Tool-agnostic; pairs with
`novel-to-teleplay.md` (mechanics of adaptation) and `shotlist-boarding.md`. Benchmark:
Murder Drones / Rick & Morty tier — tight, propulsive, character-true. This skill is
the craft under the format: structure that holds, scenes that turn, dialogue that
hides its intent, and the rewrite that makes it look effortless.

## 1. Structure that holds (per 6-8 min episode)
- **Cold open (45-75s):** a *hook*, not a recap. Open on an image or question that
  creates a gap the viewer needs closed (the orange circling back into the drain).
  Never open on exposition. End the cold open on a small turn, then the title card.
- **Acts (2-3):** each act = one escalation of the central dramatic question. Each act
  ends on an **act-out** — a button: a reveal, a reversal, a worst-possible-timing
  beat — that makes stopping impossible. The act-out is a promise the next act pays.
- **Button/cliff:** the episode's last image answers one question and opens a bigger
  one. Open-ended series (ours) resolve the *episode* question and escalate the
  *series* question; never fully resolve the spine, or there's no episode 2.
- **The 2%/scene rule:** every scene must change the character's situation or our
  understanding by some measurable amount. If you can cut a scene and lose nothing,
  cut it. No scene idles. A scene that only "establishes mood" is a shot, not a scene.

## 2. Scene craft (the unit of the whole thing)
- **One job per scene.** Name the job in a sentence before writing ("Dill learns the
  counter can go down"). If you can't, you don't have a scene yet.
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start at the last possible moment and cut on the turn.
  Never film the handshake and the hello; drop us in mid-collision and get out the
  instant the point lands.
- **Hide the job inside an activity.** Characters doing something physical while the
  real scene happens underneath reads as life, not as a delivery mechanism. Groceries,
  repairs, a meal — the activity is the alibi for the exposition.
- **Value shift (the test of a live scene).** A scene opens on one emotional charge
  (+/−) and must flip by the end. Positive-to-negative, safe-to-threatened,
  hopeful-to-trapped. A scene whose charge is the same at the end as the start is dead;
  find the flip or cut the scene.
- **Conflict is want vs obstacle, not argument.** The strongest scenes aren't fights;
  they're two people wanting the same thing for opposite reasons, each politely in the
  other's way. Overt argument is the weakest, most on-the-nose form of conflict.

## 3. Conflict & stakes (why we lean in)
- **Stakes must be personal AND escalating.** "Cancelled = erased + everyone forgets
  you existed" is personal annihilation, not an abstract loss. Abstract stakes ("the
  town is in danger") don't grip; a specific body on the line does.
- **Raise the cost each act.** If act one risks embarrassment, act two risks the
  relationship, act three risks the self. Flat stakes across acts feel like treading
  water even when plot is happening.
- **Give the antagonist a real point.** Chip isn't evil for sport — he keeps everyone
  "fine." The best villains are right from their own axis; write the scene from their
  belief and the hero's victory costs something.
- **Agency.** The protagonist must *drive* — walk toward the alley — not merely react.
  A reactive hero makes a passive episode.

## 4. Dialogue (spoken ≠ written)
- **Subtext over text.** Characters rarely say what they mean. Chip: "you want to be
  kept, don't you?" — menace wrapped in care. The line the character would never say is
  usually the true one; write around it. Never let a character state the theme aloud
  ("this is about the attention economy") — instant death.
- **Voice-print per character.** Distinct rhythm and lexicon so you could ID the speaker
  with names stripped. Keep a line-voice note. Chip = bright TV-host warmth with a
  one-frame crack; Dill = clipped, counting, sensible; Pidge = fast sideways science;
  Gran Wynn = dry mercy. Give each a tell: a filler word, a sentence length, a thing
  they never do.
- **Economy — cut to the ear.** Read every line ALOUD and trim to the fewest words
  that land. Contractions, interruptions, fragments. No one monologues unless the
  monologue *is* the event.
- **Trailing/overlap as direction.** Real talk overlaps and trails. Use "—" (cut off)
  and "…" (trail) deliberately; they are performance direction for the voice actor /
  TTS delivery, and they shape VO prosody and pause length.
- **No "As you know, Bob."** Never have characters tell each other what they both
  already know for the audience's benefit. Give exposition to the one who *doesn't*
  know, or to an image.

## 5. Action lines (the half of the script people forget)
- **Only what the camera sees and the mic hears.** Present tense, active, concrete.
  "The counter ticks to 2." Not "Dill realizes he's being watched" — that's a note,
  not a shot.
- **Short paragraphs, one image each.** White space is pacing; a wall of action text
  reads slow and renders slow. One beat, one line, line break.
- **Verbs carry the tone.** "The door *sighs* open" vs "the door opens" — the verb is
  the mood, costing zero extra shots.
- **Capitalize first appearances and key SFX** so the boarding/asset pass can scan for
  new characters, props, and sound cues.
- **Don't direct the camera in prose.** Put camera intent in `[CAMERA: push-in]` tags,
  not buried in action, so the shotlist inherits it cleanly.

## 6. Beats & propulsion
- **Beat it out before scenes.** A beat sheet (one line per story turn) is cheap to
  rearrange; a drafted scene is expensive. Reorder beats until the escalation is
  monotonic, then write.
- **Question density.** Always keep an open question pulling forward. Answer one, open
  one; never let the viewer feel caught up.
- **Vary rhythm.** Fast, cut-heavy beats against one held, quiet beat. All-fast numbs;
  all-slow drags. The quiet beat right before the act-out hits hardest.
- **Plant & payoff.** Plant early and casual; pay off later, inevitable-but-surprising
  (the counter always in Dill's blind spot → revealed → retro-recognized). Keep a
  plant/payoff list so none dangle and none pay off unplanted.

## 7. Show, don't tell (animation-specific)
- **Interiority → behaviour + image.** You cannot film a feeling. Dread = hands flat,
  flat affect, counting. The metric counter *shows* "being watched" with zero
  narration — the single highest-leverage adaptation move.
- **One image per beat.** Find the ONE picture that carries each story beat (orange in
  drain = the loop; needle on 1 = the real audience; green seam = the lie). Design the
  episode as a sequence of indelible images.
- **Visual irony.** Let picture and sound disagree (sunny town + horror hum).

## 8. Theme (keep it submerged)
State the theme once, to yourself, in a sentence ("attention given freely vs extracted
makes you real"). Then never say it on screen. Dramatize it; let the audience arrive.
If a character announces the theme, delete the line — the restraint is the craft.

## 9. The rewrite (first drafts are for finding the story)
Writing is rewriting. Make targeted passes, not one vague "polish":
1. **Structure pass:** does every scene turn? Cut idle scenes; resequence for
   escalation. Check every act ends on a button.
2. **Dialogue pass:** read the whole thing ALOUD. Cut on-the-nose lines; convert stated
   feelings to subtext; verify each voice-print with names stripped.
3. **Compression pass:** cut 10% on principle. Enter later, leave earlier. Trim action
   to one image per beat.
4. **Theme pass:** delete any line that announces meaning; confirm it's dramatized.
5. **Runtime pass:** reconcile against measured VO durations, not page count; trim or
   split to land 6-8 min.

## Self-check (run on every draft)
- [ ] Cold open hooks in <75s with an image, not info?
- [ ] Every scene changes something and flips its charge; cut any that don't.
- [ ] Every act ends on a button?
- [ ] Each character distinguishable with names stripped (read aloud)?
- [ ] Any line stating the theme/feeling directly → cut or subtext it.
- [ ] Every feeling rendered as behaviour/image, not narration?
- [ ] All plants paid off, all payoffs planted?
- [ ] Episode question resolved, series question escalated, ending open?
- [ ] Read the whole thing aloud — does it drag anywhere? Cut there.

## Pitfalls (hard-won)
- Writing prose-with-sluglines: teleplay is only what camera sees / mic hears.
- On-the-nose dialogue: the #1 amateur tell. Subtext or cut.
- Idle scenes kept for "world flavour": world through action, never tourism.
- Resolving the series question in episode 1: kills the reason to watch Ep2.
- Polishing before structure is right: rewriting lines in a scene that should be cut.
