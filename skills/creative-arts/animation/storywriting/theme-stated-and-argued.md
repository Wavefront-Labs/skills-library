# Theme stated and argued

Goal: a story with a clear meaning that the hero discovers, rather than a lesson announced.

## 1. Theme stated (Snyder)
- Early (about 5% in; page 5 of 110), someone - usually not the hero - says something that poses the
  theme: a question or statement the hero doesn't yet understand.
- It's often casual and overlooked; the story proves or disproves it.

## 2. Argue both sides
- The theme is an argument, not a moral: characters embody different positions.
- The antagonist often represents the counter-argument at its strongest.
- The B-story character often represents the truth the hero needs.

## 3. Dramatise, don't preach
- No character should state the moral at the end. Let the climax demonstrate it.
- Check scenes: does each argue for or against the theme? Cut irrelevant ones.

## 4. Writing method
1. Write the theme as a question ("Is it better to be safe or to be free?").
2. Write the controlling idea answer (value + cause).
3. Place theme stated in the setup; map scenes to arguments for and against.

## Verify
- [ ] Theme stated early by a non-hero character.
- [ ] Both sides represented by characters.
- [ ] Climax demonstrates the answer without a speech.
