# SKILL: Story development (the stage before the script)

Development is the work that happens **before a single slug line is written**. It is
where an idea is pressure-tested, shaped into a spine, and either killed cheaply or
graduated to script. In a production pipeline — a locked-canon 3D cel series where every shot
costs render time and every asset is reused — the cheapest place to fix a story is
here, in text, at the premise stage. A bad idea caught in development costs a
paragraph; caught in the reel it costs a week; caught in render it costs the slot.

Distinct from `screenwriting-craft` (which writes the actual teleplay — scenes,
dialogue, action) and `novel-to-teleplay` (which adapts finished prose canon). This
skill produces the documents a script is built *from*: logline, treatment, beat sheet,
arc, bible. If you are writing EXT. / INT. slugs, you have left development and should
be in `screenwriting-craft`.

## The ladder: six artifacts, cheapest first
Develop in order of increasing commitment. Each gate must hold before you spend on the
next. Do not write a 12-page treatment for a premise that can't survive one sentence.

1. **Premise** — one sentence of situation + central irony.
2. **Logline** — one sentence that sells: protagonist, goal, obstacle, stakes.
3. **Theme line** — the argument the episode makes about the world.
4. **Beat sheet** — 12-18 beats, the spine in order.
5. **Treatment** — 1-3 pages of prose, present tense, scene by scene.
6. **Pitch** — the 90-second verbal case plus the bible entry.

## Premise: situation + irony in one sentence
A premise is not a topic ("an episode about trust"). It is a *situation with a built-in
reversal*. Template: **"A [character] who [defining trait] must [do X], but [the trait
is exactly what makes X impossible]."** The irony is the engine.

> Weak: "Ivy investigates the old mill." (topic, no engine)
> Strong: "Ivy, who only trusts what he can prove, has to take a stranger's word to
> stop the flood — and the stranger is the one person he's already caught lying."

Test the premise with three questions. If any answer is no, the idea is not ready:
- **Does it force a choice?** A premise with no decision is a travelogue.
- **Is the irony inside the character, not just the plot?** External irony
  (coincidence) is cheap; the reversal must cost the protagonist something internal.
- **Could only THIS character be in it?** If you can swap the lead for any other
  cast member with no change, you have a plot, not a story.

## Logline: the sales sentence
The logline is the premise weaponized for a buyer (here: future-you, the board, the
reel). Four slots, no more: **protagonist (with the trait), goal, obstacle, stakes.**

> "A proof-obsessed boy must trust the town liar to open the floodgate before the
> valley drowns — or lose the only people who ever vouched for him."

Rules learned the hard way:
- Name the **trait**, not the name. "A proof-obsessed boy" tells the reader the
  internal conflict; "Ivy" tells them nothing until episode 10.
- The obstacle is a **force with a will**, not weather. "The floodgate is jammed" is a
  task; "the only person who can open it won't" is a story.
- Stakes must be **concrete and losable**. "The valley drowns" is abstract; "lose the
  people who vouched for him" is losable by this specific character.

## Theme: the argument, stated as a sentence you could disagree with
Theme is not a word ("trust," "family"). A word is a subject; a theme is a *claim*.
Write it as: **"This episode argues that ___."** A good theme line is contestable — a
reasonable person could hold the opposite.

> Subject: trust.
> Theme: "This episode argues that trust you can't verify is still worth extending —
> because the cost of never extending it is loneliness."

The theme is the invisible rail the arc runs on. The protagonist starts believing the
**opposite** of the theme (the lie they live by) and is forced, by the plot, to act as
if the theme were true. If you can't name the lie and the theme as opposites, the arc
has no shape yet.

## Character arc: the lie, the want, the need, the cost
Development owns the arc skeleton (the script dramatizes it). Four fields per episode
protagonist:
- **The lie** — what they wrongly believe at open ("I only trust proof").
- **The want** — the external, conscious goal ("open the gate myself").
- **The need** — the internal thing they actually lack ("to rely on someone").
- **The cost** — what acting on the theme forces them to surrender.

The arc is the sequence where **want and need diverge**, pressure builds, and the
protagonist chooses the need over the want — paying the cost. In a locked series, map
arcs across the season too: an episode can give a *beat* of a larger arc rather than a
full change. Note which episodes are "change" episodes and which are "test" episodes
(the belief is challenged but holds), so the season doesn't flatline or whiplash.

## Beat sheet: the spine in 12-18 lines
Before prose, lay the load-bearing beats. A workable minimal spine for a 6-8 minute
episode:

1. **Image / world** — establish the normal, show the lie in action.
2. **Inciting incident** — the thing that breaks normal.
3. **Debate** — protagonist resists the call (the lie fights back).
4. **Act-one turn** — commits to the want; crosses into the problem.
5. **Rising tries** — attempts that fail in escalating ways (each failure teaches).
6. **Midpoint** — a reversal: false win or false defeat; stakes raised, want gets harder.
7. **Bad guys close in** — the obstacle presses; allies strain.
8. **All is lost** — the want is dead by old means.
9. **Dark night** — the protagonist confronts the lie directly.
10. **Act-three turn** — chooses the need; commits to the theme.
11. **Climax** — acts on the new belief, pays the cost, resolves the external goal.
12. **Image / new normal** — mirror beat 1, now changed.

Keep each beat to one line of *what happens* (not how it's shot). If a beat needs a
paragraph to explain, it's two beats or it's mush. For data-driven downstream work,
tag each beat with its intended episode/act so it later maps cleanly onto
`shotlist-boarding` and `shot-episode-structure`.

## Treatment: prose that reads like the episode feels
The treatment is 1-3 pages, **present tense, third person, scene order**, no dialogue
except the one line that matters. It is the first artifact where the story *moves* at
reading speed. Its job is to prove the beats connect emotionally, not just logically.
Write it to be read aloud in under five minutes. If a reader's attention drops at beat
7, that's a real signal — fix it here, not in the reel. Mark beats where you're
cheating (a convenient coincidence, an unearned turn); development is the time to
confess and fix, not to paper over.

## World-building bible: the canon ledger
The bible is the shared source of truth that keeps a locked, reused-asset series
consistent across episodes and across whoever writes next. For development, the bible
holds: the rules of the world (what's possible, what isn't, and the *cost* of using
power — abilities without cost kill tension), the cast (trait / lie / want / voice),
the places (named, with a one-line mood and which locked set `.blend` represents them),
the tone bands (how funny, how dark, when music carries), and the continuity ledger
(what changed last episode that constrains this one). The bible is append-only canon:
new episodes *reference* it; they never silently contradict it. A contradiction found
in development is a cheap edit; the same contradiction found after a set is locked and
reused is a reshoot. Treat the bible as the upstream of `character-consistency`.

## Testing an idea cheaply
Before committing, run the idea through fast, free tests:
- **The logline-aloud test.** Say it to a person who doesn't know the show. If they
  can't play it back in one sentence, it's not clear yet.
- **The "so what / and then" test.** Walk the beats. If a beat prompts "so what?" it
  lacks stakes; if it prompts "and then?" with no causal link, the spine is a list, not
  a chain. Beats should connect with "therefore" and "but," never "and then."
- **The swap test.** Replace the protagonist with another cast member. If the story
  survives unchanged, the character isn't load-bearing.
- **The theme-vs-lie test.** State the theme and the opening lie. If they aren't
  opposites, the arc has no gradient.

## What graduates to a teleplay
An idea is ready to hand to `screenwriting-craft` only when **all** of these exist and
hold: a one-sentence logline a stranger can repeat; a contestable theme line; a named
lie/want/need/cost for the protagonist; a beat sheet whose beats connect by
therefore/but; a treatment that reads in under five minutes without an attention drop;
and no unresolved contradiction with the bible. Anything short of that goes back down
the ladder — it is cheaper to cut here than anywhere downstream.

## Verify
A developed episode passes when every box is true:
- [ ] **Logline** fits four slots (trait-protagonist, goal, obstacle-with-will,
      losable stakes) and a stranger can repeat it from one hearing.
- [ ] **Theme** is written as "This episode argues that ___" and is contestable (the
      opposite is arguable).
- [ ] **Lie ≠ theme**: the protagonist's opening belief is the stated opposite of the
      theme.
- [ ] **Arc fields** (lie / want / need / cost) are all filled and the climax shows the
      need chosen over the want.
- [ ] **Beat sheet** has 12-18 beats, each one line, connected by therefore/but (no
      "and then" chains); each beat tagged with its act.
- [ ] **Treatment** reads aloud in under five minutes with no flagged attention drop.
- [ ] **Bible** entry added/updated with no contradiction against prior canon.
- [ ] **Swap test** fails (the protagonist is irreplaceable) and **so-what test**
      passes (every beat has a stake).

If any box is unchecked, it has not graduated. Hand to `screenwriting-craft` only when
all are checked; downstream (`shotlist-boarding`, `story-reel-editorial`) assumes a
clean developed spine.
