# Dub script adaptation and lip-sync fit

A dub script is not a subtitle translation. It must be speakable in the same time as the original line and look acceptable against the animation. In animation, where the mouth is drawn to the source language, the fit matters most in close-ups.

## Steps
1. Start from an accurate translation of meaning, preserving jokes, register, character voice and the intent of each line.
2. **Adapt** the text to the line's timing: same start, same end, similar phrase structure and sentence-breaks. Translations often run longer than English, especially in Romance languages, so expect to condense (`isochrony-and-timing-fit-of-translated-lines.md`).
3. **Visible sync points**: in close-ups, match closed-mouth sounds (M, B, P) to closed-mouth moments of the animation, and open vowels to open mouths. In wide shots or when characters face away, timing and rhythm matter more than syllable matching.
4. Keep **efforts, laughs, gasps and breaths** at the original positions; many are recorded as separate non-verbal lines.
5. Mark off-screen versus on-screen lines; off-screen dialogue allows freer adaptation.
6. Keep character names, catchphrases and recurring terms consistent through a glossary the whole series shares.
7. Prepare the script in the format the studio or platform asks for (timecode in/out, character, original line, adapted line, notes). Check the platform's dubbing style guide, for example the Netflix partner help centre's dubbing guidance.

## Review
- Read the adapted line aloud against the picture. If you must rush or drag to fit, rewrite.
- Have a native speaker confirm natural phrasing and cultural references.
- Studio feedback changes lines on the day; keep a revision log.

## Verify
- [ ] Each line fits its in/out window when read aloud at natural pace.
- [ ] Close-up closures checked against picture.
- [ ] Glossary and revisions maintained.
