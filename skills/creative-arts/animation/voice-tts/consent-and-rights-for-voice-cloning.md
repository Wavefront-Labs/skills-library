# Consent and rights for voice cloning

A voice is a performer's property in practice, even where the law is unsettled. Cloning someone without clear permission is an ethical and potential legal problem. This is practical guidance, not legal advice; ask a lawyer for contracts.

## Before any cloning
1. **Written consent** from the speaker (and the guardian for a minor), stating: who will use the voice, for what project(s), in what media, for how long, and whether it covers future projects.
2. **Scope**: say explicitly whether the clone may be used for new lines not recorded by the actor, in other languages, or in marketing.
3. **Compensation and credit**: agree fee for the recording and for any ongoing AI use. Some performers' unions have published rules or agreements about digital replicas; if the actor is a union member, check the relevant agreement.
4. **Revocation and deletion**: how the actor can withdraw consent, and what happens to the model and dataset (delete, archive).
5. **Provider terms**: services such as ElevenLabs require you to hold the rights to the voice you clone and may demand verification for the professional tier. Read the current terms and prohibited-use policy.

## Never
- Clone a real person's voice without permission, including celebrities or an ex-cast member whose contract ended.
- Use a clone to deceive viewers about who spoke, or to produce statements the person never agreed to.

## Records to keep
- Signed consent and contract.
- The dataset, voice ID, and dates created and last used.
- Disclosure notes (where AI voice use is disclosed in credits or metadata).

## Gotchas
- Consent for a short instant clone does not automatically cover a professional clone.
- Voice actors on a series may have contracts that already forbid AI re-use; check before recycling their lines as training data.

## Verify
- [ ] Signed consent with scope, term, compensation and revocation.
- [ ] Provider terms read and met.
- [ ] Records stored with the dataset.
