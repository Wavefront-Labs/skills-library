# Bidding shots and sequences

A **bid** is a cost and time estimate for a unit of work. Studios bid for jobs; productions request bids from vendors.

## For the bidder (a vendor)
1. **Break down** the work: shot list, sequence by sequence, with a complexity grade per shot (for example simple, medium, hard) based on character count, camera, FX, duration, and asset needs.
2. **Estimate effort** per department: animation days per second or per shot, lighting, comp, FX, plus asset build time. Use your own history; averages differ widely between studios and styles.
3. Convert to cost: days x day rate; add supervision, production management, overhead, render and software costs, and a margin.
4. **List assumptions and exclusions**: asset delivery dates, number of revisions included, what counts as a new request, camera/edit locks, delivery spec.
5. State **schedule and milestones**, and payment terms.
6. Add a **contingency** for risk, or price the riskiest shots separately.

## For the buyer
- Send every vendor the **same package**: script, animatic, shot list, style guide, assets, delivery spec and revision policy (`outsourcing-service-studios.md`).
- Compare bids like for like: scope, assumptions, revisions included, rates, schedule, and the vendor's record.
- Ask for a **per-shot or per-second** price so changes can be priced later.

## After the job
- Record **actual vs bid** for each shot (`production-tracking.md`) and feed it back into your unit costs. A bid is only as good as the history behind it.

## Gotchas
- Vague scope is the biggest cause of disputes: define what a revision is, and the change-order process.
- Locked picture matters; changes after lock are separate work.
- Do not under-bid to win and then cut corners; track hours early.

## Verify
- [ ] Each shot has a complexity grade and an estimate.
- [ ] Assumptions, exclusions and revision limits are in writing.
- [ ] Actuals will be compared to the bid.
