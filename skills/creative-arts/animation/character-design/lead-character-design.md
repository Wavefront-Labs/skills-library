# Lead character design

Goal: a protagonist the audience will watch for 90 minutes or 50 episodes. Bancroft's
"lead" sits between broad and realistic: stylized enough for appeal, restrained enough
for subtle acting.

## Requirements of a lead
- **Acting range over gag range**: small eye and brow changes must read. Eyes are
  usually larger and more detailed than the supporting cast's.
- **Appeal and empathy**: the audience must like or at least understand them on sight.
  Softer shapes and balanced proportions, plus one flaw-hinting feature.
- **Durability**: drawn in thousands of shots - every line must be sustainable.
- **Neutral centre**: the lead often reads as the "normal" one against a wild cast, so
  the most restrained design in the line-up is usually the lead.

## Method
1. Write the character's want, flaw and arc in one sentence each; pick one visual
   trait per item (e.g. flaw = hunched protective shoulders).
2. Design the face first at 3/4 view: eye shape, brow travel, mouth corners. Test
   10 subtle expressions (doubt, suppressed joy, resignation) before finalising the body.
3. Choose proportions just short of broad: avoid extreme head/body ratios that
   force cartoon acting.
4. Plan the visual arc: costume, posture or colour shifts that track the story
   (beginning and end versions on one sheet).
5. Place the lead in the cast line-up and make sure it is the clearest read, not the
   loudest.

## Failure modes
- Bland "default hero" with no specific trait: the audience forgets them.
- Lead designed broader than the sidekicks - steals the comedy slot and loses subtlety.
- Detailed costume that animators simplify inconsistently over hundreds of shots.

## Verify
- [ ] Ten subtle expressions read clearly at medium shot.
- [ ] Want/flaw traits are visible in the neutral pose.
- [ ] Clearest read in the line-up.
