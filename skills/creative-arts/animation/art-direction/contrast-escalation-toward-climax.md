# Contrast escalation toward the climax

Goal: the climax looks like the most intense part of the film because the film has saved its contrast for it.

## 1. Budget the contrast
Treat each visual variable as a budget you spend over time (Block, The Visual Story):
| Variable | Low (affinity) | High (contrast) |
|---|---|---|
| Value | compressed range, mid-greys | full black-to-white, hard shadows |
| Saturation | greyed | saturated accents next to neutrals |
| Hue | analogous | complementary |
| Temperature | one temperature | warm light against cool shadow |
| Edges / space | soft, calm | hard, contrasting space |

Plan a graph per variable across the sequences; the climax is the only place where all are high together.

## 2. Ramp, don't jump
- Raise one or two variables per act so intensity grows: e.g. act 1 compressed values, act 2 adds
  temperature contrast, act 3 adds complementary hues and the full value range.
- Insert a low-contrast breather just before the climax (the dark night of the soul is often the most
  compressed palette); the jump out of it sells the peak.

## 3. After the peak
- Resolution returns to affinity but in a new key (often lighter and warmer than the opening) so the
  final image contrasts with the opening image.

## Failure modes
- Spending contrast on early action set pieces: the climax looks no bigger.
- Climax with every variable high for ten minutes: fatigue. Pulse the peaks.

## Verify
- [ ] Per-variable intensity graph exists and peaks at the climax.
- [ ] A low-contrast breather precedes the climax.
- [ ] Opening and final images differ in key.
