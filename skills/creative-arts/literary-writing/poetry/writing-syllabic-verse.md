# Writing syllabic verse

**Goal:** poems whose lines are measured by syllable count alone, regardless of stress.
Common in French, Japanese and Welsh traditions; in English, syllabics are a
constraint mostly felt by the writer — the ear hears stress, not syllable totals — so
their value is in what the constraint makes you do.

## Forms
- **Fixed count per line:** e.g., every line 7 syllables (Dylan Thomas's "Fern Hill"
  and other poems use intricate syllable patterns).
- **Stanza-patterned syllabics:** each stanza repeats the same sequence of line counts
  (Marianne Moore's "The Fish": lines of 1, 3, 9, 6, 8 syllables per stanza, with rhyme
  on some lines).
- **Cinquain** (Adelaide Crapsey): five lines of 2, 4, 6, 8, 2 syllables.
- Haiku as written in English is often treated as a loose syllabic form (see
  `writing-haiku-and-tanka`).

## Counting
- Count syllables as spoken in your dialect; mark them with slashes or dots.
- Decide your rules for ambiguous words (*fire*, *every*, *flower*) and apply them
  consistently.
- Elision is your choice; be consistent.

## Procedure
1. Draft the poem freely.
2. Choose a count or pattern that suits it (short counts for tension; varied counts for
   a visual shape; Moore-style patterns for intricacy).
3. Revise line by line to the count: the constraint forces word choices, line breaks in
   unusual places, and enjambment — let those surprises in.
4. Read aloud: the poem should still sound good as free verse; the count is scaffolding.

## Gotchas
- Padding with filler words to reach a count.
- Choosing a count so long the lines sprawl, or so short every line breaks mid-phrase awkwardly.
- Assuming readers will notice the count — they won't; the effect must be audible in
  other ways (pace, breaks, compression).

## Verify
- [ ] Syllable counting rules written down and applied consistently.
- [ ] Every line (or stanza pattern) meets the count.
- [ ] No filler words added purely to fill a count.
