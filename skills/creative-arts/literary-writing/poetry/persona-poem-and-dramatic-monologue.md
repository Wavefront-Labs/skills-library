# Persona poem and dramatic monologue

**Goal:** a poem spoken by a character — historical, mythic, invented or an object —
whose voice is convincing and whose words reveal more than they intend. Addonizio &
Laux and Oliver both treat voice and the speaker as a central craft choice: the "I" of a
poem is never simply the poet.

## Forms
- **Persona poem:** any poem in an assumed voice (Latin *persona*, mask).
- **Dramatic monologue** (Browning's "My Last Duchess"): a speaker addresses a
  **silent listener** at a specific **occasion**, and unwittingly reveals character.
  The Duke, showing a portrait to an envoy, reveals he had his wife killed.

## Procedure
1. **Choose the speaker** and find what they want right now — the poem is an action
   (persuading, confessing, justifying, mourning).
2. **Define the listener and occasion:** who is in the room, and why speak now?
3. **Gap between speaker and reader:** decide what the reader will understand that the
   speaker doesn't (irony) — this is the engine of the form.
4. **Voice:** diction, syntax, references from the speaker's world and era (see
   `creative-arts.literary-writing.character-development.speech-patterns-and-idiolect`).
   Include the listener's implied reactions ("Nay, we'll go together down, sir").
5. **Research** real or historical personas: letters, records, idioms of the period.
6. **Ethics:** speaking as a real person or a community not your own carries
   responsibility; know the history and your reasons.

## Variations
- Object or animal personas (the house speaks).
- Mythic revision: a minor figure retells a known story (Penelope, Eurydice).
- Persona sequences across a book.

## Gotchas
- The persona sounds like the poet in costume.
- No occasion: the speaker monologues into a void.
- Over-explaining the irony at the end.

## Verify
- [ ] Speaker, listener, occasion and want defined.
- [ ] Voice draws on the speaker's world.
- [ ] The poem reveals something the speaker doesn't mean to.
