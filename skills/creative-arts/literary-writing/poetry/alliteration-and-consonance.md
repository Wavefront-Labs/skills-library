# Alliteration and consonance

**Goal:** make a poem's consonants work for its meaning. Mary Oliver (*A Poetry
Handbook*, "Sound") groups consonants by how they are made and argues that their
textures — hard, soft, hushed — shape how a line feels.

## Terms
- **Alliteration:** repetition of initial consonant sounds in nearby stressed
  syllables: *"the fair breeze blew, the white foam flew"* (Coleridge).
- **Consonance:** repetition of consonant sounds anywhere in words, often at ends:
  *"stroke of luck / black rock"*.
- Sound, not spelling, counts: *cat / kite* alliterate; *cat / city* don't.

## Consonant families and textures (Oliver's groupings, roughly)
| Family | Sounds | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Plosives (mutes) | b, p, d, t, g, k | Hard, abrupt, emphatic; good for force or anger |
| Sibilants / fricatives | s, sh, z, f, v, th | Hush, hiss, whisper, wind, water |
| Liquids | l, r | Flowing, smooth |
| Nasals | m, n, ng | Humming, resonant, drowsy |
| Semivowels / aspirate | w, y, h | Soft, open |

## Procedure
1. Read the draft aloud and mark repeated consonant sounds with coloured pens.
2. Ask what the poem's emotional key is in each section; check whether the dominant
   consonants support it (a grief stanza full of clattering plosives may fight itself).
3. Revise word choices to cluster suitable sounds at key lines; weaken accidental clusters elsewhere.
4. Use alliteration to **bind** words you want linked in meaning (*"love and loss"*).

## Gotchas
- Heavy alliteration on every line becomes comic or tongue-twisting (useful only for comic verse).
- Counting spelled letters instead of sounds.
- Sound for its own sake that distorts syntax or sense.

## Verify
- [ ] Sound patterns marked on the draft.
- [ ] Dominant consonants support the emotion of each section.
- [ ] Alliteration binds meaningful words, and none is accidental-comic.
