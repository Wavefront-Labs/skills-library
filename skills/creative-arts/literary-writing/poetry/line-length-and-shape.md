# Line length and shape

**Goal:** a line length that suits the poem's pace and voice, and a page shape that
helps rather than distracts. Oliver (*A Poetry Handbook*, "The Line") discusses how
line length changes speed and emphasis in both metrical and free verse.

## What length does
| Line length | Effect | Models |
|---|---|---|
| Very short (1-4 words) | Slow, deliberate; each word weighted; thin vertical shape speeds the eye down | W.C. Williams, "The Red Wheelbarrow"; Robert Creeley |
| Medium (pentameter range, ~10 syllables) | Conversational, flexible; the English default | Most lyric poetry |
| Long (Whitmanic, 15+ syllables) | Expansive, incantatory, cataloguing, oratorical | Whitman, Ginsberg, C.K. Williams |

Note the paradox: short lines slow the *reading of each word* (many pauses) while
narrow columns move the eye quickly down the page.

## Consistent vs varied
- **Consistent length** creates a steady ground; a single short or long line then stands out.
- **Varied length** follows thought and breath (organic form); make the variation
  meaningful — a sudden short line for shock, a sprawling line for release.

## Procedure
1. Draft without worrying about length.
2. Retype the poem at a contrasting line length (halve or double); read both aloud.
3. Choose the version whose pace matches the content.
4. Check the shape on the page: does it help (a long thin poem about falling) or look
   arbitrary? Look at it from a distance.
5. Check long lines at the print width of journals/books: a line too long for the page
   will wrap; mark intended turnovers with a hanging indent in submission files.

## Gotchas
- Very short lines that chop ordinary prose into pieces without gain.
- Long lines that lose energy halfway — keep syntax driving, or use catalogue and anaphora.
- Centred alignment rarely helps; left-aligned is the default unless the shape is the point.

## Verify
- [ ] At least two line lengths tried.
- [ ] Chosen length matches the poem's pace.
- [ ] Deviations from the norm are deliberate.
