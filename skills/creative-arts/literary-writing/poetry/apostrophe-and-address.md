# Apostrophe and address

**Goal:** use the act of addressing someone or something to create intimacy, urgency or
drama in a poem.

## Terms
- **Apostrophe:** turning to address someone absent or dead, or something non-human or
  abstract, as if it could hear: *"Death, be not proud"* (Donne); *"O Rose, thou art
  sick"* (Blake); *"Milton! thou shouldst be living at this hour"* (Wordsworth).
- **Address:** any poem directed to a "you" — a lover, a reader, a self.
- **Invocation:** addressing a muse or deity at the start of an epic.

## Choosing the "you"
| "You" is... | Effect |
|---|---|
| A specific absent person | Elegy, love, grievance; the reader overhears |
| The reader | Confrontation, intimacy, implication |
| The self | Self-reproach, distance from one's own experience |
| An object or abstraction | Wonder, protest, ode (see `elegy-and-ode`) |
| Ambiguous | Can widen the poem — but must feel controlled |
Decide early; drifting "you" (sometimes the reader, sometimes the lover) confuses.

## Techniques
- **Letter poem:** epistolary form with a real addressee lets details of a shared life
  appear naturally ("Remember the kitchen in Erie").
- **Questions** to the addressee create urgency even without answers.
- **The vocative "O":** powerful but archaic; contemporary poems often omit it or use it
  knowingly.
- **Turn away:** some poems shift from addressing one figure to another, or from address
  to narration, at the turn.

## Gotchas
- Melodramatic apostrophe ("O cruel Fate!") without irony or earned feeling.
- Telling the addressee things they already know only to inform the reader — makes the
  address feel fake; let details arise from shared memory.

## Verify
- [ ] The identity of "you" is clear and consistent (or deliberately shifted).
- [ ] The address has a reason (urgency, intimacy, grief, protest).
- [ ] No "as you know" exposition aimed at the reader.
