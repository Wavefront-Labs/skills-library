# Composing for the ear

**Goal:** a poem that lands on first hearing, when listeners can't reread. Spoken word
and performance poetry draw on oral traditions (sermon, blues, hip-hop, Beat readings, the
Black Arts Movement) as well as page poetry.

## Principles
- **Clarity on first pass:** shorter syntactic units, the subject near the verb, fewer nested
  clauses. Listeners hold one idea at a time.
- **Repetition as architecture:** anaphora ("I am from..."), a refrain returning with
  changed meaning, list structures. Repetition is how the ear finds its place.
- **Sound patterning:** rhyme (internal and end), alliteration, assonance, rhythmic runs —
  but vary to avoid sing-song.
- **Concrete images** the listener can see instantly.
- **Build:** arrange intensity to rise; hold the strongest image or line for late.
- **Direct address and occasion:** "you", call-and-response, naming the room.

## Drafting process
1. Draft aloud or record yourself improvising lines.
2. Transcribe; mark breaths and emphases.
3. Revise for the ear: replace page-only effects (visual line breaks, puns that need
   spelling) with audible ones (pause, stress, repetition).
4. **Time it** — slams and open mics have limits (often 3 minutes).
5. Test on listeners: what images and lines do they repeat back?

## Page and stage
Many performance poems are published too; lineate the page version so breaks mark
breaths. Some poems work on one and not the other — decide the primary medium.

## Gotchas
- Dense allusion and ambiguity that only works on rereading.
- Relentless one-pitch intensity; the ear tires.
- Lists without progression.

## Verify
- [ ] Poem understood on one hearing by a test listener.
- [ ] Repetition structures help orientation.
- [ ] Timed against the performance limit.
