# Open field composition and the page as space

**Goal:** use the whole page — indentation, spacing, scattered lines, shape — as a
compositional element. Two traditions:
- **Projective verse / composition by field:** Charles Olson's 1950 essay: "the line comes
  (I swear it) from the breath"; the poem is energy transferred to the reader; the
  typewriter lets the poet score spacing precisely. Practised by Olson, Robert Duncan,
  Denise Levertov, Susan Howe, and later many contemporary poets.
- **Concrete / visual poetry:** shape makes meaning — George Herbert's "Easter Wings"
  (17th c.), Apollinaire's *Calligrammes*, E. E. Cummings, May Swenson, the concrete poets
  of the 1950s-60s (Eugen Gomringer, the Noigandres group).

## Field techniques
- **Indentation** to place a phrase in relation to another (echo, answer, aside).
- **Internal spaces** (gaps within a line) as pauses longer than a comma, shorter than a break.
- **Drift** across the page to show movement of attention.
- **Scoring:** treat space as rests in music; read aloud and adjust.
- Consistency: develop a spatial grammar within the poem (left margin = present, indented =
  memory).

## Visual poem techniques
- Shape that enacts subject (wings, a falling column).
- Erasure and redaction (blackout poems) as visual composition.
- Keep the text strong; shape should add, not substitute.

## Practical issues
- **Submission formatting:** use spaces/tabs carefully; send PDF if the journal allows, or
  note the spacing. Online forms often collapse spaces.
- **Ebook/web display:** reflowable formats break spatial poems; publishers may set them as
  images or fixed layout.

## Gotchas
- Arbitrary scatter without a reading logic.
- Shapes that are cute but don't deepen meaning.

## Verify
- [ ] Each spatial choice corresponds to a pause, relation or movement.
- [ ] Read aloud, spacing is performable.
- [ ] Submission format preserves the layout (PDF or note).
