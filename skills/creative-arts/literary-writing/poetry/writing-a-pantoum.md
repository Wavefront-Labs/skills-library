# Writing a pantoum

**Goal:** a pantoum whose repeated lines circle and accumulate meaning — a form suited
to memory, obsession and trauma. It derives from the Malay *pantun*, adapted into
French and English poetry in the 19th century.

## The form
- Any number of quatrains.
- Lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next.
- In the last stanza, lines 2 and 4 are lines 3 and 1 of the **first** stanza, so the
  poem ends with its opening line.
- Optional rhyme `abab`.

Scheme with line numbers:
```
1  2  3  4
2  5  4  6
5  7  6  8
...
x  3  y  1   (final stanza: new lines x,y from the previous stanza; then 3 and 1)
```

## Procedure
1. Write the first stanza with care — every line will appear twice.
2. Build a grid; fill in repeated lines first, then write the new lines (positions 2
   and 4 in each stanza).
3. Change **punctuation, line context and emphasis** so repeated lines shift meaning
   (a statement becomes a question; a fragment joins a different sentence).
4. Small word changes are allowed in contemporary practice, but too many erase the form's effect.
5. Plan the ending: line 1 returning should feel inevitable and changed.

## Choosing subject
- Repetition mimics memory replaying, ritual, inescapable situations, a mind circling.
- Narrative that must advance quickly fights the form.

## Gotchas
- Lines that only make sense in one context — repetition becomes nonsense.
- Mechanical repetition with no shift; each recurrence needs new light.
- Losing track of the pattern; the grid prevents it.

## Verify
- [ ] Repetition pattern correct in every stanza (grid checked).
- [ ] Final stanza returns lines 3 and 1.
- [ ] At least some repeated lines change meaning on recurrence.
