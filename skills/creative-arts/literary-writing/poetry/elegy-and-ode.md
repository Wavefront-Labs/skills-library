# Elegy and ode

**Goal:** write the two great occasional modes — poems of mourning and poems of praise —
without slipping into sentimentality or greeting-card generality.

## Elegy
A poem of mourning, for a person, a place, a way of life.
- **Traditional movement** (pastoral elegy: Milton's "Lycidas", Shelley's "Adonais"):
  **lament** -> **praise** of the dead -> **consolation** (immortality, nature's renewal).
- **Modern elegy** often resists consolation, ends in unresolved grief, or questions
  elegy itself (Auden, "Funeral Blues"; Bishop, "One Art").
- Craft:
  1. Specifics of the dead: habits, objects, sayings — not virtues in the abstract.
  2. The speaker's relationship and the texture of absence (the unanswered phone).
  3. Decide whether the poem consoles, refuses, or rages — and earn the ending.
  4. Restraint: understatement often carries grief better than declaration.

## Ode
A poem of praise or meditation addressed to its subject, typically elevated.
- **Pindaric ode:** public, ceremonial, in triads (strophe, antistrophe, epode).
- **Horatian ode:** calmer, regular stanzas, personal reflection.
- **Romantic ode** (Keats's odes): private meditation through an object toward a large question.
- **Neruda's *Odas elementales***: odes to everyday things (an onion, socks, salt) in
  short lines — praise of the ordinary.
- Craft: address the subject; accumulate exact detail; let praise turn toward meaning
  (what the onion teaches about the speaker's life).

## Gotchas
- Elegy that lists generic qualities ("she was kind and generous").
- Ode that only gushes — complexity (ambivalence, loss) deepens praise.
- Imitating archaic diction from classic examples.

## Verify
- [ ] Specific details of the subject drive the poem.
- [ ] The poem's ending (consolation, refusal, turn) is deliberate.
- [ ] Praise or grief is complicated by at least one unexpected note.
