# The prose poem

**Goal:** a poem written in sentences and paragraphs without line breaks, which keeps
poetry's compression, imagery, music and leaps. History: Aloysius Bertrand's *Gaspard de la
Nuit* (1842), Baudelaire's *Le Spleen de Paris* (1869), Rimbaud's *Illuminations*;
in English, Russell Edson's fables, Charles Simic's *The World Doesn't End* (Pulitzer 1990),
Carolyn Forché's "The Colonel".

## What replaces the line
- **Sentence rhythm:** vary long cumulative sentences and short ones; the full stop is your
  strongest pause.
- **Syntax as tension:** run-ons, fragments, lists, anaphora.
- **Leaps:** associative or surreal turns between sentences (Edson's logic of the absurd).
- **The box:** usually one paragraph (sometimes a few), often justified — the shape itself
  frames it as a compressed object.

## Drafting
1. Start from an image, a voice or a strange premise ("A man finds his father's
   shadow in a drawer").
2. Write in a single paragraph, following the logic wherever it goes.
3. Compress: every sentence must add image, turn or music; cut explanation.
4. **Find the turn** — a final sentence that shifts register or reveals.
5. Read aloud for rhythm; replace flat sentences.

## Prose poem vs flash fiction
The border is porous. Prose poems lean toward image, sound and lyric leap; flash toward
character and event. Choose the label that matches the market and your intent.

## Formatting for submission
- Single block, justified or left-aligned per journal style; state "prose poem" if
  formatting could be lost.

## Gotchas
- Lineated poem poured into a paragraph without reworking rhythm.
- Narrative summary with nothing compressed.
- Overlong: most prose poems are under a page.

## Verify
- [ ] No line breaks; rhythm carried by sentences.
- [ ] Every sentence adds image, leap or music.
- [ ] A final turn.
