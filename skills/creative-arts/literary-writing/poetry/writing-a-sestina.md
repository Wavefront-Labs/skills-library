# Writing a sestina

**Goal:** a sestina whose six repeated end-words become a web of shifting meaning.
Models: Elizabeth Bishop's "Sestina", Ashbery's "Farm Implements and Rutabagas in a
Landscape", Swinburne.

## The form
- Six stanzas of six lines + a three-line **envoi** (39 lines).
- No rhyme; instead the same six **end-words** recur in a fixed rotation
  (*retrogradatio cruciata*: last, first, second-last, second, third-last, third).
- Rotation, with end-words numbered 1-6:
  ```
  Stanza 1: 1 2 3 4 5 6
  Stanza 2: 6 1 5 2 4 3
  Stanza 3: 3 6 4 1 2 5
  Stanza 4: 5 3 2 6 1 4
  Stanza 5: 4 5 1 3 6 2
  Stanza 6: 2 4 6 5 3 1
  Envoi:    2-5, 4-3, 6-1  (two end-words per line: one mid-line, one at the end)
  ```
  (Envoi orderings vary in practice; the traditional one places 2 and 5 in line 1, 4 and
  3 in line 2, 6 and 1 in line 3.)

## Choosing end-words
- Mix nouns with words that can shift part of speech or meaning (*light*, *tear*,
  *grave*, *well*, *still*, *change*).
- Include at least one concrete noun to anchor imagery.
- Avoid six abstract words — the poem goes vague.
- Words tied to the poem's obsession (Bishop: *house, grandmother, child, stove, almanac, tears*).

## Procedure
1. Write the pattern grid first: the six end-words in rotation down the right margin.
2. Draft stanza by stanza; let narrative or argument develop so stanzas aren't static.
3. Exploit homonyms, puns, line enjambment into the end-word, and changes of context.
4. Allow minor variations (*tear/tears*, *light/lit*) where they serve sense.

## Gotchas
- Each stanza restating the same situation; give it a plot or progression.
- Strained sentences that exist only to land on the end-word.

## Verify
- [ ] Rotation checked against the grid for all six stanzas and the envoi.
- [ ] End-words shift sense at least a few times.
- [ ] The poem moves from where it began.
