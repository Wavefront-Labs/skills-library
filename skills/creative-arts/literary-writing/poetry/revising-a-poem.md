# Revising a poem

**Goal:** turn a draft into a finished poem. Oliver (*A Poetry Handbook*, "Revision")
treats revision as where most of the work happens; Addonizio & Laux give it a full
chapter of strategies.

## Pass 1 — find the poem
1. Wait at least a few days; read aloud.
2. Underline the **live** lines — the ones with energy or surprise. Often the real poem
   starts at the first live line.
3. Ask: what is this poem actually about? (Often not what you set out to write.) Write
   it in one sentence for yourself.

## Pass 2 — cut
- **Provisionally delete the first and last lines** (and sometimes the first stanza):
  openings often warm up, endings often explain. Restore only if missed.
- Cut abstractions that the images already carry (see `building-concrete-images`).
- Cut redundant adjectives, articles and connectives (*and, then, so*) where rhythm allows.

## Pass 3 — line and sound
- Relineate: try the poem as prose, then break it two or three different ways (see
  `creative-arts.literary-writing.poetry.line-breaks-and-metre`).
- Read for sound clashes and unintended rhymes; scan problem lines.

## Pass 4 — word by word
- Test each noun and verb: is it the most exact word? Is there a more surprising but true one?
- Check the **title**: it should add something (context, angle, irony), not repeat line one.

## Radical revision (when stuck)
- Change the form (couplets -> single block; free verse -> sonnet), the person (I -> she),
  the tense, or the order of stanzas (reverse them).
- Start from the last line and write a new poem toward it.

## When is it done?
- Further changes swap one equal option for another; read-aloud is smooth; you can't
  find a word to cut. Put it away a month and check again before submitting.

## Verify
- [ ] Poem's real subject stated (privately) in a sentence.
- [ ] First and last lines tested by deletion.
- [ ] At least one relineation tried.
- [ ] Title earns its place.
