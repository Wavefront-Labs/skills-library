# Line breaks and metre — the line is the poem's unit

**Goal:** make every line break a decision, hear the stress pattern of your lines,
and use metre (strictly or loosely) to shape pace and emphasis — whether writing
sonnets or free verse.

## 1. What a line break does
A break creates a slight pause and gives weight to the **last word** of a line and the
**first word** of the next. Use that:
- **End-stopped line:** the sentence or phrase ends with the line. Feels settled, firm.
- **Enjambment:** the sentence runs over the break. Creates momentum and can create
  double meanings — the line means one thing alone and another when the sentence completes:
  `I could not stop / myself from going back` — "I could not stop" first reads as total.
- **Caesura:** a pause inside the line (punctuation or natural phrase break). Varies rhythm.
- Breaking on weak words (*the, of, and, a*) makes the line feel arbitrary unless you
  want that hesitation.
**Test:** for each break ask "what does ending here emphasise?" If the answer is
nothing, consider moving it.

## 2. Hearing stress (scansion)
English rhythm is stress-based. To scan:
1. Mark each syllable; mark stressed (/) and unstressed (x) by saying the line
   naturally — not sing-song. Check dictionary stress for multi-syllable words.
2. Group into **feet**:
   | Foot | Pattern | Example |
   |---|---|---|
   | Iamb | x / | *a-WAY* |
   | Trochee | / x | *GAR-den* |
   | Anapest | x x / | *in the NIGHT* |
   | Dactyl | / x x | *MER-ri-ly* |
   | Spondee | / / | *HEART-BREAK* |
3. Count feet per line: trimeter (3), tetrameter (4), pentameter (5).
   **Iambic pentameter** = five iambs: `x / x / x / x / x /` —
   "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" (the first foot is often read with
   varied stress; scansion of real lines is debatable, which is normal).
One-syllable words (*and, the, in*) usually go unstressed but can take stress in context.

## 3. Using metre
- **Regularity sets an expectation; variation creates emphasis.** A trochee
  substituted at the start of an iambic line (very common) pushes force onto the first word.
- Metre controls **speed**: many unstressed syllables (anapests) move quickly;
  clustered stresses (spondees) slow down.
- **Blank verse** = unrhymed iambic pentameter; sonnets usually 14 lines of pentameter
  with a set rhyme scheme (Petrarchan, Shakespearean). Learn a form by writing several.
- **Free verse** has no fixed metre, but good free verse still has rhythm. Scan it
  anyway — you'll hear patterns and clunks.

## 4. Sound beyond metre
- **Rhyme:** full rhyme (*day/stay*), slant/half rhyme (*soul/all*), internal rhyme.
  Forced rhymes (inverting word order to get the rhyme) read as awkward; use slant
  rhyme or rewrite.
- **Assonance** (repeated vowel sounds), **consonance**, **alliteration** — use to bind
  lines and to slow or speed reading.

## 5. Revision process
1. Read aloud, slowly, pausing slightly at each line end. Mark stumbles.
2. Try **relineating**: rewrite the poem as prose, then break it again in two or three
   different ways. Compare.
3. Scan problem lines; adjust word order or word choice for stress.
4. Cut the first and last lines provisionally — they're often throat-clearing or
   over-explaining.
5. Replace abstractions (love, sadness) with concrete images where possible.

## Gotchas
- **Prose chopped into lines:** if breaks fall only at phrase ends, ask whether lineation adds anything.
- **Sing-song regularity** in strict metre: vary with substitutions and caesuras.
- **Inverted syntax for rhyme** ("to the store went I").
- Scansion is interpretive; readers and accents differ. Classic examples are
  standard teaching cases rather than settled facts.

## Verify
- [ ] Each line break can be justified by emphasis, ambiguity or rhythm.
- [ ] Mix of end-stopped and enjambed lines is deliberate.
- [ ] Problem lines scanned; variations from any metre are intentional.
- [ ] No forced rhymes or inversions.
- [ ] Poem read aloud and relineated at least once during revision.
