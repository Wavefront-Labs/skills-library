# Writing an ekphrastic poem

**Goal:** a poem in conversation with a work of visual art (or music, film, sculpture)
that does more than describe it. Classic models: Keats, "Ode on a Grecian Urn"; Auden,
"Musée des Beaux Arts" (Bruegel's *Landscape with the Fall of Icarus*); W.C. Williams's
Bruegel poems; Anne Sexton, "The Starry Night".

## Approaches
1. **Close looking:** describe what's actually there — but select details that carry
   a perception or argument (Auden notices the ploughman who doesn't look up).
2. **Enter the scene:** narrate what happened before or after the moment depicted.
3. **Speak as a figure** in the work, or as the model, the painter, the object (persona).
4. **Address the work or artist** (apostrophe).
5. **Argue with the work:** what it leaves out, whom it ignores, how its view has dated.
6. **Personal collision:** the speaker's life meets the artwork (seen in a museum on the
   day of a diagnosis).

## Procedure
1. Spend real time with the work — 20 minutes minimum, in person if possible. Notes:
   colours, objects, edges, gestures, what's in shadow, what's outside the frame.
2. Research context lightly (date, circumstances) — enough to avoid errors.
3. Choose one approach; draft.
4. Cut description the reader doesn't need to imagine the work; keep what serves the poem's argument.
5. Title or epigraph names the work: *after* [Artist], [Title], [year].

## Gotchas
- Pure description: the poem becomes a museum label.
- Requiring the reader to have seen the image to understand the poem.
- Copyright: quoting an artwork's title is fine; reproducing the image alongside the
  poem in publication needs permission unless public domain.

## Verify
- [ ] The poem makes a move beyond description.
- [ ] It stands alone for a reader who hasn't seen the work.
- [ ] Work and artist credited in title or epigraph.
