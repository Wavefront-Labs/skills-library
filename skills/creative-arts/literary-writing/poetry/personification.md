# Personification

**Goal:** give non-human things human qualities in a way that sharpens perception
instead of decorating.

## Terms
- **Personification (prosopopoeia):** attributing human traits or actions to things,
  animals or abstractions (*Death, be not proud*).
- **Pathetic fallacy** (Ruskin's term): nature reflecting human emotion (*the sullen
  sky*). Ruskin disapproved; poets use it constantly — the craft is doing it freshly.
- **Apostrophe:** addressing the personified thing directly (see
  `apostrophe-and-address`).

## Techniques
1. **Through verbs, not adjectives:** "the house *sighed* when we left" or "the
   kettle *complains*" — precise action animates more than "the sad house".
2. **Make it observational:** the human action should match something actually
   perceivable (the house settling, the kettle's whine).
3. **Keep a consistent persona:** if the sea is an old man in line 2, don't make it a
   bride in line 4 unless the shift is the point.
4. **Personify abstractions sparingly** — Keats's Autumn "sitting careless on a granary
   floor" works because it is full of concrete agricultural detail.
5. **Use to reveal the speaker:** what a speaker projects onto objects characterises them.

## Gotchas
- Stock personifications: whispering wind, dancing leaves, angry storms, smiling sun.
- Capitalised Abstractions (Hope, Despair) in a contemporary poem — they read as 18th century.
- Personification so frequent the poem feels like a cartoon.

## Verify
- [ ] Personification carried by specific verbs.
- [ ] Each personified action corresponds to a real perception.
- [ ] No stock pairings.
