# Writing an editorial letter — big-picture notes an author can act on

**Goal:** a letter (plus margin notes) that tells the author what their book is
doing, where it falls short of what they're aiming for, and options to fix it — in
priority order, specific, and kind enough that they keep working.

## 1. Before reading: scope and intent
- Confirm what's been agreed: **developmental edit** (structure, character, pacing,
  plot, POV, theme) is not a line edit or copyedit; don't spend hours on commas.
- Ask the author: genre and target readership, comparable titles, what they think the
  book is about, what worries them, where they hope to publish. You're editing toward
  *their* book, not yours.

## 2. Read twice
1. **First read as a reader,** fast, with minimal notes — only reactions: "bored here",
   "confused", "loved this", "didn't believe this". Your first-reader responses are the
   most valuable data and can't be recovered later.
2. **Build a reverse outline:** one line per chapter/scene — POV, what happens, what
   changes, word count. Track timeline, character appearances and subplots in a
   spreadsheet. Patterns appear here (a POV absent for 80 pages, a subplot dropped,
   chapters with nothing changing).
3. **Second read analytically,** checking each suspicion against the text.

## 3. Diagnose, then prioritise
Common developmental issues, roughly from largest to smallest:
1. **Concept/premise and genre fit** — is the promise clear and kept? Does the length
   suit the genre?
2. **Structure and plot** — slow opening, sagging middle, missing midpoint, climax not
   driven by the protagonist, coincidences, unresolved threads.
3. **Character** — unclear motivation, passive protagonist, arc without cause,
   interchangeable secondary characters.
4. **POV and voice** — head-hopping, inconsistent distance, wrong POV character for key scenes.
5. **Pacing** — summary where scene is needed and vice versa, repetitive beats.
6. **Setting/worldbuilding** and **theme**.
Pick the **3–5 most important issues**. Fixing the top one may solve others; a letter
listing 30 equal problems paralyses.

## 4. Write the letter
Typical length runs from a few pages to 15+ depending on scope. Structure:
1. **What the book is and does well** — specific, genuine strengths. This tells the
   author what to protect during revision, and is not just softening.
2. **Summary of the main issues** in priority order (a short list).
3. **Each issue in depth:** what you observed, **why** it matters to a reader, examples
   with page/chapter references, and **2–3 possible approaches** — the author chooses.
   Frame as questions where helpful: "What if Lena discovered the letter herself in ch. 12?"
4. **Smaller notes** grouped by type.
5. **Suggested revision order** and an encouraging close.
Use "the reader" not "I" for craft points ("the reader loses track of Tom's goal
here") — it depersonalises the note.

## 5. Margin notes
In Track Changes / comments: reactions, flags tied to the letter's issues ("see letter,
issue 2"), and a few examples of recurring line-level patterns — don't line-edit the
whole manuscript.

## 6. Collaboration
- Offer a follow-up call; authors often need to talk through the letter after the
  initial sting.
- Your suggestions are options; the book belongs to the author.

## Gotchas
- **Rewriting in your own voice** — suggest, don't impose.
- **Editing toward a different book** (literary when they're writing thriller). Re-read their goals.
- **Unprioritised lists** overwhelm. Rank.
- **Vague notes** ("pacing is off") — always cite where and why.
- Letter lengths and issue order reflect common freelance practice; adjust to the contract.

## Verify
- [ ] Scope and author's goals confirmed before reading.
- [ ] First read done with reactions only; reverse outline built.
- [ ] Letter opens with specific strengths; 3–5 issues ranked.
- [ ] Every issue has evidence with locations, a "why", and multiple options.
- [ ] Margin notes cross-reference the letter; no full line edit unless contracted.
