# Writing margin comments

**Goal:** make comments that tell the author what happened to a reader and where, without taking over the writing.

## Types worth using
- **Reader reaction:** "I was confused about who is speaking here." "I laughed." Honest, local and unarguable.
- **Question:** "What does she want in this scene?" Questions invite the author to find the answer.
- **Pattern flag:** "Third time she stares out of a window; see ch 2 and 5."
- **Structural pointer:** "This reveal arrives before the reader cares; consider moving after the funeral scene." Offer options, not orders.
- **Praise:** specific ("this image of the kitchen carries her whole childhood").
- **Query for fact or continuity:** "Earlier his eyes are brown."

## Practice
1. **Decide a density.** A comment per page or two is plenty in a developmental pass; fewer on structure-heavy drafts. A wall of comments buries the key ones.
2. **Label by importance** if useful (for example a marker such as BIG for structural notes). Keep it simple and explain the code in your cover note.
3. **Keep tone collegial.** "I wonder" and "I could not tell" beat "wrong" and "bad". Avoid sarcasm.
4. **Refer, do not rewrite.** Offer a model sentence only to show a principle, and label it as an example.
5. **Balance.** Roughly one note of praise or recognition for each few critical ones helps the author keep the whole book in view; do not fake praise.
6. **Align comments with the letter.** The margin shows evidence for points your letter makes (`creative-arts.literary-writing.developmental-editing.writing-an-editorial-letter`).
7. **Use the software well.** In Word, use comments rather than inline edits for developmental notes; for edits, track changes (see `creative-arts.literary-writing.developmental-editing.writing-author-queries`).

## Verify
- [ ] Each comment is local, specific and explains a reader effect.
- [ ] Density lets the reader find the major ones.
- [ ] Praise is specific.
