# Audience participation and call-and-response

**Goal:** turn listeners into co-tellers so they're invested — especially with
children and family audiences. Margaret Read MacDonald's books are built around
participation stories with refrains and actions.

## Types of participation
| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Refrain** | The audience repeats a line every time it occurs ("Fee-fi-fo-fum!") |
| **Call-and-response** | Teller calls, audience answers — Caribbean "Crick?" / "Crack!"; many traditions open tales this way |
| **Sound effects** | Rain (finger taps), wind, knocking, animal noises |
| **Gestures/actions** | Rowing, climbing, shivering together |
| **Choices** | "Should she open the door?" (manage the answer you'll get) |
| **Predictive fill-in** | Teller pauses before a predictable word; audience supplies it |

## How to run it
1. **Teach before you need it:** "Every time the troll says this, you say it with
   him — let's practise." Rehearse once or twice.
2. **Cue clearly:** a consistent gesture (open palms toward the audience), a pause,
   a look.
3. **Build:** the refrain grows louder or faster as tension builds.
4. **Control the energy:** a clear stop signal (hand up, drop to a whisper) brings the
   room back. Never let participation drown the story.
5. **Credit them:** "You saved the village!" — the payoff makes participation worth it.

## Adapting by audience
- Young children: simple, repeated, physical; lots of it.
- Teens and adults: lighter touch — a single call-and-response or a choice; don't force.
- Cultural openings and closings belong to traditions; use them with acknowledgement
  of their source.

## Gotchas
- Participation so frequent the plot stalls.
- Asking open questions to large groups of children (chaos) — ask yes/no or choice questions.
- Unrehearsed refrains that nobody joins.

## Verify
- [ ] Participation elements taught and cued consistently.
- [ ] A stop signal used and respected.
- [ ] Story still moves; participation peaks at the climax.
