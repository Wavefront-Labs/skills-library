# Telling myths and legends

**Goal:** tell myths and legends accurately and with the right framing for the audience.

## Know the genre
Folklorists commonly distinguish **myths** (stories held as sacred or true, set in an earlier world, often about origins and deities), **legends** (stories told as true, set in the recent historical world, about heroes, places, events) and **folktales** (stories told as fiction). William Bascom's 1965 essay "The Forms of Folklore: Prose Narratives" is the usual reference for this three-way split. The line shifts by culture: what outsiders call a myth may be living religion to believers.

## Preparing
1. **Pick a source.** Use a reliable translation or collection and note the translator and edition. Published translations are copyrighted by the translator even when the original text is old; paraphrase from the story, not from their wording.
2. **Compare more than one account.** Many myths exist in contradicting versions (Greek myths vary by author and region). Decide which you tell and say so.
3. **Check whether it is living tradition.** If the story belongs to a living religion or community, follow `creative-arts.literary-writing.oral-storytelling.cultural-ownership-and-permission-in-storytelling`.
4. **Frame it.** A short introduction (where it comes from, what it explains) orients listeners; a legend tied to a place invites local detail.
5. **Shape for telling.** Reduce genealogies; keep the vivid episode; give gods and heroes a few clear traits (see `creative-arts.literary-writing.oral-storytelling.shaping-a-story-for-telling-aloud`).
6. **Treat the dark parts deliberately.** Myths hold violence and sexual coercion; adjust for audience age and say what you changed.
7. **For legends, mark the claim.** "They say that..." signals tradition rather than fact, and is honest where evidence is thin.
