# Shaping a story for telling aloud — for the ear, not the page

**Goal:** a story (personal narrative for a slam, or a traditional tale) that you can
tell from memory in a fixed time, sounding natural rather than recited, and that
listeners can follow without rereading.

## 1. Ear vs page
Listeners can't go back. So told stories need:
- **Simpler syntax** — shorter sentences, one idea at a time.
- **Fewer characters,** clearly named and distinguished (a voice, a gesture, a place).
- **Repetition** as structure: refrains, the rule of three (three brothers, three
  tries), callbacks. On the page it's redundant; aloud it's how listeners keep their bearings.
- **Concrete images** listeners can picture, not abstract summary.

## 2. Find the story's change
Especially for personal stories (story slams such as The Moth-style events, usually
around 5 minutes): a story is not an anecdote or a list of events. Ask:
- What did I want? What was at stake?
- **How was I different at the end?** The change is the point; the events serve it.
- Why tell it now, to these people?
Cut everything that doesn't lead to that change, even if it's funny or true.

## 3. Map it as beats, don't write a script
- Write the story as a **skeleton**: 5–12 beats, one line each ("Grandma's kitchen, the
  locked drawer"; "I find the key"; "the letters").
- Memorise the **sequence of beats**, plus the exact **first line** and **last line**.
  Everything between is retold fresh each time in your own words. Word-for-word
  memorisation tends to sound recited and collapses if you lose one line.
- For a folktale, learn the tale's bones from several versions, then make your own
  telling. Note the source tradition and respect cultural ownership — some stories are
  not for retelling outside their community.

## 4. Openings and endings
- **Open in the action or with stakes**, not with preamble ("So this is a story about…").
  E.g. "The night before my wedding, I stole my father's car."
- **End on the change**, ideally with an image or line echoing the start. Don't
  explain the moral. A clean last line, then stop and hold the silence — the audience
  will know it's over.

## 5. Rehearse aloud
- Tell it out loud, standing, many times — to a mirror, a pet, a phone recorder, then
  to people. Story shape improves with each telling.
- **Time it** every rehearsal; slams often enforce limits with a signal. Aim to finish
  with margin (e.g. 4:30 for a 5-minute slot), since live audiences laugh and you'll pause.
- Listen to recordings for: filler words (um, like, so), rushed climaxes, unclear
  transitions between beats.

## 6. Delivery
- **Pause** before and after important lines; silence builds tension and lets laughs land.
- Slow down at the climax — most nervous tellers speed up.
- **Eye contact** with different parts of the room; for a large venue, pick a few faces.
- Use voice and gesture to distinguish characters lightly; full impersonation isn't needed.
- Present tense ("So I'm standing there…") can make personal stories feel immediate.
- If you lose your place, go to the next beat you remember — the audience doesn't know your map.

## Gotchas
- **Reading from notes** breaks connection; carry a beat list as backup only.
- **Too much setup** — the story often starts a minute in. Cut the first minute and test.
- **Stand-up versus story:** jokes are welcome, but a story slam needs stakes and change.
- **Venue rules:** slams often require true, first-person stories, no notes and a time
  limit — check each event's rules.
- Timing and beat-count numbers are rough practice guidelines, not standards.

## Verify
- [ ] The change in the story is stated in one sentence.
- [ ] A 5–12 beat skeleton exists; first and last lines are fixed.
- [ ] Repetition/callbacks and concrete images built in for the ear.
- [ ] Told aloud at least five times and recorded; under time with margin.
- [ ] Ends on the change with a held silence, no explained moral.
