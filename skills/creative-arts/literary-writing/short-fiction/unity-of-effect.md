# Unity of effect

**Goal:** a short story in which every element works toward one predominant effect or
impression. Edgar Allan Poe argued it in his 1842 review of Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales*
and in "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846): a tale readable in one sitting, where "in the
whole composition there should be no word written of which the tendency... is not to the one
pre-established design."

## Method
1. **Name the effect** in a few words: dread tightening into horror; tenderness undercut by
   loss; comic escalation to absurdity; quiet dawning shame.
2. **Audit each scene:** does it advance or deepen that effect? Mark it +, 0 or −.
   Cut or rewrite 0s and −s.
3. **Audit details:** setting, weather, objects, names — do they colour toward the effect?
   (Poe's House of Usher: every image tends toward decay.)
4. **Audit diction and rhythm:** sentence length and word choice should support the tone
   (short clipped lines for panic; long sinuous ones for reverie).
5. **Check the ending delivers the effect at full strength,** not a different one.

## Modern application
- Unity doesn't mean monotony: contrast (a joke in a grief story) can heighten the main
  effect if it's subordinate to it.
- Literary stories may aim at a complex effect (ambivalence), but it's still one
  complex effect, not three unrelated ones.
- Length: Poe's single-sitting logic is one reason magazines favour stories under ~7,500 words.

## Gotchas
- Subplots and second POVs in a short story usually split the effect.
- Choosing the effect after drafting is fine — but then revise everything toward it.
- Over-signalling (every adjective "dark") becomes parody; vary the means, keep the aim.

## Verify
- [ ] Effect named in one phrase.
- [ ] Every scene scored; zero-value scenes cut or rewritten.
- [ ] Ending delivers that effect at its peak.
