# Titling a story

**Goal:** a title that earns its place: memorable, accurate in tone, and doing work the
story itself doesn't — framing, irony, or a second reading. In a magazine table of
contents, the title is often the only thing a reader sees.

## Types of titles that work
| Type | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Key image or object | "The Things They Carried" (O'Brien) | Concentrates the story's central figure |
| Phrase from the text | "A Good Man Is Hard to Find" (O'Connor) | Gains weight when the reader meets it |
| Ironic | "Happy Endings" (Atwood) | Sets up tension with content |
| Situation or question | "What We Talk About When We Talk About Love" (Carver) | Names the real subject |
| Character | "Bartleby, the Scrivener" (Melville) | Points at the enigma |
| Place or time | "Araby" (Joyce) | Atmosphere, symbol |

## Process
1. Finish (or nearly finish) the story first.
2. Brainstorm 20+ candidates: key nouns, striking phrases from the draft, the story's
   question, an image from the ending, a line of dialogue.
3. Shortlist 3; read each before the first line and after the last.
4. Search it in quotes plus "short story" to check how crowded it is (titles aren't
   copyrightable, but distinctiveness helps readers find yours).
5. Ask a reader which one makes them want to read.

## Avoid
- Labels ("Grief", "The Journey") unless subverted.
- Spoilers that give away a twist or ending.
- Puns that clash with a serious tone.
- Titles that only make sense after reading and mean nothing before.

## Gotchas
- Keeping the working title by inertia. Review it at final revision.
- Changing the title between simultaneous submissions — keep one per tracking record.

## Verify
- [ ] At least 20 candidates generated.
- [ ] Final title adds a layer the text doesn't state.
- [ ] Checked for spoilers and duplication.
