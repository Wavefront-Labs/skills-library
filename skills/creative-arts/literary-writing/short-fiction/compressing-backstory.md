# Compressing backstory

**Goal:** the reader knows exactly as much history as the present-time story needs —
usually far less than the writer knows — delivered without stopping the story.

## Decide what stays
For each piece of backstory ask: **does the present action make less sense, or matter less,
without it?** If not, cut it. Keep the rest in your notes (Hemingway's iceberg: what you know
and omit still strengthens the story).

## Compression techniques (from most to least compressed)
1. **Implication:** "She still set two cups out." (A death, a habit, grief — no explanation.)
2. **A subordinate clause:** "Ruth, who had not driven since the accident, took the wheel."
3. **An object or ritual:** the wedding ring on a chain, the unopened letters.
4. **A line of dialogue** loaded with shared history: "You're doing the voice again."
5. **One summarising sentence:** "They had been married eleven years, the last four badly."
6. **A brief flashback** (a paragraph): only for a moment that must be felt, not just known.
   Enter and exit with past perfect for a line or two, then simple past.

## Placement
- **Not on page one.** Establish the present situation first; readers tolerate history
  once they care.
- Drip it at the moment it becomes relevant — just before or after the present action it
  explains.

## Gotchas
- **Backstory dump** in the second paragraph — the most common short-story fault.
- **Flashback longer than the scene it interrupts.**
- Explaining a detail you already implied ("She set out two cups. Her husband had died").

## Verify
- [ ] Every backstory element tied to a present-time moment it explains.
- [ ] No backstory paragraph on the first page.
- [ ] No flashback longer than a short scene; most history in clauses or objects.
