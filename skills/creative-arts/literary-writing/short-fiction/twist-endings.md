# Twist endings

**Goal:** a final reversal that is surprising and, on reflection, inevitable — rereading
reveals it was there all along. O. Henry ("The Gift of the Magi") is the classic model;
Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" and Ambrose Bierce's "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge" are
studied examples.

## Design backwards
1. **State the hidden truth** in one sentence (what the reader assumes vs what's true).
2. **List the reader's default assumptions** you'll exploit: gender, age, species, time,
   who's alive, who the narrator is talking to, what the ritual is for.
3. **Plant 3-5 clues** that are true under both readings — they look like texture the
   first time and evidence the second.
4. **Misdirect** by giving the wrong reading something to hold on to; never lie in the
   narration (especially in close POV — the character may be wrong, the prose shouldn't
   be false).
5. **Place the reveal** as late as possible, ideally in a concrete detail, then stop quickly.

## The reframing test
The best twists don't just surprise, they **change the meaning** of everything before (the
lottery is a stoning; the gifts cancel each other but prove love). If the story means the
same thing after the twist, it's a gimmick.

## Testing
- Give it to 3 cold readers. Ask: when did you guess? Did it feel fair? Point to clues.
- If everyone guessed early: subtler plants or stronger misdirection.
- If nobody can find clues on rereading: you cheated; add plants.

## Gotchas
- Twists that **invalidate** the story (it was all a dream, he was dead and nothing mattered).
- Withholding by **narrator deception**, e.g. a POV character not thinking about the one
  thing they'd obviously think about.
- Spending the whole story on setup; the story must also be satisfying before the twist.

## Verify
- [ ] Hidden truth written down; every clue is true in both readings.
- [ ] No false statements in narration.
- [ ] Twist reframes meaning, confirmed with cold readers.
