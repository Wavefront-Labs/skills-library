# Choosing the story's time span

**Goal:** a story-present that is as short as the central change allows. A narrow window
raises intensity; long spans thin the story into summary.

## Common spans
| Span | Effect | Examples of use |
|---|---|---|
| Minutes to an hour | Real-time pressure, near-scene throughout | Flash, single-scene stories |
| A day or night | Classic short-story unity: one occasion (party, funeral, shift) | Joyce's "The Dead" (a party night) |
| A weekend or a trip | Enough for complication and turn | Visits home, holidays |
| Weeks to years | Requires white-space jumps and summary | Alice Munro's long-span stories |

## Choosing
1. Write the change in one sentence ("She decides to sell the farm").
2. Find the **pressure occasion** when it must happen: the auction, the visit, the last
   night. Start there.
3. History before the window becomes backstory compressed into the present.
4. If you need years, structure as **episodes**: 3-5 scenes at chosen points, separated by
   white space, with a sentence of summary to bridge.

## Tests
- Could the story start later? Most can.
- Could it end earlier? If the change is complete, stop.
- Are there scenes in the span where nothing turns? Summarise or cut them.

## Gotchas
- "Day in the life" spans that pass without an event that forces the change.
- Long spans told in even summary — choose moments, skip the rest.
- Clock confusion across jumps: anchor each section's time in its first line.

## Verify
- [ ] Story-present window named; it contains the change.
- [ ] Earlier events handled as backstory.
- [ ] Any time jumps marked and anchored.
