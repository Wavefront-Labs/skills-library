# Compressing a short story — one effect, nothing wasted

**Goal:** a short story (roughly 1,000–7,500 words) or flash piece (commonly under
1,000; micro under ~100–300) in which every element serves one central change or
effect. Length labels vary by magazine — check each market's guidelines.

## 1. Find the single change
A short story usually turns on **one** shift: a decision, a realisation, a
relationship altering, a situation reversing. Write it in a sentence: "A woman
clearing her father's house realises she never knew him." Everything that doesn't bear
on that shift is a candidate for cutting. (The idea of a story building to a single
unified effect goes back at least to Edgar Allan Poe's criticism.)

## 2. Constrain the canvas
Short fiction gets its power from limits:
- **Few characters:** usually one or two who matter; others are functions.
- **Narrow time span:** an hour, a day, a weekend. Long spans need summary and lose intensity.
- **Few scenes:** often two to five. Flash may be one moment.
- **One point of view.**
If the story keeps needing more of these, it may be a novel or novella idea.

## 3. Start late
- Open as close to the change as possible, with the situation already in motion.
  Many first drafts begin a page or two too early — try deleting the opening
  paragraphs and see if the story starts better.
- The first lines should establish **voice**, **a character in a situation**, and a
  **question** the reader wants answered. Place the reader quickly (who, where, roughly when).
- Backstory comes in fragments, only when the reader needs it.

## 4. Make every detail do two jobs
- A detail should characterise *and* set the scene, or foreshadow *and* create mood.
  "He'd re-taped the cracked phone screen again" gives poverty, care and stubbornness.
- **Subtext over explanation:** let what characters avoid saying carry the weight.
  Leave out what the reader can infer (Hemingway's "iceberg" idea: omission the writer
  knows about strengthens the story).
- Use the title as working space: it can supply context or an ironic angle the text omits.

## 5. Endings
- The ending completes the change, even if the plot doesn't fully resolve. A strong
  ending often **resonates** (an image, a gesture, a line of dialogue) rather than explains.
- Avoid: summarising the moral, a dream reveal, a twist that invalidates the story
  rather than reframing it.
- Test: does the last line echo or answer something in the first? Many effective
  stories have such a loop.
- **Twist endings** work when re-reading reveals they were fairly prepared.

## 6. Cut
After a rest, cut **10–20%** as a target:
- Paragraphs that repeat a beat, explanations after showing, dialogue tags beyond
  "said", stacked adjectives, throat-clearing openings.
- Read aloud; mark where you'd skim. Cut there.
- For flash: every sentence must move or deepen. Consider ending one beat earlier.

## Submitting (brief)
Read the magazine before submitting; follow formatting (often standard manuscript
format: 12 pt, double-spaced, name and word count on page one); note simultaneous
submission policies; keep a tracking spreadsheet. Response times can be months.

## Gotchas
- **Vignette not story:** a lovely moment where nothing changes. Ask what is different at the end.
- **Too much setup** in the first third.
- **Explaining the ending** in the last paragraph.
- Word-count categories, Poe's "single effect" and Hemingway's iceberg are common
  teaching frames, not rules; many great stories break them.

## Verify
- [ ] The central change fits in one sentence.
- [ ] Characters, time span and scenes are minimal for that change.
- [ ] Opening starts in motion; first paragraph sets voice, situation, question.
- [ ] Ending completes the change without explaining it.
- [ ] Draft cut by 10–20% and read aloud.
