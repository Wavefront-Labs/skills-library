# Orienting the reader (who, where, when, what's at stake)

**Goal:** by the end of the first page the reader knows whose story it is, roughly where
and when they are, and what the problem or tension is — without a paragraph of exposition.
Disorientation should be a deliberate choice, not an accident.

## The orientation checklist (first 150-300 words)
- **Who:** the POV character's name (or the "I"'s situation), with one telling detail.
- **Where:** a concrete place, sensed — not a postcard description.
- **When:** era and time-span signals (technology, idiom, season, time of day).
- **What's at issue:** the situation, desire or disturbance driving the story.
- **Whose head:** the POV and tense set unmistakably.

## Techniques
- **Anchor dialogue:** put a name and a physical action next to the first spoken line.
- **Period by object:** "She checked the pager" vs "She checked her phone" dates the story
  in two words.
- **One fact per sentence, inside action:** "Rain had found the hole in the tent again,
  and Lev, who'd promised his daughter the camping trip would be fun, held the torch steady."
- **Name the relationship early:** "my brother", "her ex-husband's new wife" — the reader
  instantly gets dynamics.

## Diagnose in feedback
Ask a cold reader after page one: Who is this? Where? What era? What does she want or
fear? Any blank answer is an orientation gap — unless you intended it.

## Deliberate disorientation
Speculative or experimental stories may withhold. Even then, anchor something firmly
(a sensory detail, a stable voice) so the reader trusts that the confusion is designed.

## Gotchas
- Pronoun-only openings ("She waited. He was late.") for too long.
- Front-loading backstory to orient — orient on the present situation; history can wait.
- Too many names in the first paragraph.

## Verify
- [ ] Cold reader can answer who/where/when/what after page one.
- [ ] Orientation delivered within action, not a block of setup.
- [ ] Any withholding is intentional and something is firmly anchored.
