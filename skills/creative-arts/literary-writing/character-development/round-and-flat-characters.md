# Round and flat characters

**Goal:** apply E. M. Forster's distinction (*Aspects of the Novel*, 1927) to decide how
much complexity each character needs.

## Definitions
- **Flat characters** are "constructed round a single idea or quality"; in their purest form
  expressible in one sentence. Forster's example: Mrs Micawber ("I never will desert Mr
  Micawber"). Easy to recognise and remember; they don't need reintroducing.
- **Round characters** are capable of "surprising in a convincing way". They have
  contradictions, inner life and the capacity to change or reveal hidden depths.
- Flat is not bad: Dickens's flat characters are vivid. Stock types (the gruff cop, the sassy
  best friend) are flat characters that are also clichéd.

## Allocating depth
1. List your cast; mark each R (round) or F (flat) by role.
2. Protagonist, antagonist and key relationship characters: R.
3. Supporting functions (the barista, the receptionist): F — one vivid trait, consistent.
4. Allow **one surprise** for a secondary character at a key moment; it can round them.

## Rounding a character
- Contradiction (a gentle man with a vicious temper about one thing).
- Private life glimpsed beyond their plot function.
- Choices that cost them, especially against expectations.
- **Surprise test:** list moments where the character does something unexpected that, in
  hindsight, fits.

## Making flat characters work
- One memorable, specific trait in speech or behaviour.
- Consistency: the flat character's predictability is part of the comedy or texture.
- Avoid stereotyping by group identity; make the single quality individual.

## Gotchas
- A round protagonist surrounded by stock types that make the world feel thin.
- Rounding every minor character: bloats the book.

## Verify
- [ ] Cast list marked R/F by role.
- [ ] Each round character has at least one convincing surprise.
- [ ] Flat characters have one specific, non-stereotyped trait.
