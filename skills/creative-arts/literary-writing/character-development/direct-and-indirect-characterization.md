# Direct and indirect characterization

**Goal:** readers come to know characters mainly through what they do and say, with direct
statements used deliberately. Burroway's *Writing Fiction* names the methods of presenting
character: appearance, action, speech, thought, and the narrator's or other characters'
direct statements.

## The two modes
- **Direct:** the narrator or another character tells us a trait. "She was generous to a fault."
  Fast, clear, can carry voice or irony.
- **Indirect:** the reader infers the trait. Commonly taught as **STEAL**:
  - **S**peech — what and how they say.
  - **T**houghts — what they think (in their POV).
  - **E**ffect on others — how others react to them.
  - **A**ctions — what they do, especially under pressure or when unobserved.
  - **L**ooks — appearance, dress, possessions, chosen and unchosen.

## Using both
1. **Show the trait first** in an action; a later direct statement can then confirm or
   contradict it (irony).
2. Use direct characterization **for minor characters** to save words ("Mr Dyer, who had
   never forgiven anyone").
3. Use direct statements **with an unreliable or opinionated narrator** — the statement
   characterises the narrator as much as the subject.
4. **Contradiction** is rich: a narrator calls someone generous while we watch them count
   change.

## Revision pass
- Highlight every adjective of character (*kind, shy, arrogant*). For each important
  character, replace at least the first with a scene or gesture that proves it.
- Check each main character has at least three STEAL channels in play.

## Gotchas
- **Label stacking** ("brave, loyal, stubborn Jake").
- **All-show for minor characters** — wastes pages.
- Others' reactions that don't match the shown behaviour.

## Verify
- [ ] Main characters' key traits demonstrated in action before being named.
- [ ] Direct statements used for efficiency, irony or narrator voice.
- [ ] Multiple STEAL channels used for each main character.
