# Introducing a character on the page

**Goal:** a first appearance that makes the character vivid and placeable in a few lines,
with an impression that later scenes deepen or complicate.

## Elements of a strong entrance
1. **In action:** doing something characteristic, ideally under slight pressure — not
   standing for description.
2. **One telling detail** that implies a lot (a tie loosened before 9 a.m.; a child's drawing
   taped to a toolbox). One or two details beat a head-to-toe inventory.
3. **Name and role anchored:** name plus relationship or function ("Jen, the night manager,
   who'd hired him").
4. **A want or tension:** what they're after in this scene.
5. **A hook of contradiction or mystery:** something that doesn't fit and makes readers curious.
6. **POV filter:** the description tells us about the observer too. A nervous POV notices
   hands; a cop notices exits.

## Protagonist vs others
- **Protagonist:** introduce early (usually first paragraph), with a scene showing their
  normal and their flaw or desire.
- **Antagonist:** an entrance that demonstrates threat or competence.
- **Minor characters:** a tag line and a function; resist backstory.

## Revision checks
- Highlight the first appearance of each named character. Count descriptive details: more
  than three in one block is an inventory.
- Read just the first sentences of each introduction: can you picture and distinguish them?
- Check names: no two major characters with the same first letter or syllable count
  introduced together.

## Gotchas
- **Mirror description** or a list of hair, eyes, height.
- Introducing five characters at once (a party scene): stagger them or attach each to an action.
- Backstory paragraph on first appearance.

## Verify
- [ ] Each major character enters doing something.
- [ ] One or two telling details per entrance, through the POV's eyes.
- [ ] Name, role and a want established within the first appearance.
