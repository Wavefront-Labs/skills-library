# Verbal tics and signature phrases

**Goal:** a memorable verbal marker that identifies a character and reveals something
about them, without becoming a gimmick. Dickens is the classic case (Micawber's
"something will turn up", Uriah Heep's "umble") — and the classic warning about overuse.

## Choose a tic that means something
A tic should come from psychology or background:
- An evasion: "To be honest..." before every lie.
- Anxiety: apologising before speaking; ending statements as questions.
- Profession: a detective who says "Walk me through it."
- Status: a boss who never finishes sentences because others will.
- Family inheritance: a phrase their mother said.

## Ration it
- Introduce it early, use it a handful of times across the book, not in every scene.
- Vary its form so it doesn't read as copy-paste (the "honest" character might say
  "honestly", "if I'm honest", "to be fair").
- Let other characters notice or mock it once — it confirms the reader's perception.

## Make it work for the arc
- **Break the pattern at a key moment:** the character who always jokes says something
  plainly; the one who always says "fine" finally says "I'm not fine."
- **Transfer:** another character picks up the phrase (influence, grief, love).
- **Callback:** the phrase returns at the ending with changed meaning.

## Non-verbal equivalents
Gestures (cleaning glasses, cracking knuckles) work the same way and follow the same rationing rule.

## Gotchas
- Tic as the character's only trait.
- Every character getting a tic (it becomes a sitcom).
- Tics used for comedy in a serious register, or for a minority character in a way that mocks them.

## Verify
- [ ] Tic traceable to psychology or background.
- [ ] Count of uses checked across the manuscript (search the phrase).
- [ ] At least one deliberate break or callback at a turning point.
