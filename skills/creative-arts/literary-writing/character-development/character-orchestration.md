# Character orchestration

**Goal:** design the cast as a set of contrasting instruments so that every combination
of characters produces conflict and every character embodies a different response to the
story's central question. The term comes from Lajos Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing*
(1946): well-orchestrated characters are sharply defined opposites; Robert McKee's "cast
design" in *Story* is a similar idea.

## Method
1. **Name the central question or theme** ("Is loyalty worth any price?").
2. **Place each main character on the spectrum** of answers: one says yes absolutely, one
   no, one wavers, one exploits loyalty.
3. **Make contrasts sharp:** temperament (impulsive/cautious), values, social position,
   methods. Two characters with the same stance and temperament: merge or differentiate.
4. **Build a pair grid:** list every pair of main characters and write the conflict or
   tension between them. Empty cells = missed scenes or redundant characters.
5. **Check function coverage:** protagonist, antagonist, ally, mentor, love interest,
   skeptic, comic relief — not required, but gaps or duplicates show.

## Example grid (abbreviated)
| | Ana | Ben | Carla |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | — | wants his loyalty; he wants her to leave the family | rivals for the father's trust |
| Ben | | — | allies but disagree on methods |

## Gotchas
- **Interchangeable sidekicks** with the same voice and stance.
- Contrast only in surface (hair, job) rather than in values and response to conflict.
- A perfectly symmetrical cast that feels schematic — vary weight and complexity.

## Verify
- [ ] Each main character has a distinct stance on the central question.
- [ ] Pair grid has a real tension in every important cell.
- [ ] No two characters share the same function and stance.
