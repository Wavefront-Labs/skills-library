# Naming characters

**Goal:** names that are plausible for the character's time, place and family, that
are easy to tell apart, and that carry the right associations.

## Plausibility
- Work out the **birth year**, not the story year: a 60-year-old in 2025 was born in
  the mid-1960s. Use national name statistics by birth year (US Social Security
  Administration baby-name data; UK Office for National Statistics; equivalents elsewhere).
- Consider **class, region, religion, ethnicity and family tradition** (named after
  a grandparent; a parent's aspirations). A name can itself say something about the parents.
- For other cultures, check naming order, patronymics, honorifics and how nicknames form.
- Surnames: regional surname distributions and immigration history.

## Distinctness across the cast
Keep a **name list** (in the style sheet):
- Different **first letters** for major characters (Mark, Mike and Matt blur).
- Different **syllable counts** and sounds (not Jana and Jane).
- Avoid names that rhyme or end alike.
- Be consistent about what the narrator calls each character (surname vs first name);
  changes in what characters call each other can signal intimacy or distance.

## Meaning and sound
- Associations matter more than etymology most readers won't know.
- Aptronyms (names that describe: Gradgrind, Lovelace) suit comic or allegorical modes,
  rarely realism.
- Say the name aloud; check it with the surname and in dialogue.

## Checks
- Search the full name: avoid an identifiable real person in a similar role (a real
  local politician, a public figure), especially for villains.
- Check unintended meanings in other languages for important names.
- Fantasy names: see `creative-arts.literary-writing.worldbuilding` naming skills.

## Verify
- [ ] Each name checked against birth-year and cultural data.
- [ ] Name list has no two major characters with the same initial or sound.
- [ ] Full names searched for real-person conflicts.
