# Want, need and character arc — characters who make the plot happen

**Goal:** a protagonist whose desires create the plot, whose inner problem gives it
meaning, and whose change (or refusal to change) is shown through choices, not
announced.

## 1. Want: the external goal
- Concrete and visible: win the case, find the daughter, get the promotion.
- **Active:** they pursue it, so the plot can follow their attempts.
- **Stakes:** what's lost if they fail — personal, specific stakes beat abstract ones
  ("my daughter dies" over "the city falls"; both together is better).

## 2. Need: the internal problem
- A **false belief** (often called "the lie the character believes") that limits
  them: "I can only be safe if I control everything." "Love has to be earned."
- Often rooted in a **wound** or backstory event — reveal it sparingly and late;
  knowing it is more important for the writer than for the reader.
- The **need** is what they must understand or become to be whole: trust others,
  accept loss, take responsibility.
- Best arcs set want and need in tension: pursuing the want the old way fails because
  of the false belief.

## 3. Arc types
| Arc | What happens | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive (change)** | Character lets go of the lie, gains the need | Control freak learns to trust and so wins |
| **Negative** (fall/corruption) | Character clings to or embraces the lie | Ambition consumes them |
| **Flat (steadfast)** | Character already holds the truth and changes the world around them | Mentor-like heroes, many series leads |
Know which you're writing; readers sense an arc that wobbles.

## 4. Reveal through choices under pressure
- **Character is choice under pressure.** What someone does when it costs them
  tells readers more than any description. Build scenes that force choices between two
  things the character values.
- Track the arc across the plot beats: at the start, they act on the lie and it
  roughly works; at the midpoint they glimpse the truth; at the crisis the lie fails
  badly; at the climax they choose (truth or lie) — the climactic action should be
  impossible for the opening version of the character.
- **Show inner life efficiently:** a gesture or habit, a revealing possession, how
  they treat someone with less power, what they notice (a carpenter notices joinery).

## 5. The antagonist
- Give them a clear want, a reason that makes sense to *them*, and competence.
- The best antagonists press directly on the protagonist's flaw, or embody the lie
  taken to its end (a foil).
- The antagonist does not have to be a villain or a person: society, nature, or a
  loved one with opposing goals.

## 6. Distinct voices and ensembles
- Differentiate characters by vocabulary, sentence length, what they talk about, what
  they avoid, humour, and verbal habits. Test: cover the dialogue tags — can you tell
  who's speaking?
- In ensembles, give each member a distinct want and a role relative to the
  protagonist (ally, mirror, tempter, conscience). Merge characters who serve the same function.

## Gotchas
- **Passive protagonist:** things happen to them. Give them a goal and make them choose.
- **Instant change:** the epiphany arrives without pressure or cost. Earn it through failures.
- **Backstory dumps** early. Withhold; let behaviour raise questions first.
- **Perfect characters** (no flaw, no cost) can't arc and are hard to care about.
- Terms like "the lie" and "wound/ghost" come from popular craft books (e.g. K. M.
  Weiland, screenwriting texts); they're tools, not laws.

## Verify
- [ ] Protagonist's want is concrete, active and has personal stakes.
- [ ] Need and false belief written in one line each, in tension with the want.
- [ ] Arc type chosen; climax choice is impossible for the opening version of them.
- [ ] At least three scenes force a costly choice.
- [ ] Antagonist has their own logic and presses on the flaw; voices are distinguishable without tags.
