# Drafting and revising a novel — finish, then fix in order

**Goal:** get from idea to a complete draft (most unfinished novels die in the
middle), then revise from the biggest problems down to sentences, so effort isn't
wasted polishing scenes that get cut.

## 1. Know the scale
A typical adult novel is roughly 70,000–100,000 words (genre norms differ: many
fantasy books run longer, many romances and middle-grade shorter — check comparable
recent titles). At 500 words a day that is about five to seven months of drafting.

## 2. Pick a planning level
- **Outliners (plotters):** scene list or chapter outline before drafting; risk is a
  dead, mechanical draft. Leave room to deviate.
- **Discovery writers (pantsers):** write to find the story; risk is a sprawling draft
  needing heavy structural revision.
- Most writers mix: know the opening, a few key turns and roughly the ending, discover
  the rest. Whatever you choose, know the protagonist's goal and what stands in the way —
  see creative-arts.literary-writing.plot-structure.outlining-with-scene-and-sequel.

## 3. Drafting habits
- **Fixed daily or weekly target** (words or time) you can hit on bad days. Track it.
- **Draft forward.** Don't revise earlier chapters until the draft is done. When you
  change your mind about something earlier, leave a note (`[TK: Mara is now his sister —
  fix ch 3]`) and write as if it's already fixed.
- Use placeholders (`TK` is the traditional mark — it rarely appears in real words,
  so it's searchable) for names, facts and research you'd otherwise stop to look up.
- **Stop mid-scene** with the next line in mind; starting tomorrow is easier.
- The middle sag: if stuck, raise the stakes, reveal a secret, or make the protagonist's
  plan fail. Skipping ahead to a scene you're excited about is allowed.

## 4. Rest, then revise in passes
Put the draft away for a few weeks if possible. Then work big to small:
1. **Read-through and reverse outline:** read in as few sittings as you can, making no
   line edits. Write one line per scene: what happens, whose POV, what changes. Note
   where you got bored or confused.
2. **Structural pass:** does each scene change something (situation, knowledge,
   relationship)? Cut or merge scenes that don't. Check the arc, the causality chain,
   the midpoint and the climax pay off the opening's promise. Move, add or cut scenes.
3. **Scene pass:** within each scene: clear goal and conflict, enters late, exits
   early, consistent POV (no head-hopping), sensory grounding in the first lines.
4. **Line pass:** sentences, dialogue, repetition, filter words ("she saw", "he felt"),
   adverbs carrying weak verbs, crutch words (search your own — e.g. *just*, *really*, *that*).
5. **Copy/proof pass:** consistency of names, timeline, spelling; read aloud or use
   text-to-speech to catch errors.

## 5. Beta readers
- After your own structural and scene passes, give the manuscript to 3–5 readers of the genre.
- Ask specific questions: where did you lose interest? what confused you? which
  character did you care about? what did you expect to happen?
- Treat a note raised by several readers as a real problem; their suggested fix is optional.

## Gotchas
- **Endless revising of chapter one** before the draft is finished is the classic trap.
- **Polishing before restructuring** wastes work on scenes you'll cut.
- **Comparing your first draft to published books** — those are tenth drafts.
- **Losing the manuscript:** keep versioned backups off the machine (cloud plus one other).
- Word-count ranges are approximate and market-dependent; verify against current
  agents' or publishers' guidance for your genre.

## Verify
- [ ] A complete draft exists start to finish, with `TK` notes instead of mid-draft rewrites.
- [ ] A reverse outline lists every scene and what it changes.
- [ ] Revision done in order: structure → scene → line → proof.
- [ ] Beta-reader notes collected; repeated issues addressed.
- [ ] Word count within the genre's usual range, or a reason it isn't.
