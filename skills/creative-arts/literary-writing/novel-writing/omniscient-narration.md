# Omniscient narration

**Goal:** a narrator who knows everything — any character's thoughts, past and future,
facts no character knows — used with control so readers trust it. Classic examples:
*Middlemarch*, *War and Peace*, *Pride and Prejudice* (partly), and in modern fiction
Zadie Smith's *White Teeth* or Markus Zusak's *The Book Thief* (narrated by Death).

## What makes it omniscient, not head-hopping
- **The narrator has a voice of its own** — opinions, generalisations, wit ("It is a truth
  universally acknowledged..."). Readers follow the narrator, not a character.
- **Movement between minds is deliberate and signalled**, usually by pulling out to a
  wider view before entering another head (raise psychic distance, then lower it).
- It is consistent across the book, established early.

## Techniques
1. **Establish the voice in the opening pages** — a generalisation, a view from above, a
   comment on the characters.
2. **Transition through distance:** close in A's thoughts -> a neutral view of the room ->
   into B. Gardner's psychic distance scale is the tool.
3. **Use prolepsis** (flash-forward): "She would remember this evening thirty years later."
4. **Summarise collective states:** "The town had decided, by then, that..."
5. Usually stay with one mind per scene or paragraph group, even though you could move.

## When to choose it
- Large casts and social panoramas; satire; stories where irony between what characters
  believe and what's true is the point; sagas spanning generations.
- Less suited to suspense that relies on not knowing what another character thinks
  (you'll need to explain why the narrator withholds).

## Gotchas
- **Rapid unsignalled switching** within a paragraph reads as an error, not omniscience.
- **A voiceless omniscient** — knows everything but has no personality — feels like
  head-hopping to editors.
- **Withholding inconsistently:** if the narrator knows the murderer, readers must
  accept a reason for silence.

## Verify
- [ ] Narrator's voice and omniscience established in the first chapter.
- [ ] Each change of mind is preceded by a pull-out or a clear transition.
- [ ] Withheld information follows a consistent principle.
