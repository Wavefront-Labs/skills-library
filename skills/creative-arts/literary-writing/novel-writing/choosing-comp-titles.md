# Choosing comp titles

**Goal:** tell an agent or editor in one sentence where the book sits on the shelf and
who will buy it. Comps are a sales tool; publishers use them to project sales.

## Criteria for a good comp
- **Recent:** published in roughly the last 3-5 years (older classics say nothing about
  today's market).
- **Same category and age group:** adult/YA/middle grade, and the same genre or
  subgenre shelf.
- **Successful but plausible:** solid sellers, not phenomena. Comparing yourself to a
  multi-million-copy bestseller reads as naive.
- **Similar on a real axis:** tone, premise, structure, audience — say which.
- Film/TV comps can supplement but not replace book comps.

## Finding them
1. Search your genre's recent releases (bookstore shelves, Goodreads lists, publisher
   catalogues, BISAC category bestseller lists below the top 20).
2. Check deal reports in Publishers Marketplace for books "in the vein of" yours.
3. Read them — a comp you have not read is a liability in a call with an agent.
4. Check reader overlap: "readers also bought" lists that link your candidates.

## Phrasing
- "TITLE will appeal to readers of A and B."
- "A meets B" when the combination is genuinely informative ("the voice of A with the
  structure of B").
- Name what is comparable: "the dual timeline of A and the coastal setting of B".

## Gotchas
- Comping only to classics or to your influences.
- Comps from the wrong age category (YA comps for an adult novel).
- Comps whose sales were poor (a publisher will look them up).
- Too many comps (more than three) dilutes the signal.

## Verify
- [ ] Each comp published within about five years.
- [ ] Each comp matches category and readership.
- [ ] None is a once-a-decade phenomenon.
- [ ] You have read each comp and can say what is similar.
