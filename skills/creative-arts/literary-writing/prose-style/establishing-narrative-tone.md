# Establishing narrative tone

**Goal:** a consistent, deliberate attitude in the narration so readers know how to
take what happens. **Tone** is the narrator's (or implied author's) attitude toward the
subject and reader — ironic, tender, clinical, comic, elegiac. **Mood** is the feeling
the reader experiences. A deadpan tone can create a horrified mood.

## Where tone comes from
- **Diction:** formal/informal, Latinate/plain, slang, profanity (see `diction-and-register`).
- **Syntax and rhythm:** long and lyrical vs clipped.
- **Selection:** which details are noticed (the comic narrator notices the absurd).
- **Distance:** ironic distance vs closeness.
- **Figurative language:** source and frequency of comparisons.
- **What's withheld or understated:** understatement is a tone (British reserve, noir cool).

## Procedure
1. Write a **tone statement** for the book in three adjectives ("wry, warm,
   unsentimental"). Keep it beside the draft.
2. Make the first page establish it — readers calibrate quickly and feel betrayed by
   unmotivated shifts later.
3. In revision, flag passages that break the statement. Are they deliberate shifts?
4. **Deliberate shifts**: when a comic book turns dark, prepare it (small shadows earlier)
   and change tone at a structural point; the contrast then becomes the effect.

## Tone and genre
- Readers expect certain tonal ranges (cozy mystery: light even about murder; horror:
  dread). Departures can be fresh but should be signalled early.

## Gotchas
- **Tonal whiplash:** slapstick next to a sincere grief scene without control.
- **Sentimentality:** telling the reader to feel more than the material earns —
  understatement usually moves readers more.
- **Unintended sarcasm** from overuse of scare quotes or rhetorical questions.

## Verify
- [ ] Tone statement written; first page matches it.
- [ ] All tonal shifts are deliberate and placed at structural points.
- [ ] Beta readers describe the tone with adjectives close to the statement.
