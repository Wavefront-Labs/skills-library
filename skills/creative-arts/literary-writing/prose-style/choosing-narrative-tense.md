# Choosing past or present tense

**Goal:** pick the narrative tense deliberately and keep it consistent.

## Past tense (the default)
- Invisible to most readers; maximum flexibility.
- Implies a narrator telling after the fact, allowing **reflection and foreshadowing**
  ("I didn't know then...").
- Easy movement into deeper past with past perfect (see
  `creative-arts.literary-writing.plot-structure.writing-flashbacks`).

## Present tense
- **Immediacy**, uncertainty: neither narrator nor reader knows what happens next.
- Common in contemporary literary fiction and much YA (often first-person present).
- Constraints: reflection must be in the moment; summary is harder; flashbacks move into
  simple past; some readers find it tiring over a long book.

## Other options
- **Future** or **second person** for short passages or experimental works.
- Mixed: present for one timeline, past for another in dual-timeline novels — a useful
  orientation cue.

## Choosing
1. Write the same opening page in both tenses; read aloud.
2. Ask: does the book need retrospective wisdom (past) or moment-to-moment suspense (present)?
3. Check genre norms and comparable titles.

## Converting a draft
- Global find-and-replace is unreliable; convert manually, chapter by chapter.
- Watch for: irregular verbs, "would" habitual constructions, past perfect becoming
  present perfect or simple past, and dialogue (which keeps its own tenses).
- After conversion, search for common past forms ("was", "had", "said") and check each.

## Gotchas
- Unintended tense slips (the most common copyedit issue in present-tense drafts).
- Using present tense only because it seems "literary".
- Historical present in summary passages of a past-tense book without intent.

- [ ] Tense chosen after comparing test pages.
