# Period and historical detail

**Goal:** a past that feels lived-in and is accurate enough to survive knowledgeable
readers, without stopping the story for history lessons.

## Research the texture, not just the events
Collect the daily-life details characters would take for granted:
- Money and prices, wages, what things cost.
- Light, heat, water, sanitation, smells.
- Clothes and how long it takes to put them on; laundry.
- Food, mealtimes, who cooks; transport times between places.
- Communication speed (letters, telegrams, phones) — it shapes plots.
- Social rules: forms of address, what can be said in mixed company, legal status of women, servants, minorities.
Sources: diaries, letters, newspapers and their adverts, catalogues, court records,
photographs, museum collections, period novels (as evidence of voice and assumptions).

## Filter through period consciousness
- Characters don't notice what is normal to them; a Victorian doesn't marvel at gaslight.
  Describe what *they* would notice (a new invention, a stain, a social slight).
- Give characters period attitudes, not modern ones in costume — while choosing what
  the narrative frames critically.

## Language and anachronism
- Check suspicious words and idioms in the **Oxford English Dictionary** (first-use
  dates) or the Online Etymology Dictionary ("okay", "teenager", "boyfriend" are later than many assume).
- Dialogue: aim for an impression of period speech — avoid modern slang, but don't
  write pastiche archaism that tires readers.
- Units, titles, place names as they were then.

## Avoiding the research dump
- Keep a research file; use perhaps a tenth of it on the page.
- One concrete period detail per scene beats a paragraph of context.
- An author's note can handle liberties and context.

## Verify
- [ ] Daily-life details researched for each major setting.
- [ ] Suspicious words checked for first-use dates.
- [ ] No paragraph exists only to convey history.
- [ ] Deliberate liberties listed for an author's note.
