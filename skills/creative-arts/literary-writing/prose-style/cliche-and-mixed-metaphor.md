# Cliché and mixed metaphor

**Goal:** prose free of worn phrases and of figures that collide. Orwell ("Politics and
the English Language"): "Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which
you are used to seeing in print."

## Spotting clichés
- Phrases that complete themselves: *at the end of the ___*, *heart ___ (pounding)*,
  *a shiver ran down her ___*, *time ___ (stood still)*.
- Stock body language: jaw clenched, breath she didn't know she was holding, eyes
  widened, let out a sigh.
- Stock descriptions: piercing blue eyes, raven hair, chiselled jaw.
- Test: if you've read the phrase many times, readers have too.

## Fixing a cliché (three options)
1. **Cut** — often the plain statement is stronger ("She was afraid").
2. **Literalise / specify** — replace with the concrete particular of this moment
   ("her hands wouldn't stop arranging the cutlery").
3. **Freshen** — twist the expected phrase so it surprises (only occasionally; it can feel cute).
Clichés in **dialogue** may be correct characterisation — people talk in clichés.

## Mixed metaphors
A mixed metaphor combines incompatible vehicles: *"We'll have to step up to the plate
and nip this in the bud before it snowballs."*
- Check every extended figure: if the first vehicle is a ship, don't switch to a
  battlefield mid-sentence.
- **Dead metaphors** (bottleneck, grasp an idea) are normally invisible, but placing two
  near each other can revive them absurdly ("the bottleneck grasped the opportunity").

## Procedure
1. Read for completion-phrases and stock reactions; highlight.
2. Keep a personal list of your own recurring clichés and search for them each draft.
3. For each figure longer than a phrase, write its vehicle in the margin; look for switches.

## Verify
- [ ] Personal cliché list searched.
- [ ] Narration free of stock body language in key scenes.
- [ ] Every extended figure uses a single, consistent vehicle.
