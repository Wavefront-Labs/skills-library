# Symbol and motif

**Goal:** objects, images and patterns that accumulate meaning across a story, so
theme is felt rather than stated.

## Definitions
- **Symbol:** a concrete thing that also suggests something beyond itself (the green
  light in *The Great Gatsby*). It must work literally first.
- **Motif:** a recurring element — image, phrase, object, situation — whose repetition
  creates pattern and resonance (water in *Mrs Dalloway*, birds, mirrors).

## Grow them, don't impose them
1. After drafting, **search your draft** for images you return to without having planned
   to (rain, hands, keys, a song). These are often your real motifs.
2. Choose 1-3 to develop. Ask what each is connected to emotionally or thematically.
3. **Place them at structural points:** opening, midpoint, crisis, ending.
4. **Let the meaning change:** the motif should look different as the character
   changes (the locked box is fear, then secret, then release when opened).
5. Tie symbols to **characters' actions** (someone keeps, breaks, gives away, returns the object).

## Calibrating subtlety
- The literal story must make sense without decoding.
- Don't have characters explain the symbol ("the bird is like my freedom").
- Mention a motif often enough to register (three or more appearances) but in varied
  contexts and forms.
- Conventional symbols (doves, crosses, red roses) carry baggage; use deliberately or
  make them specific to this story.

## Gotchas
- Symbolism planted from a theme list that the story doesn't need.
- Allegorical one-to-one codes in a realist story (see `allegory-and-objective-correlative`).
- Overloading one object with several unrelated meanings.

## Verify
- [ ] 1-3 motifs identified, each appearing at structural points.
- [ ] Each motif's meaning develops across the book.
- [ ] No character explains a symbol.
