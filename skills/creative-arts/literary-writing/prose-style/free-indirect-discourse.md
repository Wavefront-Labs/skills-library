# Free indirect discourse

**Goal:** render a character's thoughts in their own idiom within third-person narration,
so the narrative voice and the character's mind merge. Associated with Austen and
Flaubert; the default mode of modern close third person.

## The three ways to render thought
| Mode | Example |
|---|---|
| Direct thought | *She thought, I can't stand another minute of this.* |
| Indirect (reported) | *She thought that she couldn't stand another minute of it.* |
| **Free indirect** | *She couldn't stand another minute of this. God, the man never stopped talking.* |

## Mechanics
- **Person and tense** stay as the narration (third person, past): *couldn't*, *was*.
- **Deixis and idiom** come from the character: *this*, *now*, *here*, *tomorrow*, and
  their vocabulary, exclamations, questions, slang ("God", "Honestly", "Was he mad?").
- **No tags or italics** — no "she thought".
- The character's judgements appear as if narrated: *The party was a disaster, naturally.*

## Moving in and out
- Enter from a neutral narrated sentence anchoring whose head we're in: *Emma looked at
  the letter.* -> *What could he mean by it? Nothing good, certainly.*
- Exit with a return to external action or a more neutral register.
- Psychic distance controls depth (see `controlling-psychic-distance`).

## Uses
- Irony: the narration can voice a character's self-deceptions without endorsing them
  (Austen's Emma); the reader sees what the character can't.
- Economy: thought and narration flow without tags, keeping pace.

## Gotchas
- **Ambiguity of attribution:** in multi-character scenes, readers may not know whose
  thought it is — anchor with the viewpoint character's name at scene start and avoid
  switching mid-scene (head-hopping).
- Mixing in first-person present ("I can't...") without italics or tags reads as an error.
- Character idiom that the narrator would never use leaking into neutral narration
  when you didn't intend it.

## Verify
- [ ] Thought passages stay in narrative person and tense.
- [ ] Character idiom marks the free indirect passages.
- [ ] Whose mind we are in is never in doubt.
