# Allegory and the objective correlative

**Goal:** carry emotion and meaning through concrete situations rather than statement.

## The objective correlative
T.S. Eliot ("Hamlet and His Problems", 1919): the only way to express emotion in art is
by finding "a set of objects, a situation, a chain of events which shall be the formula
of that particular emotion", so that when these are given, the emotion is evoked.

Eliot formulated it about drama, and it is taught equally in poetry workshops (Oliver's
*A Poetry Handbook*, "Imagery"): a poem's images, not its statements, should carry its
feeling. The procedure below works line by line in a poem as well as scene by scene in prose.

Applying it:
1. Name the precise emotion for the scene (not just "sad" — "the guilt of relief at a
   death").
2. Find the situation, object or action that *produces* that emotion in the reader:
   the widow cancelling the second newspaper subscription.
3. Present it without naming the emotion; trust it.
4. Check: if readers report the emotion you named in step 1, it worked. If they report
   nothing, the correlative is too private; if they report it too strongly, you may be
   over-determining with extra signals.

## Allegory, fable, parable
- **Allegory:** a narrative where characters and events systematically stand for
  something else (*Animal Farm*, *Pilgrim's Progress*).
- **Fable / parable:** short tales built to carry a lesson.
- Choose allegory deliberately; it works best when the surface story is also gripping
  and the mapping isn't one-to-one rigid. Readers resent being lectured.
- In realist fiction, avoid accidental allegory: characters named for virtues, plots
  that exist to prove a thesis.

## Gotchas
- Naming the emotion right after the correlative ("...She felt guilty.") — redundant.
- Private correlatives that mean something only to the author.
- Allegory so obvious the story can be skipped.

## Verify
- [ ] Key emotional scenes rely on a concrete situation, not named emotion.
- [ ] Test readers name the intended emotion unprompted.
- [ ] If allegorical, the surface story stands on its own.
