# Rendering direct thought

**Goal:** present a character's verbatim thoughts so they're clear on the page and
don't clutter the prose. Browne & King (*Self-Editing for Fiction Writers*, "Interior
Monologue") recommend rendering most thought indirectly and keeping direct thought for
emphasis.

## Options
| Form | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Free indirect (preferred) | *He'd blown it. Again.* | Narrative tense and person, no tag |
| Direct, italics, no tag | *I've blown it again.* | First person present, italic signals thought |
| Direct, roman, tagged | I've blown it again, he thought. | Tag does the work; no italics needed |
| Direct, italic + tag | *I've blown it again,* he thought. | Redundant; editors usually cut one |

Chicago style allows direct thought in italics or roman with a tag; do **not** use
quotation marks for unspoken thought (they signal speech).

## Rules of thumb
- Choose one convention for the book and record it in the style sheet.
- Use direct italic thought **sparingly** — for sudden, emphatic or verbatim moments
  (a prayer, a shock). Pages of italics are tiring.
- Keep the tense/person shift clear: italic direct thought is usually first person,
  present tense; surrounding narration stays third/past.
- First-person narration doesn't need italics for thought at all — the whole narration is the mind.
- Telepathic speech in SFF often uses italics; then avoid italics for ordinary thought to prevent confusion.

## Gotchas
- "*I'm so tired,* she thought tiredly" — redundant tag, italic and adverb.
- Thoughts that explain what the scene already showed.
- Characters thinking in complete, formal sentences — thought is fragmentary.

## Verify
- [ ] One thought convention used consistently, recorded in the style sheet.
- [ ] No quotation marks around thoughts.
- [ ] Direct italic thought reserved for emphasis.
