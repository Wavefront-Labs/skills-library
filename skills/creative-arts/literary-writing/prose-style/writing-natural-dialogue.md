# Writing natural dialogue — the illusion of real talk

**Goal:** dialogue that feels like real people talking, moves the story, reveals
character, and is easy to follow on the page. Real speech transcribed is unreadable;
good dialogue is speech with the boring parts removed.

## 1. Cut the filler
Drop greetings, small talk, "ums", and exchanges that only confirm logistics
("Want coffee?" "Sure." "Milk?" "Please."). Enter the conversation late and leave early,
like a scene. Keep a filler beat only when it does work (awkwardness, avoidance).

## 2. Every exchange needs tension
- Characters want different things in the conversation, even slightly. Agreement is
  flat; disagreement, evasion or misunderstanding has energy.
- **Subtext:** people rarely say exactly what they mean. A couple arguing about the
  dishes may really be arguing about respect. Let the surface topic carry the real one.
- **Answering a question with something else** is a strong subtext tool: evasion
  tells the reader as much as an answer.

## 3. Tags and beats
- **"Said" is invisible** — readers skip it. Use it (and "asked") by default. "Said
  bookisms" (*expostulated, hissed, chortled*) draw attention and often describe what the
  line should already convey. Note: you can't "smile" or "laugh" a line of dialogue.
- **Adverbs on tags** ("she said angrily") usually signal the line isn't doing its job;
  rewrite the line.
- **Action beats** replace tags and add physical grounding:
  `Mara set down the knife. "You knew."` — the beat identifies the speaker.
- In a two-person exchange, after establishing speakers you can drop tags for several
  lines; re-anchor every few lines so readers don't count back.
- Position: tag after the first natural phrase in a long line — `"I didn't,"
  he said, "and I won't."`

## 4. Don't make characters explain
- **"As you know, Bob"**: characters telling each other what both already know for the
  reader's benefit. Fix by giving the information to someone who doesn't know it, by
  conflict ("You promised you'd never go back there"), or by leaving it implied.
- Break long speeches with interruptions, reactions or beats unless the monologue is the point.

## 5. Voice
- Each character's speech differs in vocabulary, sentence length, formality, rhythm,
  and what they talk about. A teen and a judge don't share idioms.
- **Dialect:** suggest it through word choice and syntax rather than heavy phonetic
  spelling, which is hard to read and can caricature. A few markers go a long way.
- Swearing, slang: match character and period; dated slang ages a book quickly.

## 6. Punctuation (US/UK conventions differ — follow your publisher's style)
- Comma inside the quote before a tag: `"Come here," she said.`
- Question or exclamation keeps its mark, tag stays lowercase: `"Where?" he asked.`
- Beat, not tag, gets a full stop: `"Fine." She left.`
- New speaker = new paragraph. Interruptions with an em dash (`"I was—"`), trailing off
  with an ellipsis.
- US style uses double quotes outside, single inside; UK publishing often the reverse.

## 7. Test by reading aloud
Read every dialogue scene out loud, or have text-to-speech read it. Stumbles, lines
nobody would say, and unclear speakers jump out immediately.

## Gotchas
- **Characters using each other's names** constantly — real people rarely do.
- **Everyone sounds like the author:** cover names; can you identify speakers?
- **Perfectly grammatical speech** sounds stiff — people use fragments and contractions.
- Punctuation conventions shown are common US practice; house styles vary.

## Verify
- [ ] No greeting/logistics filler unless it does work.
- [ ] Each exchange has conflicting wants or subtext.
- [ ] Tags mostly "said"/"asked" or replaced by action beats; no adverb-propped tags.
- [ ] No "as you know" exposition; speeches broken up.
- [ ] Read aloud; speakers distinguishable with tags covered; punctuation consistent.
