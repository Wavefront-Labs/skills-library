# Concrete over abstract language

**Goal:** prose the reader can see, hear and feel. Abstract words name ideas, emotions
and categories (*love, fear, beautiful, vehicle, cruelty*); concrete words name things
the senses register (*a dented thermos, the smell of diesel*). S.I. Hayakawa's **ladder
of abstraction** runs from the specific (Bessie the cow) up to the general (livestock,
farm assets, wealth).

## Revision pass
1. Highlight **named emotions** (angry, sad, nervous, happy, jealous) in narration.
2. Highlight **evaluative adjectives** (beautiful, amazing, terrible, interesting) —
   they tell the reader what to think.
3. Highlight **category nouns** (vehicle, building, food, tree, weapon).
4. For each, step down the ladder:
   - Emotion -> behaviour, body, thought or object: *nervous* -> *she checked the
     oven was off for the third time*.
   - Evaluation -> the thing that causes the evaluation: *beautiful garden* -> *roses
     tied up with strips of old nightdress*.
   - Category -> instance: *tree* -> *a lightning-split oak*.

## Keep abstraction where it works
- Summary and transitions ("Those were hard years") — abstraction compresses time.
- After concrete evidence, a single abstract word can land with force.
- Thematic statements by characters (in voice), and essayistic narration where the
  narrator's ideas are the point.
- Varying the ladder: good prose moves up and down — a concrete image, then what it means.

## Gotchas
- Replacing abstract emotion words with stock body language ("her heart pounded",
  "he clenched his jaw") — that's concrete but cliché; find the specific action.
- Over-concreteness: every noun specified becomes cluttered; choose the telling one
  (see `significant-detail`).

## Verify
- [ ] Named emotions in narration reduced to deliberate uses.
- [ ] Evaluative adjectives replaced by their causes in key passages.
- [ ] Category nouns specified where the specific instance matters.
