# Controlling psychic distance

**Goal:** choose and vary how close the narration sits to a character's mind. John
Gardner (*The Art of Fiction*) called this **psychic distance** — "the distance the
reader feels between himself and the events in the story".

## Gardner's scale (his example, paraphrased)
1. *It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway.* — distant, historical.
2. *Henry J. Warburton had never much cared for snowstorms.* — named, external.
3. *Henry hated snowstorms.* — closer, his attitude.
4. *God how he hated these damn snowstorms.* — his idiom (free indirect).
5. *Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your
   miserable soul...* — inside his consciousness.

## Using the scale
- **Open distant, then move in:** a scene or chapter often starts at 1-2 to orient
  (place, time, who) then closes to 3-4 for the action.
- **Move in for emotional peaks**, out for summary, transitions and endings that need
  perspective.
- **Shift gradually:** jumping from 1 to 5 in one sentence jars (Gardner's warning).
  Step through intermediate levels.
- **Close distance requires the character's vocabulary**; far distance uses the narrator's.
- Name usage signals distance: full name (far), surname or first name (middle), pronoun (near).

## Diagnosing problems
- Reader feels "held at arm's length": prose sits at 1-2 too long in emotional scenes.
- Reader feels claustrophobic or disoriented: stuck at 5, no orientation.
- Jarring shifts: e.g. "Mr. Hale considered the evidence. Damn, she was lying." with
  no transition.

## Gotchas
- Filter words keep distance high (see `removing-filter-words`).
- Distance changes are not POV changes — staying with one character but varying closeness
  is fine; shifting to another character's mind mid-scene is head-hopping.

## Verify
- [ ] Each scene's opening orients at a moderate distance.
- [ ] Emotional peaks rendered at close distance.
- [ ] No abrupt jumps of more than one or two levels without transition.
