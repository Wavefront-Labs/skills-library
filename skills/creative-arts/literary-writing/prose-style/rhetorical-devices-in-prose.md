# Rhetorical devices in prose

**Goal:** use the patterns of classical rhetoric to give prose rhythm and force at key
moments.

## Devices
| Device | Pattern | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anaphora** | Repeat the start of successive clauses | Build, insistence | *She wanted the house. She wanted the dog. She wanted him gone.* |
| **Epistrophe** | Repeat the end | Emphasis, closure | *...of the people, by the people, for the people* |
| **Tricolon** | Three parallel items, often growing | Completeness, cadence | *He came, he lied, he left with the silver.* |
| **Polysyndeton** | Many conjunctions | Breathless accumulation, solemnity | *and the rain and the dark and the long road home* |
| **Asyndeton** | No conjunctions | Speed, abruptness | *She packed, locked up, drove.* |
| **Antithesis** | Contrasting ideas in parallel form | Sharp opposition | *It was the best of times, it was the worst of times* |
| **Chiasmus** | ABBA reversal | Memorability | *She lived to write and wrote to live.* |
| **Parallelism** | Matching grammatical structure | Clarity, balance | Lists and comparisons |

## Using them in fiction
- Reserve for **peaks**: a climax, a turning-point realisation, a chapter's last line,
  a speech a character would really make.
- Place the strongest item last in a tricolon (end weight); break the pattern on the
  third item for surprise ("He was rich, handsome, and on fire").
- Rhetoric in dialogue characterises (preachers, lawyers, politicians speak this way).
- Use within the narrator's voice: a lyrical narrator tolerates more than a plain one.

## Gotchas
- Every paragraph in triplets reads as mannered.
- Faulty parallelism ("She liked swimming, to read, and the cinema") — fix grammar.
- Rhetorical flourishes at low-stakes moments inflate them.

## Verify
- [ ] Devices concentrated at peaks, not spread evenly.
- [ ] Lists and series are grammatically parallel.
- [ ] Read aloud: the rhythm sounds intended, not automatic.
