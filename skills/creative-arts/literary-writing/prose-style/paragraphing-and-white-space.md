# Paragraphing and white space

**Goal:** paragraphs that guide the reader's attention and control pace. Browne & King
("Breaking Up Is Easy to Do") note that long, unbroken paragraphs slow readers and hide
key moments, while frequent breaks speed the page.

## When to start a new paragraph
- **New speaker** in dialogue — always; a character's action beat goes in the same
  paragraph as their line.
- **New actor**: a different character acts or reacts.
- **Shift of focus**: from action to reflection, from one object of attention to another.
- **Shift of time or place** (bigger shifts get a scene break).
- **Emphasis**: isolate a line to make it land.

## Pace
- Longer paragraphs slow reading — useful for reflection, immersion, summary.
- Short paragraphs and white space accelerate — action, tension, comic timing.
- One-sentence paragraphs are strong punctuation; overuse flattens them.

## Fixing walls of text
1. Find paragraphs over roughly 10-12 lines on a manuscript page.
2. Look for natural seams: a change of subject, a new beat of action, a line of dialogue
   buried mid-paragraph.
3. Break there; check each new paragraph has a single focus.

## Fixing fragmentation
- A page of one-line paragraphs in a quiet scene feels breathless or like a screenplay.
- Merge related beats of the same character into one paragraph.

## Dialogue specifics
- Never put two speakers' lines in one paragraph.
- A long speech broken into paragraphs: no closing quotation mark at the end of each
  paragraph except the last, but an opening quotation mark at the start of each (CMOS).

## Verify
- [ ] Every new speaker starts a new paragraph, with their beats attached.
- [ ] No wall-of-text paragraph in tense scenes.
- [ ] One-line paragraphs reserved for emphasis.
