# Sentence variety and length

**Goal:** prose with rhythm — sentences whose length and shape fit their content and
whose variety keeps the reader's ear awake.

## Diagnose monotony
- Highlight sentence starts: many beginning with "He/She/The" or "-ing" phrases?
- Count words per sentence for a page; plot them. A flat line (all 12-18 words) = drone.
- Read aloud: monotony is easier to hear than see.

## Tools
- **Short sentence after long ones** = emphasis. Put the key fact there.
- **Long cumulative sentence** (main clause first, details added after): *She ran,
  her bag banging against her hip, the bus already pulling away, the driver's face
  indifferent behind the glass.* — immersive, flowing.
- **Periodic sentence** (main clause withheld until the end): *Of all the people who
  could have walked through that door, on that night, in that rain, it was her
  father.* — suspense, formality.
- **End weight:** English stresses the end of a sentence. Put the important word last
  ("He was dead" beats "Dead was what he was, apparently, by then").
- **Fragments**, sparingly, for impact or voice. *Nothing. Not a sound.*
- **Vary openings:** subject first most of the time, with occasional adverbial,
  prepositional or subordinate-clause openings — not a rotation for its own sake.

## Match length to content
- Action and shock: shorter, simpler syntax.
- Reflection, sensory immersion, rush of emotion: longer, flowing syntax.
- Dialogue rhythm follows the speaker's idiolect, not this scheme.

## Gotchas
- Mechanical variety (short, long, short, long) is its own monotony.
- Overusing fragments for drama in every paragraph.
- Long sentences that lose their grammar: check the main clause survives.

## Verify
- [ ] Sentence-length plot for key pages shows real variation.
- [ ] Key words sit at sentence ends in important moments.
- [ ] Read-aloud reveals no drone patches.
