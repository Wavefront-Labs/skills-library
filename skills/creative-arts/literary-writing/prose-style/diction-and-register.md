# Diction and register

**Goal:** word choices that fit the voice, carry the right connotations, and stay at a
consistent level of formality unless shifted on purpose.

## Concepts
- **Diction:** word choice. **Register:** the level of formality appropriate to a
  context (frozen, formal, consultative, casual, intimate — Joos's scale).
- **Connotation vs denotation:** *thin, slender, skinny, gaunt* denote the same and
  connote very different judgements.
- **Latinate vs Anglo-Saxon (Germanic):** *commence / begin*, *perspire / sweat*,
  *residence / home*. Latinate words tend formal, abstract, distanced; Anglo-Saxon words
  short, concrete, emotional. Powerful moments often use short native words.

## Procedure
1. Define the narrator's register: who is speaking, to whom, in what situation?
   (A first-person teenager vs an omniscient Victorian narrator.)
2. Revise for **slips**: words outside that register (a hard-boiled narrator saying
   "utilise"; a medieval knight saying "okay").
3. Check connotations of descriptive words for characters you want readers to like or distrust.
4. In emotional peaks, prefer short concrete words; save Latinate abstraction for
   irony, comedy, or a pompous character.
5. Jargon: use enough to convince (a surgeon's terms), translate through context, not glossary.

## Deliberate register shifts
- Comic effect: high diction for low subjects (mock-heroic) and vice versa.
- Characterisation: a character moving up-register when lying or nervous.
- Free indirect passages adopt the character's register (see `free-indirect-discourse`).

## Gotchas
- Thesaurus words: synonyms chosen for variety that carry the wrong connotation.
- Mixed registers within a single sentence without intent.
- Elevated diction mistaken for "literary" — clarity first.

## Verify
- [ ] Narrator's register defined in the style sheet.
- [ ] Register slips found and fixed or justified.
- [ ] Key emotional moments use plain, concrete words.
