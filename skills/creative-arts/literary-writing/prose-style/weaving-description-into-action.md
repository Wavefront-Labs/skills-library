# Weaving description into action

**Goal:** readers see the setting without the story stopping. Large static blocks of
description are among the most skimmed text in fiction.

## Technique
1. **Orient quickly:** two or three details at scene start (where, when, one sensory
   anchor). Enough to stage the action, not the whole room.
2. **Reveal on contact:** describe objects as characters use, touch, avoid or notice them.
   - Static: *The kitchen had a long oak table and a cracked window.*
   - Woven: *She dragged a chair from the long oak table and sat where the cracked
     window couldn't reach her with its draught.*
3. **Movement as camera:** describe in the order the character moves through space.
4. **Reaction:** characters' responses to the setting (wiping a dusty chair before sitting)
   describe and characterise at once.
5. **Dialogue interplay:** small setting beats between lines of dialogue serve as action
   beats and keep the scene grounded.

## Pace description to tension
- High tension: minimal description, sharp single details, short sentences.
- Low tension / arrivals / lulls: room for a fuller paragraph — especially for a
  setting's first appearance or a place that matters emotionally.
- After a big event, slowing into description can be a deliberate breath.

## Procedure
- Find any paragraph of pure description longer than ~5 sentences.
- Keep the 2-3 most significant details as orientation; distribute others to moments
  of contact later in the scene, or cut them.

## Gotchas
- Moving description into action in a way that makes characters do pointless business
  just to touch things.
- Delaying orientation so long the reader can't stage the scene.

## Verify
- [ ] No static description block longer than about five sentences in tense scenes.
- [ ] Each scene orients within its first paragraph.
- [ ] Most setting details appear through use or reaction.
