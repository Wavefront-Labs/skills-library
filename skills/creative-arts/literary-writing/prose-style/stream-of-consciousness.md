# Stream of consciousness

**Goal:** render the flow of a mind — perceptions, memories, associations and half-thoughts
— as it happens. The term comes from William James; the technique is associated with
Joyce (*Ulysses*, Molly Bloom's soliloquy), Woolf (*Mrs Dalloway*), Faulkner.

## Techniques
- **Associative logic:** thoughts link by association (a smell -> a memory -> a worry),
  not argument. Each jump needs a trigger the reader can follow, even if loosely.
- **Sensory anchors:** return to a present perception (Big Ben striking in *Mrs Dalloway*)
  to keep readers located in time and place.
- **Syntax follows state:** long, run-on, comma-spliced sentences for flooding thought;
  fragments for shock; punctuation dropped only in the most extreme passages.
- **Time collapse:** past and present interleave without tense signposts; tense can
  shift mid-sentence.
- **Idiom:** the character's private words, repetitions and obsessions recur like motifs.

## Calibrating
- Most novels use stream of consciousness in **passages** — at moments of crisis, reverie
  or exhaustion — rather than throughout.
- The more radical the syntax, the shorter the passage tolerable to modern readers.
- Use free indirect discourse as the bridge in and out (see `free-indirect-discourse`).

## Procedure
1. Decide the trigger and the emotional destination of the passage.
2. Draft fast, following associations, without correcting.
3. Revise for **legibility**: is each jump traceable? Are there anchors every so often?
4. Read aloud; the rhythm should mimic the mental state.

## Gotchas
- Random association with no emotional arc — the passage must go somewhere.
- Losing track of what's happening in the external scene.
- Using it as an excuse for unrevised prose.

## Verify
- [ ] Each associative jump has a trigger.
- [ ] Present-moment anchors recur.
- [ ] The passage moves the character to a new emotional state or realisation.
