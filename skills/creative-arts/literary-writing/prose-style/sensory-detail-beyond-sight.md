# Sensory detail beyond sight

**Goal:** immersive scenes that engage more than the eyes. Most drafts are heavily
visual; smell and touch in particular tie strongly to memory and emotion.

## Audit
1. Take a key scene and highlight sensory details by sense (five colours).
2. Count. Typical draft: mostly sight, a little sound, almost no smell/touch/taste.
3. Ask which other sense would carry **this scene's emotion** best.

## Senses and what they do
| Sense | Strengths | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sound | Off-page threat, rhythm, atmosphere | the click of the gate latch at 3 a.m. |
| Smell | Memory, intimacy, disgust | his father's coat: tobacco and wet wool |
| Touch / texture | Intimacy, discomfort, physicality | the sticky laminate of the diner menu |
| Temperature | Mood, environment | the radiator's dry heat |
| Taste | Specificity, culture, intimacy | coins in the mouth after the punch |
| Body sense (proprioception, balance, pain, fatigue) | Grounding action | knees locking after hours of standing |

## Techniques
- **One unexpected sense per scene** is often enough to make it real.
- Use precise nouns and verbs over adjectives ("the fridge hummed" vs "a loud noise").
- Let senses conflict for unease (the room looks clean but smells of rot).
- Sounds can signal events before characters see them — useful for suspense.
- Limit synaesthesia (sound described as colour) to voices that warrant it.

## Gotchas
- The five-senses checklist paragraph: every sense dutifully listed, none meaningful.
- Clichéd smells (fresh coffee, freshly cut grass) — find the particular.
- Sensations the viewpoint character couldn't plausibly register at that moment.

## Verify
- [ ] Key scenes use at least one non-visual sense meaningfully.
- [ ] Sensory details tie to the scene's emotion or plot.
- [ ] No checklist paragraphs.
