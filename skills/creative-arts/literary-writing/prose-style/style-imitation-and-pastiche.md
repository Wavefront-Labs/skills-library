# Style imitation and pastiche exercises

**Goal:** build stylistic range and find your own voice by consciously imitating others.
Robert Louis Stevenson described learning to write by playing "the sedulous ape" to
admired authors; Mary Oliver's *A Poetry Handbook* devotes a chapter to imitation; many
MFA workshops assign imitation exercises.

## Exercises (escalating)
1. **Copywork:** type or hand-copy 1-2 pages of a passage you admire, exactly. Notice
   punctuation, paragraph length, sentence openings.
2. **Syntactic imitation:** take a paragraph; write a new paragraph on your own subject
   matching each sentence's structure (same clause pattern, same length, same
   punctuation), replacing every content word.
3. **Pastiche scene:** write an original scene "as if by" the author — their typical
   subject, POV, tone, diction. Aim for something they could have written.
4. **Parody:** exaggerate the author's tics until they're visible. Comedy makes you
   identify exactly what the style is.
5. **Transposition:** rewrite one of your own scenes in two contrasting styles (e.g.
   Hemingway and Woolf). Compare what each style makes possible.

## Analyse afterwards
Write a short list: what does this style do with sentence length, verbs, adjectives,
dialogue, interiority, detail selection, paragraphing? Which features would you keep?

## Ethics and use
- Imitation exercises are for practice. Published pastiche must not copy text; homage and
  parody are traditions, but don't reproduce passages.
- Rotating models prevents simply becoming one writer's clone; your voice emerges from
  the combination plus your own material.

- [ ] At least one syntactic imitation completed per model author.
