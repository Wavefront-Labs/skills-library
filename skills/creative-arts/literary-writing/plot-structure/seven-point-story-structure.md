# Seven-point story structure

**Goal:** outline a story by its seven anchor points, a method popularised by author Dan
Wells (in talks and on the *Writing Excuses* podcast), who credited the *Star Trek*
roleplaying game's narrator guide as his starting point.

## The seven points
1. **Hook:** the starting state — the opposite of the resolution.
2. **Plot Turn 1:** the event that sets the story moving (call to adventure, new world).
3. **Pinch 1:** pressure applied — the antagonist's force shows; something goes wrong,
   forcing action.
4. **Midpoint:** the character shifts from reaction to action.
5. **Pinch 2:** worse pressure — plan fails, mentor dies, all seems lost.
6. **Plot Turn 2:** the character obtains the final piece (insight, power, ally) needed.
7. **Resolution:** the climax and new state.

## Build order (Wells's advice)
1. **Resolution first** — where does the character end?
2. **Hook** — the opposite state (coward → hero; lonely → loved).
3. **Midpoint** — the moment they move from one state toward the other.
4. **Plot Turn 1** and **Plot Turn 2** — how they enter the conflict and how they gain the
   means to finish it.
5. **Pinch 1** and **Pinch 2** — the pressures that force change.

## Using it for complex books
- Run a **separate seven-point set per subplot or character arc** (romance, mystery, the
  sidekick's arc), then braid them in a scene list.
- Points may share scenes across subplots (a midpoint for one arc is a pinch for another).

## Gotchas
- Treating the points as evenly spaced; proportions vary.
- Hooks that don't contrast with the resolution — the arc has no distance to travel.
- Plot Turn 2 arriving from nowhere — set up its source earlier.

## Verify
- [ ] Resolution and hook are opposites.
- [ ] Midpoint shows reaction turning to action.
- [ ] Each subplot has its own seven points, braided into the scene list.
