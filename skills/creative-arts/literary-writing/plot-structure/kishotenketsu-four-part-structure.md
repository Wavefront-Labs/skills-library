# Kishōtenketsu four-part structure

**Goal:** an alternative to conflict-driven structure. Kishōtenketsu (起承転結) comes
from classical Chinese poetry (qǐ chéng zhuǎn hé) and is used in Japanese and Korean
writing, including four-panel comics (yonkoma). Its engine is a **twist that recontextualises**,
not a struggle between opposing forces.

## The four parts
1. **Ki (introduction):** establish characters, setting and situation.
2. **Shō (development):** develop it further, deepen, with no major change.
3. **Ten (twist):** introduce something unexpected — often seemingly unrelated — a new
   element, viewpoint or event.
4. **Ketsu (reconciliation / conclusion):** bring the elements together so the reader
   sees a new relationship or meaning between them.

## Example shape
1. A girl tends her late grandmother's garden every morning.
2. We see her care for the plants, neighbours passing, the routine.
3. Across town, an old man finds a packet of seeds in a library book with a note in
   handwriting.
4. The man arrives at the garden gate with the seeds; the handwriting is the grandmother's.

## Writing with it
- Make parts 1-2 vivid and specific; their calm matters for the twist's effect.
- The twist should be surprising but not arbitrary — the conclusion must reveal a link.
- The conclusion often lands as an image or quiet realisation rather than a resolved conflict.
- Works especially well for flash fiction, short stories, literary vignettes, poems, and
  comics; can shape chapters or subplots within a longer conflict-driven book.
- Conflict can still exist; it just isn't the structural engine.

## Gotchas
- Twist that's simply random — the reconciliation must justify it.
- Treating it as "story without plot": the development and twist still require design.
- Over-explaining the connection in the conclusion.

## Verify
- [ ] Four parts identifiable in the outline.
- [ ] Twist introduces a genuinely new element or angle.
- [ ] Conclusion reveals the relationship without explaining it away.
