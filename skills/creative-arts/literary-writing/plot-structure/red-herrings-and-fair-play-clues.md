# Red herrings and fair-play clues

**Goal:** a mystery the reader could solve but doesn't — and on the reveal, sees every
clue was there. The Golden Age "fair play" tradition (Knox's Decalogue, the Detection
Club oath): the reader must be given the clues the detective uses.

## Build backwards
1. Fix the truth first: culprit, method, motive, opportunity, and the timeline of what
   really happened (minute by minute for the crime).
2. List the **deductions** the detective makes to reach it; for each deduction, the
   **clue(s)** that support it.
3. List suspects; for each innocent suspect, a reason to suspect them (motive,
   opportunity, lie) and a hidden innocent explanation.

## Placing true clues
- Put true clues **in plain sight but in noise**: in a list of items, during an action
  scene, in dialogue where attention is elsewhere.
- Misdirect by meaning, not omission: the reader sees the clue but interprets it wrongly
  (the stopped clock "proves" the time of death).
- Place the decisive clue before the reveal, ideally before the 75% mark.

## Red herrings
- A red herring is a misleading clue or suspect. Each needs:
  - a plausible reason to mislead (someone lying to protect a secret of their own);
  - an **explanation on resolution** — unresolved herrings feel like cheating.
- Use secrets: innocent characters hiding unrelated sins make the best herrings.

## Clue audit
Table: clue | chapter | what reader thinks it means | what it really means | used in reveal?
- Every deduction in the reveal must map to a clue already shown.
- Count: if the detective relies on information the reader never saw, fix it.

## Gotchas
- Withholding the detective's key observation ("He noticed something but said nothing")
  — overused and resented.
- Twins, secret passages, unknown poisons and coincidence as solutions break fair play.
- A culprit introduced late or barely on page.

## Verify
- [ ] Every reveal deduction maps to an on-page clue.
- [ ] Every red herring is explained by the end.
- [ ] The culprit appears early and more than once.
- [ ] A test reader can, re-reading, find the clues they missed.
