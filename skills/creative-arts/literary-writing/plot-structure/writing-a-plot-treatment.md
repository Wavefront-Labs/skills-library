# Writing a plot treatment

**Goal:** a narrative outline in prose that tells the whole story start to finish, so
you can test the shape before investing in full scenes. Lamott (*Bird by Bird*, "Plot
Treatment") describes writing one after a messy draft: a chapter-by-chapter account of
what happens, what each chapter needs to accomplish, and where the characters end up.
Screenwriters use the same tool (a film treatment).

## Format
- Present tense, third person, plain prose. Length: about 1 page per 10,000 words of
  the planned book is a workable ratio (so 8-12 pages for a novel), though treatments vary widely.
- One or more paragraphs per chapter or sequence, headed with the chapter number.
- Each chapter paragraph covers: what happens, what changes, which question carries the
  reader into the next chapter, and character state at the end.

## Procedure
1. Write fast, without stopping to research or polish.
2. Where you don't know what happens, write the **need**: "Something here must make her
   distrust Tom." Mark it with `[?]`.
3. When finished, read it in one sitting and mark: slack chapters, unearned turns,
   missing causality, characters who vanish.
4. Revise the treatment (cheap) until the story holds, then draft or redraft from it.
5. Keep the treatment updated as a map during drafting, or let it go once it has done its job.

## Treatment vs synopsis vs outline
- **Treatment:** working document for the writer, chapter by chapter, with doubts and notes.
- **Synopsis:** polished 1-2 page sales document (see
  `creative-arts.literary-writing.novel-writing.writing-a-novel-synopsis`).
- **Outline/beat sheet:** bullet structure, less narrative flow.

## Gotchas
- Writing the treatment as backstory and world notes instead of events.
- Polishing the treatment's prose — it is a tool, not a product.
- Treating it as binding: drafting will change it.

## Verify
- [ ] Covers the whole story through the ending.
- [ ] Every chapter states what changes.
- [ ] All `[?]` gaps resolved or consciously deferred.
