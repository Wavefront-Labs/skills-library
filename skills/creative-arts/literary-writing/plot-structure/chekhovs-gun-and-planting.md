# Chekhov's gun and planting

**Goal:** every emphasised element earns its place, and every solution is set up
before it is used. Chekhov's principle (from his letters): if a gun hangs on the wall in
the first act, it must be fired later; otherwise remove it.

## The two directions
1. **Setup without payoff** (orphaned plant): the story emphasises something
   — a locked drawer, a skill, a threat — and never uses it. Readers feel cheated.
2. **Payoff without setup** (deus ex machina): a problem is solved by something never
   introduced — a new ally, an unmentioned skill, luck.

## Plant/payoff ledger (revision tool)
Build a table while reading the draft:
| Plant (chapter) | How emphasised | Payoff (chapter) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grandfather's revolver, ch 2 | described, handled | used at climax, ch 28 | ok |
| Sister's swimming medals, ch 4 | two mentions | — | orphan: cut or pay off |
| Hero picks the lock, ch 22 | — | needs plant | add lockpicking in ch 6 |

Rules of thumb:
- **Emphasis creates a promise.** A detail mentioned once in passing is texture; a
  detail lingered on, repeated, or given to a viewpoint character's attention is a plant.
- Plant **early and in passing**, inside a scene with another purpose.
- The earlier the plant, the more it may need a reminder mid-book.
- Let the payoff use the plant in a way the reader didn't predict (the medal-winning
  swimmer saves herself, not the expected someone else).

## Gotchas
- Red herrings are deliberate unpaid plants — they still need a payoff: the reader must
  learn they were misleading (see `red-herrings-and-fair-play-clues`).
- Over-planting makes the ending guessable; one clear plant plus one reminder is often enough.
- Series fiction may carry plants across books — log them in the series bible.

## Verify
- [ ] Ledger complete; every emphasised element has a payoff or is de-emphasised.
- [ ] Every climax solution traces to an earlier plant.
- [ ] No new skills, allies or objects appear at the moment they're needed.
