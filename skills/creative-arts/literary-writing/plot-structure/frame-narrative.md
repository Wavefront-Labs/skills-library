# Frame narratives

**Goal:** use an outer story (the frame) to present an inner story so that the act of
telling adds meaning — as in *Frankenstein*, *Heart of Darkness*, *Wuthering Heights*,
*The Canterbury Tales*.

## What a frame can add
- **Reliability questions:** who is telling, why, and what are they hiding?
- **Stakes in the telling:** a confession, a warning, a bargain (Scheherazade tells to survive).
- **Dramatic irony:** the frame reveals the outcome so the inner story becomes "how", not "what".
- **Distance:** a teller looking back can reflect, which the inner scene cannot.

## Design decisions
1. **Who tells, to whom, when, and why now?** Write these four answers down.
2. **How much frame?** Options: opening and closing only (bookends); periodic returns
   at key moments; a full parallel plot in the frame.
3. **Does the frame change?** The strongest frames have their own small arc — the
   listener is altered by the tale, or the teller's motive is revealed at the end.
4. **Voice:** if the inner story is told aloud or in letters, decide how literally to
   keep that texture (interruptions, "you will say...") versus slipping into ordinary
   narrative after the opening.
5. **Nesting depth:** each level of nesting costs clarity; more than two levels needs
   strong cues.

## Returning to the frame
- Return at points where the listener's reaction adds something (doubt, horror), or
  where a break heightens tension in the inner story.
- Keep returns short unless the frame has its own plot.

## Gotchas
- A frame that only says "let me tell you a story" and never returns: cut it.
- Inner narrators who report dialogue and detail they could not know — either accept the
  convention openly or account for it.
- Ending without closing the frame leaves readers wondering why it was there.

## Verify
- [ ] Teller, listener, occasion and motive defined.
- [ ] The frame returns at the end and something has changed.
- [ ] Each return earns its interruption.
