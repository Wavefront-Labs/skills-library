# Resolution and denouement

**Goal:** after the climax decides the outcome, let the reader feel what it meant.
Burroway's pattern is conflict - crisis - resolution: the resolution shows the changed
world, not more action. Freytag's final phase is the *dénouement* ("untying").

## What the resolution does
1. **Shows the new equilibrium:** how the world and the protagonist are now different.
2. **Settles the emotional question** (the relationship, the internal flaw), which often
   lags a beat behind the external climax.
3. **Ties off threads**: remaining subplots get a line or short scene each.
4. **Echoes the opening** — a mirrored image, place or line that measures change.

## Length
- Short: usually a chapter or a few pages in a novel; a paragraph or two in a short story.
- The longer it runs after the climax, the more the energy leaks. If you have many
  threads, tie the minor ones before the climax.

## Procedure
1. List what the reader still wants to know after the climax; cross out what doesn't matter.
2. Order the rest from least to most important; end on the most emotionally important.
3. Write one scene that **dramatises** the new normal rather than summarising everyone's fate.
4. End on an image or action rather than a moral or summary.

## Epilogues
- Earn their place when a jump in time shows a consequence that couldn't otherwise be
  seen (the child grown up; the town rebuilt) or sets up a series.
- Cut them if they only repeat the resolution or explain the theme.

## Gotchas
- A new conflict introduced in the last pages (unless setting up a sequel, and even then lightly).
- Character "wrap-up parade" — everyone gets a speech.
- Explaining the ending's meaning outright.

## Verify
- [ ] The outcome's meaning is felt in one dramatised moment.
- [ ] Every loose thread is either tied or deliberately left open.
- [ ] Final image echoes or answers the opening.
- [ ] Resolution is short relative to the climax.
