# Writing flashbacks

**Goal:** step into the past only when the present story needs it, and get back
without losing the reader. A flashback (analepsis) stops forward motion; it must pay
for that with information or emotion the present scene needs right now.

## When to use one
- The reader **needs** the past event to understand a present choice — and needs it now.
- The past event is dramatic enough to deserve scene treatment; otherwise use a line
  or two of summary backstory.
- Test: if the flashback were cut, would the next present scene still make sense? If yes, cut or compress.

## Triggering
- Launch from a present moment of tension or a sensory trigger (a smell, an object, a
  phrase someone says) — never from a character idly reminiscing.
- Place the flashback *after* the reader cares about the present question it answers.

## Tense technique (past-tense narratives)
1. **Enter** with two or three verbs in past perfect: "She *had been* nineteen that
   summer. Her father *had driven* her to the station..."
2. **Shift to simple past** once the reader is oriented: "The train was late. He bought
   her a coffee..." (Staying in "had" for pages is clunky.)
3. **Exit** with one or two past-perfect verbs or a clear time cue, then a sharp present
   anchor: "That *had been* twenty years ago. Now the phone was ringing."
- Present-tense narratives: flashbacks go into simple past.
- Long flashbacks can be their own chapter with a heading/date instead.

## Length and frequency
- Keep early flashbacks short; front-loading the past in the first chapters stalls the story.
- Avoid flashbacks within flashbacks unless the structure is the point.

## Gotchas
- Flashback as an info-dump of backstory with no scene.
- Breaking into a flashback at the height of a present scene's tension (kills the tension)
  — unless deliberately used as a cliffhanger device.
- Ambiguous return: reader unsure whether we are back in the present.

## Verify
- [ ] Each flashback answers a question the present has made urgent.
- [ ] Entry and exit each marked by tense or a time cue.
- [ ] No flashback in the first few pages that could be delayed.
- [ ] Cutting any flashback would lose something the present needs.
