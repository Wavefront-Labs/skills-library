# Reversal and recognition

**Goal:** design turning points that feel inevitable and surprising. Aristotle's
*Poetics* names the two most powerful plot elements:
- **Peripeteia (reversal):** an action produces the opposite of what was intended
  (the messenger who comes to cheer Oedipus reveals his parentage).
- **Anagnorisis (recognition):** a change from ignorance to knowledge, especially of
  identity or of one's own nature.
The strongest turns combine both in one moment.

## Building a reversal
1. State the character's intention in the scene ("she lies to protect her brother").
2. Find a consequence of that very action that undoes the intention ("the lie gives the
   police the alibi gap that convicts him").
3. Plant what makes the reversal possible earlier, so it reads as causal irony, not luck.

## Building a recognition
- External: who someone really is, what really happened.
- Internal: the character recognises their flaw or false belief — the core of an arc
  (see `creative-arts.literary-writing.character-development.want-need-and-character-arc`).
- Show the recognition through a changed action, not only a thought ("She put the
  letter in the fire").

## Where to use them
- Midpoint: a reversal that flips the protagonist from reactive to proactive, or false victory to defeat.
- Crisis/climax: recognition that makes the final choice possible.
- Scene level: each scene's "turn" can be a small reversal (expectation vs result).

## Gotchas
- Reversals caused by coincidence, not by the character's action, lose the irony.
- Recognition delivered by another character's lecture rather than discovered.
- Too many big reversals numb the reader; scale them.

## Verify
- [ ] Each major turn is either a reversal, a recognition, or both.
- [ ] Each reversal results from the character's own action.
- [ ] Each recognition changes subsequent behaviour on the page.
