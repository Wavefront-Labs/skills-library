# The iceberg principle in worldbuilding

**Goal:** a world that feels deep because the writer knows far more than the page
shows. Hemingway's iceberg theory (the dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only
one-eighth of it being above water) applied to setting: knowledge omitted with intent
still gives the prose confidence and implication.

## Above and below the water
- **Below:** the story bible — history, politics, geography, languages, rules.
- **Above:** what a scene needs for clarity, stakes or texture — often one detail.
- Rule of thumb many fantasy writers use: **show a fraction, imply the rest.**

## Techniques for implication
- **Casual reference:** characters mention things as known ("since the Drowning, nobody
  fished the west bank"). The reader infers history without a lecture.
- **Specific, unexplained details:** a name, an artefact, an oath — consistent across
  appearances so they gain meaning.
- **Partial knowledge:** characters themselves don't know everything; rumours and
  contradicting accounts.
- **Material culture:** objects and rituals that embody history.

## Deciding what surfaces
For each world element in a scene, ask:
1. Does the reader need it to understand what's happening now?
2. Does it raise stakes or tension?
3. Does it add texture in a sentence or less?
If none, leave it underwater.

## Gotchas
- **Research guilt:** including material because you worked hard on it.
- **Mystery fog:** too many unexplained references at once; anchor the reader in a clear
  situation first.
- **Inconsistency** between casual references; log every mention in the bible.

## Verify
- [ ] Story bible substantially larger than on-page world content.
- [ ] Each worldbuilding passage passes one of the three surface tests.
- [ ] Unexplained references are consistent and anchored.
