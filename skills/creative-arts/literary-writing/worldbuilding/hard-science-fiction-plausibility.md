# Hard science fiction plausibility

**Goal:** fiction where the science is accurate or rigorously extrapolated, and errors
don't break expert readers' trust. Examples: Hal Clement, Larry Niven's *Ringworld*,
Kim Stanley Robinson's Mars trilogy, Andy Weir's *The Martian*.

## Process
1. **List the load-bearing science:** every physical fact your plot depends on
   (travel times, survival limits, how the tech works).
2. **Do the numbers:** travel time vs distance and acceleration, delta-v budgets (the
   Tsiolkovsky rocket equation), light-speed communication lag (Earth-Mars roughly 3-22
   minutes one way depending on positions), radiation doses, gravity, heat dissipation.
   The Atomic Rockets website is a widely used reference for SF writers.
3. **Pick deliberate exceptions:** most hard SF allows one or two "handwaves" (FTL, AI,
   longevity). State them to yourself and keep everything else consistent with them.
4. **Consult sources:** textbooks, NASA/ESA public documents, review papers; ask experts —
   many scientists will answer a polite, specific question.
5. **Beta readers with expertise** in the relevant fields.

## On the page
- Let **problem-solving** be the drama (Weir's "science the hell out of it").
- **Explain through stakes:** numbers matter when they threaten (oxygen remaining).
- Avoid lectures; characters explain only to someone who needs to know.

## Common errors
- Sound in space, instant communication across light-hours, artificial gravity without
  rotation or acceleration, stealth in space (hard to hide heat), bullets and explosions
  behaving like on Earth, orbits treated like flight paths.

## Gotchas
- Accuracy at the expense of story; pick the science that creates conflict.
- Out-of-date science; check the latest consensus.

## Verify
- [ ] Load-bearing science listed and numbers checked.
- [ ] Deliberate handwaves named and consistent.
- [ ] At least one expert or informed reader checked key details.
