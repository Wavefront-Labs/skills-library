# Soft magic and wonder

**Goal:** magic that feels mysterious, ancient or numinous — Gandalf in *The Lord of the
Rings*, the Force in early *Star Wars*, Le Guin's Earthsea dragons, Studio Ghibli's
spirits. Under Sanderson's First Law, the less readers understand the magic, the less it
can be used to resolve conflict without feeling like a cheat.

## Design principles
- **Unknown rules, consistent feel:** the reader doesn't know how it works, but tone,
  imagery and effects are consistent (always cold, always costly, always strange).
- **Raises problems, rarely solves them:** use it to create danger, mood, mystery or
  awe. If it solves the protagonist's problem, it's deus ex machina.
- **Implied cost or price:** old bargains, exhaustion, the sense that power takes
  something. Mystery about the price amplifies dread.
- **Scarcity:** rare, unpredictable appearances keep wonder alive.
- **Perspective:** characters who don't understand it either; reverence or fear rather
  than technique.

## When soft magic can help heroes
- **Help that comes with prior foreshadowing and cost** (Gandalf's return costs him; Frodo
  still has to carry the ring).
- **Assistance at the margins,** not the decisive act — the protagonist's choice still
  decides the climax.

## Writing techniques
- Sensory, oblique description over mechanism.
- Folklore logic: taboos, names, thresholds, seasons.
- Leave some questions permanently unanswered.

## Gotchas
- Explaining it later, collapsing the mystery (and creating a hard system by accident).
- Inconsistent tone — whimsical one chapter, eldritch the next.
- Climax won by unexplained power.

## Verify
- [ ] Tone and effects consistent across appearances.
- [ ] Magic never solves the main conflict unaided.
- [ ] Some mystery deliberately left unexplained.
