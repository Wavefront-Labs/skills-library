# Neologisms and invented terminology

**Goal:** invented words that make a world feel distinct without making the book a
vocabulary test.

## When to coin
Coin a word only when:
- The thing doesn't exist in our world (a technology, creature, social role, magic act), **and**
- An existing word would mislead or flatten it.
Otherwise use plain English: "horse" not "hrask" for an ordinary horse (the "calling a
rabbit a smeerp" problem).

## Building coinages
| Method | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Compound of English words | *starship*, *skinsuit*, *spellbook* | Instantly readable |
| Familiar roots / affixes | *teleport* (Greek tele-), *cyberspace* | Readers infer |
| Repurposed existing words | *Fremen* "water of life"; *the Force* | Shifted meaning |
| Invented-language words | from your conlang (see `sketching-a-conlang-phonology`) | Needs context support |
| Slang drawn from a real language | Burgess's *nadsat* (Russian-derived) in *A Clockwork Orange* | Bold; teaches through repetition |

## Teaching through context
1. Introduce the term in a sentence where action makes its meaning clear:
   *She thumbed the slate awake and the day's orders scrolled up.*
2. Reinforce within the next few uses with different context clues.
3. Avoid stopping for definitions ("A slate, which was a kind of tablet...").

## Rationing
- Introduce few new terms per chapter early on, more once readers trust you.
- Prefer terms that recur; one-off coinages cost more than they give.
- Keep a list (style sheet/glossary) with spelling, capitalisation, plural forms.

## Gotchas
- Inconsistent capitalisation or spelling (*Mage-light / magelight*).
- Synonym drift: three coinages for the same thing.
- Comic-sounding or unfortunate coinages — read aloud, search the web.

## Verify
- [ ] Each coined term names something without an adequate English word.
- [ ] Meaning inferable from context at first use.
- [ ] Terms logged with spelling and capitalisation.
