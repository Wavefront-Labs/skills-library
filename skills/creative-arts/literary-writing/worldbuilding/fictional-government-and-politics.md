# Government and politics

**Goal:** a political system that explains who decides, how power is kept and contested,
and where it can break — so plots involving politics feel grounded.

## Core questions
1. **Form:** monarchy, oligarchy, theocracy, republic, federation, empire, tribal council,
   corporate rule; often a mix (a king who needs a council's taxes).
2. **Legitimacy:** why people accept it — divine right, election, tradition, force,
   prosperity, magic. When legitimacy fails, rebellion becomes plausible.
3. **Succession and transfer:** inheritance rules (primogeniture? elective?), elections,
   coups. Unclear succession is a classic plot engine.
4. **Law and enforcement:** who makes, judges and enforces law; punishments; who is above it.
5. **Revenue:** taxation, tribute, trade monopolies — money drives politics.
6. **Reach:** how far central power extends (distance, communication speed); frontier vs capital.
7. **Factions:** nobles, merchants, clergy, military, guilds, regions, minorities — list
   each faction's interests and leverage.

## Make it dynamic
- **Current tensions:** a weak heir, a drought straining taxes, a rising merchant class.
- **Power map:** a diagram of factions with alliances and rivalries; update it as the
  plot shifts.
- **Personal stakes:** politics through people — a minister who needs a favour, a guard
  who looks away.

## Research analogues
Real systems are messier than textbook forms: look at historical examples (Venetian
Republic's elections, the Holy Roman Empire's electors, the Ottoman court).

## Gotchas
- An absolute ruler who never has to manage anyone.
- Instant communication across a pre-modern empire.
- Politics told in exposition instead of negotiation scenes.

## Verify
- [ ] Form, legitimacy, succession, law, revenue and reach defined.
- [ ] Faction list with interests and leverage.
- [ ] At least one current tension that pressures the plot.
