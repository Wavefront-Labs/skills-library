# Hard magic systems

**Goal:** a magic system readers understand well enough that characters can solve
problems with it satisfyingly. Brandon Sanderson's First Law: an author's ability to solve
conflict with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic.
Examples: Allomancy in *Mistborn* (specific metals, specific effects); Fullmetal Alchemist's
equivalent exchange; bending in *Avatar: The Last Airbender*.

## Specify
1. **Inputs:** what's required (a metal, a word, a sacrifice, line of sight).
2. **Outputs:** exactly what each use does, with ranges and magnitudes.
3. **Costs and limits:** what it takes and what it can't do (limits are more interesting
   than powers — Sanderson's Second Law).
4. **Edge cases:** what happens when combined, overused, or used on oneself.
5. **Who can use it** and how it's learned.
Write this as a one-page rules sheet; it's your contract with the reader.

## Teach it on the page
- **Demonstrate before it matters:** low-stakes use early (training, a mundane task).
- **Show limits failing a character** before the climax so readers know the edges.
- **Incremental rule reveals** tied to scenes; avoid lectures.
- Let **clever use within rules** be the climax payoff (readers feel they could have
  guessed).

## Stress-test
- For each plot problem: why can't the magic solve it? Answer from established limits.
- Look for exploits: readers will (combinations, scaling, economic uses).
- Track every on-page use in a story bible with chapter references.

## Gotchas
- New rule introduced at the climax.
- Systems so detailed the book becomes a manual — reveal what scenes need.
- Ignoring societal consequences of reliable power (see magic system design).
