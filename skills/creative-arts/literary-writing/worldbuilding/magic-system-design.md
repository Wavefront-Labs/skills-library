# Magic system design — rules that create story, not lectures

**Goal:** a magic or speculative-technology system that produces conflict and
satisfying solutions, stays consistent across a book or series, and reaches readers
through action instead of exposition.

## 1. Decide hard or soft (a spectrum)
- **Hard magic:** explicit rules readers understand (cost, source, limits). Because
  readers know the rules, characters can **solve problems** with it satisfyingly.
- **Soft magic:** mysterious, unexplained, awe-inspiring. Good for atmosphere and
  danger, but should rarely solve the protagonist's problems — that reads as a cheat.
A widely cited formulation (Brandon Sanderson's "first law") is roughly: an author's
ability to resolve conflict with magic is proportional to how well the reader
understands it. Most stories sit somewhere between the poles.

## 2. Define source, cost and limits
Write a one-page spec:
- **Source:** where power comes from (bloodline, study, gods, a substance, technology).
  Determines who has it, and so who holds power socially.
- **Method:** how it's done (words, gestures, ingredients, devices). Shapes scenes.
- **Cost:** what it takes — energy, lifespan, memories, money, a substance that runs
  out, social stigma. **Costs create choices**, and choices create drama.
- **Limits:** what it cannot do, explicitly. Limitations are more interesting than
  powers (another popular Sanderson formulation). Common: range, time, line of
  sight, one effect at a time, can't affect the living, can't create from nothing.
- **Failure modes:** what happens if misused or overused.

## 3. Trace consequences (second-order thinking)
Ask what the world looks like if this were real, and has been for centuries:
- **Economy:** if healing magic exists, what happened to medicine, mortality, population?
- **Power and law:** who regulates it? who is excluded? how do the powerful protect
  themselves from it?
- **War:** how does it change fortifications, armies, assassination?
- **Daily life:** language, idioms, religion, architecture, crime.
Pick the consequences your story will touch; you don't need all, but obvious gaps
(teleportation exists but nobody uses it in a crisis) break belief.

## 4. Stress-test against your plot
- For each major problem in the plot, ask: "Why doesn't magic just fix this?" You need
  an answer grounded in costs or limits already established.
- Plan your climax solution and make sure its rule is **set up early** — ideally shown
  in a low-stakes context first.
- Watch for **power creep** in series; new abilities must respect existing limits or
  come at a visible price.

## 5. Reveal through use, not lectures
- Show magic in action with consequences before (or instead of) explaining it.
- Explain only what the reader needs for the current scene; let curiosity build.
- Use a character who doesn't know the rules (apprentice, outsider) only if it fits —
  it's a familiar device and can feel like a tutorial.
- The **iceberg:** know far more than you put on the page; the confidence shows.

## 6. Keep a story bible
A document (or wiki/Scrivener folder/World Anvil-type tool) with rules, costs,
terminology, who can do what, and every on-page use with chapter references. Check new
scenes against it; consistency errors are what attentive readers notice most.

## Gotchas
- **Deus ex machina:** magic solving the climax without established rules or cost.
- **Unlimited power** removes tension; add limits or costs.
- **Encyclopedia openings:** opening chapters of history and rules lose readers.
- **Cultural borrowing:** systems drawn from real religions or cultures need research
  and care; sensitivity readers help.
- Sanderson's "laws" are popular craft heuristics, quoted from memory and paraphrased
  here; check his essays for exact wording.

## Verify
- [ ] One-page spec covers source, method, cost, limits and failure modes.
- [ ] Societal consequences considered for at least economy, power and daily life.
- [ ] Every plot problem has a rules-based reason magic doesn't trivially solve it.
- [ ] The climax's magic was set up earlier on the page.
- [ ] Story bible exists and records each on-page use.
