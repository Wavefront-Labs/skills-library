# Segmented and collage essays

**Goal:** assemble an essay from discrete pieces (numbered sections, quotations, lists, found text, photograph captions) whose arrangement makes the argument.

Examples in print include Maggie Nelson's *Bluets* (2009), built from numbered propositions, and David Shields's *Reality Hunger* (2010), which argues for collage and appropriation; both show the range of the approach. Study how they sequence, not just their tone.

## Method
1. **Gather material, unsorted.** Memories, facts, quotations, dialogue scraps, lists, images, definitions. Aim for more than you need.
2. **Find the organising principle** and decide it deliberately: numbered, alphabetical, by season, by object, by repeated question, by a countdown. A visible principle reassures the reader.
3. **Sequence by echo and contrast.** Place neighbouring pieces so they rhyme (shared image) or collide (tone shift). Test by reading in order and in reverse.
4. **Use white space and sections as pacing.** Short pieces speed up, long pieces slow down. Put a long piece in the middle as an anchor.
5. **Attribute borrowed text.** Quotations and found language need credit or a clear note; do not present others' words as yours. For reuse of copyrighted material, see `creative-arts.literary-writing.creative-nonfiction.permissions-and-fair-use-for-quotations`.
6. **Close with a section that resolves the pattern**, not a summary.

## Failure modes
- A heap: no principle, arbitrary order.
- Segmentation as a way to avoid transitions the piece actually needs.
- Unattributed quotation, a legal and ethical risk.

## Verify
- [ ] The organising principle can be stated in one sentence.
- [ ] Every borrowed fragment is credited or cleared.
- [ ] Order tested in a read-through.
