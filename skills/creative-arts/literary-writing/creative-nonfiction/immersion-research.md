# Immersion research

**Goal:** gather the raw material for true scenes by being there — for weeks, months or
years. Gutkind (*You Can't Make This Stuff Up*) describes immersion as the backbone of
creative nonfiction: you can't write scenes you didn't witness or reconstruct carefully.

## Access
1. Identify the world and a **gatekeeper** (hospital administrator, coach, family elder).
2. Explain plainly what you're doing, how long, and how people may appear (names,
   disguise, review rights you will or won't give). Get permissions in writing where
   institutions require it.
3. Start small (a day, an event), build trust, expand.

## Being there
- **Stay long enough that people stop performing** for you; the first weeks often
  produce the least usable material.
- Show up for the boring parts — routine reveals character and gives scene texture.
- Participate as appropriate (participant observation) but keep your role clear.

## Field notes
- Write **during** (small notebook, phone notes) and **immediately after** (fuller
  expansion the same day).
- Capture: exact dialogue (quote marks only for verbatim), sensory details, sequence and
  times, gestures, what people wear and carry, your own reactions (separately marked).
- Record (audio) only with consent and where legal.
- Date and label every entry; back up.
- Note **scene potential**: moments with conflict, change, or revealing action.

## Ethics
- People forget you're a writer; remind them periodically, especially before sensitive moments.
- Don't manipulate events for a better story.
- Consider the consequences of publication for vulnerable subjects (see
  `privacy-and-consent-in-nonfiction`).

## Gotchas
- Interview-only research for a book that needs scenes.
- Relying on memory instead of same-day notes.
- Becoming an advocate or participant who changes the outcome without disclosing it.

## Verify
- [ ] Access and terms agreed and recorded.
- [ ] Same-day field notes for every visit.
- [ ] Scene-potential moments flagged in notes.
