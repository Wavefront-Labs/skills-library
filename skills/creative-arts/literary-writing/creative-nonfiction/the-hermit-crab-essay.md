# The hermit-crab essay

**Goal:** use a non-literary form as a shell for personal material, so the formal conventions create irony, restraint or surprise.

The name is associated with Brenda Miller (see *Tell It Slant*, by Miller and Suzanne Paola): like a hermit crab, the essay takes up a form made for another purpose and lives in it.

## Choosing a shell
Forms with strong, recognisable conventions work best: recipe, instruction manual, application form, to-do list, FAQ, product review, field guide entry, syllabus, obituary, legal notice, itinerary, glossary, lab report. Pick a form whose rules resonate with your subject (a divorce as an assembly manual; grief as a weather report).

## Method
1. **Know the content first.** Write the core story plainly so you know what must come out.
2. **List the shell's parts and conventions** (for a recipe: title, yield, ingredients, steps, notes, serving).
3. **Map content to parts.** Each convention carries one beat. The constraint forces compression and selection.
4. **Honour the form, then break it once.** Keep the format convincing; let one element misbehave (a step that cannot be done, a blank that is left empty) at the emotional turn.
5. **Keep the voice human.** The form's flat register against emotional content is the engine; do not drown it in explanation.
6. **Revise for fit.** If you are forcing the content, change the shell, not the truth.

## Failure modes
- Gimmick: the form is a joke with nothing underneath.
- Shell too loose to constrain anything.
- Over-explaining; the form should do the interpretive work.

## Verify
- [ ] Reader can recognise the form at once.
- [ ] Each form element holds a real beat.
- [ ] One deliberate break lands at the turn.
