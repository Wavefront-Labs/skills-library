# The lyric essay

**Goal:** write nonfiction that moves by image, sound and association, trusting the reader to build the connections.

The term is generally traced to Deborah Tall and John D'Agata's 1997 *Seneca Review* editorial "A New Kind of Creative Nonfiction", and D'Agata's anthology *The Next American Essay* (2003) extended it. A lyric essay borrows from poetry (image, compression, leaps) while staying tied to fact or lived experience.

## How it works
- **Subject and obsession.** Start from an object, place, word or fact that will not leave you alone (a river, a diagnosis, a phrase). The essay circles it rather than arguing a thesis.
- **Juxtaposition.** Put two unlike materials side by side (a scientific fact and a family memory) and let the gap carry meaning. Do not explain the link.
- **Fragment and white space.** Sections may be short, numbered or separated by blank space; the silence between them is part of the design.
- **Sound and repetition.** Use refrain, anaphora, a recurring image; read aloud for rhythm.
- **Honesty about the facts.** The form allows leaps of association, not invented facts presented as true. Debate over how far an essayist may alter fact is live (D'Agata and Jim Fingal's *The Lifespan of a Fact*, 2012, dramatises it). Decide your contract with the reader and state it if you bend anything.

## Drafting steps
1. List 15-20 fragments about the subject: facts, images, memories, quotations, questions.
2. Cut to the ones that vibrate against each other; sequence by echo and contrast, not chronology.
3. Find the turn: a section where the essay's real question surfaces.
4. End on an image or open question, not a summary.

## Failure modes
- Murky rather than suggestive: the reader cannot find any thread. Make at least one concrete image recur.
- Pretty language with nothing at stake. Ask what you do not know that the essay is trying to find out.

## Verify
- [ ] Read aloud; the leaps feel earned.
- [ ] A recurring image or phrase ties sections.
- [ ] Facts are accurate or flagged.
