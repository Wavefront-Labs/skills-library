# Memoir scene reconstruction and truth — vivid without lying

**Goal:** dramatise real events as scenes readers can live inside, while keeping
faith with the truth, and dealing responsibly with the real people in the story.

## 1. Gather evidence before writing the scene
Memory is reconstructive and unreliable, especially for detail and dialogue.
Support it with:
- **Documents:** diaries, letters, emails, texts, social media posts, calendars, photos
  (check metadata for dates), medical or school records.
- **Other witnesses:** interview family and friends; note where versions differ —
  disagreement can become material.
- **Place and period research:** weather records, newspapers, maps, prices, songs on
  the radio that year. Visiting the location brings back sensory memory.
Keep a file noting the source for each concrete detail.

## 2. Reconstruct the scene
- Write what you remember strongly first, then fill with researched detail.
- **Dialogue:** nobody remembers exact words years later. Reconstruct speech that is
  true to the person's way of speaking and the substance of what was said. Many writers
  accept this; some readers don't — consider signalling it ("I remember her saying
  something like…") for contested or important moments, or note it in an author's note.
- **Never invent** events, people or significant facts. Where you don't know, say so
  on the page — uncertainty, handled openly, is often powerful ("I don't remember
  who drove us home. I remember the smell of the car.").

## 3. Compression and composites — disclose
- **Compression** (merging several similar conversations into one scene, or
  telescoping time) is common; be careful it doesn't change meaning.
- **Composite characters** (merging two people) and **changed names/identifying
  details** are used to protect privacy, but are contested. If you use them, say so in
  an author's note. Publishers' and readers' tolerance varies; famous memoir scandals
  usually involved invention, not disclosed compression.

## 4. Two selves: scene and reflection
Memoir has two narrators: the **experiencing self** (in the moment, not knowing what
comes next) and the **narrating self** (now, with hindsight).
- Scenes put readers in the experiencing self's moment.
- Reflection is where the narrating self makes meaning: what it meant then vs what it
  means now. Memoir without reflection is a diary; without scenes, a lecture.
- Alternate: scene → brief reflection → scene. Avoid explaining the emotion right
  after the scene has shown it.

## 5. Structure
Not necessarily chronological. Common shapes: a frame story in the present with
flashbacks; braided essays (several threads alternating); thematic chapters. Choose the
structure that reveals the **question** your memoir is asking, not just the sequence of
your life.

## 6. Ethics and legal exposure
- You're entitled to your story, but others appear in it. Consider: what's necessary
  to the story? what harm might it cause? would you say this to their face?
- Some writers share drafts with people portrayed; this isn't obligatory and has trade-offs.
- **Legal risk:** defamation (false statements of fact harming reputation) and privacy
  laws vary by country. Truthful writing reduces but doesn't remove risk; publishers
  may do a legal read. This is not legal advice — consult a lawyer for real concerns.
- Children, vulnerable people and third-party medical details deserve extra care.

## Gotchas
- **Over-confident detail** — the more specific the invented-feeling detail, the more
  readers suspect it. Keep sources.
- **Score-settling** reads as such and loses readers' trust; turn the lens on yourself too.
- **Writing too close to the event:** many memoirists find they need distance to reflect.
- Norms on reconstructed dialogue and composites are debated within the field; the
  stance here (allow with disclosure) is a common one, not a consensus.

## Verify
- [ ] Each concrete detail in key scenes has a source (memory, document, witness, research).
- [ ] Nothing invented; uncertainty acknowledged on the page.
- [ ] Compression, composites and name changes disclosed in an author's note.
- [ ] Scenes are balanced with reflection that makes meaning.
- [ ] Portrayals of real people reviewed for necessity, harm and legal risk.
