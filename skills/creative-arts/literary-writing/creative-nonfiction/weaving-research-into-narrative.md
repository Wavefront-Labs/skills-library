# Weaving research into narrative

**Goal:** readers learn a lot without feeling taught. Gutkind's narrative nonfiction
model alternates story (scenes) with information, so facts arrive embedded in a narrative frame.

## Principles
1. **Deliver information when the story raises the question.** Explain how a
   pacemaker works at the moment the patient's heart falters, not in chapter one.
2. **Alternate:** scene -> information -> scene. Long information passages stall; short
   ones between scenes refresh.
3. **Use people as guides:** an expert character explains, demonstrates or argues;
   information arrives through dialogue and action.
4. **Make information concrete:** numbers in human scale ("enough water to fill X"),
   examples, anecdotes, a single case standing for many.
5. **Select ruthlessly:** you'll use a fraction of your research. Keep what serves the
   narrative question.

## Techniques
- **Braiding:** a personal or narrative strand alternates with an informational strand
  in separate sections, each strand illuminating the other.
- **The pivot sentence:** end a scene on a question that the next informational section answers.
- **Voice in exposition:** the narrator's attitude, curiosity and humour keep facts alive.

## Sourcing
- Keep the text clean; move citations to **endnotes or source notes** keyed by page
  and phrase (common in trade nonfiction; see CMOS notes chapter).
- Keep a research log (source, page, quote, what it supports) so fact-checking is possible
  (see `creative-arts.literary-writing.creative-nonfiction.memoir-scene-reconstruction-and-truth`).

## Gotchas
- Front-loading context ("Before I tell you the story, you need to understand...").
- Research that impresses but doesn't advance the narrative.
- Inventing scenes to carry information — every scene must be true.

## Verify
- [ ] Each informational passage answers a question the story just raised.
- [ ] No informational stretch runs long without a return to scene.
- [ ] Every fact traceable in the research log or notes.
