# Building an armature for clay modelling

**Goal:** a skeleton that holds a clay sculpture up while you model it, sits
entirely inside the final form, and doesn't stop you firing, moulding or changing
the pose. A bad armature can't be fixed later without starting again.

## 1. Decide what happens after modelling
This determines the armature:
- **Fire it in a kiln** (water-based ceramic clay): **no internal armature** in the
  finished piece — either model solid and hollow it out, build hollow (coil/slab),
  or use a removable armature (e.g. a padded pipe the work slides off). Metal and
  wood inside fired clay cause cracks and explosions.
- **Make a mould and cast** (plaster, resin, bronze via foundry): a permanent internal
  armature is fine — the clay original is destroyed or recycled.
- **Oil-based clay** (plastilina, e.g. Chavant, Monster Clay): never dries, never
  fired; always on an armature, usually for moulding.
- **Polymer clay**: armature of foil and wire, baked in place at oven temperature.

## 2. Plan it on paper
Draw the pose at **actual size** (or scale) front and side. Mark the centre line,
the line of action, and joint positions. The armature follows the **core**
of each form — roughly the skeleton — and must stay at least a clay-thickness
(commonly 1.5–2.5 cm for a figure, more on a large work) inside the outer surface
everywhere. Note where the figure touches the base (the support point).

## 3. Materials
- **Aluminium armature wire** (soft, holds a bend, doesn't rust): gauges roughly
  3–6 mm depending on size. Twist two strands together for limbs; it's stronger and
  clay grips it better.
- **Backiron / external support**: an L-shaped steel pipe or flat bar screwed to a
  heavy base, entering the figure at the lower back or pelvis (the centre of mass),
  so the legs carry no weight while you model.
- **Base**: plywood heavy enough not to tip; a turntable/banding wheel underneath.
- **Butterflies**: small wooden crosses wired together, hung inside large masses
  (a torso, a big head) to stop clay slumping.
- **Bulk**: wrap the wire core with aluminium foil or tie on wood blocks to reduce the
  clay needed (only for non-fired work).
- **Head armatures**: a vertical pipe with a loop or a wooden "head peg" inside
  the cranium mass; leave the face region clay-only so you can carve the features.

## 4. Build
1. Fix the backiron solidly to the base. Wobble now becomes collapse later.
2. Bend the wire figure: head loop, spine, pelvis, legs, arms, using your drawing
   as a template. Use limbs slightly long — you can push them in, not pull them out.
3. Attach the wire figure to the backiron at the pelvis with wire wrapping
   (not tape, which lets go).
4. Pose it, check from all sides against the drawing, then wrap with thin wire or
   string so clay grips.
5. Apply clay in small, firmly pressed pieces, building out from the armature.

## Gotchas
- **Armature poking through** at the surface — usually elbows, knees, fingers or the
  top of the head. Keep it deep and short in extremities.
- **Too-rigid steel** in limbs: you can't adjust the pose once clay is on. Use
  bendable wire in limbs, rigid support in the spine/backiron.
- **Wood swells** when water clay soaks it — seal wooden parts or keep them minimal.
- **Water-based clay on a permanent armature cracks as it dries and shrinks** around
  the metal. Keep it damp (wrap in plastic) until moulded.
- **Fired work**: unhollowed thick sections (over roughly 2–3 cm, depending on clay
  and kiln schedule) risk cracking or bursting. Check guidelines for your clay body.

## Verify
- [ ] You know before you build whether the piece will be fired, moulded or kept.
- [ ] The armature sits at least a full clay-thickness inside the form everywhere.
- [ ] The figure is supported at its centre of mass; legs carry no load.
- [ ] Nothing wobbles when the turntable spins.
- [ ] For firing: no metal or wood remains inside, and thick masses are hollowed.
