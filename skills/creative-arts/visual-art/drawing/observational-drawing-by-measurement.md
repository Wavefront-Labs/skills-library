# Observational drawing by measurement

**Goal:** draw a still life, interior or figure with correct proportions by
measuring and checking, instead of drawing what you *think* things look like.
Accuracy here is a learnable procedure, not talent.

## Setup
- Fixed viewpoint: sit or stand in the same place; mark chair or feet positions
  with tape. Close one eye when measuring (two eyes give two views).
- One clear light source on the subject (a lamp from one side and above) — it
  makes values readable.
- Board upright or tilted so you look at it square-on; a flat board on a table
  distorts proportions.
- Media: vine/willow charcoal or a hard pencil (2H–HB) for the block-in;
  softer (2B–6B) or compressed charcoal for darks later.

## 1. Comparative measurement
1. Hold a pencil at **full arm's length**, elbow locked, perpendicular to your
   line of sight.
2. Pick a **unit** — a clear, medium-sized measurement (the height of a jug,
   the head of a figure).
3. Sight the unit along the pencil, thumbnail marking its length; then compare
   other distances: "the table is 3½ jugs wide".
4. Draw everything in proportion to that unit, at whatever size suits your paper.
Locked arm is essential; a bent elbow changes every measurement.

**Sight-size** (alternative, used in ateliers): place the drawing beside the
subject and step back so they appear the same size, then compare directly.
Accurate but needs a set-up and distance.

## 2. Envelope and block-in
- Mark the **top, bottom, left and right extremes** of the whole subject group.
- Connect the outermost points with straight lines to make an **envelope** — a
  simple polygon that contains everything.
- Subdivide with straight lines: big shapes first, then smaller. Use straight
  lines even for curves at this stage; they make angles measurable.

## 3. Angles and plumb lines
- **Angles:** hold the pencil along an edge in the subject, keep that angle as you
  move it to the paper, and copy it — or compare it to the clock face ("ten past").
- **Plumb and level lines:** hold the pencil vertical (or horizontal) to see which
  points line up: "the jug handle is directly above the lemon". Lightly draw these
  alignments; they catch most errors.

## 4. Negative shapes
Draw the **shapes of the space** between and around objects (the gap between a
chair leg and the table). Your brain doesn't have an expectation for them, so
you draw them more truthfully, and they cross-check the positive shapes.

## 5. Check before rendering
- Step back (or photograph and view small or mirrored — a mirror reveals asymmetry).
- Re-measure three or four key relationships.
- Fix errors now: rendering on wrong proportions wastes hours.

## 6. Value grouping
**Squint** to merge detail into two or three value masses (light, halftone,
shadow). Block in the shadow shapes as one flat value first; only then model
the halftones and add the darkest accents (where forms touch, the shadow core)
and highlights. Keep shadow shapes simple; most beginners over-model inside shadows.

## Gotchas
- **Symbol drawing**: drawing an "eye" or "bottle" from memory instead of the shape
  in front of you. Measure the shape.
- **Ellipses** in cylinders: the further below eye level, the rounder; never
  pointed ends. Check each ellipse's width against its height.
- **Moving your head** between measurements breaks consistency.
- **Detail too early**: a perfect detail in the wrong place is still wrong.

## Verify
- [ ] An envelope and block-in in straight lines exist before any shading.
- [ ] At least three proportions re-measured against the unit after block-in.
- [ ] Plumb lines confirm key vertical/horizontal alignments.
- [ ] A mirrored view or photo shows no obvious lean or distortion.
- [ ] Squinting at the drawing and the subject gives the same two or three value masses.
