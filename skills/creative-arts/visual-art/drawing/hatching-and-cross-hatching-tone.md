# Hatching and cross-hatching tone

**Goal:** create controlled value with parallel lines rather than smudging, so the
drawing keeps clarity, paper sparkle and a visible hand. Essential in pen and ink
and much academic graphite work.

## Basic hatching
- Parallel strokes of even length and spacing. Value is controlled by **spacing**,
  **line thickness** and **pressure**.
- Use the wrist or fingers for short strokes (1-3 cm); keep each stroke flicked so
  it tapers at one or both ends, which blends neighbouring patches.
- Most right-handers find a lower-left to upper-right diagonal easiest; pick one
  default direction for flat tones so the drawing has a unified texture.

## Cross-hatching
- Add a second layer at an angle (often 30-45 degrees, not 90, which reads as a mesh/
  grid). A third and fourth layer at new angles build deep darks.
- Keep the angle between layers consistent within an area to avoid moire.

## Contour hatching
- Strokes curve along the form's cross-contours, describing volume as well as
  value. Use on rounded forms (figure, drapery); straight hatching suits flat planes.

## Practice drill
1. Draw a strip of 6 boxes; fill each with one more layer than the last.
2. Draw a sphere; shade it with contour hatching only, then another with cross-hatching only.

## Gotchas
- Scribbling (strokes changing direction randomly) looks muddy.
- Strokes starting with a blob (pen hesitation): begin moving before the pen touches.
- In graphite, pressing hard early burnishes paper so later layers will not take.
- In ink, you cannot erase; build up slowly from light.

## Verify
- [ ] Tone changes come from spacing and layers, not scribble.
- [ ] Layer angles are deliberate and avoid moire.
- [ ] Round forms use hatching that follows the form.
