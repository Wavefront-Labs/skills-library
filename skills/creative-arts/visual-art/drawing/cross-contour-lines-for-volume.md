# Cross-contour lines for volume

**Goal:** describe the 3D surface of a form with lines that run **across** it,
as if drawing on the object with a pen or slicing it like a loaf, so the reader
feels its volume without any shading.

## How to draw them
1. Start with the silhouette and the main axis of the form (e.g. a bent arm,
   a pear, a fold of fabric).
2. Imagine evenly spaced slices perpendicular to the axis. Each slice's edge on the
   visible surface is a cross-contour: an elliptical arc on a cylinder, a curved
   line bending with every bulge and hollow on organic forms.
3. Draw these lines with consistent spacing **on the surface**; as the surface
   turns away from you, they bunch together; on near-facing planes they spread.
4. Lines must meet the silhouette tangentially where the surface turns away; a
   line hitting the outline at a sharp angle flattens the form.
5. A second set of lines running along the form (a "mesh") makes a wireframe, the
   tool used in construction-drawing courses like Drawabox.

## Uses
- Diagnostic: if you cannot draw the cross-contour, you do not yet understand the form.
- Hatching direction: shading strokes that follow cross-contours model the form.
- Figure and drapery: ribbons of cross-contour across torso, limbs and folds.

## Gotchas
- Drawing cross-contours as flat horizontal lines: they must curve with the surface.
- Ellipse degree errors: slices near eye level are thin ellipses; further below or
  above eye level they open up. Keep this consistent along the form.
- Overusing them in a finished drawing can look diagrammatic; often they stay in
  the underdrawing.

## Verify
- [ ] Lines curve with every change of surface.
- [ ] Spacing compresses where the surface turns away.
- [ ] Lines meet the silhouette tangentially.
