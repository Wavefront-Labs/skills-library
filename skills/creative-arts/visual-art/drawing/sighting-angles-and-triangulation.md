# Sighting angles and triangulation

**Goal:** locate each landmark by its angle from points you already trust, so the
drawing is built like a survey rather than guessed.

## Sighting an angle
1. Hold a pencil or needle at arm's length in the plane of the picture (not
   tilted toward you). Align it with the edge or with two points on the subject.
2. Compare it to a **true vertical or horizontal**: the edge of the board, a plumb
   line, or a held level. Judge it as a clock time or degrees off vertical
   ("about 20 degrees right of vertical", "two o'clock").
3. Keeping that tilt, bring the pencil down to the paper and draw a light line at
   the same angle. Many artists use a cheap angle-finder or two hinged sticks.

## Triangulation
- From two established points A and B, sight the angle A-to-C and B-to-C. The new
  point C is where the two lines cross. One measurement can be wrong; two
  independent angles cross-check each other.
- Use a third line (a plumb or level from another landmark) as a tiebreaker when
  the two angles meet at a shallow, uncertain crossing.

## Plumb and level lines
- Drop vertical lines from key points (the pit of the neck, a corner of a table)
  and note what they pass through on the subject; check the same on the drawing.
- Horizontal alignments ("the ear's base is level with the nose's base") are
  among the most reliable checks you have.

## Gotchas
- Judging angles of curves: break curves into straight segments first.
- Angles near 45 degrees are hard to judge; compare them to each other instead
  ("steeper than that one").
- Rotating the board while drawing changes your vertical reference. Keep it fixed.

## Verify
- [ ] Every major point was placed from two independent references.
- [ ] Plumb and level checks agree between subject and drawing.
