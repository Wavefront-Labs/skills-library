# Making and using a value scale

**Goal:** a calibrated strip of greys from white to black that trains your eye
and lets you measure the value of anything you draw or paint.

## Making one
1. Draw a strip of 9 or 10 boxes about 2.5 x 2.5 cm (Munsell uses 0 = black to
   10 = white; a 9-step scale drops the ends or merges them).
2. Leave the lightest box as paper white. Make the darkest as dark as your medium
   allows (heavy 8B, compressed charcoal, ivory black paint).
3. Fill the **middle** box next: a true middle grey (Munsell value 5, about 18-20
   percent reflectance). Compare against a photographer's 18 percent grey card if
   you have one; most people make it too light.
4. Fill the remaining steps so each looks an **equal visual step** from its
   neighbours. Squint and adjust until no two adjacent steps seem closer than others.
5. For paint: mix titanium white and ivory black (or a neutral black) and paint
   chips on card; label them 1-9.

## Using it
- **Value finder**: punch a small hole in each box. Hold the scale at arm's length
  and look through the holes at a spot on the subject; the hole whose surrounding
  box matches is that spot's value. Isolation defeats simultaneous contrast.
- **Planning**: decide which steps a drawing uses (e.g. light family 7-9, shadow
  family 2-4), keeping a gap between families.
- **Checking**: hold the scale against your drawing; the darkest accent and the
  lightest light should land where you planned.

## Gotchas
- Graphite looks lighter at a glancing angle (shine); check values straight on.
- Your scale will drift; remake it when you change medium.
- Value of a coloured object is judged better with a grey filter, black mirror
  or phone set to monochrome.

## Verify
- [ ] Steps look visually even under squinting.
- [ ] Middle box matches an 18 percent grey card closely.
- [ ] You can read a subject's value through the holes consistently.
