# Writing a public art commission proposal

**Goal:** a proposal a selection panel can say yes to and a commissioner can
actually build, insure and maintain. Panels reject beautiful ideas that won't
survive weather, budget or the public, so the practical sections carry as much
weight as the concept.

## 1. Read the call like a contract
Calls often run in stages: **EOI/RFQ** (qualifications: CV, past work, statement)
then a shortlist paid to develop an **RFP**/concept proposal. Extract:
- Total budget and **what it must cover** (artist fee, fabrication, installation,
  engineering, insurance, lighting, plaques, travel, taxes?).
- Site, dimensions, permanent or temporary, lifespan expected.
- Required deliverables, format, page limits, deadline (time zone).
- Selection criteria and who is on the panel.
- Maintenance expectations and ownership/rights terms.
Ask questions in the official Q&A window; answers are often shared with everyone.

## 2. Site and community response
- Visit the site at different times: light, foot traffic, vandalism risk,
  wind, drainage, sightlines, nearby utilities.
- Research history, ecology and the people who use it. Many calls expect a
  **community engagement** plan — say concretely who you'll talk to, how, and how
  it can shape the work (and how much it can change, honestly).
- Explain why *this* work belongs *here*. A generic concept that could sit anywhere
  scores poorly.

## 3. The concept and visuals
- One paragraph that a panel member can repeat to a council meeting.
- Visuals: a photomontage of the work on the site at human scale, plan and
  elevation with dimensions, and a material/colour sample board. A physical or 3D
  maquette helps for sculpture.
- Show the experience: day/night, how people approach, touch, sit, walk through.

## 4. Materials, safety and maintenance
- Specify materials and finishes for outdoor durability (e.g. marine-grade stainless,
  weathering steel, sealed concrete, anti-graffiti coatings for murals).
- State a **maintenance plan**: cleaning frequency and method, expected life,
  repairs and who pays. Commissioners fear unmaintainable art.
- Structural works need a **licensed structural engineer** to stamp footing and
  fixings; climbable or touchable works need safety review (edges, pinch points,
  heights, accessibility). Permits vary by jurisdiction (unconfirmed for yours —
  ask the commissioner who leads permitting).

## 5. Budget
Build it line by line:
- **Artist fee** — commonly cited guidance is 15–20% of the budget; check local
  artist-fee guidance. Don't let fabrication eat it.
- Design development, engineering, fabrication (with fabricator quotes),
  transport, installation (cranes, traffic management), lighting/electrical,
  documentation, insurance (public liability, often required), permits, travel.
- **Contingency** of about 10–15% for permanent work; materials prices move.
- Taxes: whether the budget is inclusive of VAT/sales tax matters.

## 6. Timeline and team
Show milestones (design sign-off, engineering, fabrication, installation, unveiling)
with buffer for permits and weather. Name your fabricator, engineer and installer, with
their relevant past projects — panels look for a team that has delivered before.

## Gotchas
- Over-promising engagement, then delivering a finished design anyway.
- Budgets with no artist fee, no contingency, or fabrication quotes from memory.
- Ignoring the contract: copyright usually stays with the artist, but check moral
  rights, reproduction rights, deaccession and relocation clauses.
- Murals: wall condition and primer matter more than paint; get the wall assessed.

## Verify
- [ ] Every required deliverable is included in the requested format and page limits.
- [ ] Concept explicitly responds to this site and its users.
- [ ] Visuals show the work on site, at human scale, with dimensions.
- [ ] Budget covers the artist fee, every cost line and a contingency.
- [ ] Engineering, permits, insurance and maintenance are addressed with named responsibility.
