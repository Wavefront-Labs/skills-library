# Risk assessment for installations

**Goal:** show you have identified and controlled hazards to the public, staff and yourself. Law and standards differ by country; follow your local regulator and the venue's rules. The UK Health and Safety Executive describes five steps that work anywhere.

## The five steps
1. **Identify the hazards:** structure, moving parts, electrical equipment, heat, sharp edges, trip hazards, falling objects, work at height, noise, flashing light, fire load, crowds, interaction by children, water, chemicals and materials.
2. **Decide who might be harmed and how:** visitors, children, disabled people, staff, installers, cleaners, passers-by.
3. **Evaluate the risks and decide on controls.** Use the hierarchy of controls: eliminate, substitute, engineer (guards, barriers, fixings), administrative (signs, supervision, training) and, last, PPE. Rate likelihood and severity to prioritise.
4. **Record and implement.** Write it down, share it with the venue and the team, and add a **method statement** describing how work will be done safely.
5. **Review** after install, after incidents and when anything changes.

## Specific topics
- **Structure:** calculate or get an engineer to check loads, wind and anchor points. Public structures should be designed for foreseeable misuse such as climbing.
- **Fire:** keep escape routes clear, check the flame-retardancy of fabrics and materials against venue and local requirements, and consult the venue's fire officer.
- **Electrical:** use qualified electricians, residual current protection, safe low-voltage supplies where possible, and have equipment tested as locally required.
- **Accessibility:** consider step-free routes and the needs of visitors with disabilities.
- **Moving or interactive parts:** guard pinch points, limit forces and speeds, and add emergency stops.
- **Insurance:** public liability and, for contractors, employer's liability or equivalent. Venues often require a certificate.
- **Emergency plan:** first aid, contact numbers, evacuation, who can shut off power.

## Gotchas
- Generic templates that ignore the specific site are not accepted by good venues.
- Materials with unknown fire properties are a frequent reason for refusal.
- Remember installers' risk as well as the public's.
