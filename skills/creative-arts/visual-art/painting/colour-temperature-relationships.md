# Colour temperature relationships

**Goal:** use warm and cool shifts to describe light, form and space.

## Temperature is relative
- Warm = toward red/orange/yellow; cool = toward blue/blue-green/violet.
- Any colour has warmer and cooler versions: a cool red (alizarin, toward violet),
  a warm red (cadmium, toward orange). Judge colours **against their neighbours**.

## Light and shadow temperature
- Under **warm light** (sun, incandescent, firelight), lit planes are warm and
  shadows look comparatively cool, often because the shadow is filled by cooler
  skylight (outdoors).
- Under **cool light** (overcast, north window), lights are cool and shadows look
  warmer.
- Gurney stresses that the rule follows the actual sources: it is not "always warm
  light, cool shadow". Identify the key light and the fill/ambient light colours.

## Temperature on a form
- A form lit by a warm key and cool sky fill shifts from warm in the light through
  a greyer halftone to cool reflected tones in the shadow; reflected light from a
  warm ground can make the lower shadow warm again.
- Edges of the terminator can show a slight chroma rise ("the hot line"),
  especially on skin (subsurface scattering).

## Mixing temperature changes
- Warm up: add a touch of a warmer neighbour hue (yellow ochre, cadmium orange),
  not just red.
- Cool down: add a cooler neighbour (ultramarine, viridian) or a cool grey.
- Keep value constant while shifting temperature: compare on a value scale.

## Gotchas
- Blue shadows on every subject regardless of light: looks formulaic.
- Shifting temperature by adding white (cools and greys most colours).

## Verify
- [ ] Light and shadow temperatures follow the identified sources.
- [ ] Temperature changes happen without unintended value changes.
