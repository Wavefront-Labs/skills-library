# Glazing transparent colour

**Goal:** a thin transparent layer of colour over a dry, lighter passage so light
passes through, reflects off the layer below and returns coloured, giving depth
and luminosity impossible with direct mixing (Mayer; manufacturer guides).

## Transparent pigments (check tube labels)
Quinacridones (PR122, PV19, PO48), phthalo blue/green (PB15, PG7), ultramarine
(PB29, semi), alizarin crimson/permanent alizarin, transparent iron oxides
(transparent red/yellow oxide, PR101/PY42 transparent), Indian yellow
substitutes, dioxazine violet (PV23). Opaque paints (cadmiums, titanium, ochres)
make cloudy glazes.

## Glazing medium
- Oil: linseed or stand oil + solvent, or a ready alkyd glazing medium (Liquin
  Original, Galkyd) which dries faster and levels. Typical start: a little paint
  in plenty of medium. Glazes are fat layers (fat over lean).
- Acrylic: gloss or matte glazing medium / fluid matte medium with a little
  paint; dries in minutes; many layers in a session.

## Procedure
1. Ensure the underlayer is **dry** (oil: touch dry, preferably days).
2. "Oil out" (very thin medium film) only if the surface is absorbent or sunken.
3. Brush the glaze on thinly with a soft brush; even it with a soft blender.
4. **Wipe back** with a lint-free rag to lighten highlights or reveal texture.
5. Let dry fully before the next glaze. Several thin glazes beat one heavy one.

## Effects
- Deepen shadows while keeping them transparent.
- Shift colour (a blue glaze over yellow = luminous green).
- Unify an area (one warm glaze over a whole landscape).

## Gotchas
- Too much oil in a glaze: wrinkles, yellows, pools in texture.
- Glazing onto tacky paint: lifts and smears.
- Beading (glaze crawls into droplets) on a slick surface: rub a little medium
  first or lightly sand once fully dry.

## Verify
- [ ] Underlayer dry; glaze pigments transparent.
- [ ] Thin, even application with no pooling.
- [ ] Each glaze dry before the next.
