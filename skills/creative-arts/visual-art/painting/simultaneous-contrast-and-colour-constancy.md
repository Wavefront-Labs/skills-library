# Simultaneous contrast and colour constancy

**Goal:** see colours as they actually appear in the scene, not as the brain
"corrects" them, and use contrast effects deliberately (Albers, *Interaction of
Color*; Gurney, *Color and Light*).

## Simultaneous contrast
- A colour looks different depending on its surroundings: a grey looks warmer
  on blue, lighter on black, darker on white. Complements surrounding a colour
  push it toward its own complement.
- Albers' classic exercise: make one colour look like two (place it on two
  different grounds), and two colours look alike.

## Colour constancy
- The brain discounts the colour of the light to keep objects' "local colour"
  stable: a white shirt looks white in orange sunset light, though it reflects orange.
- Painters must paint the **perceived-in-context** colour (orange-tinted white),
  or the painting loses its light.

## Isolating colours to judge them
1. **Peephole**: look through a small hole in a grey card (neutral mid value) at
   the subject: the colour is seen without context.
2. **Colour checker**: hold a palette knife with your mixture up against the
   subject, squinting, and compare directly (common in plein air).
3. **Compare pairs**: judge relationships ("this shadow is cooler and darker than that one")
   rather than absolute colours.
4. **Toned ground**: painting on white makes all early colours look too dark;
   a mid-tone ground gives a fairer context.

## Using contrast deliberately
- Make a dull colour look brighter by surrounding it with its greyed complement.
- A neutral grey area surrounded by warm colours reads cool: free "blue" without blue paint.

## Gotchas
- Judging a mixture on a white palette: it looks different on the canvas. Test on
  the canvas or a grey palette.
- Painting the "known" colour (green leaves, blue sky) instead of the seen one.

## Verify
- [ ] Key colours were checked in isolation or against the subject.
- [ ] Whites and neutrals carry the colour of the light.
