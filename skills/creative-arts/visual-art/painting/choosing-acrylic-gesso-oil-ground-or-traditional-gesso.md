# Choosing acrylic gesso, oil ground or traditional gesso

**Goal:** pick the ground that suits the support, the paint and the painting's
look (Mayer, grounds chapter).

## Comparison
| Ground | Composition | Supports | Paints | Character |
|--------|-------------|----------|--------|-----------|
| Acrylic "gesso" | Acrylic polymer, titanium white, chalk | Canvas, panel, paper | Acrylic, oil (when labelled for oil) | Flexible, fairly absorbent, toothy; dries in hours |
| Oil / alkyd ground | Oil or alkyd + white (titanium or lead) | Sized canvas or panel | Oil only | Less absorbent, smooth, "slippery"; luminous; days-weeks to cure |
| Traditional (true) gesso | Chalk/gypsum + animal glue (rabbit-skin) | **Rigid panels only** | Egg tempera, oil (sealed) | Very absorbent, brilliant white, sandable to ivory smoothness; brittle |

## Guidance
- Acrylic over oil ground: **no** (acrylic will not adhere to oil).
- Oil over acrylic gesso: yes. Many oil painters prefer an oil ground for its
  surface; acrylic gesso is more absorbent and can make first layers sink.
- Traditional gesso on canvas: no, it cracks.
- Absorbency control: thin oil painting medium or a coat of acrylic medium on gesso
  reduces drag; a toned imprimatura also seals.
- Lead grounds (lead carbonate) are prized for flexibility and drying, but toxic;
  handle with gloves, never sand.

## Toning
Tone any ground with a thin, lean wash (acrylic or oil) of a mid-value colour once
dry; see imprimatura skill.

## Gotchas
- Cheap student gesso: high chalk, low binder, chalky and over-absorbent.
- Oil grounds painted on too soon: still curing, can wrinkle.

## Verify
- [ ] Ground compatible with paint (no acrylic on oil).
- [ ] Traditional gesso only on rigid panels.
- [ ] Absorbency suits the technique.
