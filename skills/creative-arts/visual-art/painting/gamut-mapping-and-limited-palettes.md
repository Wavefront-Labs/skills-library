# Gamut mapping and limited palettes

**Goal:** guarantee colour harmony by deciding in advance which colours the
painting may contain (Gurney, *Color and Light*, "gamut mapping").

## Gamut mapping
1. Draw a colour wheel (hue around the edge, chroma from grey centre outward;
   Munsell-like).
2. Draw a **shape** (triangle, square, etc.) on it: only colours inside the shape
   are allowed. A triangle centred off to one side gives a dominant hue family;
   its vertices are the most saturated colours available.
3. Mix "source" colours at the shape's vertices, then mix everything else from
   those (and white) so nothing falls outside.
4. Common shapes: a narrow triangle (analogous with an accent), a triangle across
   the wheel with low saturation at one end (complementary but muted), a small
   shape near the centre (atmospheric, greyed scene).
Digital tools: gamut mask features (Krita's Advanced Colour Selector supports masks;
Gurney's online tool).

## Limited palettes
- **Zorn palette**: yellow ochre, cadmium (or vermilion) red, ivory black, titanium
  white. Black acts as a weak blue; great for portraits; no strong greens or blues.
- **Primary limited**: one warm red, one yellow, one blue, white (e.g. cad red
  light, cad yellow light, ultramarine, white): wide range, harmony through
  shared ingredients.
- **Earth palette**: ochre, burnt sienna, raw umber, ultramarine, white: muted, unified.

## Steps for a painting
1. Choose the gamut for mood (warm afternoon: a shape biased to orange with muted blues).
2. Mix a set of strings or piles from the gamut before painting.
3. Paint only from those piles.

## Gotchas
- Adding a tube colour outside the gamut "just for one area": breaks unity.
- Gamuts so small the painting has no accent; leave one vertex for a highlight colour.

## Verify
- [ ] Every mixed colour sits inside the chosen shape.
- [ ] The painting has a clear dominant colour family.
