# Oil painting: fat over lean layering

**Goal:** build an oil painting in layers that stay flexible and bonded for
decades, without cracking, wrinkling or sinking-in, and choose between painting
it all at once (alla prima) and in stages (indirect).

## 1. The rule and why it exists
Oil paint dries by **oxidation**, not evaporation, and stays slightly flexible.
Layers with **more oil** ("fat") dry slower and are more flexible than layers with
less oil ("lean"). If a lean layer goes over a fat one, the top dries brittle while
the layer under it is still moving — the classic result is cracks.
So each layer should be **at least as flexible and slow-drying as the one beneath**:
- **Fat over lean** — oil content increases layer by layer.
- **Thick over thin** — heavy impasto belongs late, not in the underpainting.
- **Slow over fast** — fast-drying paints/mediums (alkyd, lead white, earth colours)
  go under, slow ones (many cadmiums, some blacks, safflower-bound whites) on top.

## 2. A working layer recipe
Ratios are a common studio starting point, not a standard — test with your paints.
1. **Ground**: acrylic "gesso" (suitable for oils) or oil ground. Avoid painting oils
   onto raw canvas or unprimed wood — the oil rots fibres and sinks in.
2. **Imprimatura / underpainting**: thin paint diluted with solvent only (or very
   lean), e.g. burnt umber or a mid-tone wash. Dries in hours to a day.
3. **Middle layers**: paint with a lean medium, roughly 1 part oil to 2–3 parts solvent.
4. **Final layers / glazes**: richer medium, e.g. equal parts oil and solvent, or
   a glazing medium; or paint straight from the tube (tube paint is already fat).
Avoid excess oil in any layer — too much causes wrinkling and yellowing.

## 3. Drying between layers
"Touch dry" (doesn't lift on a light finger) is the usual signal to overpaint.
Times vary from a day (thin, earth colours, alkyd mediums) to a week or more
(thick, slow pigments, cool rooms). Warmth and air help; darkness can yellow
linseed temporarily.

## 4. Alla prima versus indirect
- **Alla prima (wet-into-wet)**: finish in one session while everything is wet.
  The fat-over-lean concern is mostly moot — keep paint consistency similar and
  avoid thinning late passages with solvent. Fast, direct, fresh brushwork.
- **Indirect**: underpainting, then glazes (thin transparent colour over dry paint)
  and scumbles (thin opaque/semi-opaque paint dragged over). Gives depth and
  luminous colour; needs the layer rules above.
Many painters combine: a lean underpainting, then a mostly alla prima session.

## 5. Finishing
- **Sinking-in** (dull patches where a layer absorbed oil from above) can be
  **oiled out** with a thin wipe of medium before continuing.
- **Varnish** only when thoroughly dry — commonly cited as 6–12 months for normal
  thickness (unconfirmed for every paint system; follow your varnish maker). A
  removable retouch varnish can be used earlier for exhibition.

## 6. Studio safety
- Use low-aromatic **odourless mineral spirits** with ventilation; turpentine is
  more toxic and a skin sensitiser. Keep solvent in a closed jar; reuse after settling.
- **Rags soaked with linseed oil can spontaneously combust.** Lay them flat to dry
  outdoors, or store in water in a sealed metal can before disposal.
- Treat pigments containing cadmium, cobalt and lead as toxic: no eating while
  painting, wash hands, never sand dry paint.

## Gotchas
- Thinning top layers with solvent to "make it flow" — the commonest fat-over-lean error.
- Acrylic over oil never works; oil over acrylic is fine.
- Cheap student paints with fillers dry and sink differently from artist grade.
- Lead white use is restricted in some regions — check availability and rules.

## Verify
- [ ] Each layer has equal or more oil than the one below; solvent decreases upward.
- [ ] Impasto only in final layers.
- [ ] Each layer was touch-dry before overpainting.
- [ ] No dull, sunken patches remain (or they were oiled out).
- [ ] Oily rags are stored or disposed of safely.
