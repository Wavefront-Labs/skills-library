# Grisaille and dead colouring

**Goal:** separate the problems of drawing/value and colour by painting a
monochrome underpainting first, then colour over it (Mayer, *The Artist's Handbook*,
oil painting techniques; National Gallery technical glossary).

## Monochrome underpainting types
- **Grisaille**: greys (white + black, or white + raw umber/black).
- **Brunaille**: browns (burnt or raw umber + white).
- **Verdaccio**: green-grey (traditional under flesh in tempera and early oil
  painting; e.g. black, ochre and white).

## Painting the grisaille
1. Transfer an accurate drawing.
2. Paint the full value structure in monochrome: forms modelled, edges decided,
   values **slightly lighter** than final (glazes darken).
3. Use lean paint: little or no medium, fast-drying whites (lead or flake white
   traditionally; titanium-zinc mixes now) and umber (dries quickly).
4. Let it dry thoroughly (touch dry plus a few days).

## Dead colouring
- A thin, muted, opaque or semi-opaque colour layer over the grisaille: local
  colours established at low chroma, with the grisaille's modelling showing through.
- Historical "dead colour" in 17th-century Dutch practice was a muted first colour
  layer; brighter colours, glazes and scumbles come after.

## Next layers
Glazes (transparent colour) and scumbles (semi-opaque lighter veils) build chroma
and depth; see glazing-transparent-colour and scumbling skill.

## Gotchas
- Grisaille too dark: glazes make it muddy.
- Fat grisaille (oily): breaks fat-over-lean for later layers.
- Cool grey under flesh without warmth on top: corpse-like skin.

## Verify
- [ ] Underpainting resolves drawing and values fully.
- [ ] Values slightly high; paint lean; fully dry before colour.
