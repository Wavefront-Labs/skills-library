# Planning for longevity of mixed materials

**Goal:** reduce future damage in works with many materials. **Inherent vice** is the tendency of a material to deteriorate because of its own composition.

## Common problem materials
- **Acidic paper and board:** yellows and embrittles, and migrates acid to neighbours.
- **Adhesives and tapes:** many pressure-sensitive tapes yellow, ooze and stain; some glues turn brittle.
- **Plastics:** some deteriorate with off-gassing, plasticiser migration or crumbling (for example polyurethane foam; old PVC; cellulose nitrate). Identify the plastic if you can and check the conservation literature.
- **Metals:** rust and corrosion stain neighbouring materials; copper alloys and brass corrode.
- **Organic materials:** insects, mould and rot (feathers, seeds, wood, food).
- **Rubber:** perishes and sticks.

## Plan
1. **Choose stable materials** where the object's meaning does not depend on the unstable one; if it does, document the decision.
2. **Isolate** incompatible items with a barrier (inert polyester film or archival board) or coat metal with a stable clear lacquer or wax.
3. **Test** adhesives and combinations on samples before final assembly.
4. **Clean and treat** found objects (dry clean, dry and freeze or isolate pests as needed).
5. **Document:** keep a materials list, supplier and product names, photos, and construction notes with the work, so a conservator can act on it.
6. **Display and store** in stable temperature and humidity, away from direct sun.

## Safety
- Found objects may carry lead paint, mould, asbestos or biological contaminants; handle with gloves and a dust mask, and avoid sanding or breaking unknown items.

## Gotchas
- Combining highly reactive materials in a sealed frame traps off-gassing; allow some ventilation.
- Never assume plastics are stable; test or consult a conservator.

## Verify
- [ ] A written materials list accompanies the work.
- [ ] Reactive materials are isolated or documented as an artistic choice.
