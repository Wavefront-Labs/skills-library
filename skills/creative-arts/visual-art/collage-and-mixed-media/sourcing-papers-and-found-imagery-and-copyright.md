# Sourcing and preparing papers; copyright of found imagery

**Goal:** collect collage material that will last and that you may legally use. This is craft guidance, not legal advice.

## Papers
- Newsprint, magazines and cheap pulp paper yellow and turn brittle (acidic, lignin-rich). For work meant to last, use acid-free or archival papers, or isolate acidic items: colour-photocopy or scan them and print on archival paper with pigment inks.
- Test unknown papers for bleed with a drop of water and the adhesive you plan to use. Glossy magazine stock can ghost through thin paper when wet.
- Flatten and pre-size delicate pieces; cut with the grain where possible to reduce curling when glued.
- Inkjet dye prints bleed and fade under wet adhesive and light; pigment-ink prints are more stable. Seal inkjet prints with a spray fixative before gluing (spray outdoors or with ventilation).

## Found imagery and copyright
- Material you find is usually still under copyright. Cutting up a published image to make a new work is not automatically permitted anywhere.
- **United States:** fair use is decided case by case using four factors (purpose and character, nature of the work, amount used, effect on the market). There is no safe percentage.
- **United Kingdom:** there is no general fair use; fair dealing exceptions cover limited purposes such as criticism, review, quotation, caricature, parody and pastiche. Check the current government guidance.
- **Safer sources:** public-domain works (rules vary by country and date, so verify), your own photographs, licensed stock, and museum open-access collections. The Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Smithsonian publish large sets of open-access images under CC0; confirm the licence on each item.
- Faces of identifiable people can raise privacy or publicity-rights issues independent of copyright, especially for commercial use.
- Record where each source came from in a log; it helps if a gallery, publisher or customer asks.

## Gotchas
- Vintage ephemera may be in copyright even when old (publication date and author death date matter).
- Using a recognisable brand mark prominently can raise trademark issues for commercial products.
- Do not claim public domain simply because an image is online.

## Verify
- [ ] Every source is archival, scanned or logged with a licence note.
- [ ] A test scrap with your adhesive shows no bleed-through or ink transfer.
