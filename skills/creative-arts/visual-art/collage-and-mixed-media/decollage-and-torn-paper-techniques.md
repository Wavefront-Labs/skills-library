# Decollage and torn-paper techniques

**Goal:** create images by removing layers, the reverse of collage. Decollage is associated with artists such as Raymond Hains, Jacques Villeglé and Mimmo Rotella, who worked with torn posters.

## Torn-paper edge
- To make a controlled tear, wet a line with a brush, a fingertip or a damp ruler edge, then tear along it. Tearing toward yourself and along the grain gives a soft, feathered edge; against the grain is rougher.
- Handmade or rag paper tears with a fibrous deckle; cheap pulp paper tears flat.

## Building and revealing
1. On a rigid, sealed support (board or panel, primed), glue layered posters, printed paper or painted sheets, one over another, with a PVA or acrylic matte medium, or wheat paste for a flexible, reversible layer.
2. Let each layer dry flat before the next. Different colours and printed text in each layer make the reveal more varied.
3. When dry, tear back by hand, scrape with a palette knife, or sand lightly with fine paper to open windows into the layers beneath.
4. For a poster-wall look, leave some layers only partially stuck so they peel in sheets.
5. Seal with a clear acrylic medium or varnish to stabilise the surface and protect the torn edges.

## Safety and materials
- Sanding paper and old posters produces fine dust; wear a dust mask and sand outdoors or with ventilation, especially if the printed matter might include old inks.
- Wheat paste is cheap and reversible but can mould in damp conditions; PVA is stable and water-resistant once dry. Matching the adhesive to the layer count matters; too much wet paste buckles thin paper.
- Test whether poster inks bleed with the adhesive on a scrap before the main piece.

## Gotchas
- Thick layers on flexible canvas can crack; use a rigid support for heavy builds.
- Over-sanding tears holes through to the support unintentionally; check often.
- Unsealed torn layers pick up dirt and fray.

## Verify
- [ ] Layers adhere well enough that removed areas tear cleanly rather than lifting the whole stack.
- [ ] The final surface is sealed and stable when touched.
