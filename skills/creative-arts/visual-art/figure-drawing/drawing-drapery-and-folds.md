# Drawing drapery and folds

**Goal:** convincing cloth on figures and in still life. Burne Hogarth's
*Dynamic Wrinkles and Drapery* names seven fold types that cover most cases.

## Points of tension
Cloth hangs from or is pulled by **support points** (shoulders, knees, a nail).
Folds radiate from them and run between them. Find these points first.

## The seven folds
| Fold | Where |
|------|-------|
| Pipe | Hanging fabric from one support (curtains, skirt): cylinders |
| Zigzag | Fabric compressed along a tube (bent sleeve or trouser at the knee) |
| Spiral | Fabric wrapped around a cylinder (forearm sleeve) |
| Half-lock | Sharp change of direction (inside of a bent elbow or knee) |
| Diaper | Fabric hanging between two supports (a sagging U shape) |
| Drop | Cloth falling off a support's edge (tablecloth corner) |
| Inert | Fabric lying on the ground, no tension |

## Fabric weight
Thin fabrics (silk, cotton lawn) have many small, sharp folds; heavy ones (wool,
velvet) fewer, bigger, rounder folds. Stiff fabric (canvas, denim) breaks angularly.

## Drawing steps
1. Draw the body form underneath first (cloth reveals and hides it).
2. Mark tension points; draw the major folds as directional lines from them.
3. Simplify: choose a few large folds that describe the form; ignore minor creases.
4. Render each fold as a **cylinder** or **plane change** in light: light top,
   halftone, core shadow, reflected light in the trough, cast shadows.
5. Vary edges: sharp at crisp creases, soft on round folds.

## Practice
Pin a sheet from one nail and draw; then two nails; then on a chair; then on a model.

## Gotchas
- Drawing every wrinkle seen: chaos. Design the folds.
- Folds that do not lead to tension points.
- Fabric not following the body form underneath.

## Verify
- [ ] Folds radiate from identifiable tension points.
- [ ] Fold types fit the situation (pipe hanging, zigzag compressed).
- [ ] Folds rendered as forms with consistent light.
