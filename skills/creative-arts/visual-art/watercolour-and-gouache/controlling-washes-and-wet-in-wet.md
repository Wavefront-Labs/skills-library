# Controlling washes and wet-in-wet

**Goal:** make watercolour do what you intend — even washes, soft blends,
crisp edges and clean whites — by managing the water in the brush versus the
water on the paper. Almost every watercolour problem is a water ratio problem.

## Paper first
- Use **100% cotton** watercolour paper, 300 gsm (140 lb) or heavier, for wet
  techniques; wood-pulp papers dry fast and blotchily.
- **Cold-press (NOT)** for general work; **hot-press** for fine detail and gouache;
  **rough** for texture and granulation.
- Lighter paper buckles when wet: **stretch** it (soak briefly, tape to a board with
  gummed tape, dry flat) or use a block glued on all sides.

## 1. Read the sheen: the five stages of wet paper
1. **Soaking/puddled** — standing water. Paint spreads wildly.
2. **Shiny wet** — glossy all over. Paint diffuses softly: ideal for skies and soft
   backgrounds.
3. **Damp / satin** — sheen fading. Paint spreads a little; soft but controlled edges.
4. **Barely damp / matt** — the danger zone: adding a wetter brush now causes
   **blooms** (backruns, "cauliflowers").
5. **Dry** — hard edges, glazing.
Tilt the paper to the light to see the sheen. Rule: **the brush must be drier than
the paper** when you add paint to a wet area, or water flows back and blooms.

## 2. Flat wash
- Mix **more paint than you think** — running out mid-wash is fatal.
- Tilt the board slightly (10–15°). Load a large brush; paint a stroke across the
  top so a **bead** of liquid forms along the bottom edge.
- Each next stroke picks up and carries the bead down. Don't go back into partly
  dried areas.
- At the bottom, lift the final bead with a squeezed-out (thirsty) brush.

## 3. Graded wash
Same as flat, but add water to the mix (or dip in clean water) before each stroke
so the colour lightens progressively. Or vary pigment instead for a colour-to-colour
gradient.

## 4. Wet-in-wet
Wet the area (clear water or a first colour). At the shiny-to-satin stage, drop in
stronger, less watery paint and let it diffuse. Don't stir it around. Let it dry
untouched; watercolour dries noticeably **lighter** (often by a third or more), so
mix darker than it looks.

## 5. Reserving whites
Watercolour has no white paint in the traditional approach — the paper is the white.
Plan lights before you start:
- Paint around them (most reliable).
- **Masking fluid**: apply with an old brush or a nib, let dry fully, remove only when
  the paint is completely dry. Leaving it on for weeks or in sun can make it hard to
  remove — test on your paper.
- Lifting with a damp brush or tissue works with non-staining pigments, not
  staining ones (phthalos, quinacridones stain).

## 6. Glazing
On fully dry layers, a transparent second colour modifies the first without mixing
physically. Use transparent pigments; work quickly and don't scrub, or the lower
layer lifts. Check each paint's transparency/staining/granulation on the tube or
the maker's chart.

## When to use gouache
Gouache is opaque watercolour: it covers mistakes and allows light-over-dark, flat
graphic colour, and a matte finish. Mix to the consistency of cream; too thick
cracks, too thin goes streaky. It reactivates with water, so layer with light
strokes. Expect colour shift: lights dry darker, darks dry lighter — test swatches.

## Gotchas
- Fiddling: re-working a drying wash is the main cause of muddy, blotchy work.
- Mixing more than two or three pigments makes mud.
- Sizing problems: some papers repel paint at first; a quick wipe of clean water helps.
- Hair dryers speed drying but can blow pools into backruns — hold it far away.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name the paper stage before each wet-in-wet addition.
- [ ] A test flat wash dries with no visible stripes or blooms.
- [ ] Whites were planned and are clean.
- [ ] Mixes were checked on a scrap sheet dried, not wet.
- [ ] Paper stayed flat (stretched or blocked).
