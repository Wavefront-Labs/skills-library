# Additive and subtractive monotype

**Goal:** a unique painterly print made from an unetched plate (Grabowski and
Fick, monotype chapter; the Met's Heilbrunn essay on monotypes and Degas).

## Plate and inks
- Plate: plexiglass (acrylic) or polished metal, edges bevelled. A drawing can be
  placed under clear plexi as a guide.
- Inks: oil-based relief or etching inks with modifiers (easy wipe, transparent
  base), or water-based/Akua inks (stay open long). Oil paints with a little oil
  also work.

## Subtractive (dark-field)
1. Roll a thin, even layer of ink over the whole plate with a brayer.
2. Remove ink to make lights: rags, cotton buds, brushes dipped in solvent or oil,
   fingers, combs, erasers.
3. The more ink removed, the lighter; clean plate = paper white.

## Additive (light-field)
1. Paint the image onto a clean plate with brushes, rollers, stencils.
2. Use thinner ink for transparency, thicker for opacity; keep ink not too thick
   (it squashes).

## Printing
- **Press**: dampened printmaking paper (or dry for some inks), moderate pressure;
  one pass. Etching press preferred.
- **Hand**: dry or slightly damp thin paper, burnish with a baren or spoon (works best
  with thinner ink layers).
- **Ghost print**: a second pull from the remaining ink gives a paler print; it can
  be reworked into a new monotype.
- **Layering**: register paper and plate (see registration skills) to print several
  runs, building colour.

## Gotchas
- Ink too thick: spreads and blobs under pressure.
- Ink drying on the plate (water-based) before printing: work fast or use open inks.
- Expect variation: each print is unique.

## Verify
- [ ] Even ink layer for subtractive; controlled thickness for additive.
- [ ] Print pulled with appropriate pressure; ghost considered.
