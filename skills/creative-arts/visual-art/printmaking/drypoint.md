# Drypoint

Lines are scratched straight into the plate - no ground, no acid. The needle throws up a **burr** of metal (or plastic) beside the groove; the burr holds ink and gives drypoint its velvety, soft line.

## Plates
- **Copper**: best burr, the classic drypoint metal.
- **Zinc**: softer, burr wears fast.
- **Acrylic/Perspex/PETG**: cheap, transparent (trace a drawing beneath), no chemicals.
- **Tetra Pak / drink cartons**: teaching and quick work; can also cut and peel layers for tone.

## Drawing
- Hold the needle (steel or diamond/carbide tip) like a pencil, at an angle. Pulling it raises a burr on one side.
- **More pressure + steeper angle** = deeper groove and larger burr = darker, fuzzier line.
- Tone by hatching and cross-hatching; **roulettes** and sandpaper for textures.
- Mistakes: scrape off the burr and burnish the groove (on metal); on acrylic, small marks can be polished out.

## Inking and printing
- Warm ink rubbed in gently with card or a dabber; wipe with tarlatan **lightly** - aggressive wiping flattens the burr.
- Damp paper, etching press, firm pressure.

## Edition life
- The burr wears down with every pass - often only about 10-20 good prints from copper, fewer from zinc or plastic. Rich early prints differ from later ones.
- **Steel facing** (electroplating a thin layer of iron onto copper) preserves the burr for larger editions.

## Gotchas
- Pressing too lightly makes only a scratch with no burr: prints are pale grey.
- Polishing the plate after drawing removes burr.
- Acrylic burr is weaker; proof sparingly.

## Verify
- [ ] Test lines at several pressures proofed.
- [ ] Wiping gentle enough to keep the burr.
- [ ] Edition size realistic for the plate material.
