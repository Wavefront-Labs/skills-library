# Hand-cut and drawing-fluid screen stencils

## Paper stencils
- Cut the design from thin paper (newsprint, copy paper). The cut-out areas print.
- Lay it on the print paper under the screen, flood and pull - the ink sticks the stencil to the mesh. Good for bold shapes and a few to a few dozen prints.
- Islands (centres of letters like O) fall out; leave bridges or place them separately.

## Cut film stencils
- Water- or lacquer-adhering stencil film (e.g. a knife-cut film): cut through the emulsion layer only, peel away the printing areas, adhere to the mesh with water or adhering fluid, peel the backing. Sharp edges and longer runs than paper.

## Drawing fluid and screen filler
1. Paint or draw the **image** directly on the mesh with **drawing fluid** (a water-soluble resist). It shows brush marks, crayon textures (with litho crayon).
2. Let it dry. Squeegee a thin even coat of **screen filler** (blockout) over the whole screen with a card or squeegee.
3. When dry, wash out the drawing fluid with cold water (scrub gently); it lifts and leaves open mesh where you painted. The filler remains as the stencil.
4. Print. The image is positive: what you painted prints.
- Painterly, autographic marks without a darkroom.

## Direct blockout
- Paint screen filler or blockout directly on the mesh where you **don't** want ink - a negative method; the unpainted areas print.

## Limits
- Hand stencils are softer-edged; filler can crack on long runs.
- Too-thick filler blocks wash-out; too-thin develops pinholes - hold up to light and touch up.

## Gotchas
- Drawing fluid applied too thin washes out partially.
- Water-based filler with water-based ink breaks down over long runs; use a stencil compatible with your ink.

## Verify
- [ ] Stencil checked against light for pinholes.
- [ ] Positive or negative method chosen deliberately.
- [ ] Stencil type suited to ink and edition length.
