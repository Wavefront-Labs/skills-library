# Staging a tableau: from concept to frame

**Goal:** realise an idea as one constructed photograph — the "directorial mode"
associated with artists like Jeff Wall, Cindy Sherman and Gregory Crewdson — with
every element in the frame chosen, at a scale you can afford.

## 1. Develop the idea
- Write the concept in **one sentence** and the *feeling* in three words. If you can't,
  the picture won't know what it's for.
- Avoid the **literal-illustration trap**: a picture that only depicts its caption
  ("loneliness = person alone on a bench") is closed. Aim for an image that raises a
  question, holds ambiguity or has a "moment before/after" quality.
- Gather references (paintings, film stills, other photographs) on a **mood board**:
  palette, light, posture, framing.

## 2. Sketch and scout
- Draw thumbnail **composition sketches** at the final aspect ratio — placement of
  figures, horizon, key props.
- **Scout** locations at the time of day you'll shoot; photograph them from the planned
  camera position with the planned focal length. Note power, access, permission
  needs and ambient light.
- Decide: single exposure or **composite** (several frames combined)? Composites
  let you light areas separately and choose the best expression per person, but
  require a locked-off camera and consistent light direction.

## 3. Set, props and styling
- Every object in the frame is a statement; remove anything that isn't helping.
- Colour-plan: limit the palette (2–3 dominant hues) across wardrobe, props and set.
- Age and wear props so they look lived-in; new things look like a catalogue.
- Build only what the camera sees — check through the lens constantly.

## 4. Lighting plan
- Write a **lighting diagram**: key, fill, practicals (lamps in the scene), background,
  and ambient/sky. Decide where shadows fall and what stays dark.
- Practical lights in frame give motivation; match them with off-camera sources.
- For dusk "cinematic" looks, plan for a short window (blue hour) and pre-light
  so you shoot the instant the sky balance is right.

## 5. Directing sitters
- Give **actions and situations**, not poses ("you've just heard the phone ring in
  the other room"), then refine posture and gaze.
- Small adjustments: weight shift, hands, chin, eye line. Hands and eyes carry most
  of the story.
- Non-professional sitters tire fast — rehearse with stand-ins, then bring them in.
- Get a signed **model/property release** for anyone recognisable and for private
  locations, especially if the work may sell or be published.

## 6. On the day
- **Shoot tethered** to a laptop to check focus, detail and composition at full size.
- Lock the camera on a tripod; mark the position.
- Shoot plates (empty set, each lighting pass) for possible compositing.
- Keep a shot list and tick off variants (expression, gesture, prop positions).

## Gotchas
- Over-production can kill life in the image — leave room for accidents.
- Continuity errors (moved props, changing light) ruin composites; photograph
  reference frames after each change.
- Uncertain: scale of crews in well-known tableau practices varies; plan for your
  real budget.

## Verify
- [ ] Concept in one sentence; image is not a literal illustration of it.
- [ ] Sketch and scouting photos match the final framing.
- [ ] Lighting diagram drawn and executed.
- [ ] Releases signed for all recognisable people and private locations.
- [ ] Tethered review confirms focus and detail at 100%.
