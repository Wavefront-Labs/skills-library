# View camera movements and sheet film workflow

**Goal:** make a deliberate, technically controlled exposure on a 4×5 (or larger)
view camera, using movements to control perspective and plane of focus.

## 1. Set up
1. Tripod level; camera zeroed (all movements at neutral detents).
2. Lens open at maximum aperture, shutter on **T/B or open** (press-focus lever) to
   see the ground glass.
3. **Dark cloth** over head and camera back; use a 4–8× **loupe** on the ground glass
   for critical focus. The image is upside down and reversed — normal.

## 2. Movements and what they do
- **Rise / fall** (front standard up/down): include more top or bottom of the scene
  **without tilting the camera**, so verticals stay parallel. The standard fix for
  converging verticals (keystoning) in architecture.
- **Shift** (side to side): the same idea horizontally — e.g. photographing a mirror
  or window without appearing in it.
- **Tilt** (rotate lens forward/back) and **swing** (rotate around vertical axis):
  move the **plane of focus**. Tilt the lens forward to place focus along the ground
  from near foreground to far horizon.
- **Back (rear) movements:** tilting/swinging the back changes **shape/perspective**
  (and focus); front movements change focus without altering shape.
- **Scheimpflug principle:** the film plane, lens plane and plane of sharp focus meet
  in one line. Practically:
  1. Focus on a far point (with back), 2. tilt the front until a near point sharpens,
  3. refocus far, 4. repeat until both are sharp. Small tilts (a few degrees) are
  usually enough.
- Check **image circle**: excessive movement shows as dark corners (vignetting) —
  look in the corners of the ground glass through the cut corners or by moving your
  eye.

## 3. Exposure
- Meter with a **spot meter**; zone system routine: place the important shadow
  in **Zone III** (meter reading minus two stops), read where highlights fall. If
  highlights land above Zone VIII, plan reduced development (N−1); if they fall low,
  extended (N+1). Mark the holder accordingly.
- **Bellows factor:** at close focus, the lens is extended further than its focal
  length; exposure factor = (bellows extension ÷ focal length)². Example: 150 mm lens
  at 225 mm extension → (1.5)² = 2.25 → about +1 1/6 stops.
- **Reciprocity:** long exposures (often beyond 1 s) need extra exposure per the film's
  datasheet.

## 4. Sheet film handling
- Load holders in **total darkness**: notches at the top-right edge (when held
  vertically) mean emulsion faces you. Slide film under the guides, close the flap,
  insert dark slide with the **white/silver band out** for unexposed (common convention
  — confirm your holders).
- Label holders; dust them before loading.
- **Exposure sequence:** close shutter, set aperture and speed, cock shutter, insert
  holder, pull dark slide, expose, reinsert slide **reversed** (black band out =
  exposed), remove holder.
- Cover the camera back with the dark cloth while the slide is pulled in bright sun.

## Gotchas
- Forgetting to close the shutter before inserting the holder fogs the sheet — most
  common error.
- Wind shakes the bellows; shield the camera, use a cable release.
- Stopping down increases depth of field around the plane of focus — check at taking
  aperture (dim ground glass) where possible.
- Uncertain: holder band conventions and detent designs differ by brand.

## Verify
- [ ] Verticals parallel on the ground glass (or convergence intended).
- [ ] Near and far points sharp at taking aperture with the loupe.
- [ ] Corners free of vignetting after movements.
- [ ] Bellows factor and reciprocity applied; zone notes on holder.
- [ ] Shutter closed before holder insertion; slide reversed after exposure.
