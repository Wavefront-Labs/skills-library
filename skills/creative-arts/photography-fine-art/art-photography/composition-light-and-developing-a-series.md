# Composition, light and developing a series

**Goal:** make images that hold attention as art rather than as records, and build
them into a body of work that says something greater than any single frame.

## 1. Composition: organise the frame
- **Edges first:** check all four edges before pressing the shutter. Cropped limbs,
  stray objects and lines escaping the corners pull the eye out.
- **Figure–ground:** the subject must separate from the background by tone, colour,
  focus or shape. Squint at the scene — what reads?
- **Visual weight:** bright, sharp, warm, high-contrast and human elements are heavy.
  Balance heavy against light, not centre-left vs. centre-right by area.
- **Rule of thirds** is a starting point; central, symmetrical and edge-heavy
  compositions are just as valid when chosen deliberately.
- **Vantage point** changes everything: move your feet, height and distance before
  zooming.
- **Layers:** foreground, middle ground, background each with content creates depth.
- Shore's framing: think about the *flat* image (the photograph as a 2D surface), the
  *depictive* (what it shows) and the *mental* level (how the viewer reads it).

## 2. Light: quality, direction, colour
- **Quality:** hard light (sun, bare bulb) gives crisp shadows and texture; soft light
  (overcast, window, open shade) wraps and flatters. Size of the source *relative to
  the subject* decides it.
- **Direction:** front light flattens; side light reveals form and texture; back light
  gives rim and silhouette.
- **Colour:** golden hour warm, blue hour cool, mixed light (tungsten + daylight)
  creates colour contrast you can use or must correct.
- Spend time watching the same place at different hours; return when the light suits
  the picture.
- Expose for the highlights you care about in digital; for colour negative film,
  expose for shadows.

## 3. From single images to a series
1. **Start from a question or constraint**, not a subject list: a place you can
   return to, a theme, a formal rule (same distance, same light, same format).
2. **Shoot a lot, often.** Return to the same subject many times; early pictures are
   rarely the strongest.
3. **Edit separately from shooting.** Make contact sheets or small prints; pin them on
   a wall. Pick by feeling first, then examine why.
4. Look for **connections**: recurring forms, colours, gestures. These become the
   series' grammar.
5. **Cut hard.** A tight 12-image series beats 40 mixed ones. Remove the "good picture
   that doesn't belong".
6. Write a **working statement** (100–150 words) about what the work is doing; revise
   it as the work changes. It sharpens choices.
7. Show work-in-progress to a trusted critique group; listen for what they *see*, not
   just what they like.

## 4. Personal style
Style is the residue of consistent choices — subject, distance, light, palette,
format — repeated over a long time. Don't chase it directly; notice what you keep
doing and decide whether to commit to it.

## Gotchas
- Gear changes rarely fix weak pictures; seeing does.
- Editing immediately after shooting biases you toward the effort of getting the
  shot. Wait a few days.
- Uncertain: series length and statement word counts are common norms, not rules.
