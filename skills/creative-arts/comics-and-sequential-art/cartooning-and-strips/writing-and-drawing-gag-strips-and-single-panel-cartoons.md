# Writing and drawing gag strips and single-panel cartoons

**Goal:** produce strips and cartoons that land a joke in seconds, drawn simply enough to
read at small size, and on a schedule you can keep up.

## 1. The 3–4 panel gag structure
A daily-style strip is a joke in miniature:
| Panel | Job |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Setup:** who, where, the situation (often one line) |
| 2 | **Development:** the premise sharpens or a complication appears |
| 3 | **Beat/pause** (optional): a silent reaction panel that builds anticipation |
| 4 | **Punch:** the reveal or twist — funniest word last in the balloon, or a visual payoff |
Japanese **yonkoma** (four-panel, vertical) follows a related ki-shō-ten-ketsu shape:
introduction, development, twist, conclusion — the twist comes in panel 3.
- Some of the best strips land the joke in the **art** of the final panel with no words.
- The **beat panel** is the strip's equivalent of a comic's pause; use it when the punch
  needs timing.

## 2. Single-panel gag cartoons
- One image + (often) one caption. The image sets the situation; the caption delivers the twist,
  or the image *is* the twist and the caption is the straight line.
- Write captions short and in a natural voice; read them aloud. Cut every word you can.
- The reader should get the setup at a glance; complicated scenes kill single-panel gags.
- Editorial cartoons use the same economy with a point of view; labels are used sparingly.

## 3. Writing a lot of jokes
- Keep a notebook of premises from life and your characters' personalities.
- Write in batches: 20 rough ideas, keep the best 5.
- Recurring characters with strong traits generate jokes: put the trait in a new situation.
- Rough-draw the joke as stick-figure thumbnails to test timing before final art.

## 4. Simplified style that reads small
- Strips are often seen at a few cm tall or on phones. Test at that size.
- **Silhouette and shape language:** each character recognisable by outline alone (hair
  shape, body shape, a hat).
- Few lines, clear expressions; eyes and mouth do most of the acting.
- Consistent model sheets: turnarounds, expressions and size relations for the cast.
- Simple backgrounds: establish once, then suggest with a line or a prop.

## 5. Format and production
- Draw at a fixed panel size and ratio for consistency; leave room for lettering.
- Newspaper-style dailies are wide horizontal strips; Sunday-style pages have larger layouts
  with a droppable title panel. Web strips are free-form but should fit phone screens
  (consider a stacked/vertical version). *Syndicate/platform specs vary.*
- Lettering by hand or with a font made from your own handwriting keeps the style personal.

## 6. Schedule
- Build a **buffer** (e.g. 2–4 weeks of finished strips) before launching. Life will
  interrupt, and missed updates lose readers.
- Batch tasks: write a week, pencil a week, ink a week.
- Keep an archive with dates and tags for reruns and collections.

## Gotchas
- Too much text in panel 1; the setup should be quick.
- The punchline revealed in panel 2 or 3 (often by a too-eager expression).
- Overdrawing: detail slows reading and the joke.
- Characters without clear personalities — jokes then rely only on wordplay.
- Launching without a buffer.

## Verify
- [ ] Each strip's setup is clear by the end of panel 1.
- [ ] The punch lands in the last panel (word last, or visual).
- [ ] Characters recognisable in silhouette at phone size.
- [ ] Captions cut to the minimum and read aloud.
- [ ] A buffer of finished strips exists before publishing begins.
