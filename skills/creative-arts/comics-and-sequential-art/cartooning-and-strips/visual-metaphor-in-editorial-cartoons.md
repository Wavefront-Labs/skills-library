# Visual metaphor in editorial cartoons

An editorial cartoon makes an argument through one image: the issue is shown *as* something else whose
meaning readers already know.

## Process
1. State the opinion in one sentence ("The new tax burdens renters, not landlords").
2. List the actors and the relationship (who does what to whom).
3. Brainstorm analogies for that relationship: sinking ship, house of cards, elephant in the room,
   emperor's new clothes, sword of Damocles, a leash, a lifeboat, a game of chess. Aim for 15-20.
4. Pick the analogy whose structure matches the argument, not just the mood.
5. Twist it: the surprise is what makes it a cartoon (the lifeboat is full of landlords; the
   renters are rowing).
6. Assign roles so each actor is identifiable (caricature, label, costume, symbol).

## Stock symbols readers decode instantly
- Uncle Sam, Lady Liberty, Britannia, donkey/elephant (US parties), the Capitol dome, scales of justice,
  the grim reaper, a ticking bomb, a melting clock, an ostrich with head in sand, a dove/hawk.
- Use them as a starting point; a fresh image beats a cliche, but a cliche with a twist beats a puzzle.

## Composition
- One focal point; the metaphor should occupy most of the frame.
- Scale encodes power (big vs tiny figures).
- Keep the background minimal so the symbol reads at newspaper or phone size.

## Gotchas
- Mixed metaphors (a sinking ship that's also on fire in a casino) confuse.
- A metaphor that needs three labels is the wrong metaphor.
- Historical or religious imagery (e.g. Holocaust, crucifixion) invites backlash that drowns the point;
  use only if the argument genuinely requires it.

## Verify
- [ ] Cover the caption and labels: can a reader state your opinion?
- [ ] Does the twist land in under five seconds?
