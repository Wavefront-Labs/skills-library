# Labelling conventions in editorial cartoons

Labels (a word written on a figure, object or sack: "CONGRESS", "INFLATION", "BIG TECH") identify what
a symbol stands for. They are the oldest editorial convention and the most overused.

## When a label earns its place
- Abstract concepts with no agreed image ("THE DEBT", "MISINFORMATION").
- Institutions that are not people (a bill, an agency, a company).
- Disambiguating a generic figure (a man in a suit = "VOTERS"? "LOBBYISTS"?).

## When to drop it
- The figure is a recognisable caricature: never label a well-known politician.
- The symbol already reads (scales = justice, a donkey = Democrats in US papers).
- More than about three labels in one cartoon: the metaphor is not working, redesign it.

## Placement
- On the object itself: sash, sack, briefcase, hull of a ship, a tag tied with string, a sign the figure
  holds, the label on a bottle. It should look like part of the drawing.
- Avoid floating words with arrows except for deliberate humour.
- Hand-lettered, all caps, sized to read at print/phone reduction; follow the surface curvature.

## Captions vs labels
- Caption below the panel or a speech balloon carries the punchline; labels only identify.
- Don't put the joke in a label.

## Gotchas
- Labelling an ethnic or religious group as a stand-in for a policy problem is the commonest source of
  complaints; label the policy or institution.
- Labels in another language need translation in syndication; keep them few.

## Verify
- [ ] Remove each label in turn: if the cartoon still reads, delete it.
- [ ] Labels legible at 50% reduction.
