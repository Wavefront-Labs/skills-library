# Stabilisation stitching onto a support fabric

**Goal:** give a fragile textile (split silk, worn tapestry, flag, costume panel) a
sound backing and secure its weak areas with fine, widely spaced stitches, so it can be
handled and displayed without further loss — and so every stitch can be cut out later.

> **Status: draft.** This describes the standard support-and-couching approach; stitch
> spacing, thread and fabric choices are typical values and must be adjusted to the
> object. Adhesive supports are a different method not covered here.

## Decide whether to stitch at all
Stitching adds holes. It suits textiles that still have enough strength to hold a stitch
(most wool, linen, cotton, sound silk). **Shattered or heavily weighted silks** that
crumble around a needle may need an overlay (crepeline over the top, stitched around the
area) or an adhesive method instead. Test on an edge or turning first.

## Materials
- **Support fabric** — similar fibre and weight to the original or lighter: cotton lawn
  or linen for linens/wools, silk habotai or **silk crepeline** for silks; nylon net for
  overlays. Wash it first (to remove sizing and pre-shrink), then **dye to a neutral
  tone just a shade darker** than the original so losses recede rather than glow.
- **Thread** — fine polyester (strong, stable) or silk/hair silk (closer match, weaker),
  dyed to match each local colour. Use a single strand.
- **Needles** — fine straight needles for flat work on a frame, fine **curved needles**
  for work you can't reach from behind (mounted or 3D objects).
- A slate frame or padded table, pins (stainless/fine entomology pins), magnification,
  good diffuse light.

## Method
1. **Clean and humidify first** if needed, so the textile goes onto the support in a
   relaxed, aligned state. Stitching in a distortion locks it in.
2. **Align grain.** Mount the support on the frame with warp and weft square. Lay the
   textile on it, matching **warp to warp, weft to weft**; ease (don't stretch) the
   original into alignment and pin through weak areas only with very fine pins.
3. **Anchor lines.** Sew lines of long running stitches in a grid (e.g. every 5–15 cm,
   following warp or weft) to fix the object to the support overall.
4. **Laid couching over weak areas:** lay a long thread across the damaged area in the
   direction of the original yarns (usually weft for splits along the warp), then hold it
   down with tiny perpendicular **couching stitches** every few mm, staggered so the holes
   don't line up into a new tear line. Rows sit about 2–5 mm apart, tighter where weaker.
5. **Losses:** the support shows through; edges of the loss are secured with couching
   around the perimeter so they don't fray back.
6. **Tension check:** the original should lie flat with no puckers or tight stitches. A
   stitch that dimples the surface is too tight; redo it.

## Gotchas
- **Never stitch through sound original unnecessarily** — keep stitches in damaged areas
  and along the grid.
- **Thread stronger than the fibre cuts it** over time; thin, soft thread is the safer error.
- Stitches running in straight lines through brittle silk make a perforation that splits.
  Stagger.
- Pins left in during long sessions rust or mark; use stainless and remove progressively.
- Hanging textiles need the support to carry the weight (Velcro tape sewn to a webbing on the
  support, not to the object).

## Safety
Stitching is low-risk. If washing or solvent spot-cleaning precedes it, follow the
detergent/solvent SDS, work with extraction for solvents, and remember old textiles may
carry historic **pesticide residues** (arsenic, mercury, naphthalene) — gloves and a dust
mask when handling uncleaned museum textiles of unknown history.

## Verify
- [ ] Stitching judged appropriate (fibre holds a test stitch); overlay considered otherwise.
- [ ] Support washed, dyed slightly darker, grain square on the frame.
- [ ] Original aligned warp-to-warp, relaxed, no distortion locked in.
- [ ] Couching staggered, follows original yarn direction, no puckers.
- [ ] Threads, fabrics, stitch spacing and areas recorded in the treatment report.
