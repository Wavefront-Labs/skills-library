# Recognising weighted silk

**Goal:** know when silk is weighted because it deteriorates differently and handling and storage must change.

## Background
In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, silk was commonly weighted with metal salts (often tin, sometimes iron, lead and other compounds) to add body and weight. Weighted silk can become brittle and split, called shattering, especially along folds, in areas exposed to light and perspiration, and along stress points.

## Clues
- Date of manufacture, roughly 1870 to 1930 and later; black silk was often heavily weighted.
- Cracking and splitting in lines, loss of strength, fine powdery debris, hard handle.
- Garment areas under strain (shoulders, underarms) failing first.

## Confirmation
1. **X-ray fluorescence (XRF):** a portable XRF instrument can detect tin or other metals on silk non-destructively; interpret with the fibre and dye in mind.
2. **Burn test (destructive):** with consent, a very small thread from a hidden area is held in a flame; unweighted silk burns to a crumbly ash, while weighted silk can leave an ash that keeps the shape of the thread. Use with care and only when XRF is unavailable.

## Consequences
- Treat as fragile: support with a fabric backing (see `stabilisation-stitching-onto-a-support-fabric`), pad storage, avoid folding on the same lines, limit light.
- Avoid wet cleaning and avoid hanging garments from the shoulders.
