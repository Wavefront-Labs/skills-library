# Identifying textile fibres and weave structure

**Goal:** describe what a textile is made of and how it is constructed, as a basis for treatment and storage choices.

## Fibres by microscopy
1. Take the smallest sample possible, with owner consent, from an edge or hidden area; a few millimetres of loose thread is often enough.
2. Mount in water or glycerine on a slide; examine at about 100 to 400x with transmitted and, if available, polarised light.
3. Typical features: cotton, a flat twisted ribbon; flax (linen), straight with cross-markings (nodes); wool, overlapping surface scales; silk, smooth and structureless rods without scales. Man-made fibres (viscose, acetate, nylon, polyester) have distinctive cross-sections and surfaces; compare with reference slides or a reference atlas.
4. Fibres may be degraded or dyed; confirm with a second feature or with a specialist.
Burn tests are destructive and only a last resort.

## Weave and construction
1. Use a pick glass (linen tester) or a stereomicroscope; identify warp (threads running along the loom length, often the finer, more tightly twisted set) and weft; selvedges help.
2. Name the weave: plain (tabby), twill, satin, basket, pile (velvet), tapestry weave, brocade or lampas.
3. Count warp and weft threads per centimetre in several places.
4. Note yarn twist direction (S or Z) and ply; record finishing such as napping or calendering.
5. Draw a weave diagram on point paper; photograph with scale.

## Verify
- [ ] Sample size minimal and recorded; owner consented.
- [ ] Fibre identification supported by two features.
- [ ] Weave, count and twist recorded with scale images.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
