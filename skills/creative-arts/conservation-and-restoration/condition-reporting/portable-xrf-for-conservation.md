# Portable XRF for conservation

**Goal:** identify elements present in a surface, without sampling.

## What it does and does not do
- X-ray fluorescence detects chemical elements (from about magnesium or heavier, depending on instrument and air path), not compounds or molecular structure.
- It samples a shallow depth of the surface and underlying layers contribute, so results mix layers and substrates.
- It is good for pigments with heavy elements (lead, mercury, cadmium, iron, copper, cobalt), metals alloys and weighted silk (tin).

## Procedure
1. Check licensing and training; follow radiation safety rules (see below).
2. Choose the mode, voltage and filter recommended for the material.
3. Place the instrument at the recommended distance (usually close and fixed) using a stable stand; mark the spot on a diagram.
4. Measure for tens of seconds to minutes; repeat on several spots.
5. Check with standards; record settings and spectra.
6. Interpret with art history and microscopy; confirm important identifications with other methods.

## Safety
The instrument emits X-rays; only trained, authorised operators use it, never pointing it at people; follow the instrument's interlocks and local regulations.

- [ ] Spots mapped and settings saved.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
