# Stereomicroscopy and paint cross-sections

**Goal:** understand structure and materials with the smallest possible loss of original.

## Stereomicroscope examination
1. Use a stereomicroscope with magnification (about 5 to 50x), good lighting (raking, fibre-optic, UV) and a camera.
2. Examine losses, cracks, retouching, varnish layers and tool marks; record with photographs and location marks.
3. Decide on sampling only after examination, to place it where it gives the most information with the least damage.

## Sampling
1. Obtain owner consent and record why the sample is needed.
2. Under the microscope, take a very small sample (typically well under a millimetre) from the edge of an existing loss using a scalpel, sampling all layers.
3. Label, record the location on a diagram and photograph.

## Cross-section preparation
1. Embed the sample in a clear resin (for example polyester or acrylic resin, or a light-curing resin) in a mould.
2. Cut and polish the block with progressively finer abrasives until the layers are exposed.
3. Examine in reflected visible and UV light; photograph with scale.
4. Interpret layer order, thickness, and pigments with analysis (see `portable-xrf-for-conservation`, `ftir-spectroscopy-for-conservation`).

## Safety
Resins, solvents and polishing dust require ventilation and gloves.

## Verify
- [ ] Consent and sample location recorded.
- [ ] Layers visible and photographed with scale.
- [ ] Remaining sample and block stored and labelled.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
