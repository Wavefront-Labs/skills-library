# X-radiography of objects and paintings

**Goal:** see structure and materials inside or beneath the surface without sampling.

## What it shows
- Paintings: canvas weave, tacks, panel joins, cracks, losses, and underlying compositions; lead-containing paint absorbs X-rays strongly and appears light.
- Objects: internal structure, pins, repairs, casting cores, and metal under corrosion (for example iron and bronze).

## Procedure (overview)
1. Plan the view: the object may be imaged in sections, with tiling.
2. Choose the detector (film, computed or digital plates) and the energy and exposure; lower voltages are used for thin, low-density items and higher for dense metals. Settings depend on the material and equipment.
3. Place markers for scale and orientation; protect the object.
4. Expose within a shielded room or cabinet; develop or process the image.
5. Stitch and interpret with knowledge of materials.

## Safety
X-rays are ionising radiation. Only trained operators in licensed facilities may use equipment, following national ionising radiation regulations, shielding, exclusion zones and dosimetry (as low as reasonably achievable).

## Interpretation notes
- Overlapping layers confuse images; compare with other techniques.
- A dense area may be lead white, repairs or paint of other heavy elements; do not assume.

## Verify
- [ ] Facility licensed and operators trained.
- [ ] Scale and orientation recorded.
- [ ] Images stored with metadata.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
