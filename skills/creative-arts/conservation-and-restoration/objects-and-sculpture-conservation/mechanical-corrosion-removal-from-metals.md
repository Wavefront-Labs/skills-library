# Mechanical corrosion removal from metals

**Goal:** expose the surface chosen for display or stability without scratching or removing original material.

## Decide the target surface
- Use X-radiography, analysis and examination to decide how much of the corrosion layer to retain; stable patina is part of the object.
- Record the work in photographs before, during and after.

## Tools and method
1. Work under a stereomicroscope with good light.
2. **Scalpel and picks:** lift crusts in small pieces, moving the blade away from the original surface.
3. **Glass bristle or fine brushes:** for powdery material.
4. **Air abrasion (micro-blasting):** fine powders at low pressure, directed in small bursts; test on a hidden area; use dust extraction.
5. **Ultrasonic or needle scaler:** carefully, for hard crusts; control energy.
6. Remove all dust; do not leave abrasive residue in pits.
7. Where active corrosion is exposed, plan the inhibition steps (see `treating-bronze-disease-and-benzotriazole-inhibition`).

## Safety
- Metal dust (lead, copper, arsenic and others) is hazardous: use extraction, a fitted respirator suited to the dust, gloves and eye protection; clean the area with a HEPA vacuum, not a broom.
- Hearing protection with air tools.

## Failure modes
- Scratching the surface, or abrading beyond the chosen layer.
- Removing evidence of manufacture (tool marks, inlays).

## Verify
- [ ] Target surface agreed and documented.
- [ ] No tool marks beyond what is necessary.
- [ ] Waste and dust handled safely.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
