# Silver tarnish removal and tarnish-inhibiting storage

**Goal:** remove tarnish safely when justified and slow its return with the right storage.

## Tarnish
Silver tarnish is silver sulphide formed from reduced sulphur gases and compounds in air and some materials such as wool, rubber and certain papers and felts. Heavy tarnish can obscure detail.

## Removal
1. Decide whether to clean: some tarnish is part of the object history; lacquered, plated, gilded or niello pieces need special care.
2. Use a slurry of precipitated calcium carbonate (chalk) in water or ethanol, applied with a cotton swab, working lightly and rinsing; no pressure on thin silver or plated surfaces.
3. Remove all chalk residue (cotton swabs, soft brush), and dry.
4. Avoid commercial dips (they dissolve and strip the surface, and can roughen it), and avoid the aluminium foil and bicarbonate method for historic pieces.

## Storage
- Store clean, dry silver in tarnish-inhibiting materials: silver cloth, activated charcoal cloth, or specialised polymer films that react with sulphur gases (Intercept is one such product); test materials or follow supplier data.
- Avoid wool, felts, rubber bands, and untested paper, wood and paint in contact or in cases (see `oddy-test-for-display-materials`).
- Keep RH moderate and cases clean.

## Verify
- [ ] Surface features intact and no abrasive left.
- [ ] Storage materials tested or documented.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
