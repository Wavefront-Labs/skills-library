# Desalinating ceramics

**Goal:** reduce soluble salts that crystallise in the body and flake or spall surfaces.

## When to do it
- Marine or burial ceramics with efflorescence, powdering surfaces or salt crusts.
- Only on pieces strong enough to be immersed: unfired or low-fired and fragile ceramics, painted, gilded or poorly bonded pieces may disintegrate or lose decoration, and need other methods (poultice, local).

## Method
1. Examine and test the pigment, glaze and previous repairs for water stability.
2. Use deionised water in a clean bath, with the object supported by a net or inert tray; if the object is porous, immerse slowly.
3. Measure conductivity (microsiemens per cm) at start and after each bath.
4. Change the bath at intervals (every few days or as the conductivity rises); chloride test strips (such as Quantab) or silver nitrate tests detect chloride.
5. Continue until conductivity stays near the deionised baseline.
6. Dry slowly on blotters in a stable environment to avoid redepositing salts at the surface.

## Cautions
- Old repairs may release in water.
- Sulphates and nitrates are not detected by chloride tests; consider additional analysis.
- Rapid drying leaves surface salts.

## Verify
- [ ] Conductivity at baseline and recorded.
- [ ] Surface and decoration stable after drying.
- [ ] Number of baths and timing recorded.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
