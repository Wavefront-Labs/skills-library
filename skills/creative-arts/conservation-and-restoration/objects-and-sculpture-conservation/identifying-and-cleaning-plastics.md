# Identifying and cleaning plastics

**Goal:** know what the plastic is, since each type deteriorates and responds to cleaning differently.

## Identification
- Look for manufacturer marks, recycling codes, patents, dates and design history.
- Observe condition: stickiness, crazing, discolouration, odour, warping.
- Simple tests (density, solubility) can mislead; flame and chemical tests damage objects.
- Instrumental identification: FTIR (see `ftir-spectroscopy-for-conservation`) or Raman is the reliable route. Decision tools from projects such as POPART guide identification.

## Cleaning
1. Dust with a soft brush and low suction through a screen.
2. If the plastic is stable, clean with a mild non-ionic detergent solution on a swab after a spot test, rinsing with deionised water.
3. Avoid solvents unless tested, because many plastics swell, craze or dissolve; plasticised PVC loses plasticiser; polyurethane foams crumble.
4. Do not polish with abrasives; do not use alcohol on cracking acrylic.
5. Dry and re-house in inert enclosures, away from other plastics that may exchange plasticiser.

## Cautions
- Deteriorating plastics may release acids or be unstable (see `degrading-plastics-and-rubber`).
- Some modern plastics are art; consult the artist if possible.

## Verify
- [ ] Type identified with method recorded.
- [ ] Spot test negative before cleaning.
- [ ] Storage separated by type.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
