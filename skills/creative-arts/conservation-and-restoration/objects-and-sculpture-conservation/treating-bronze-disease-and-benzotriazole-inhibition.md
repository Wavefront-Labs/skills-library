# Treating bronze disease and benzotriazole inhibition

**Goal:** identify active corrosion and stop or slow it with the least damage to the original surface.

## Recognition
Bronze disease is cyclic corrosion of copper alloys driven by chloride, shown by powdery, light green or pale green pustules, often over small pits, which return after cleaning. It starts from cuprous chloride (nantokite) in the corrosion, which reacts with moisture and oxygen to form copper trihydroxychlorides. Dark green stable patina (for example malachite) is not the same and should usually be kept.

## Steps
1. Examine under magnification; map the active spots; decide what surface is original, using X-radiography where useful.
2. Control the environment first: dry storage. Many sources advise keeping relative humidity below about 40 percent, and lower for active cases; monitor (see `data-logger-placement-and-calibration`).
3. Remove active corrosion locally and mechanically (see `mechanical-corrosion-removal-from-metals`).
4. Inhibit: benzotriazole (BTA) forms a protective film with copper; it is applied as a solution in ethanol or water, sometimes under vacuum, and is rinsed and dried. Concentrations and times are given in the literature (low single-digit percent); follow a recognised protocol.
5. Local alternative: silver oxide paste applied to active spots has been used where BTA is not wanted.
6. Seal with a coating or wax if appropriate (see `lacquers-and-waxes-for-metal-objects`).

## Safety and ethics
- BTA is toxic and is treated as a suspected carcinogen in some assessments; use gloves, fume extraction, and dispose of waste per regulations.
- BTA can interfere with later analysis and is not reversible; the decision must be documented and agreed.

## Verify
- [ ] Storage dry and monitored.
- [ ] Active spots cleared and checked at intervals.
- [ ] Inhibitor, concentration and time recorded.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
