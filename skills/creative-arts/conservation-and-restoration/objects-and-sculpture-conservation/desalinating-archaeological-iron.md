# Desalinating archaeological iron

**Goal:** reduce chloride in corroding iron so it stays stable, preserving the surface under the corrosion.

## Problem
Iron from burial often holds chloride in corrosion crusts. Chloride supports formation of akaganeite, a corrosion product that can crack objects and give orange droplets ("weeping"), so objects keep corroding after excavation.

## Steps
1. Keep finds dry or in controlled conditions on excavation; record any weeping.
2. X-radiograph before cleaning to see the original shape and any inlay or decoration under the crust (see `x-radiography-of-objects-and-paintings`).
3. Clean mechanically to the surface chosen using the X-ray.
4. Wash in deoxygenated alkaline solutions, such as dilute sodium hydroxide or an alkaline sulphite solution, in sealed containers; change the solution periodically and measure chloride in the washing (ion chromatography or a chloride test) until release falls to a low plateau.
5. Rinse, dry, and apply a coating if agreed (see `lacquers-and-waxes-for-metal-objects`).
6. Store in a very dry environment with desiccant and an indicator; monitor.

## Cautions
- Sodium hydroxide and sulphite solutions are corrosive and irritant; follow safety data sheets and local waste rules.
- Treatment can take weeks to months; do not shorten it.
- Large collections are better handled by a conservator and a lab.

## Verify
- [ ] X-ray image on file.
- [ ] Chloride levels monitored to a plateau.
- [ ] Final storage RH recorded and low.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
