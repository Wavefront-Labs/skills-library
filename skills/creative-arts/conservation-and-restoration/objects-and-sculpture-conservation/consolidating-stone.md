# Consolidating stone

**Goal:** restore cohesion to powdering or friable stone without sealing the surface.

## Choosing
- **Ethyl silicate (tetraethoxysilane) consolidants:** react with moisture to form silica gel in the pores; they work best on siliceous stones such as many sandstones and less well on carbonate stone unless modified.
- **Lime-based (nanolime and limewater):** compatible with limestone and lime mortars; may need repeated applications.
- **Acrylic consolidants:** sometimes used; hard to remove and can seal the surface.
Decisions come from trials on the specific stone.

## Procedure
1. Diagnose the cause of decay (salts, water, frost, pollution); address water first.
2. Clean the surface and make sure the stone is dry; ethyl silicate needs a dry, workable moisture level.
3. Test on trial areas or similar stone: absorption, depth of penetration, colour change, water vapour permeability.
4. Apply by brush, spray, poultice or flow, until refusal as defined by the trial; do not overload.
5. Allow curing (ethyl silicate needs days to weeks); protect from rain.
6. Evaluate by scratch, drill resistance or absorption tests.

## Cautions
- Consolidation is effectively irreversible.
- Poorly chosen consolidants can form a hard shell and cause scaling.
- Solvents and reagents need ventilation.

## Verify
- [ ] Trial area performed acceptably.
- [ ] Treated stone not harder at the surface than the interior.
- [ ] Product, batch and application recorded.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
