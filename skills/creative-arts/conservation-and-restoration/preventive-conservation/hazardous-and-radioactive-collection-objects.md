# Hazardous and radioactive collection objects

**Goal:** protect staff, visitors and collections from materials that can harm.

## Common hazards
- Pesticide residues (arsenic, mercury, DDT and others; see `pesticide-contaminated-objects`).
- Lead (paints, glazes, toys, pigments), arsenic pigments (for example Paris and Scheele's green), mercury (instruments, gilding), asbestos (insulation, some old materials), formaldehyde and alcohol in preserved specimens.
- Cellulose nitrate (fire hazard), firearms and ammunition (explosive hazard).
- Radioactive materials: uranium glass and glazes, radium luminous paint on dials, some minerals and scientific instruments.

## Assessment
1. Review records and inspect; use informed suspicion by object type and date.
2. Where radioactivity is suspected, use a calibrated radiation survey meter (such as a Geiger-Muller counter) operated by a trained person, and compare with background.
3. Take advice from a health and safety officer and, for radiation, a radiation protection adviser; follow national rules (for example the Ionising Radiations Regulations in the UK, or Nuclear Regulatory Commission rules in the US).

## Controls
- Limit handling time and increase distance for radioactive items; use shielding where advised; never ingest, never open sealed sources.
- Use ventilation, gloves and appropriate respiratory protection (see `respirator-selection`), wipe cleaning and HEPA vacuums for dust.
- Label and segregate; control access; record the hazard in the object file.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
