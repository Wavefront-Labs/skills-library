# Cold storage for collections

**Goal:** slow chemical decay of unstable materials, such as acetate and nitrate film and some photographs, and manage access safely.

## Principle
Chemical decay roughly halves with each drop of several degrees. The Image Permanence Institute relates temperature and humidity to the expected life of materials, using its Preservation Index; check IPI guidance for values.

## Options
- Cool or cold rooms, commercial freezers, or domestic freezers with moisture-barrier packaging.
- Use cold storage with controlled RH where possible; RH extremes cause other damage.

## Method
1. Select material and decide whether cold storage is the right response (see `detecting-and-managing-vinegar-syndrome`).
2. Package in sealed moisture-barrier bags with the air pressed out and labels outside; group items sensibly.
3. Place in the cold space; avoid direct contact with ice or cooling surfaces; log temperature.
4. Retrieval: keep the bag sealed and let it warm in stages until its temperature is above the room dew point (see `analysing-climate-data-and-dew-point`); only then open.
5. Keep an inventory and a retrieval record.

## Cautions
- Condensation on cold items causes damage; never open cold packages in a warm humid room.
- Power failure plans: alarms, backup, and access.
- Cellulose nitrate has fire safety rules; follow local regulation.

## Verify
- [ ] Packaging sealed and labelled.
- [ ] Warm-up procedure used before opening.
- [ ] Storage temperature logged and alarmed.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
