# UV filtering for light sources and glazing

**Goal:** minimise the most damaging part of the spectrum for light-sensitive objects.

## Sources
- Daylight and some fluorescent lamps emit significant UV; tungsten halogen emits some; LED lamps designed for display emit very little UV, but check the specification.

## Methods
1. Fit UV-absorbing films to windows, or laminated or UV-filtering glass; acrylic with UV absorber is used for glazing.
2. Use filters on lamps where needed.
3. Choose lamps with low UV output.
4. Add blinds and shades to control daylight and visible light.

## Measuring
- Use a UV meter to measure microwatts per lumen. A traditional limit of 75 microwatts per lumen exists in guidance (for example CIE and museum standards), but many institutions work lower; follow your policy.
- Measure at the object position, record, and repeat annually, because filters age.

## Cautions
- UV filtering does not remove visible light damage; combine with light levels and a light budget (see `light-budgets-and-environmental-monitoring`).
- Films peel, yellow and lose absorption; replace as specified.
- Acrylic glazing can attract static; avoid on pastel and similar.

## Verify
- [ ] UV readings recorded at objects.
- [ ] Filter ages and replacement dates logged.
- [ ] Visible-light levels also controlled.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
