# Oddy test for display materials

**Goal:** avoid materials that emit corrosive gases into cases and stores.

## The test
The Oddy test, developed by Andrew Oddy at the British Museum and later modified (Green and Thickett), checks for volatile emissions that corrode metals.
1. Place a small sample of the material in a sealed glass tube or vessel with polished coupons of silver, copper and lead.
2. Add a small vial of water for humidity, and run a control without sample.
3. Heat at about 60 C for 28 days.
4. Compare the coupons with the control, by eye, with a standard scheme.
5. Rate: P permanent use (no or negligible corrosion), T temporary use (limited corrosion, commonly up to about six months), U unsuitable.

## Reading results
- Use published Oddy results carefully: formulations change, so a result applies to that specific product and batch; look for dates.
- CAMEO and other sources list tested materials.

## Selecting materials
- Generally accepted: inert plastics (polyethylene, polypropylene, polyester film, acrylic), glass, aluminium, stainless steel, cotton (washed, undyed), acid-free paper and board, and tested sealants.
- Usually avoid: untested wood and wood-based boards, wool, felts, PVC, rubber, many paints and adhesives, moisture-cure silicone sealants, and fabrics with sulphur dyes.
- Seal wood, if unavoidable, with a barrier film and ventilate.

## Practice
Keep a log of materials used in each case and storage area with their Oddy results, supplier and batch.

- [ ] Each material has a test result or is known inert.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
