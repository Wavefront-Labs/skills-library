# Iron gall ink corrosion management

**Goal:** identify ink that is damaging the paper and choose a treatment or management plan that matches risk and value.

## The problem
Iron gall ink (tannin plus iron salts) can corrode paper over time: acid and free iron(II) ions catalyse oxidation of cellulose, leading to haloing, browning, cracking and eventual perforation of the writing lines.

## Assessment
1. Examine for symptoms: brown halos, ink showing through the back, cracked lines, loss of text.
2. Test for free iron(II) with bathophenanthroline indicator paper: moisten the strip, press to the ink; a pink-red colour indicates iron(II). Use a minimal moisture and the test results are indicative only.
3. Record the extent on a map; decide whether treatment is necessary.

## Management options
- **Environmental control:** stable moderate RH reduces further degradation; high RH accelerates corrosion. Avoid pollutants.
- **Housing:** good enclosures, interleaving with buffered tissue in bound volumes, and good handling.
- **Calcium phytate treatment:** a method developed by Neevel and Reissland at the Netherlands Institute for Cultural Heritage (now the Cultural Heritage Agency of the Netherlands): a calcium phytate bath binds free iron, followed by a calcium hydrogen carbonate bath to neutralise acid. Concentrations, times and sequence are published on the Ink Corrosion Website and should be followed exactly; do not improvise.
- **Do not** treat with aggressive bleaches or strong reducing agents.

## Cautions
- Aqueous treatments require water-stability of media; many accompanying inks or colours may not be.
- Treatment is an irreversible chemical change; document before and after.

## Verify
- [ ] Iron(II) test recorded.
- [ ] Treatment follows a published protocol exactly.
- [ ] Environmental conditions recorded.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
