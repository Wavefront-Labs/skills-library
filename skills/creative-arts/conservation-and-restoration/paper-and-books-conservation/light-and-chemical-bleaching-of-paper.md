# Light and chemical bleaching of paper

**Goal:** judge whether bleaching is warranted and, if so, apply it with maximum control. Bleaching is irreversible and can weaken paper.

## When it might be considered
- Severe discolouration that obscures content, where washing or milder methods have failed or are impossible.
- Never for cosmetic reasons alone or without the owner's informed consent. See the AIC Code of Ethics on minimal intervention and the ECCO and Icon guidance.

## Light bleaching
- Paper is immersed in water, usually alkaline-adjusted, and exposed to strong light. It can reduce some yellowing and stains but may also cause photochemical damage to cellulose, and it is hard to control.
- Not suited to light-sensitive media.

## Sodium borohydride
- A reducing agent that converts coloured carbonyl groups in degraded cellulose to colourless groups; used in dilute buffered solution, typically mildly alkaline.
- Reacts with water and acid and releases hydrogen gas, which is flammable; use in a ventilated space with no ignition sources.
- Excess reagent must be fully neutralised and rinsed out; do not leave residues in the paper.
- Different papers and inks react differently; always test.

## Other agents
Oxidative bleaches (hydrogen peroxide solutions, chloramine-T) have been used but can leave residues and cause damage; many conservators avoid them.

## Safety
- Wear gloves, eye protection and a lab coat; work in a fume cupboard with a procedure for spills; follow local chemical-hygiene and waste rules.
- Sodium borohydride is toxic and water-reactive; check the safety data sheet before purchase.

- [ ] Alternatives recorded as tried or rejected.
- [ ] Informed consent from owner.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
