# Dry cleaning paper with sponges and erasers

**Goal:** lift loose surface soil without abrading the paper or smearing dirt into it.

## When it is and is not suitable
- Suitable: sound, stable paper with a plain surface and stable media.
- Not suitable: friable media (pastel, charcoal, soft graphite; see `testing-and-protecting-friable-media`), flaking paint, weak or brittle paper, heavily textured or laid surfaces that trap crumbs, and anything where soil is greasy (sponges only spread it).

## Tools
- Soft, clean brush (goat hair or similar) for first removal of dust.
- Vulcanised rubber (smoke or "chemical") sponge; cut a fresh face often as the working face fills with dirt. Test an inconspicuous area first because some products leave residue or can burnish.
- White vinyl or plastic eraser, grated or cut to crumbs. Avoid coloured, scented or abrasive erasers.
- A clean work surface with a smooth support; magnification and raking light to watch the result.

## Procedure
1. Brush off loose dust, moving away from the sheet edges and toward a waste area.
2. Test a small area with the chosen sponge or crumbs; stop if the surface shines, fibres lift, or media transfers.
3. With the sponge, use light, even strokes in one direction, lifting after each stroke; do not scrub in circles.
4. With eraser crumbs, roll them gently on the paper using a fingertip or soft brush; do not press.
5. Brush off all crumbs and sponge debris completely. Crumbs left in the fibres can later stain or attract pests.
6. Record the method and what the paper tolerated.

## Failure modes
- Residue from low-grade sponges leaving smears; stop and change product.
- Burnishing or thinning of the sheet; check in raking light.
- Embedded dirt in rough paper; accept some soiling rather than cause damage.

## Verify
- [ ] Test area unchanged in raking light.
- [ ] No crumbs left in folds or fibres.
- [ ] Method and tools recorded.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention.
