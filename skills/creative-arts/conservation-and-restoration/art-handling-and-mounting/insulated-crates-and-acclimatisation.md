# Insulated crates and acclimatisation

**Goal:** limit temperature and humidity change in transit, and avoid damage on arrival.

## Insulated crates
1. Line the crate walls with a thermal insulation such as extruded polystyrene or polyurethane board with a sealed inner skin (tested materials; see `oddy-test-for-display-materials`).
2. Seal joints and use gaskets so air exchange is low.
3. For humidity-sensitive items, add a moisture buffer such as conditioned silica gel or buffering board, and wrap the object in a barrier film where appropriate.
4. Fit loggers to monitor inside the crate during transit (temperature and RH).
5. Remember that insulation slows change but does not stop it, and can slow equalisation on arrival.

## Acclimatisation
1. On arrival, move the crate unopened into the destination room, away from vents and sunlight.
2. Leave it until the contents have reached room temperature: many guidelines say at least about 24 hours, and longer for large or heavily insulated crates; measure with a probe thermometer or logger rather than guess.
3. The risk is condensation: when a cold object meets warm humid air, moisture forms on the object if its temperature is below the dew point.
4. Open, remove packing in the planned order, and do a condition check against the departure report.
5. Keep the packing materials for the return trip.

## Verify
- [ ] Logger data reviewed.
- [ ] Acclimatisation time recorded and temperature verified.
- [ ] Condition check completed after unpacking.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
