# Making metal mounts for museum objects

**Goal:** support an object safely, discreetly and removably.

## Design
1. Study the object's weight, centre of gravity (see `centre-of-gravity-and-load-calculation`), fragile areas and strong support points.
2. Choose materials: brass or stainless steel rod and plate are common; avoid metals that corrode against the object (see `oddy-test-for-display-materials`).
3. Draw the mount and make a mock-up in foam or card.

## Making
1. Cut and bend rod or sheet; join by soldering, brazing or welding; clean.
2. Pad contact points with a soft inert layer (polyester film, felt-free foam, or Plastazote) or a heat-shrink sleeve.
3. Finish with a coating such as powder coat or paint (tested for off-gassing) to blend with the object and prevent contact with bare metal.
4. Fit to the object; the mount should hold with gravity and not grip.

## Seismic protection
- In earthquake areas, use museum wax or putty (check manufacturer data) to hold objects, secure them to bases with rods or cables, and consider base isolators; research from the Getty Villa seismic programme has informed these methods.
- Test mounts for stability with a shaking table or consult an engineer for heavy or tall objects.

## Verify
- [ ] Contact points padded and not clamping.
- [ ] Mount stable and removable.
- [ ] Materials and coatings recorded.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
