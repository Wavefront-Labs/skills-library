# Safe handling and carrying of artworks

**Goal:** most damage to art happens while it's being moved, not on the wall. Get an
object from A to B with no knocks, drops, fingerprints or strain — by planning before
lifting and by holding it where it is strongest.

> **Status: draft.** These are widely shared museum handling rules. Institutions have
> their own policies (glove type, two-person rules, approved trolleys); follow those
> where they differ.

## Before anyone touches it
1. **Plan the route.** Walk it first: doors (and which way they swing), corners,
   steps, lifts, floor surfaces, where you will set it down. Clear obstacles and prop doors.
2. **Prepare the destination:** padded blocks, a cleared table, the wall hook in place.
3. **Look at the object.** Read its condition report; look for flaking paint, loose
   parts, previous repairs, weak handles, loose frame mouldings, cracked glazing,
   and what's attached (labels, hanging wire, D-rings). Report anything new before moving it.
4. **Decide how many people** — a rule of thumb: anything you can't hold comfortably
   close to your body with both hands, anything over about a metre, or anything fragile
   and awkward = two people. One person leads and **calls the moves** ("lifting on three").
5. Remove jewellery, watches, badges, lanyards; pens out of pockets.

## Gloves
- **Nitrile** (powder-free) is the general default now: good grip, no fibres, protects
  from residues.
- **Clean cotton** is still used by some for matte surfaces and frames, but it is slippery
  on glazed ceramic and glass, snags on rough surfaces, and lets skin salts through when
  damp (bad for polished metals, which need nitrile) — **use nitrile or clean bare hands
  for glass and glazed ceramics** per many institutions' policies.
- No gloves for some paper handling (clean, dry hands give better feel) — follow policy.
- Change gloves when dirty; never touch your face then the object.

## Framed paintings and works on paper
- **Carry one at a time**, picture facing you, by the **sides of the frame** (one hand
  under the bottom edge, one on the side) — never by the top rail or the hanging wire.
- Large works: two people, one at each side, frame vertical, moving at the slower
  person's pace.
- Check the frame is sound first: old gesso ornaments break off, corners can be loose.
- Don't press on the back of a canvas or the surface of glazing; stretched canvas dents
  from a knuckle.
- Set down **on padded blocks, never directly on the floor**, leaning against the wall
  at a slight angle, face to the wall only if it's glazed and protected. Never stack faces
  against each other without interleaving.
- Unframed works on paper: carry flat on a rigid support (board or folder), not by a corner.

## Three-dimensional objects
- Lift by the **base or the strongest part**, never handles, rims, spouts, limbs or
  projecting elements — those are where old repairs live.
- One object at a time; two hands; support from underneath.
- Remove lids and detachable parts and carry them separately.
- Use a padded tray or trolley for small objects instead of carrying in hand.
- Heavy sculpture: specialist lifting gear, straps on known lifting points, and a written plan.

## Trolleys and transit
- A-frame trolleys for paintings, padded flatbed or tray carts for objects; strap or wedge
  so nothing shifts. Push, don't pull; slow at thresholds.
- Glazed works in transit: tape glass with low-tack tape in a grid (glass only, not acrylic)
  so if it breaks shards don't cut the work — or swap to acrylic glazing.

## Verify
- [ ] Route walked and destination prepared before lifting.
- [ ] Condition checked; new damage reported before moving.
- [ ] Correct gloves for the material; jewellery removed.
- [ ] Held by frame sides / base, never by wire, rims or handles.
- [ ] Set down on padding; a leader called every lift as a team.
