# Air freight and CITES permits for artworks

**Goal:** a shipment that arrives intact and legal.

## Air freight
1. Book carriers experienced with fine art; choose direct flights where possible, to reduce transfers.
2. Prepare crates with lifting points and weights marked (see `crate-design-construction-and-ispm-15`).
3. Pallet build-up: crates are stacked or secured on aircraft pallets or in containers by the handler, with lower heavy items; request that fine art not be stacked beneath others, and keep orientation.
4. Security screening: cargo may be screened by X-ray or inspected; arrange for a courier to be present at screening and re-closing, with agreed procedures (see `courier-duties-and-shock-monitoring`).
5. Plan ground transport at both ends to avoid long waits and weather exposure.

## CITES and export licences
- The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) controls trade in listed species and products: for example elephant ivory, many corals, tortoiseshell, some feathers, certain rosewoods (Dalbergia) and other timbers.
- Check whether any material in the object is listed, and whether permits (export, import, re-export) are required; rules differ between countries and are updated.
- Some countries also require cultural property export licences.
- Allow time: permits may take weeks. Contact the national CITES management authority.

## Verify
- [ ] Materials checked against CITES lists.
- [ ] Permits and licences in hand before shipping.
- [ ] Screening and handling arrangements agreed.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
