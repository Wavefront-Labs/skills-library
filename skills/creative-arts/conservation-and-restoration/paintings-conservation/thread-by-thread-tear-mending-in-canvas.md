# Thread-by-thread tear mending in canvas

**Goal:** restore the continuity of a torn canvas so the tear no longer opens, deforms or
shows as a ridge, using the original threads wherever possible.

## Prepare
1. Face the paint along the tear if it is flaking (see
   `creative-arts.conservation-and-restoration.paintings-conservation.facing-a-painting`).
2. Work from the reverse with the painting face down on a padded, flat surface, or
   unstretched if the tear is near the edge.
3. Relax tear edges: local humidity (damp blotter through Gore-Tex or a humid poultice)
   and light weights bring the deformed threads back into plane.
4. Realign: under a stereomicroscope, tease each frayed thread back towards its partner
   with a fine probe or tweezers.

## Join
- **Adhesive:** the classic Heiber mix is sturgeon glue with wheat starch paste (strong,
  workable, aqueous); heat-set alternatives include polyamide welding powder or BEVA 371
  where water is a problem.
- Apply a tiny amount to the thread ends with a fine brush; overlap or butt them; set with a
  warm spatula through release film and let cool under a small weight.
- **Bridging threads:** where threads are missing, lay in new fine linen or polyester
  threads, or inlay a piece of matching canvas, bonded the same way.
- A thin local reinforcement (fine polyester or nylon fabric) can be added over the mend if
  the canvas is weak, but keep it small; patches print through.

## Gotchas
- Old tears with overlapping, cut or frayed threads need patience; trim nothing until you
  know which thread belongs where.
- Too much adhesive makes a stiff ridge that shows on the front.
- Tension: restretching too tight reopens a fresh mend; let it cure fully.

## Verify
- [ ] Tear aligned in plane (raking light from the front).
- [ ] Mend holds under gentle flexing; no adhesive on the paint side.
- [ ] Materials, thread count joined and bridges recorded.
