# Treating unvarnished and matte surfaces

**Goal:** remove dust without altering matte texture or adding a coating the artist never intended.

## Key points
- Many modern and contemporary paintings were intentionally left unvarnished; adding a varnish changes saturation, gloss and character irreversibly in practice.
- Matte, porous paint traps dirt and shows marks readily; abrasion burnishes (polishes) high points.

## Procedure
1. Find the artist's intent: documentation, studio records, interviews (see `artist-interviews-for-contemporary-art`).
2. Test any cleaning method on a hidden area, view in raking light afterwards.
3. Start with the least contact: soft brush with a low-suction vacuum held at a distance and screened.
4. Rubber (smoke) sponges and other dry sponges can leave residue or burnish on some matte paints; test before using, and avoid on friable or soft paint.
5. Avoid solvents and water unless proven safe; many matte paints are sensitive.
6. Protect with glazing or display distance rather than a coating where needed.

## Cautions
- If paint is powdery or friable, consolidate or refer; cleaning may lose pigment.
- Never add varnish without the owner's informed agreement and documented decision.

## Verify
- [ ] Surface gloss unchanged in raking light.
- [ ] No pigment on the cleaning tool beyond dust.
- [ ] Decision and method documented.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
