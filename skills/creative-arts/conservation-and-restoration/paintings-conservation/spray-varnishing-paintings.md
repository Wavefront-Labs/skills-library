# Spray varnishing

**Goal:** an even, thin varnish film without brush marks, built from several light passes.

## Equipment
- Air source and a low-pressure gun or airbrush (HVLP-type) or a conservation aerosol; a spray booth or fume cabinet with extraction, or a purpose-ventilated area.
- Respiratory protection suited to the solvent (a fitted half or full mask with organic-vapour cartridges is the usual minimum; see `respirator-selection`), nitrile gloves and eye protection.
- Painting laid flat or upright at the height recommended for your booth; a clean test board.

## Procedure
1. Check the painting is clean, isolated where needed and fully dry; mask frame edges.
2. Mix varnish to a spraying concentration (typically a more dilute solution than for brushing). Choose a solvent of low-to-moderate volatility so droplets level before drying; very fast solvents give a rough, hazy "orange peel" surface.
3. Spray the test board; adjust distance, pressure and dilution until the film wets out and levels.
4. Spray in overlapping passes at a constant distance, edge to edge, moving beyond the painting before releasing the trigger. Build up thin coats and let each flash off.
5. Watch the surface obliquely: gloss should be uniform, with no dry spray.
6. Leave the painting in a ventilated space until solvent has gone, then record.

## Failure modes
- Blush (milky haze) from fast solvent evaporation chilling the film in humid air; reduce humidity or use a slower solvent.
- Dry spray (rough, matt specks) from too much distance or too fast a solvent.
- Runs from too much material on vertical surfaces.

## Safety
Solvent vapour is flammable and harmful: no ignition sources, use extraction rated for the solvent, follow COSHH or local equivalent and the safety data sheet. Compressed air equipment needs regular inspection.

## Verify
- [ ] Test board levelled with correct gloss.
- [ ] Extraction and respirator fit checked before starting.
- [ ] Solvent, resin concentration and number of coats recorded.

## Professional note
General guidance only, not a treatment recommendation for a specific object. Work on heritage objects needs the judgement of a qualified conservator, working to the AIC Code of Ethics and Guidelines for Practice (or the ECCO / Icon equivalent) and the owner's consent; test, document and prefer the minimum intervention. Check current safety data sheets and local regulations for every material named.
