# Rigid gel cleaning with agarose and gellan

**Goal:** deliver an aqueous solution to a paint or varnish surface at a controlled rate
and only at the surface, then lift it away with the soil.

## Make the gel
1. Weigh agarose (2-4% w/v) or gellan gum (about 2% w/v) into the chosen solution
   (buffer, chelator) or deionised water.
2. Heat to boiling while stirring (microwave in short bursts or hotplate) until fully
   clear. Gellan usually needs a little calcium acetate to set well.
3. Pour into a flat tray to the thickness needed (3-5 mm typical); let it set.
4. Cut pieces to the area; store covered and refrigerated; use within days.
- Higher concentration = stiffer gel, less water released. Test both.
- Gels can also be loaded afterwards by soaking a water-cast sheet in the solution.

## Apply
1. Blot the gel surface on tissue so it is damp, not wet.
2. Lay on the test area; contact time is the main variable (start 30 s - 1 min, work up).
3. Lift, then roll a dry or barely damp swab over the area to pick up loosened grime.
4. Check under magnification and raking light; gel imprint or gloss change = too long.
- A thin layer of Japanese tissue between gel and paint helps on cracked or uneven
  surfaces; on impasto, warm gel (just set) conforms better.

## When gels are the right choice
- Water-sensitive paint, cracked surfaces where water would reach the canvas, gilding,
  unvarnished modern paint, and where a residue-free, rinse-light method is needed.

## Gotchas
- Agarose itself leaves little residue, but whatever is dissolved in it does: clear as
  for any aqueous system.
- Gels dry out on large areas; work small, keep the rest covered.
- Do not reuse a gel on a different area: it carries grime and dissolved material.

- [ ] Gel recipe, concentration and contact time recorded.
- [ ] No tide lines, gel marks or gloss change in raking light.
- [ ] Clearance step completed.
