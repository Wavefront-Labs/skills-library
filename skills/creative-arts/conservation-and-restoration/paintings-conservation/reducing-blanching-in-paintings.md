# Reducing blanching in paintings

**Goal:** restore the transparency of a surface that has gone milky, without driving the
damage deeper.

## Diagnose
| Symptom | Likely cause | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| Whitish bloom that disappears when wetted with solvent | moisture or micro-voids in the varnish | varnish layer |
| White haze returning after varnish removal, in the paint itself | micro-cavities in paint, often after water or aggressive solvent, or ageing of lean paint | paint layer |
| Grey crust that does not change when wetted | dirt or efflorescence | surface deposit |

Test: a drop of hydrocarbon on a corner of the haze; if the area clears while wet,
the haze is light scattering in a porous layer.

## Varnish blanching
- Often resolves by removing and replacing the varnish, or by solvent reforming with
  a polar solvent brushed or swabbed lightly so the resin re-flows.
- Historic Pettenkofer treatment used alcohol vapour; it is now used rarely and only
  with testing because vapour also acts on the paint.

## Paint blanching
- Resaturate with a low-viscosity varnish or a dilute resin (Regalrez 1094 or similar)
  to fill the micro-voids optically.
- Research (for example on solvent and water induced blanching in oil paint) supports
  local application of selected polar solvents in controlled doses; treat only after
  test windows reviewed over days, as blanching can return.

## Prevention
- Keep swabs barely damp; avoid long water exposure on lean, matte or aged paint.
- Dry the room: blanching appears more readily in high RH or when a cold painting is
  cleaned in a warm studio.

## Verify
- [ ] Diagnosis recorded with the wetting test result.
- [ ] Treatment window reviewed after at least several days in normal light.
- [ ] Final surface free of haze in raking light.
