# Clearing residues after aqueous cleaning

**Goal:** leave nothing behind from the cleaning system. Residues of buffers, chelators and
surfactants stay hygroscopic, keep acting on metal ions and pull dirt back in.

## The clearance sequence
1. **Same system without the active additive:** the buffered, conductivity-matched
   solution minus chelator or surfactant, applied with a barely damp swab. This removes
   the additive without a pH or conductivity shock.
2. **Water step:** deionised water adjusted to the surface conductivity, or brief plain
   deionised water where the paint tolerates it.
3. **Optional hydrocarbon wipe:** a light aliphatic (isooctane, low-aromatic mineral
   spirits) swab removes greasy residues and helps the surface dry evenly. Use only on
   paint known not to be sensitive to it.
- After gels: the same sequence, plus a dry swab to check for gel fragments in cracks.
- After solvent gels with amine neutralisers (Ethomeen-type) the clearance solvent is
  usually a hydrocarbon or the parent solvent; check the system's own guidance.

## How much
- At least as many clearance passes as cleaning passes is a common rule of thumb.
- Work while the surface is still slightly damp from cleaning, then let it dry fully.

## Detecting residues
- Under UV some surfactants fluoresce; a uniform haze that was not there before is a sign.
- Re-measure surface pH and conductivity with an agarose plug: values should be close to
  the pre-cleaning readings.
- Where available, FTIR of a swab or the surface confirms surfactant or citrate removal.

## Gotchas
- Skipping clearance because "the result looks good" is the commonest failure; problems
  appear months later as greyness or tackiness.
- Over-wet clearance swabs cause their own damage; roll and blot.

## Verify
- [ ] Clearance steps and number of passes recorded.
- [ ] Post-clearance surface pH/conductivity close to baseline.
- [ ] No haze in UV or raking light.
