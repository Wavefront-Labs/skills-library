# Overpaint and old retouching removal

**Goal:** remove later additions that distort the image, only where removal is justified,
leaving original paint intact.

## Decide first
- Map additions: in UV they usually read dark over aged varnish fluorescence; IR
  reflectography and X-radiography show whether there is original paint, damage or a
  loss underneath.
- Ask: is the overpaint covering damage that will need retouching anyway? Is it
  historically significant (an artist's own change, a period alteration)? Record the
  decision and the owner's agreement (see the AIC Code on informed consent).
- Remove only what is understood; partial removal is a legitimate outcome.

## Remove by solvent
- Overpaint is often more resistant than the varnish (oil-bound, aged). Test locally with
  stronger systems than used for the varnish, ideally as gels to keep them on the
  addition only.
- Benzyl alcohol, ketones, and amine-containing gels are typical stronger options;
  each must be tested against the original paint at the edge of the addition.

## Remove mechanically
- Under a stereomicroscope at 6-20x, with a fresh scalpel blade (No. 15 or 15T) held low,
  pare or flick the softened overpaint; a solvent gel applied first can swell it.
- Work from a loss outwards; original paint usually sits lower and has a different crack
  pattern than the overpaint.
- Stop when original paint, ground or a fill appears.

## Gotchas
- Old fills under overpaint may also be removed; decide whether to keep stable old fills.
- "Pentimenti" and artist's revisions are not overpaint.
- Overpaint over abraded original paint lifts the abraded fragments with it if you pull.

## Verify
- [ ] Before, during and after photography (visible and UV).
- [ ] Each addition removed is recorded with method and reason.
- [ ] No original paint on swabs or blade debris (check under magnification).
