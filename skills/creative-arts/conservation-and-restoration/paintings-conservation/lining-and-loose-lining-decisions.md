# Lining and loose lining decisions

**Goal:** give a canvas painting enough support to survive, with the least change to its
surface, texture and future options.

## Lining types
| Method | Adhesive | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Glue-paste lining | animal glue and flour paste, warm iron | historic French/Italian; moisture and heat can shrink canvas and flatten impasto |
| Wax-resin lining | beeswax and dammar (Dutch method) | waterproofing; impregnates and darkens canvas; hard to reverse fully |
| Heat-seal synthetic | BEVA 371 film, Plextol/Lascaux heat-seal | controllable, reversible with hydrocarbons, lower temperatures |
| Cold lining | acrylic dispersions on a low-pressure table | no heat; pressure-sensitive bond |
| Loose lining | none - second fabric beneath the canvas on the stretcher | protection, damping of vibration, minor support; fully reversible |

## Decision points
- **Is there a structural failure?** Widespread tears, weak brittle canvas that cannot
  hold tension, or cleavage that local consolidation cannot fix.
- **Has it been lined before?** Old linings that still work are often left; removal of a
  wax-resin lining rarely restores the original state.
- **Texture and impasto:** heat and pressure change surfaces. Since the 1974 Greenwich
  conference and later "lining moratorium" practice, lining is no longer routine.
- **Alternatives:** local tear mending, strip lining, consolidation, a loose lining and a
  backboard often achieve enough.
- **Future:** whatever is chosen must leave the canvas re-treatable.

## Loose lining in practice
- Stretch a fine polyester or linen fabric on the stretcher first, then the painting over it.
- Add a backboard (polycarbonate twin-wall or acid-free board) to buffer and protect.

## Verify
- [ ] Reason for lining (or not) recorded with evidence.
- [ ] Owner consent for an invasive structural treatment.
- [ ] Before/after raking light documentation of texture.
