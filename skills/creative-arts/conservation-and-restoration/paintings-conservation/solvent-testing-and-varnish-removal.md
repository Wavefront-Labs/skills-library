# Solvent testing and varnish removal on easel paintings

**Goal:** take off an aged, yellowed or blanched varnish layer while leaving the paint,
glazes and original surface untouched — using the *weakest* solvent system that works,
proved on test spots first. This is a treatment for trained conservators or students
under supervision; the method below is the decision logic, not permission to practise
on a valuable work.

> **Status: draft.** The workflow is standard practice as described in the conservation
> literature; specific solvent mixtures and percentages below are typical starting
> points, not a recipe. Every painting responds differently.

## 1. Examine before you touch anything
- **Normal light, raking light and UV (longwave, ~365 nm)** — see
  `creative-arts.conservation-and-restoration.condition-reporting.examination-under-raking-light-and-uv`.
  Aged natural-resin varnishes (mastic, dammar) fluoresce a milky greenish glow;
  later retouching and overpaint usually show as **dark patches** on top of it. Map them.
- **Is it really varnish?** Grime sits on top of varnish; remove surface dirt first
  (often aqueous, low-risk) and re-assess — sometimes the "yellowing" was dirt.
- **Previous cleaning history:** uneven fluorescence, abraded peaks, "skinned" glazes
  in the darks mean an earlier restorer was aggressive — the paint is thinner than it looks.
- **Sensitive passages:** glazes, lake pigments, copper greens, bitumen-rich darks, and
  any paint that is young (under ~50–100 years oil is still relatively solvent-sensitive).
  Test there, not only in a safe sky.
- Photograph before treatment; record in the treatment report.

## 2. The test ladder
Work from least to most polar/strong. A classic sequence (Feller-style, also mapped on
the Teas solubility triangle) uses an aliphatic hydrocarbon raised stepwise with a polar
solvent, e.g.:

| Step | Typical mix (by volume) | Notes |
|------|------------------------|-------|
| 1 | isooctane or low-aromatic mineral spirits | removes many synthetic low-MW varnishes (e.g. Regalrez-type) |
| 2 | hydrocarbon + 25% ethanol or isopropanol | |
| 3 | hydrocarbon + 50% alcohol | many aged mastic/dammar varnishes yield around here |
| 4 | hydrocarbon + 75% alcohol / acetone mixes | approaching the risk zone for oil paint |
| 5 | pure ethanol / acetone | rarely appropriate on oil paint; very risky on glazes |

Procedure for each test spot (~1 cm², at edges and in each sensitive colour):
1. Tightly wound cotton swab, barely damp — blot excess on tissue.
2. **Roll, don't scrub.** 5–10 rolls, then look at the swab and the surface under magnification.
3. **Yellow-brown on the swab = varnish.** **Colour on the swab = paint. Stop**, note the step, go back one.
4. Record: mixture, number of rolls, result, location. The working system is the lowest
   step that clears varnish evenly without pigment pickup or blanching.

If no free solvent works safely, gelled solvents (to limit penetration) or aqueous
systems with controlled pH and conductivity are the next options — these need specific
training; do not improvise them.

## 3. Cleaning a window, then the field
- Clean a small **test window** in an inconspicuous but representative area; review it
  in daylight and UV the next day (solvent can make an area look fine until it dries).
- Then work in small sections (a few cm), keeping swabs fresh, following the form so
  any residual unevenness reads with the image rather than across it.
- Leave a thin **residual layer** in delicate passages rather than chase the last of it;
  "partial" or "selective" cleaning is a legitimate, documented choice.
- Dispose of swabs in a lidded metal bin; never leave them heaped (solvent-soaked
  cotton is a fire risk).

## 4. Safety (not optional)
- Work under **local exhaust ventilation** (a fume extraction arm or snorkel positioned
  close to the swab), not just an open window.
- Nitrile gloves for most of these solvents; check the SDS — acetone and aromatics
  degrade some glove materials quickly.
- No ignition sources; acetone, ethanol and hydrocarbons are highly flammable.
- Keep only small working amounts in dispensing bottles; cap them between dips.
- Toluene/xylene and chlorinated solvents appear in older literature; most studios now
  avoid them because of toxicity — prefer the least hazardous system that works.

## Gotchas
- **Blanching** (white haze) after solvent can mean micro-voids in paint or varnish — stop and assess.
- Old overpaint is often more resistant than varnish; it is a separate decision, not a reason to escalate solvents over the whole picture.
- Different areas of one painting need different systems — a single mix for the whole surface is a red flag.
- Swabs dry fast: an overloaded swab floods, a dry one abrades.

## Verify
- [ ] Pre-treatment photos, UV map and test results recorded.
- [ ] Working system is the lowest step that removes varnish with no pigment on swabs.
- [ ] Test window reviewed dry, in daylight and UV, before full cleaning.
- [ ] Fume extraction running, gloves suitable for the solvent, swabs in a lidded bin.
- [ ] Treatment report states solvents, areas, and any residual varnish left deliberately.
