# False bottoms and hidden compartments

**Goal:** understand how stage props hide space, so they can be chosen, staged and shown
convincingly.

## Principle
Audiences estimate depth and thickness poorly, especially from a distance and under
stage light. A box, platform or table that looks thin can contain a surprising volume.
Hidden compartments are usually disguised by **visual cues of thinness**: bevelled edges,
decorative bands, dark colours at the base, and legs that suggest the base is shallow.
Guy Jarrett was particularly scathing about the over-thick bases common in his day.

## Showing the prop
- **Show it from sides that look thin.** Open doors so the audience sees through.
- **Move it** (on castors) to suggest lightness.
- **Lift or tilt** a side to show "nothing underneath".
- Avoid letting anyone handle it.

## Limits
- High stages expose the underside; front rows look up.
- Thick bases look suspicious to modern audiences; Steinmeyer and Jarrett both argue for
  methods that do not depend on them.

## Common faults
- Showing the prop from an angle that reveals depth.
- Painted lines that do not match the real joins.
- Too-heavy props that need visible effort to move.

## Verify
- [ ] Prop viewed from the front row under show light.
- [ ] Showing sequence rehearsed so only thin sides are presented.
- [ ] Prop moved by one person without visible strain.
