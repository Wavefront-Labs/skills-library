# Thin model and body displacement

**Goal:** understand how illusions exploit the gap between how much space a person appears
to need and how little they can actually occupy.

## Principle
Audiences assume a person fills a body-shaped volume. A trained, flexible person can fold
into a far smaller space, or distribute their body across separate compartments. Many
illusions (boxes that appear too small, sections that appear to separate) depend on this
**body displacement**. Steinmeyer discusses the related "thin model" concept: the prop
looks too thin to hide anyone because the audience's mental model of the person is wrong.

## Design and casting
- Choose the assistant for the illusion: size, flexibility and endurance.
- Measure the real space needed in the concealment position, with margin.
- Time how long the position can be held safely; rehearse entries and exits.
- The prop's visible dimensions should make the audience think "nobody could fit".

## Safety
- Never cast someone at the limit of their flexibility for a long hold.
- Ventilation and an abort signal are mandatory for any enclosed position.
- Medical clearance and gradual training for contortion positions.

## Common faults
- Casting a replacement without re-measuring.
- Long holds that cause injury.
- Visible bulges in fabric or panels.

## Verify
- [ ] Concealment positions measured and timed for each cast member.
- [ ] Abort signal agreed for every enclosed position.
- [ ] Prop dimensions tested on lay viewers as "too small".
