# Stage illusion: sightlines, staging and assistant choreography

**Goal:** present a large-scale illusion so that it is deceptive from every seat that
has been sold, looks like choreography rather than mechanics, and is safe for the
people inside it. This skill covers staging and performance, not prop construction
(that sits under handcrafts / stage-props).

## 1. Every illusion has an angle sheet
Each prop is designed for a stated viewing envelope: e.g. "deceptive from the front
within 60° and from no higher than the first balcony". Get that envelope from the
builder or the plans *before* booking a venue.
- **Venue survey:** walk to the extreme seats — front-row ends, side boxes, balcony
  front rail — and look at the prop position. Sketch the house on a plan with the
  illusion's envelope drawn on it.
- **Kill seats** that fall outside the envelope; agree this with the venue before tickets
  go on sale. It is far cheaper than a ruined effect.
- **Front-row height:** in a theatre with a high stage, front-row spectators look *up*
  and can see under platforms. Raised-base illusions are the usual casualty.

## 2. Lighting is part of the method
- **Black art** (classic, publicly documented): black objects against a black velvet
  background become invisible when the performer is lit brightly from the front and the
  background is kept dark. It depends on contrast — any light spill onto the backdrop
  ruins it.
- **Pepper's ghost** (19th c., public): a large angled glass reflects a hidden lit
  figure into the scene; the brightness balance between the two scenes sets visibility.
- Light the *apparent* action; leave the area of the secret in relative darkness.
  Side light from booms is safer than heavy backlight, which silhouettes edges.
- Haze and follow spots can betray methods; test with the actual rig.

## 3. Assistant choreography
The assistant is a co-performer, not furniture. The illusion is a piece of dance.
- **Count it:** set every move to musical counts (e.g. "on 5-6 the door opens,
  on 7 the turn"). Music gives both performers the same clock.
- **Motivate every move.** Spinning a cabinet, opening doors, displaying sides — each
  must look like showing, not like hiding.
- **Focus:** both performers look at the moment of magic, not at the mechanism.
- **Swap cues** for vision-restricted moments: tap signals, light cues or music, never
  a whispered word picked up by a mic.

## 4. Safety (non-negotiable)
- Any illusion enclosing a person needs an **agreed abort signal** and a crew member
  whose only job during that segment is watching for it.
- Time the confined portion; keep it short and consistent.
- Inspect locks, castors, hinges and blades at every show; a pre-show checklist signed
  by the stage manager.
- Never adapt a prop that holds a person without the builder's approval.

## 5. Reveals
A reveal lands when the audience's eye is already where the surprise appears. Use a
music hit, a light change and a performer gesture all on the same count; then hold
still for applause. Don't move on until the reaction peaks.

## Gotchas
- Mirrors and glossy floors reflect what you meant to hide — check the stage surface.
- Touring: each venue changes sightlines and wing space; re-survey every house.
- Uncertain: angle tolerances quoted here are illustrative; take real numbers from
  the specific prop's documentation.

## Verify
- [ ] House plan marked with the illusion's envelope; outside seats killed.
- [ ] Viewed from extreme seats under show lighting during tech.
- [ ] Choreography set to counts and run 20+ times with music.
- [ ] Abort signal agreed and rehearsed; watcher assigned.
- [ ] Pre-show inspection checklist completed and signed each performance.
