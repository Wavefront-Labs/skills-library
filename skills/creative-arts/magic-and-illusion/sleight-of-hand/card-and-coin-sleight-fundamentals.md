# Card and coin sleight fundamentals — the handful of moves everything else is built on

**Goal:** learn the small set of sleights that underpin most close-up card and coin
magic, practise them so they look like nothing at all, and know how to test that they
are invisible before a spectator does. Everything here is standard material from the
classic instruction books listed in `sources`; descriptions are paraphrased.

## 1. The governing principle: the move must look like a natural action
A sleight is a secret action hidden inside an ordinary one. The audience should see
"he squared the deck" or "she took the coin", never a special gesture. Before learning
any move, do the *real* action (genuinely take the coin, genuinely turn one card) a
dozen times and watch your hands. The sleight must copy that rhythm, tension and
speed. A sleight done faster than the real action is a tell.

## 2. Card fundamentals
- **Mechanic's grip** (dealing grip): deck in the left hand, first finger curled at the
  outer end, other fingers along the right side, thumb on top. Most card moves start here.
- **Overhand shuffle control:** run single cards to bring the top card to the bottom
  and back, or *injog* a card (step it towards you) to mark a break. Keeps a chosen card
  under control while the shuffle looks honest. Learn running cards with the thumb
  first; it is the engine of every overhand control.
- **Holding a break:** a tiny gap held by the flesh of the left little finger. Invisible
  from the front if the deck is held square; visible from the side if you tilt it.
- **Double lift:** turning two cards as one to show the "top card". Get a break under two
  cards, lift both by their ends from above (Biddle grip) or turn them as one; the
  failure mode is the two cards splaying at the edges. Square them against the deck
  before turning.
- **Simple forces:** the *riffle force* and *cross-cut force* are easier than any
  sleight and often stronger; see the mentalism skill for force psychology.

## 3. Coin fundamentals
- **Finger palm:** coin rests on the second and third fingers at the base, held by a
  slight curl. The hand still looks relaxed. The most useful coin concealment.
- **Classic palm:** coin gripped by its edges in the centre of the palm by the thenar
  and hypothenar muscles. Hardest to learn (weeks, not days); hand must not look cramped.
- **French drop:** coin held by left fingertips; right hand appears to take it, coin
  drops into left finger palm. Classic but widely known — the motion of the taking hand
  must *follow* the coin with your eyes.
- **Retention vanish:** coin is pressed into the left palm, which closes; the right hand
  leaves with it. The image of the coin "lingers" in the palm. Stronger than the
  French drop once learned; timing matters more than grip.

## 4. Misdirection rules that make sleights disappear
- **Eyes lead attention.** The audience looks where you look. Look at the hand that
  supposedly holds the coin, never at the hand that really does.
- **Big motion covers small motion.** Do the secret move while a larger action happens
  (turning to a spectator, gesturing with the other hand, putting the deck down).
- **Off-beat moments:** moves done just after a climax, a laugh, or a question are
  rarely watched. Classic advice: "do the work before the effect starts".
- **Don't prove too much.** Showing a hand empty repeatedly draws suspicion to it.

## 5. How to practise
- Learn the move slowly in front of a **mirror**, then **film from audience angle**
  (phone at eye height of a seated spectator, and from the sides). The camera is
  harsher than a mirror because you can't unconsciously adjust.
- Practise the move **inside a routine**, not in isolation; the in-context version is
  the one that must look natural.
- 10–15 focused minutes daily beats a long weekly session. Expect months for a palm
  that survives close scrutiny.

## Gotchas
- **Angles:** most sleights are safe only from the front. Know each move's angle
  limits; walk-around performers get flanked.
- **Tension:** a hand concealing something stiffens. Practise holding the palm while
  doing unrelated things (opening a door, drinking) until the hand relaxes.
- **New cards / coins behave differently.** Practise with what you'll perform with;
  fresh decks are slippery, worn decks clump. Coin size (e.g. US half dollar vs. a
  smaller local coin) changes every palm.
- Uncertain: exact grip names and finger numbering vary between books; follow one
  source consistently.

## Verify
- [ ] Filmed from three angles, the move is invisible at normal speed.
- [ ] The sleight's speed and rhythm match the genuine action.
- [ ] Your eyes and body point at the "apparent" location of the object.
- [ ] Concealing hand looks relaxed for at least 30 seconds of normal activity.
- [ ] You know the angles the move is NOT safe from and staged around them.
