# Repetition and variation of phases

**Goal:** repeat an effect so the audience enjoys the pattern and the surprise of change,
without bringing them closer to the method.

## Why repeat
Repetition teaches the audience the pattern so that **variations** carry surprise. Classic
multi-phase routines such as the cups and balls rely on this: the same action, a new
result each time.

## The risk
Repeating the *same* method with the same actions shows the audience the move, and can
reduce the effect. Repetition is a double-edged tool.

## Rules of thumb
1. **Vary the method** (not only the outcome) between phases so a theory formed in one
   phase fails in the next. See
   `creative-arts.magic-and-illusion.magic-performance-and-scripting.layering-methods-and-cancelling-solutions`.
2. **Vary the outcome** on a rising scale (one object, then two, then something
   unexpected).
3. **Keep the phase count modest**; three is a common starting point and more must earn
   its place by new surprise.
4. **Vary tempo** and where attention goes, not just the props.
5. **Stop before it drags**: if the audience begins to guess, finish with the strongest
   variation.

## Test
Record the routine and watch it as an audience member: at which phase do you start to
predict the method? Cut or change phases after that point.

## Verify
- [ ] Each repeated phase differs in method or outcome.
- [ ] The strongest variation is last.
- [ ] A fresh viewer cannot predict the next phase.
