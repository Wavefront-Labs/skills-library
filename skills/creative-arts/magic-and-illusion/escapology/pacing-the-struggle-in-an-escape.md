# Pacing the struggle in an escape

**Goal:** an escape that feels like it might fail, builds to a release, and ends on time -
regardless of how fast the method actually works.

## The shape of a good escape
1. **Restraint**: show and test it thoroughly (committee, tug tests). Belief in the
   restraint sets the stakes.
2. **Initial effort**: struggle with no visible progress; the audience realises it is hard.
3. **First progress**: one arm loosens, a buckle undone.
4. **Setback**: the progress stalls, a rest, a gasp; tension rises.
5. **Push**: a burst of effort and the decisive release.
6. **Triumph**: arms up, restraint thrown away, applause cue.

## Time
- Most stage escapes run 1-5 minutes visible struggle. Longer loses attention unless the
  danger is visible (water tank, suspension).
- A visible clock or countdown makes time the antagonist.
- If the method is fast, hold the release; if slow, rehearse to make the slow parts look
  dramatic.

## Music and sound
- Music building in intensity; silence for the setback; a drum roll for the final push.
- In a cabinet escape, the audience hears effort and rattling.

## Ethics and safety
- Faked distress must never mask real distress: the safety officer watches for the abort
  signal, not the performance (see escape safety protocols).
- Never extend real breath-holds or suspension beyond rehearsed limits for drama.

## Verify
- [ ] Escape has a scripted shape with timings.
- [ ] Real time limits written separately from dramatic timings.
