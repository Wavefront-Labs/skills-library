# Escape act: safety protocols and risk dramaturgy

**Goal:** perform escapes that feel dangerous while the real risk is engineered down
to near zero. In escapology the danger is *theatrical*; the safety is real.
This skill covers act structure and safety; it does not teach defeating restraints.

## 1. Escalate risk in the act, not in reality
A typical act builds in three stages:
1. **Rope or tie escape** with volunteers tying you — establishes fairness and fun.
2. **Restraint escape** (straitjacket, cuffs) — establishes skill and struggle.
3. **Closer with jeopardy** — timer, suspension, or a visible "consequence" — the
   *appearance* of stakes.
Each stage raises the felt danger; none should raise the real danger beyond what has
been rehearsed hundreds of times.

## 2. Safety protocol (write it down)
- **Risk assessment** for every escape: what can go wrong (airway, circulation, fall,
  drowning, heat), likelihood, control measure. Venues and insurers increasingly ask
  for this document.
- **Safety officer:** one trained person whose *only* role during the escape is you. Not
  the stage manager, not the MC. They hold the keys/release tools and a stopwatch.
- **Abort signal:** agreed, non-verbal, rehearsed (e.g. a specific foot tap, dropping an
  object). Plus a **hard time limit**: if you're not out at T, the officer intervenes
  regardless of signals.
- **Release tools on hand:** shears, keys to every lock in use, a knife for rope.
  Tested before the show.
- **Never perform water, suspension, burial, or neck/airway escapes** without
  professional rigging, medical cover and extensive supervised training. Most deaths in
  the history of escapology involve these categories.

## 3. Volunteer committees
Inviting audience members to inspect and apply restraints creates credibility.
- Brief volunteers briefly and specifically ("pull it tight, but not on my neck").
- Your safety officer watches their tying and steps in on anything near the throat,
  joints or circulation.
- Have a polite exit line for over-zealous volunteers.

## 4. Dramaturgy: making struggle readable
- **Show the restraint's strength** before you're in it: pull, rattle, let the audience
  test.
- **Visible struggle** sells difficulty; an instant escape can read as "it was easy".
  Houdini famously stayed behind a curtain longer than needed to build suspense.
- **Use a clock**: a large visible timer or counted music cue gives the audience
  something to fear.
- **Breath and voice:** pant, pause, reset. Silence is powerful before the final release.
- **Hit the ending:** freed, arms up, still. Hold for applause.

## Gotchas
- Fatigue across multiple shows per day slows you; build time-limit margins for show
  four, not show one.
- Restraints wear — inspect hinges and fabric before every show.
- Weather (heat, cold, wet rope) changes outdoor escapes significantly.
- Uncertain: there's no single international escapology safety standard; use local
  stunt and event safety guidance and your insurer's requirements.

## Verify
- [ ] Written risk assessment exists for every escape in the act.
- [ ] Safety officer assigned, briefed, holding release tools and stopwatch.
- [ ] Abort signal and hard time limit rehearsed in full dress.
- [ ] No water/suspension/airway escapes without professional rigging and medics.
- [ ] Act escalates *felt* risk across three stages; ending is clean and held.
