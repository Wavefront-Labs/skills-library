# Arranging for frequency space

**Goal:** an arrangement where every part is audible without heavy EQ, because parts don't
compete for the same space.

## 1. Three dimensions of space
1. **Register (frequency)**: low (bass/kick 40-200 Hz), low-mid (pads, guitars' body 200-500 Hz), mid (vocal, lead 500 Hz-3 kHz), high (hats, air, shakers 5 kHz+).
2. **Time (rhythm)**: parts that play in each other's gaps (call-and-response) don't mask.
3. **Stereo**: centre for lead vocal, kick, snare, bass; supporting parts panned.

## 2. Rules of thumb
- **One foreground element at a time** (usually lead vocal). Hooks and fills go in the vocal's gaps.
- Limit to ~3-5 parts in any one register simultaneously; mid-range is most crowded.
- **Octave placement**: if piano and guitar both strum chords in the same octave, move one up an octave or give one a sparser rhythm (stabs vs sustained).
- Pads should be quiet and filtered; drop them in verses if the vocal needs space.
- Bass and kick: decide who's lower and how rhythms interlock (see low-end skill).

## 3. Sound selection
- Choose sounds for the space they fill: a thin, bright synth for a lead over a full band; a warm, dark pad under a bright vocal.
- Swap a sound before EQing a bad fit; arrangement problems disguised as mix problems are the most common.

## 4. Method
1. Solo the lead vocal and the bass: the song's frame.
2. Add parts one at a time; for each, ask what register and rhythmic slot it owns.
3. Mute test: if muting a part changes nothing important, cut or simplify it.
4. Check density per section (see energy-arc skill): verses lighter than choruses.

## Gotchas
- Doubling the vocal melody on a synth in the same octave buries the singer.
- Three different chordal instruments playing the same voicing = mud.
- "Fixing" with EQ cuts that make each part sound thin in solo.

## Verify
- Static mix with faders only (no EQ): can you hear every part?
- Spectrum analyser on the master bus: no heavy build-up in 200-500 Hz.
