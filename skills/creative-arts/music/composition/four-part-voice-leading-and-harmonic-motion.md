# Four-part voice leading and harmonic motion

**Goal:** turn a chord progression into four independent, singable lines that move
smoothly and give the harmony direction. This is the common-practice core of
composition, and the same logic drives string pads, choir writing and film cues.

## 1. Set up the voices
| Voice | Comfortable range (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Soprano | C4–G5 |
| Alto | G3–C5 |
| Tenor | C3–G4 |
| Bass | E2–C4 |
- **Spacing:** no more than an octave between soprano–alto and alto–tenor; the
  tenor–bass gap can be wider.
- **Doubling:** in root-position major/minor triads, usually double the root. Avoid
  doubling the leading tone (scale degree 7) — it wants to resolve up to tonic, and two
  voices doing that makes parallel octaves.

## 2. Motion rules (why they exist)
- **No parallel perfect 5ths or octaves** between any two voices: they collapse two
  lines into one and weaken independence.
- **Hidden (direct) 5ths/8ves** into outer voices by similar motion with a leap in the
  soprano are traditionally avoided; inner-voice cases matter less.
- **Keep common tones** where chords share a note; move other voices by step to the
  nearest chord tone.
- **Bass and soprano in contrary motion** where possible — strongest outer-voice frame.
- **Resolve tendency tones:** leading tone up to tonic (in an outer voice), chordal 7th
  down by step.
- **Avoid voice crossing and overlap** unless there's a melodic reason.

## 3. Harmonic direction: function
Common-practice harmony cycles **Tonic → Predominant → Dominant → Tonic**:
- Tonic: I (vi, sometimes iii)
- Predominant: IV, ii (ii6 especially)
- Dominant: V, V7, vii°6
Retrogressions (V → IV) weaken direction; fine in pop, used sparingly in classical style.

## 4. Cadences
- **Perfect authentic:** V–I, both root position, tonic in soprano — full stop.
- **Imperfect authentic:** V–I with inversion or 3rd/5th in soprano — comma.
- **Half cadence:** ends on V — question.
- **Plagal:** IV–I — "amen".
- **Deceptive:** V–vi — surprise, extends the phrase.
Plan phrases around their cadences first, then fill in.

## 5. Inversions for a smooth bass
First inversion (6) and second inversion (6/4) let the bass move by step. Use the
**cadential 6/4** (I6/4–V) to approach a cadence; avoid free-standing 6/4 chords
otherwise. Passing 6/4 (I–V6/4–I6) is the other standard use.

## 6. Workflow
1. Write the bass and soprano first as a two-voice counterpoint.
2. Add Roman numerals; check function order.
3. Fill alto and tenor with the nearest chord tones, keeping common tones.
4. Scan every adjacent pair of voices for parallels (6 pairs per chord change).
5. Sing or play each line alone — each should be a decent melody.

## Gotchas
- Notation software won't flag parallels by default; some (e.g. MuseScore plugins)
  can — verify with a plugin or by hand.
- "Rules" are style descriptions of Bach-era chorales; pop, jazz and film music
  deliberately use parallel 5ths (power chords, planing). Break them knowingly.
- Uncertain: exact ranges differ by textbook and by choir level; keep amateur choirs
  well inside these.

## Verify
- [ ] All voices within range; spacing rule satisfied.
- [ ] No parallel perfect 5ths/8ves in any of the six voice pairs.
- [ ] Leading tone and 7ths resolve correctly.
- [ ] Each phrase ends on an intended cadence.
- [ ] Each line, played alone, is singable.
