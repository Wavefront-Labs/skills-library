# Orchestral balance, doubling and timbre blending

**Goal:** the melody is always audible, the bass is solid, and the colours mix rather
than fight.

## 1. Relative weight (rough, at the same written dynamic)
Rimsky-Korsakov's rule of thumb: at forte, one trumpet or trombone is about two horns,
and one horn is roughly two woodwinds; strings need numbers (a section, not a player).
Low registers and brass carry more; flutes in the low register carry least.

## 2. Layers
Think in three or four layers: **melody, counterline, harmony (pad), bass**.
- Give the melody the most distinctive colour or doubling.
- Keep harmony instruments a dynamic level lower and out of the melody's register.
- Bass doubled at the octave (cellos plus basses, bassoon plus tuba).

## 3. Doubling choices
- **Unison doubling** of different colours creates a new blended colour (flute + violin,
  oboe + horn); it reduces individual character.
- **Octave doubling** makes the line project without changing its colour much.
- Doubling a melody in three octaves is a classic tutti climax.
- Do not double everything; un-doubled lines sound clearer.

## 4. Blending
- Horns are the "glue": they blend with winds and strings.
- Same-family chords blend best; mixed-family chords are more colourful.
- Overlap colours in hand-offs (one instrument starts as the other ends).

## 5. Dynamics as balance tools
Write different dynamics per section in the same bar (brass mf, strings f) to balance.

## Gotchas
- Accompaniment in the same register as the melody masks it more than loud
  accompaniment elsewhere.
- Thick low-register harmony (close voicing below about C3) is muddy.
- Sample mockups mislead on balance; trust ratios and experience.

## Verify
- [ ] Melody is doubled or coloured to stand out in every tutti.
- [ ] Harmony and accompaniment are below the melody's register or marked softer.
- [ ] Low register is open-spaced.
