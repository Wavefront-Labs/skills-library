# Soloing over chord changes

**Goal:** solos that outline the harmony, so a listener could hear the chords from the
line alone.

## 1. Arpeggios in one position
- For each chord in the tune, learn its arpeggio (1-3-5-7) **in the same position** of the neck, so you don't jump around: e.g. ii-V-I in C around the 7th-10th frets (Dm7, G7, Cmaj7).
- Then learn them in a second position (CAGED shapes help: each chord shape has its arpeggio).

## 2. Target notes
- On each chord change, land on a **3rd or 7th** (guide tones) on the downbeat. These define the chord quality.
- Guide-tone line first: write a slow line using just 3rds and 7ths moving stepwise through the changes; then decorate.
- Example ii-V-I in C: F (3rd of Dm7) -> F (7th of G7) -> E (3rd of Cmaj7).

## 3. Connecting with approach notes
- **Chromatic approach**: one semitone below the target.
- **Scale approach**: one scale step above.
- **Enclosure**: above then below (or below then above) the target, landing on the beat.
- Bebop rule of thumb: chord tones on downbeats, passing notes on upbeats.

## 4. Practice method
1. Loop one 4-bar progression from a tune (iReal Pro, a looper, or a DAW).
2. Play arpeggios only in quarter notes, then 8ths, changing exactly on the barline.
3. Play guide-tone targets with one approach note each.
4. Add pentatonic/blues phrases between targets for idiom.
5. Record, then check: does the line change when the chord changes?

## Gotchas
- One scale over everything (e.g. A minor pentatonic over a jazz tune) ignores the changes; fine for blues, wrong for ii-V tunes.
- Landing on the 4th over a major chord on the downbeat clashes.
- Losing the form: always count bars or sing the melody in your head.

## Verify
- Play the solo with no backing: can a friend hear the changes?
- Mark the first note of each bar on the recording: 3rds/7ths/roots on most chord changes.
