# Object writing and sense-bound imagery

**Goal:** lyrics full of specific, sensory images that make listeners feel, instead of telling
them what to feel.

## 1. The exercise (Pattison)
1. Pick an object at random (a word from a book, "kitchen table", "rain gutter").
2. Set a timer for **10 minutes** (Pattison also uses 90-second and 5-minute versions).
3. Write fast, non-stop, about anything the object connects to, using **all seven senses**: sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, **organic** (body sensations: heartbeat, hunger, tiredness) and **kinesthetic** (sense of motion/position: dizziness, swaying).
4. Don't edit, rhyme or stay "on topic"; follow associations into memories and places.
5. Do it **daily, first thing**, for months.

## 2. Mining for lyrics
- Underline the most vivid lines; collect them in a notebook by emotion or setting.
- When writing a song, use **destination writing**: object-write on a place or moment from your song's story to find concrete details for verses.

## 3. Show, don't tell
- Abstract: "I was so lonely after you left." Sense-bound: "Two mugs in the sink, one still clean."
- Verses carry images; the chorus can state the summarising idea or title (the images earn it).
- Use sensory verbs and nouns; fewer adjectives.

## 4. Tips
- Go beyond sight: smell and organic/kinesthetic details are the most underused and most powerful.
- Second person and present tense increase immediacy (see point-of-view skill).

## Gotchas
- Writing slowly and editing during the exercise: defeats the purpose (accessing the senses fast).
- Stuffing every image into one song; choose the 3-5 strongest that serve the story.

## Verify
- In a finished lyric, mark each line as abstract or sensory: verses should be mostly sensory.
- Ask a listener what they "saw" in verse 1; specific answers mean the imagery works.
