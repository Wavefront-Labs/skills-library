# Writing a topline over a track

**Goal:** a melody and lyric that sit in the gaps of an existing beat and give the
chorus an obvious lift.

## 1. Learn the track first
- Loop each section; note the key, tempo, chord loop and where the drums and synths
  leave **space** (the pockets).
- Find the singer's comfortable range in this key before writing; transpose the track
  early if needed.

## 2. Mumble (melody) passes
- Record several free improvisations on nonsense syllables over a loop of each section.
  Do not stop to fix; record everything.
- Pick the best rhythmic cells and contours from the takes. Rhythm and phrasing matter
  more than pitch at this stage.
- Keep the mumble vowels - they often suggest the final words ("oh", "ay" sounds).

## 3. Shape the sections
- **Verse:** lower range, more syllables, conversational rhythm, often starting after
  beat one.
- **Pre:** rising contour, shorter phrases, building.
- **Chorus:** higher range, longer notes, repetition, the title on the strongest cell.
  Change at least two of range, rhythm density and phrase length versus the verse.
- **Post-chorus:** a chant or vocal-chop hook if the genre expects it.

## 4. Words
Fit lyrics to the melody's stresses (prosody). Keep the best mumble sounds on long
notes. Title on the hook cell.

## 5. Demo vocal
Record a clean guide with doubles on the chorus; comp quickly. The demo sells the
topline.

## Gotchas
- Melody that copies the synth line clashes rhythmically; answer it instead.
- Too many words in the chorus kills the hook; cut to the title plus a few words.
- Check you have not lifted a known melody; sing it to a co-writer.

## Verify
- [ ] Chorus differs from verse in range and rhythm.
- [ ] Melody sits in pockets, not on top of the busiest track elements.
- [ ] Singer can perform the whole topline in the chosen key.
