# Lyric point of view and narrative

**Goal:** the listener always knows who is speaking, to whom, and when.

## 1. Point-of-view options (Pattison)
- **First person ("I")** - intimate, confessional; the singer becomes the speaker.
- **Second person, direct address ("you")** - the singer talks to someone; strongest
  emotional pull, because the listener can occupy "you".
- **Third person ("she/he/they")** - a story told about others; good for narrative
  and character songs, more distance.
- **Combined "I"-to-"you"** is the pop default; pure third person suits story songs
  (country, folk).
Pick the POV that puts the listener closest to the emotion you want.

## 2. Hold it
- Do not slip between "you" as the lover and "you" as the generic person.
- If the POV changes (for example verses in third person, chorus in first), make it
  deliberate and structural, and mark it at a section boundary.

## 3. Tense and time
- Present tense feels immediate; past tense feels reflective.
- A narrative lyric needs **time movement**: each verse moves forward (V1 meeting,
  V2 years later, V3 at the end). Signal jumps with time words ("now", "by June").
- The chorus can stay the same while its meaning changes because the verses moved
  (the "evolving chorus" effect).

## 4. Narrative craft
- Start in the middle of action, with a concrete detail.
- Each verse adds new information; none repeats the previous verse's job.
- Leave the moral unsaid; let the final detail land it.

## Gotchas
- Pronoun confusion: "she said you" inside a "you" song breaks the address.
- Tense drift inside one verse (was/is) is the most common draft error.
- Too much backstory before the chorus delays the hook.

## Verify
- [ ] Mark every pronoun: each refers to one consistent person.
- [ ] Each verse is a different time or development.
- [ ] Tense is consistent within each section.
