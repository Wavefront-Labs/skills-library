# Song structure, hooks and prosody

**Goal:** write a song where the listener knows the chorus by the second time it
comes round, the lyric sounds natural when sung, and every section has a job.

## 1. Pick a form and give each section a job
Most popular songs use a variant of:
`Intro – V1 – (Pre) – Chorus – V2 – (Pre) – Chorus – Bridge – Chorus (– Outro)`
- **Verse:** detail and story; lower energy, more words, melody often lower in range.
- **Pre-chorus:** tension; rising melody, shorter phrases, harmonic lift (often to IV
  or a non-tonic chord).
- **Chorus:** the point of the song; title lives here, highest/longest notes,
  repetition, simpler words.
- **Bridge:** new angle — different chord, rhythm or perspective — so the last chorus
  feels fresh.
Aim to reach the first chorus within roughly 45–60 s in commercial pop (a market
convention, not a law).

## 2. The hook
- Usually the **title** sung on the most memorable melodic figure, repeated.
- Make it contrast with the verse: if the verse is busy, the hook is long notes; if the
  verse is low, the hook jumps up.
- Place the title at the **first or last line** of the chorus (or both).
- Instrumental hooks (a riff, a synth line) count — many songs have both.
- Test: sing the chorus to someone once, then ask them to sing it back an hour later.

## 3. Prosody: making words and music agree
- **Stress match:** stressed syllables land on strong beats / longer or higher notes.
  "be-CAUSE" sung as "BE-cause" sounds wrong immediately. Speak the line, mark the
  stresses, then set it.
- **Line length = emotion:** short, even lines feel stable; unequal or extra lines feel
  unsettled. Pattison's key insight — use stability to match meaning (resolved chorus,
  unresolved verse).
- **Rhyme types:** perfect (love/glove), family (cup/love), additive/subtractive
  (free/freeze), assonance (time/line), consonance. Perfect rhyme closes a section
  strongly; slant rhyme keeps it moving and avoids clichés.
- Put the **important word at the end of the line**; it's where ears rest.
- **Show, don't tell** in verses: concrete images ("your coat still on the hook")
  beat abstract statements ("I miss you").

## 4. Workflow
1. Start with a **title/concept** that has a twist or tension.
2. Write the chorus first (melody + lyric) so verses can point to it.
3. Record every idea on a **phone voice memo** immediately; name files with title and
   key.
4. Draft V1 on the "who/where/when", V2 on "what's changed".
5. Rewrite: cut every line that doesn't add new information.
6. Make a rough demo (voice + one instrument) to judge the song, not the production.

## 5. Co-writing etiquette
Agree splits up front (equal splits among writers in the room is a common default,
but practices vary). Say "yes, and" before criticising; record everything; one person
holds the master lyric doc.

## Gotchas
- Chorus melody too similar to verse = no lift. Change range, rhythm, or both.
- Forced rhymes (inverting word order to rhyme) are audible and dated.
- Uncertain: split conventions and timing norms differ by genre and territory.

## Verify
- [ ] Each section has a distinct job and audible contrast with its neighbour.
- [ ] Title sits in the chorus at a line start or end.
- [ ] All stressed syllables land on strong beats when sung.
- [ ] A listener can sing the hook back after one or two hearings.
- [ ] Rough demo exists; splits are agreed in writing if co-written.
