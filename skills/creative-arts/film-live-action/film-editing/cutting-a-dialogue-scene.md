# Cutting a dialogue scene

**Goal:** build the best version of the scene from the coverage — the strongest
performances, the clearest story, the right rhythm — rather than the version the script
or shot list implies. Tool-agnostic: works in Avid Media Composer, Premiere Pro,
DaVinci Resolve or any NLE.

## 1. Watch everything first
Watch every take of every setup, with sound, before cutting. Mark (markers or a
notes sheet): the best reading of each line, surprising moments, reactions, mistakes
to avoid, and moments that happen *between* lines (looks, pauses). Check script
supervisor's notes for director's circled takes, but don't treat them as final.

## 2. Build selects
Make a **selects sequence** per character or per setup: the usable pieces in script
order. This is the palette you cut from, and it makes comparing performances fast.

## 3. First assembly
Cut the scene through roughly as scripted (wide → closer as it intensifies is a common
default), choosing the best line readings. Don't polish yet — get the whole scene
working end to end.

## 4. Choosing when to cut and to whom
- **Cut for the story beat, not the speaker.** Often the most important shot is the
  person *listening* — the reaction tells the audience how to feel about the line.
- **Cut on motivation:** a look, a turn of the head, a new thought, or an action
  (cutting on action hides the cut). Murch's priorities are a useful frame: emotion
  first, then story, then rhythm, then eye-trace, then 2D and 3D continuity — emotion
  outranks a continuity error.
- **Shot size follows intensity:** move tighter as stakes rise; going back to a wide
  can release tension or reveal geography.
- **Eyelines and screen direction:** cutting between singles from matching sides keeps
  the conversation coherent; a crossed axis reads as a shift in power or a mistake.

## 5. Split edits and overlap
- **J-cut:** the next shot's audio starts before its picture (we hear B before we see B).
- **L-cut:** picture cuts to B while A's audio continues (we see B listening to the end
  of A's line).
These make dialogue flow naturally and let you hold on reactions. Overlapping lines
from different takes may not match — use clean lines plus reaction shots to build overlap.

## 6. Pace and rhythm
- Trim the air: actors' pauses that work on set can drag on screen — but keep pauses
  that carry meaning.
- Tighten the scene's entry and exit: arrive late, leave early.
- Vary rhythm; a scene cut evenly at every line change becomes monotonous.
- Performance can be **built**: combine the best halves of lines from different takes,
  borrow a reaction from elsewhere in the scene, remove a line entirely. Check the result
  is honest to the character and lip-sync is invisible.

## 7. Test cold
After a break, watch the scene in context (with the scenes before and after) at full
screen with good sound. Then show someone who hasn't read the script: do they understand
what happened and how each character feels? Their confusion is your next note.

## Gotchas
- Cutting the scene to match the script page exactly when the actors found something better.
- Too many cuts: every cut must justify itself; holding a shot can be stronger.
- Sound bumps at every cut — smooth with short audio crossfades and room tone; final
  dialogue editing comes later, but a bumpy temp mix distorts how the scene plays.
- Falling in love with a moment that slows the film; be willing to lose it.

## Verify
- [ ] Every take watched and noted before cutting.
- [ ] Selects built; best line readings chosen deliberately.
- [ ] Cuts are motivated (beat, look, action), with reactions used for key lines.
- [ ] Split edits used where they help the flow; no audio bumps at cuts.
- [ ] Scene viewed in context and by a fresh viewer, notes recorded.
