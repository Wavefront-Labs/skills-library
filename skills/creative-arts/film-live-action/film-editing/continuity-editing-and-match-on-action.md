# Continuity editing and match on action

**Goal:** cuts the audience doesn't see. Continuity style builds a coherent space and
time out of shots taken hours apart; when it's right, attention stays on the people.

## 1. Match on action
Cut *during* a movement so the motion carries the eye across the cut:
- Choose the moment of **maximum motion** or the instant just after it begins - a hand
  reaching, a head turning, a door swinging, someone sitting. The eye tracks the motion
  and misses the change of angle.
- **Overlap**: the outgoing shot ends a few frames into the action; the incoming shot
  begins at the matching point *plus* roughly 2-4 frames further along (the brain
  perceives repeated frames as a stutter; a slight skip reads as continuous).
  Faster action can take more skip; slow deliberate action less. Judge by watching at speed.
- Cutting on a static beat (nobody moving) is also fine - it's the half-motion cut that
  jars.
- Avoid cutting on the blink or a mid-syllable; cut just before or after.

## 2. Space: the line, eyelines and the 30-degree rule
- **180-degree rule**: all shots in a scene keep the characters on their sides of the
  axis; A looks right, B looks left. Crossing is allowed when a shot *shows* the
  crossing (a move, a neutral shot on the line, a cutaway), or the whole scene has a
  reason to flip.
- **Eyelines**: the direction and height of a look must point at where the other
  character would be. A reverse with an eyeline a few degrees off reads as "looking at
  nothing". Use the take where the eyeline is right over the better performance if the
  eyeline problem is visible.
- **30-degree rule**: two shots of the same subject from angles less than ~30° apart
  (and similar size) look like a jump; change angle *and* size together. Deliberate
  jump cuts are a different grammar (see pacing/jump cuts).
- **Screen direction**: an exit frame-left is an entry frame-right; travel left-to-right
  stays that way across cuts unless a neutral shot resets it.

## 3. Time: elision and the illusion of continuity
Shots only need to *seem* continuous. You can lose seconds across a cut (walk to the door
in the wide, already opening it in the close-up) as long as the action matches in the
frames adjacent to the cut. Audiences forgive time compression inside a scene but notice
a repeated action (the door opened twice).

## 4. Hiding continuity errors
Prop in the wrong hand, hair over the ear, cigarette length, wardrobe mismatches: the
tools are
- **cut away** (reaction, insert, cutaway) and come back in a new position;
- **cut tighter**, so the error is out of frame;
- **cut on action** so the eye is elsewhere;
- **reorder**: use the take where it matches, even if performance is second-best, when
  the error is prominent (the glass that refills itself is remembered longer than a
  slightly flatter line);
- **flop** a shot (mirror) to fix screen direction - only if no text/asymmetric detail;
- VFX fix as a last resort (paint-out, split screen between two takes).

## 5. Choosing the frame
When both shots are static, cut where the energy changes: the end of a line, the start of
a reaction, the moment eyes shift. Prefer cutting *to* a shot a beat **before** the
information lands (the audience arrives as the moment happens) rather than after.
In dialogue, the reaction is often more important than the speaker (see the dialogue
scene skill).

## Gotchas
- Cutting the exact same frame on both sides (the stutter).
- Reverses with a different lens/size that make the match feel wrong even when it technically matches.
- Correcting the line with a shot that breaks the 30-degree rule - two problems.
- Obsessing over a prop mismatch the audience never sees while the emotional continuity is off.
- Montage/elliptical grammar mixed into a continuity scene by accident.

## Verify
- [ ] Each action cut overlaps with a small skip and plays smoothly at speed.
- [ ] Characters stay on their sides; every crossing is motivated or neutralised.
- [ ] Eyelines point correctly in every reverse.
- [ ] Known continuity errors hidden by cutaway, size or ordering.
