# Pacing, rhythm and the Rule of Six

**Goal:** every cut lands where the audience is ready to move on - not before, not
after - and the film's tempo rises and falls on purpose.

## 1. Murch's Rule of Six (what a cut must satisfy, weighted)
1. **Emotion** (51%) - is the cut true to the emotion of the moment?
2. **Story** (23%) - does it advance the story?
3. **Rhythm** (10%) - does it occur at a moment that is rhythmically "right"?
4. **Eye-trace** (7%) - does it respect where the audience's eye is in the frame?
5. **Two-dimensional plane of screen** (5%) - does it respect the 180° line?
6. **Three-dimensional space** (4%) - is the physical continuity right?
When you must sacrifice, sacrifice from the bottom. A cut that matches action perfectly
but breaks the emotion is wrong; the reverse is usually fine.

## 2. The blink
Murch's observation: people blink at the moment a thought completes; a good cut sits at
the same place - the audience "blinks" with the cut. Practically: watch the take at speed
and press a mark at the instant you *feel* done with the shot; that's usually within a
few frames of the cut. Trust the first instinct on fresh eyes, then test it.

## 3. How long to hold a shot
A shot's duration is the time needed to **read** it: new information (a wide, a face
we haven't seen, a prop) needs longer; a return to a known angle needs less. Rules of thumb:
- A new wide needs ~2-3 s before a cut feels earned; a returning close-up can be 12 frames.
- Hold on a reaction until the thought *changes* in the face, not until the line ends.
- The audience reads the frame from the point of previous attention (eye-trace); place
  the next shot's subject near where their eye already is for a quick read, far from it
  to force a scan (slower, deliberate).
- Hold a shot *too long* on purpose for discomfort or weight - rarely, so it counts.

## 4. Tempo across the scene and the film
- Pacing is relative: a scene feels fast after a slow one. Design the sequence of
  tempos per reel like music - don't let every scene be mid-tempo.
- Inside a scene, cut rate often accelerates toward the turn and relaxes after; the
  cut *rate* and the *shot sizes* work together (tighter + faster = intensity).
- Trim "air": the walk in, the breath before the line, the look after the look. Then
  put some air back where the audience needs to feel the moment. A cut that is too
  tight reads as nervous.
- Watch with the sound off to see visual rhythm; with the picture off to hear dialogue
  rhythm. Each exposes different dead spots.
- Scene length in the script (one page ≈ one minute) is only a starting guess.

## 5. Jump cuts and ellipsis (on purpose)
- **Jump cut**: same angle, time removed - a visible stutter that says "time passes" or
  "this person is unstable/impatient" (Godard, documentary interviews, vlogs). Use with
  consistent frame size and keep the subject's position near-identical for cleanliness,
  or very different for violence.
- **Elliptical editing**: compress a process (a journey, a search) to its essential
  steps - the match-on-action across time (hand on key -> car on highway). The audience
  fills gaps if the steps are legible and the direction of travel is consistent.
- **Time compression within a take** (speed ramps, hidden cuts behind foreground
  wipes) when a jump cut would be wrong for the style.

## 6. Reviewing for rhythm
Watch the whole reel at speed, no stopping, with a notepad: where did attention slip?
Where did you want to go on and it stayed? Fix only after watching the whole thing;
local fixes to a scene can break the reel's rhythm. Leave a day between review passes
when possible - fresh eyes are the only reliable instrument.

## Gotchas
- Cutting to the tempo of the temp music rather than the scene.
- Uniform shot lengths - the metronome cut.
- Trimming every pause because "it's slow"; the scene loses meaning, not length.
- Jump cuts by accident in a continuity scene (see the 30-degree rule).
- Judging pace from a 30-second excerpt rather than the reel.

## Verify
- [ ] Each cut passes the Rule of Six top three (emotion, story, rhythm).
- [ ] Shot lengths vary with information load; reactions held to the thought change.
- [ ] Reel-level tempo map has rises and falls; no two consecutive scenes at the same tempo by accident.
- [ ] Jump cuts/ellipsis only where chosen and consistent in style.
