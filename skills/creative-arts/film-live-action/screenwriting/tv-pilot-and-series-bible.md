# TV pilot and series bible

**Goal:** a pilot script that proves the show can run for years, plus a bible a commissioner
can read in fifteen minutes and see season one, season three and the poster.

## 1. The engine first
Before the pilot, write one paragraph answering: *what generates episodes?* The engine is a
situation that produces conflict every week without being resolved: a job with cases, a
family that can't leave, a lie that must be maintained, a world with rules to break.
Test: list **ten episode ideas** in one line each from the engine alone. If you can't
reach ten, the premise is a feature, not a series.

Classify the show and write the pilot accordingly:
- **Procedural / case-of-the-week:** A-story closes each episode; serialised B/C runners.
- **Serialised drama:** episode = chapter; each still needs its own shape and ending turn.
- **Half-hour comedy (single/multi-cam):** ~30 pages, A/B(/C) stories, character flaws
  are the engine, nobody learns permanently.

## 2. Pilot types
- **Premise pilot:** shows the situation being created (the diagnosis, the arrival, the crime).
  Easier to write, harder to make feel like a typical episode.
- **Typical-episode pilot:** drops into a world already running; the series engine is visible
  from minute one. Preferred by many buyers because it proves the format.
Pick deliberately; if premise, make sure the engine is running by the final act.

## 3. Structure and act breaks
- **One-hour drama:** teaser/cold open (2-6 pp) + 4-5 acts, 55-65 pages. Each act ends on a
  turn big enough to survive an ad break even on a streamer (the habit still shapes pace).
- **Half-hour:** cold open + 2-3 acts + tag, 28-34 pages.
- End the pilot on a question that *demands* episode two, not on a resolution.
Mark act breaks in the script (`END OF ACT ONE`) for broadcast; optional for streaming specs.

## 4. Character set
Design a cast where any two characters put in a room produce a scene. Standard engines:
opposite worldviews under one roof, a hierarchy that resents itself, an outsider in a
closed system. Each series regular needs a *long-term* want (series), a *season* want and
an *episode* want. Secondary characters should be castable in one line.

## 5. The bible (8-20 pages)
1. **Logline and tone** (comparables: "X meets Y" is fine if accurate).
2. **The world and the engine** - the paragraph from section 1, expanded.
3. **Characters** - half a page each for regulars: want, flaw, role in the engine, how they
   change over the series.
4. **Pilot synopsis** (one page).
5. **Season one episode guide** - a paragraph per episode (6-10 episodes). This proves the engine.
6. **Future seasons** - a paragraph each for seasons two and three; where it ends.
7. **Visual/tonal references** - sparingly, optional.
Keep the voice of the show in the prose. A bible written like a legal document undersells.

## Gotchas
- A pilot that spends 40 pages on setup before the engine starts. Open in the engine.
- A protagonist who solves the series problem in the pilot.
- Too many regulars introduced with equal weight - give the pilot a clear point-of-view character.
- Bible episode guide that lists plots with no character turns - buyers read for change.

## Verify
- [ ] Ten episode ideas generated from the engine in one sitting.
- [ ] Act breaks land on turns; the pilot ends on a question.
- [ ] Every regular has series, season and episode wants.
- [ ] Bible contains a season-one episode guide and a future-seasons page.
