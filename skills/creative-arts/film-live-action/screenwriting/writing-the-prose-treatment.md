# Writing the prose treatment

**Goal:** a document a producer reads in fifteen minutes and comes away knowing the
film - its people, its shape, its ending and its tone - well enough to say yes, no, or
"write it". A treatment is the story told, not described.

## 1. Pick the length for the job
| Purpose | Length | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Pitch leave-behind** | 1-3 pages | Logline, tone paragraph, the story in acts, ending |
| **Development treatment** (producer/financier) | 5-12 pages | Full story scene by scene in prose, main characters introduced in-line |
| **Scriptment** (writer's working doc) | 20-40 pages | Every scene, key dialogue exchanges, almost a draft |
| **Series treatment** | 8-15 pages | Engine, characters, pilot story, season shape, future seasons |
Ask what the reader expects before writing - "treatment" means different lengths to
different buyers. When in doubt, 6-8 pages.

## 2. Structure of the document
1. **Title, writer, contact**, "Treatment", draft date.
2. **Logline** (one sentence) and **tone** (one paragraph: genre, comparables, what it
   feels like to watch).
3. **The story**, in present tense, third person, in order. Use act headings or
   simple white space; no scene headings (`INT./EXT.`), no camera language.
   Introduce characters **in bold or caps on first appearance** with age and one
   defining line ("MARTA, 42, a nurse who has stopped expecting good news").
4. **Ending included.** A treatment that withholds the ending reads as a writer who
   doesn't have one.
5. Optional: a closing paragraph on theme or "why this film now" - one paragraph, not an essay.

## 3. Writing it
- **Tell, don't summarise.** "Marta finds the ledger and realises her father knew"
  beats "Marta discovers a shocking secret". Every scene described should contain its
  turn.
- Keep the **proportions** of the film: act two gets half the pages. If act two is two
  paragraphs, you've found the structural problem early - good.
- **A line of dialogue** here and there carries voice; more than one exchange per page
  turns it into a script.
- Write the **emotional through-line** on the page: what the protagonist wants, what
  they believe, what changes - a treatment that lists events without wants reads flat.
- Keep subplots as short, labelled threads inside the main flow, not separate sections.
- Voice: confident, specific, zero hedging ("perhaps", "could be"). The treatment is
  the film as if it existed.

## 4. The read test
Give it to someone who hasn't heard the pitch; ask them to tell the story back and
name the ending and the main character's problem. Where they stumble, the treatment
(or the story) has a hole. Then cut 20%: treatments are always too long on setup.

## 5. Protect it
Register with the WGA (West or East) or an equivalent registry/copyright office before
sending; treatments are the stage at which stories most often get "borrowed". Date
every version.

## Gotchas
- Writing the treatment as a list of scenes with headings; it reads as an outline, not a film.
- Hiding the ending "to keep it a surprise".
- Describing the theme instead of dramatising it ("this is a film about grief").
- Characters introduced by physical description only.
- Hedging language that invites the reader to doubt the writer.

## Verify
- [ ] Length matches the reader's expectation; present tense, no sluglines or camera.
- [ ] Logline and tone up front; ending on the page.
- [ ] Act two is at least half the story pages; protagonist's want and change visible.
- [ ] A cold reader retold the story and named the ending.
