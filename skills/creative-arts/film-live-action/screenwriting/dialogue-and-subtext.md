# Dialogue and subtext

**Goal:** lines that an actor can *play* - each one a tactic toward an objective - with the
real meaning sitting underneath. Dialogue that only transmits information is read as
"on the nose" by every reader in the industry and gets a script passed on.

## 1. Every line is an action
Before writing a line, know what the speaker is trying to *do* to the listener: persuade,
wound, deflect, test, seduce, stall. Write the tactic, then find the words a person using
that tactic would actually say. The stated subject of the line is rarely the real one.

## 2. Subtext techniques
- **Talk about something else.** Two people arguing about who loads the dishwasher are
  arguing about who left whom. Pick a concrete surface topic; let the real stakes press on it.
- **Answer the question that wasn't asked.** Deflection reveals more than a direct reply.
- **Say the opposite.** "I'm fine" under pressure. The audience reads the gap.
- **Silence and interruption.** A beat of nothing, or a character cutting another off,
  is a line. Mark with `(beat)` sparingly or a short action line.
- **Let the audience know more than the listener** (dramatic irony) so a plain line lands loaded.

## 3. Voice
Give each major character a distinct way of speaking you could identify with the names
covered: sentence length, vocabulary register, whether they finish sentences, what they joke
about, what they never say. Write a short "voice note" per character and test by reading
a page with names blacked out. If two characters are interchangeable, merge or differentiate.

## 4. Exposition without the dump
- Information is a weapon: have a character *use* it in a fight rather than *explain* it.
- Make the listener resist: a person who doesn't want to hear the information forces the
  speaker to work, which turns a lecture into a scene.
- Fragment it across scenes. Nobody needs the whole backstory at once.

## 5. The dialogue pass (do it last)
1. Read the whole script aloud, or have actors table-read it. Mark every line that stumbled.
2. Cut the first and last line of most speeches - the warm-up and the summary.
3. Cut any line that restates what the action already showed.
4. Remove greetings, goodbyes, names used as address, "well", "so", "look", "I mean".
5. Replace any line that explains a feeling with a behaviour or a deflection.
6. Target: 20-30% fewer words with nothing lost. Speeches longer than four lines must earn it.

## Parentheticals
Use only for a reading the line can't carry alone (`(lying)`, `(to Sam)`) or a small
physical action that interrupts. Never direct the actor's emotion line by line - it reads as
distrust and gets ignored.

## Gotchas
- Characters stating the theme. If a line could be the film's poster tagline, cut it.
- Everyone witty in the same way - that's the writer's voice, not the characters'.
- Dialect spelt phonetically. Indicate rhythm and word choice; let the actor supply accent.
- Phone calls and video calls written as two full halves - one side is usually stronger.

## Verify
- [ ] Each line has a tactic you could name in a verb.
- [ ] A reader can identify speakers with names hidden.
- [ ] No character tells another something both already know.
- [ ] The dialogue pass removed at least a fifth of the words.
