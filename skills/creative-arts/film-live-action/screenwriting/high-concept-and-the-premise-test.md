# High concept and the premise test

**Goal:** know within an hour whether an idea is a film someone will pay for and you
can write - before spending six months discovering it isn't.

## 1. Write the premise line
`What if [a protagonist with a specific situation] had to [goal] but [the obstacle or
twist]?` One sentence. Examples of the shape: "What if a timid aquarium employee had to
smuggle a killer whale back to the sea?" The **what-if** should contain an irony - the
least suited person for the problem, or the problem that attacks exactly their flaw.
If the sentence needs a second one to explain it, the concept isn't yet a concept.

## 2. The high-concept checklist
Score honestly; three or more "no" means the idea sells on execution only (possible,
but harder to finance):
- **Instantly visual**: can a stranger picture the poster and the trailer from the line?
- **Built-in conflict**: is the obstacle in the premise, not in the subplot?
- **Clear protagonist and goal**: one person, one want.
- **Stakes** that a stranger cares about (life, love, freedom, home).
- **Irony/twist** that makes it fresh against its genre.
- **Scalable**: does it generate a second act of escalating complications, or is it a
  one-joke short?
- **Audience and format** obvious: who goes to see this, feature or series?

## 3. Genre positioning and comparables
Name the genre in the buyer's language (contained thriller, elevated horror, romantic
comedy, grounded sci-fi). Pick two **comparables** ("comps") from the last 5-10 years
at the budget level you imagine - recent, successful, not masterpieces ("Get Out meets
Parasite" says nothing; "a *Coherence*-scale thriller" says budget and tone). Comps
tell a financier the number; the twist tells them why yours.

## 4. The title
A working title that states concept or tone: short (1-3 words), sayable, searchable,
not already a well-known film (check IMDb and the title registry if you can). Titles that
are the protagonist's name sell only with a star. Keep a list of ten and test which one a
listener repeats back.

## 5. Kill it or keep it
Pitch the premise line to five people with no context. Count how many ask a question
about the story (good) versus politely nod (bad). If three or more ask "and then what
happens?", outline it. If nobody does, change the irony or the protagonist, not the
wording.

## Gotchas
- A premise that's a *setting* ("a hotel in 1920s Shanghai") or a *theme* ("a film about forgiveness") - no protagonist, no goal.
- The twist is in act three; the premise line can't mention it, so the concept reads flat. Move the twist earlier or find another hook.
- Comps that are decades old or outliers.
- Title explains the plot ("The Woman Who Lost Her Daughter").
- Testing the idea only on friends who like you.

## Verify
- [ ] One-sentence what-if with an irony, stated without a second sentence.
- [ ] Checklist scored; weaknesses named rather than argued away.
- [ ] Genre and two recent comps at the right budget level.
- [ ] Five cold listeners; at least three asked a story question.
