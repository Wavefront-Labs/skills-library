# Writing a scene that turns

**Goal:** every scene changes something. A scene that ends with the story in the same
condition it began is a scene a reader skims and a producer cuts.

## 1. Before writing: the scene card
Fill in on an index card (or the step outline line):
1. **Whose scene is it?** The character who drives it - usually the one with the most to lose.
2. **Objective:** what they want *right now*, stated as a verb against another person
   ("get Marta to sign", not "resolve the tension").
3. **Obstacle:** who or what stops them, and why that opposition is reasonable from its side.
4. **Value at stake** and its charge on entry and exit: alive/dead, trust/suspicion,
   free/trapped. Entry `+`, exit `-` (or the reverse). Same charge in and out = no scene.
5. **The turn:** the moment the charge flips - a revelation (information) or an action
   (a decision). Name it in one line.

## 2. Build in beats
A beat = one exchange of action and reaction (a tactic and its answer). Escalate:
- Beat 1: the polite tactic (ask).
- Beat 2: the tactic changes when the first fails (charm, bargain, threat).
- Beat 3+: cost rises; subtext becomes text, or a secret surfaces.
- Turn: the last tactic triggers the revelation/decision.
Three to six beats is typical for a two-to-three-page scene. If you have one beat repeated
("they argue"), you have a static scene - change the tactic each exchange.

## 3. Enter late, leave early
Start on the first beat that matters (not the knock on the door, not the greeting) and cut
on the turn or the first reaction to it. The next scene starts in the consequence. Readers
feel a late entry as pace; writers feel it as losing their favourite setup line - cut it anyway.

## 4. Exposition rules
- Bury information inside conflict: a character reveals what the audience needs because
  it serves their tactic (a weapon, a bargaining chip), never because the audience needs it.
- If two characters both know it, neither should say it. Introduce an outsider or dramatise it.
- One piece of exposition per scene is enough; the audience holds more than you think.

## 5. Action lines
- Present tense, what the camera sees and hears, no interior states ("she feels sad" -> show
  the behaviour).
- Paragraphs of 1-3 lines; a new paragraph per new image or beat.
- White space is pace. A page of unbroken action reads as slow regardless of content.

## Gotchas
- Two characters agreeing. Someone must want something the other won't give.
- The "dinner scene" with four people and no driver: give one character the objective and
  make the others obstacles or instruments.
- The turn placed in the middle and then discussed for a page. Cut on it.
- Scenes that exist to move characters geographically. Collapse them into a cut.

## Verify
- [ ] Objective, obstacle, value charge in/out and turn are each written in one line.
- [ ] Tactics change at least twice; nothing is repeated.
- [ ] First line is already in the conflict; last line is the turn or its first echo.
- [ ] Nothing is said that both characters already know.
