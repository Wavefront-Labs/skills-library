# Character arc: want versus need

**Goal:** a protagonist built so that plot and change are the same thing. If you can
state the want, the need, the flaw and the wound in one sentence each, the structure
almost writes itself; if you can't, no amount of plotting will fix the second act.

## 1. The four sentences (write these before the outline)
| Element | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Want** (external goal) | The concrete, visible thing they pursue - win the case, get the girl, escape the island. | Could an audience see it achieved on screen? If not, it's not a want. |
| **Need** (internal) | What they must learn or accept to be whole - usually the opposite of the flaw. | Can you state it as a lesson a wise friend would tell them in act one and they'd reject? |
| **Flaw** | The behaviour that costs them and others - the way they get it wrong. | Does it actively sabotage scenes, or is it just a trait? Traits are decoration; flaws cost. |
| **Wound / ghost** | The past event that produced the flaw and the lie they believe ("if I let people in, they leave"). | Does it stay mostly offscreen? Backstory you dramatise in full is a different film. |

Write them for the antagonist too: the best antagonists want the *same thing* as the hero
or hold the lie the hero must shed.

## 2. Map the arc onto the structure
- **Setup (act 1):** show the flaw *in action* and costing something small - not explained in
  dialogue. Plant the lie. The inciting incident makes the want urgent.
- **First half of act 2:** pursuing the want *with* the flaw. Wins happen but the flaw keeps
  the real problem alive.
- **Midpoint:** false victory or false defeat - the want seems achieved or lost, and the need
  becomes visible to the audience (not yet to the character).
- **Second half of act 2:** the flaw now causes losses; allies name the need; the character
  refuses. Low point: the want and the need appear mutually exclusive.
- **Act 3:** the choice - usually giving up the want (or the old way of getting it) to satisfy
  the need. In a **negative arc** they keep the want and lose the need (tragedy); in a
  **flat arc** (action heroes, detectives) the protagonist holds a truth and changes the
  world around them instead - the arc belongs to the supporting cast.

## 3. Per-scene test
For every scene in the step outline, answer: what does the protagonist want *in this scene*,
and how does the flaw shape how they go about it? A scene where the character behaves
without the flaw, before the change, is a scene where the arc is paused - cut it or
re-engineer it.

## 4. Supporting characters earn their place by pressure
Each major supporting character should embody one position on the hero's central
question (the mentor already learned the need; the shadow lives the flaw fully; the love
interest pays for the flaw). If two characters hold the same position, merge them.

## Gotchas
- A want with no deadline or opposition - "be happy", "find himself" - gives the second act
  nothing to push against. Make it concrete and give it a ticking clock.
- Explaining the wound in a monologue in act 1. Hold it; let it leak out in behaviour and
  reveal it when it will cost the most (usually just before the low point).
- Change with no cause: the character "realises" at the low point because the plot needs it.
  The realisation must be bought by a loss that the flaw directly caused.
- Confusing arc with likeability. Unlikeable protagonists work if the want is clear and the
  stakes are felt.

## Verify
- [ ] Want, need, flaw and wound each fit in one sentence and the need is the flaw's opposite.
- [ ] The flaw costs the protagonist something visible before page 15.
- [ ] The midpoint and low point can be described in terms of want vs need, not just events.
- [ ] Climax forces a choice between want and need (or deliberately refuses it).
