# Voice-over and narration in fiction

**Goal:** narration that adds something the images can't - voice, irony, time, a
perspective - rather than describing what's already on screen.

## 1. Choose the narrator (Kozloff)
- **First-person protagonist narrator** (retrospective or present) - voice, confession,
  irony between then and now.
- **Character narrator who is not the protagonist** (witness, friend) - the gap between
  what they understand and what we see.
- **Omniscient third-person narrator** - fable or storybook tone, wry authority.
- **Unreliable narrator** - VO and image disagree; the audience catches the lie.
- **Epistolary** - letters, diaries, voice memos read aloud.
Pick one and keep its rules (who is speaking, from when, to whom).

## 2. What good VO does
- **Counterpoint**: image says one thing, voice another ("I was a happy child," over
  a kid alone at a birthday).
- **Compression**: summarises years in a montage that would take an act.
- **Interiority** the actor can't play: a thought, a lie the character tells
  themselves.
- **Tone**: sets genre and humour quickly.
Bad VO says "I walked into the room" while we watch them walk into the room.

## 3. Placement and dosage
Establish it in the first ten minutes if you're going to use it; introducing VO at
the midpoint feels like a rescue. Use it at the opening, the act breaks and the ending;
don't let it run under every scene. Bookending (open and close) gives it shape.

## 4. Format
`MARTA (V.O.)` for narration and for a character speaking from another place/time
not present in the scene; `(O.S.)` / `(O.C.)` for someone in the scene's space but off
camera. Narration paragraphs follow normal dialogue formatting; long narration over a
montage can be broken with action lines between dialogue blocks.

## 5. The cut-it test
Read the script without the VO. If the story is clear and loses nothing, the VO is
crutch exposition - cut or rewrite it to add counterpoint. If it becomes incomprehensible,
the VO is carrying plot the scenes should carry - fix the scenes. Good VO leaves the
story intact but changes its meaning or flavour.

## Gotchas
- VO introduced late to explain a confusing script (often a note from an edit).
- Narrator who knows things they couldn't, breaking their own rules.
- VO tone inconsistent with the film's tone.
- O.S. vs V.O. confusion on the page.

## Verify
- [ ] Narrator type, time position and addressee defined.
- [ ] Every VO passage adds counterpoint, compression, interiority or tone.
- [ ] Established early; not under every scene.
- [ ] Cut-it test passed; V.O./O.S. formatted correctly.
