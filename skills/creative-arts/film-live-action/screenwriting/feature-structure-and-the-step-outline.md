# Feature structure and the step outline

**Goal:** know the story's shape before writing pages, so the draft has a spine and the
rewrite is revision rather than rescue. For scene-level craft (conflict, subtext,
dialogue, action lines) see `creative-arts.animation.storywriting.screenwriting-craft`,
which applies equally to live action; this skill covers the feature-length frame and
live-action production realities.

## 1. Logline first
One sentence: protagonist + goal + main obstacle + stakes (and the ironic or fresh hook).
If you can't write it, you don't yet know what the film is about. Test it on people: do
they ask "and then what?"

## 2. Act shape and page targets
Standard US-format scripts run very roughly one page per minute of screen time, so a
90–120 minute feature is ~90–120 pages. Common (not mandatory) landmarks for a ~110-page
script:
- **Setup and inciting incident** — the event that disrupts normal life, often within
  the first ~10–15 pages.
- **End of Act 1 (~p. 25–30):** the protagonist commits to the goal; no going back.
- **Midpoint (~p. 55):** a reversal or revelation that changes the nature of the pursuit
  (false victory/defeat, new information, stakes raised).
- **End of Act 2 (~p. 75–90):** the lowest point or crisis; the old approach is dead.
- **Climax and resolution:** the protagonist's final choice, then a short aftermath.
An alternative useful frame is the **sequence approach**: roughly eight 10–15 page
sequences, each with its own mini-goal and turn. Use whichever helps you diagnose;
audiences feel sagging, not paradigms.

## 3. Step outline
A numbered list of every scene in order, one or two lines each:
`14. INT. GARAGE – NIGHT. Maya finds the ledger hidden in the toolbox; realises her
father knew. [Turn: trust → suspicion]`
- Each scene must **turn** something (a value shifts). A scene with no turn is a cut or a merge.
- Mark act breaks and the midpoint on the outline. Check the protagonist drives most
  scenes rather than reacting.
- Expect 40–60+ scenes for a feature; put them on cards to rearrange physically.
- Write the treatment (prose, a few pages) from the outline when you need to pitch or
  get notes before drafting.

## 4. Producibility (the live-action reality)
Every page costs money in live action — unlike animation, locations, cast, night
shoots, weather, vehicles, crowds, animals, children (who have restricted working
hours) and stunts drive the budget. Before drafting, mark scenes with heavy elements.
For a low-budget film: fewer locations, mostly day, small cast, contained action. Keep
those limits as creative constraints, not afterthoughts.

## 5. Rewriting from notes
1. Read all notes, then wait a day.
2. Find the **note behind the note**: "the middle drags" may mean the stakes don't
   escalate, not that scenes need cutting.
3. Sort notes: structural (outline-level), character, scene, line. Fix in that order —
   polishing dialogue in a scene that will be cut is wasted.
4. Revise the step outline first; then the pages.
5. Keep a change log and a version number on the title page; save every draft.
Notes from multiple people that point at the same place are reliable even when their
suggested fixes differ.

## Gotchas
- Drafting from a vague premise and "finding it in the writing" for 120 pages.
- A passive protagonist the plot happens to.
- An overweight Act 2 — check outline page estimates before drafting.
- Over-directing on the page (camera angles in a spec script) — write what we see and hear.

## Verify
- [ ] A one-sentence logline exists and gets an "and then what?".
- [ ] Step outline lists every scene with its turn; act breaks marked.
- [ ] Rough page estimates put landmarks in sensible places for the target length.
- [ ] Production-heavy scenes flagged and justified.
- [ ] Notes sorted structural → line and applied to the outline first.
