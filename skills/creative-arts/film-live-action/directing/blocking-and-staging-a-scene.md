# Blocking and staging a scene

**Goal:** where people are, how far apart, and when they move - chosen so that the beats
of the scene are visible in bodies before a line is spoken, and so the camera can cover
it without breaking the geography.

## 1. Block from the beats, not the set
Take the beat breakdown (see `script-analysis-and-scene-beats`). For each beat, ask:
who has status, who wants proximity and who refuses it, what changes? Then:
- **Distance** = intimacy/threat. A beat shift is often a change of distance.
- **Levels** = status (standing over seated, on the stairs).
- **Facing** = openness. Backs, profiles and turning away are lines of dialogue.
- **Movement on the turn** - the biggest physical change lands on the scene's event.
- **Business** (pouring, packing, cooking) gives the actor a task and the editor cut points;
  it also means a line can be played *against* an action.
Start every scene static in your head; add a move only when a beat motivates it. Three
well-motivated moves beat ten.

## 2. Use the space in depth
Flat, side-by-side staging reads as TV; staging on the diagonal and in depth gives the
camera foreground/background relationships and lets one setup hold two beats. Place the
scene's power position (where the dominant character ends) with the best background.

## 3. Camera and actor together
- Decide the **axis** (the line) from the staging and keep the camera on one side; a
  crossing is permitted when motivated by a move the audience sees.
- Each move should be coverable by a shot that gets better as it happens (an actor
  walking *into* a close-up beats cutting to one).
- Walk-and-talks: block the dialogue to land the turn where the walk stops or the
  direction changes; give the Steadicam/dolly a repeatable path.
- Consider the **oner** when the staging already carries the beats; cover it anyway with
  one safety angle.

## 4. The on-set cycle
1. **Block** with actors on the set, crew watching from the edge (the "marking rehearsal").
   Let actors offer first; adjust. Settle it in 10-20 minutes.
2. 2nd AC sets **marks** (tape on the floor) at each position; 1st AC measures focus.
3. Actors go to hair/make-up; **stand-ins** hold positions while the DP lights.
4. **Rehearse** with camera when lit; one or two passes.
5. **Shoot** - wide/master first usually, then coverage, unless performance argues otherwise.
Changing the blocking after lighting costs the day; change it during step 1.

## 5. Overheads
Draw a floor plan for each scene in prep: positions numbered per beat (A1, A2, B1...),
camera positions lettered. Share with the DP and 1st AD. On the day it's a conversation
starter, not a contract.

## Gotchas
- Staging for the shot list instead of for the scene - the camera serves the staging.
- Two characters seated across a table for four pages. Find a reason for one to stand.
- Moves on every line. Movement loses meaning when constant.
- Blocking that actors can't repeat (improvised wander) - the editor can't cut it and
  focus can't be pulled. Freedom inside fixed marks is the deal.
- Forgetting eyelines when moving actors: a move can put the listener behind the camera.

## Verify
- [ ] Each beat has a physical expression (distance, level, facing or move).
- [ ] The scene's event has the biggest physical change.
- [ ] Camera stays on one side of the line or crosses on a visible move.
- [ ] Marks set and repeatable; blocking locked before lighting starts.
