# Directing the edit and the director's cut

**Goal:** a cut that is the film you shot, not the film you remember shooting - reached
with an editor who stays your collaborator rather than your operator.

## 1. During the shoot
- Send the editor **notes with dailies**: circle takes with the reason, scene intentions,
  what went wrong. The script supervisor's notes carry take status; yours carry meaning.
- Watch the editor's **assembled scenes** every few days. You're checking for coverage
  holes while you can still fix them, not for pace.
- Don't cut yourself and don't direct the assembly line by line. Let the editor make a
  first cut from the script and the material.

## 2. The first assembly
It will be long and bad. Watch it once, alone or with the editor only, start to finish,
without stopping. Write down only *feelings* with timecodes: bored here, confused here,
didn't believe her there. No fixes yet. Then sleep.

## 3. Giving notes on cuts
- Describe the **problem**, not the solution: "I don't believe she'd go back in" rather
  than "use take 4". Let the editor find the fix - they know the material better now.
- Prioritise: structure first (does the story work, scene order), then scenes, then
  performance choices, then rhythm, then frames. Don't trim shots while a reel is still
  in the wrong order.
- Separate the three questions: Is the scene needed? Is it in the right place? Is it cut right?
- Be ready to lose the scenes that were hardest to shoot. Cost of shooting is not an argument.
- Agree a cadence: editor cuts alone for a stretch, then you sit together. Living in the
  edit room all day slows the editor and dulls your eye.

## 4. Screenings
- First screening: trusted filmmakers only, no notes session - just watch the room.
- Later: a wider audience, questionnaires or a facilitated discussion. Listen for *where*
  people lost the thread, ignore *how* they'd fix it. If three people stumble in the same
  place it's real, even if their diagnoses differ.
- Keep a running list of problems each cut solved and created.

## 5. Pickups and reshoots
Decide early - crews and locations disappear. Cut with the shot you wish you had (a card,
a temp shot) to prove it's necessary. Typical pickups: inserts, a reaction, a line of
ADR-able dialogue, an establishing shot. Reshoots need a producer conversation about cost
and should fix *story* problems that no cut solves.

## 6. Delivering the director's cut
Under the DGA Basic Agreement a director has a defined period for a director's cut (e.g.
10 weeks for features after the editor's assembly) and the right to be present for
later cuts. Whatever the contract, lock to the date: an unfinished cut forfeits the
argument. Deliver with a memo of what you'd still change given time.

## Gotchas
- Watching scenes out of context and judging their pace.
- Falling back in love with the script - the audience never reads it.
- Noting performance when the problem is structure.
- Letting a producer give the editor notes directly; one channel.
- Temp music that over-sells a scene so that the cut's weakness is hidden until the composer arrives.

## Verify
- [ ] Editor received intent notes with dailies throughout the shoot.
- [ ] First-assembly notes are feelings with timecodes, not fixes.
- [ ] Each round of notes addresses structure before trims.
- [ ] Pickup list decided while cast and locations are still available.
