# Shot listing and coverage planning

**Goal:** arrive on set knowing exactly what each shot is *for*, so the day is spent
getting performances instead of debating angles — while still giving the editor enough
to build and fix the scene.

## 1. Break the scene into beats
Read the scene and mark each **beat**: the moment the power, intention or information
shifts (a reveal, a decision, a change of tactic). For each beat write what the
audience must feel/understand and **whose scene it is** at that moment. Shots serve beats;
the camera generally gets closer as stakes rise, and goes to the character whose point of
view matters.

## 2. Choose a coverage strategy (on purpose)
- **Master + coverage (traditional):** a wide master of the whole scene, then mediums,
  overs, singles and inserts. Safest for the edit, most time-consuming.
- **Designed coverage / "cutting in the camera":** only the specific shots needed per
  beat. Faster, stronger style, little room to fix in the edit.
- **Oner / long take:** the scene in one moving shot. Demands heavy rehearsal; if one
  element fails, the take fails. Have a protection plan (an insert or cutaway that could
  hide a cut).
- **Two cameras** cross-shooting singles saves time and catches overlap, but compromises
  lighting and lens choice for both.
Match to schedule: a rough planning figure is that a setup (moving camera/lights to a
new position) costs significant time; count setups, not shots. Your 1st AD will tell you
what the day actually allows.

## 3. Block on a floor plan
Draw an overhead plan: walls, doors, furniture, actors' starting positions and moves
(arrows), and camera positions numbered. Then:
- **Axis of action (180° rule):** draw the line between the two characters in a dialogue.
  Keep cameras on one side so screen direction and eyelines stay consistent. Cross it only
  deliberately — with a camera move across the line, a character crossing, or a neutral shot.
- Check **eyelines**: singles should be at matching lens heights and similar sizes for an
  even conversation (unless imbalance is the point).
- Use movement within the scene (actors moving) to give new compositions without new setups.

## 4. Write the shot list
Columns: scene, shot ID, size (WS/MS/MCU/CU/ECU/insert), angle/height, lens (if known),
movement, subject/action, beat it covers, notes (VFX, props, sound). Then **reorder by
setup**: shoot everything facing one direction before turning around (reverses), and
generally wides first (they set continuity and lighting), then tighter.

## 5. Protect the edit
- Get **overlap** in every shot: start and end each piece of coverage with action and
  lines before and after the part you need, so the editor can choose cut points.
- Shoot at least one **insert or cutaway** per scene that could bridge a problem.
- Make sure each beat exists in more than one shot size when using traditional coverage.
- Note the "must-have" shots; if the day collapses, those are shot first.

## Gotchas
- Shot lists of 40 setups for a half-day — prioritise A (must), B (want), C (nice) shots.
- Planning coverage before seeing the location; walk it with the DP and adjust the plan.
- Never-shown geography: viewers get lost if no shot establishes where people are.
- Ignoring the actors: rehearse the blocking with them first; let good instincts change the plan.

## Verify
- [ ] Every beat is marked and assigned at least one shot.
- [ ] Floor plan shows actor moves, camera positions and the axis line.
- [ ] Shot list ordered by setups, with A/B/C priority.
- [ ] Each piece of coverage includes overlap and the scene has at least one cutaway.
- [ ] 1st AD has confirmed the setup count fits the day.
