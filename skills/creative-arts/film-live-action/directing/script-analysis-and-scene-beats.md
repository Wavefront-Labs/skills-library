# Script analysis and scene beats (director's breakdown)

**Goal:** know what every scene is *for* and what happens inside it moment to moment, so
that actor direction, blocking and shot design all serve one reading of the script - and
so you can answer any question on set in one sentence.

## 1. Whole-script pass
Read it three times: once as an audience, once marking every fact (given circumstances:
who, where, when, what has just happened, what they know), once marking questions.
Then write:
- **Spine:** one sentence - what the film is about as an action ("a man tries to buy back
  his dead brother's respect").
- **Through-line per main character:** the super-objective that explains every scene they're in.
- **The central event:** the moment the film exists to deliver. Everything points to it.
- **Facts list vs assumptions list.** Facts are in the text; assumptions are your choices
  and should be made deliberately (and shared with actors only as facts of the world).

## 2. Scene pass - one page per scene
For each scene:
1. **Function:** why the scene exists in the film (reveals X, turns Y, sets up Z). If you
   cannot say, flag it for the writer/producer conversation now, not on the day.
2. **Event:** the single thing that changes. Phrase it as "the moment when...". Shots and
   performance are organised around delivering this moment.
3. **Objectives:** each character's want in the scene, as a verb directed at another
   person ("to make him admit it"). Plus the *obstacle* that stops them.
4. **Beats:** divide the scene where a tactic changes or new information lands. Give each
   beat an active verb for each character (to charm, to test, to threaten, to retreat).
   Three to seven beats per scene is usual.
5. **Adjustments:** the "as if" or the image you'd offer an actor if the beat isn't landing
   (e.g. "as if you're talking to a child"). Prepare two per beat; use none if it works.
6. **Subtext / what is unsaid.** The line under the line.
7. **Questions for the writer**, and your answer if no writer is available.

## 3. From analysis to prep
- The **event** determines the key shot of the scene; the beats determine where the camera
  needs to be *during* each one - this feeds shot listing.
- Beats with a physical change (someone stands, crosses, touches) become the spine of
  blocking.
- Objectives and obstacles are what you give actors in rehearsal; verbs are what you give
  on set when a take is flat.
- Note which scenes share a day-in-story: continuity of emotional state across scenes
  shot out of order needs a written "where is she coming from, where is she going".

## Gotchas
- Analysing in adjectives ("she's angry") instead of verbs ("she wants to shame him").
  Adjectives are unplayable; verbs are directions.
- Deciding the *result* of a scene before finding the *event* - the result is what the
  audience gets, the event is what you shoot.
- Treating backstory as fact when it's your assumption, then arguing with the actor about it.
- Beat sheets so detailed they leave no room for discovery in rehearsal. The breakdown is
  a floor, not a ceiling.

## Verify
- [ ] Spine and each lead's through-line written in one sentence each.
- [ ] Every scene has a function, an event and at least three beats with verbs.
- [ ] Two prepared adjustments per beat for difficult scenes.
- [ ] Questions for the writer resolved before rehearsal.
