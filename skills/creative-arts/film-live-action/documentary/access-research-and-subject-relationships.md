# Access, research and subject relationships

**Goal:** people let you film what matters, for as long as it takes, and still trust
you when the film comes out - because the relationship was built on an honest
description of what you're doing.

## 1. Research until the film changes
- Read everything: press, court records, academic work, forums, local papers; talk to
  people *around* the story (neighbours, ex-colleagues, opponents) before the principals.
  Keep a **research log** (source, date, what they said, what to verify).
- Look for the **story engine**: a process with an outcome (a trial, a season, a
  move, a diagnosis) gives a film shape; "a portrait of X" rarely sustains 90 minutes.
- Identify the **gatekeepers** (a press office, a family matriarch, a union rep) and
  the real decision-makers - they're rarely the same person.
- Research the *visual* world too: what will we see? If the story happens on phones
  and in emails, that's a structural problem to solve now.
- Fact-check as you go (journalism standards apply even to "art" docs): two sources
  for every claim you may put on screen; keep documents.

## 2. Pre-interviews
Phone/video or a coffee, recorded with permission as notes, not for use:
- Ask open questions; listen for how they tell stories, which subjects make them
  animated, what they avoid. The best contributors *talk in scenes*.
- Explain what the film is, how long it takes, what will be asked of them, where it
  might be seen; don't promise approval of the cut (you won't give it) or money (unless
  paying participants is your policy).
- Note practicalities: availability, health, legal exposure, other media deals,
  whether they'd allow verite access or only interviews.
- Decide: principal, supporting, or no. Cast for contrast and for who changes.

## 3. Approaching access
- **Individuals**: in person where possible, introduced through someone they trust;
  bring a one-page description of the film (title, logline, who's making it, funder,
  intended outlets, time commitment). Give time; the second meeting is where yes happens.
- **Institutions** (hospitals, police, schools, companies, courts): a formal proposal,
  a named decision-maker, their legal/press offices; expect an **access agreement**
  covering what can be filmed, patient/student consent procedures, confidentiality,
  security vetting, embargoes, and *review for factual accuracy or safety only* - never
  editorial approval. Push back on "right to review the cut" clauses; offer a viewing
  and a correction process instead.
- **Fixers/local producers** abroad: translate culture as well as language; agree
  fees, credits and safety protocols; they hold relationships after you leave.
- Film the first day small: one camera, one person, no lights - the crew is the
  intrusion.

## 4. Setting expectations (and keeping them)
Say early and repeat: the film is yours editorially; they can withdraw from future
filming at any time but what's shot stays unless agreed otherwise (or state your own
rule); you can't show them the cut for approval but you will show them before release
and listen; the schedule is unpredictable; being in a documentary changes how people
see you afterwards. Write it down (the **consent conversation**, see releases) and
revisit when circumstances change (a legal case, a death, a child's involvement).

## 5. Sustaining the relationship
- Be there when you're not filming; show up to the birthday without a camera.
- Communicate schedule changes yourself, not via a runner.
- Don't become their lawyer, therapist or bank - but know where to refer them (duty of care).
- Keep boundaries about information: what a subject tells you off camera stays off camera
  unless they agree it can be used.
- Watch for **access fatigue** in year two; renegotiate the amount of filming.
- At the end: a screening for them before release; a plan for the publicity period;
  continued contact after.

## Gotchas
- Promising "it'll be positive" to get in the door; the cut can't honour it.
- Editorial-approval clauses signed to secure an institution; the film is now theirs.
- Filming the vulnerable without a gatekeeper's informed consent (minors, patients, prisoners).
- Research that stops when the first great character appears.
- Dropping the subject the day filming ends.

## Verify
- [ ] Research log with sources; story engine identified; facts double-sourced.
- [ ] Pre-interviews done; cast chosen for contrast and change.
- [ ] Access agreements signed with no editorial approval; one-page description shared with every participant.
- [ ] Expectations stated and written; regular contact and a pre-release screening planned.
