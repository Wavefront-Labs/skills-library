# Documentary editing: paper edit, structure and narration

**Goal:** from a hundred hours with no script, a film with a spine, rising questions
and an ending that was earned - built by a method that lets you rebuild the structure
in a day instead of a month.

## 1. Know the material
- **Transcribe everything spoken** (auto-transcription in Premiere/Resolve/Avid
  ScriptSync, or a service; verify names). Transcripts live in a searchable doc or the
  NLE's text panel linked to timecode.
- **Log verite** by scene with a one-line description, who's in it, what happens, what
  it *means* (the subtext), and a quality grade. Screen every frame once; you will not
  have time again, and the moment you don't remember is the one that saves act three.
- Make **selects**: pull every usable statement and every scene moment into selects
  sequences by theme/character; mark the gold in a colour.

## 2. Paper edit and radio cut
- **Paper edit**: in a document or cards, assemble the story from transcript
  excerpts and scene descriptions - the sequence of ideas and events, with timecodes.
  It's cheap to rewrite; move paragraphs, not clips. Several versions on day one are normal.
- **Radio cut**: cut the audio of the paper edit (interviews, key verite sound) with
  no concern for picture. If the story works as radio, it will work; if it doesn't, no
  b-roll will fix it. Listen to it with eyes closed. Interview-driven films live or
  die here.
- Then lay picture: verite scenes as scenes (not illustration), archive where it
  *tells*, b-roll only to cover cuts that need covering (and even then prefer a jump cut
  or a reaction).

## 3. Finding the structure
- **Spine**: what is this film *doing* in one sentence (a man tries to clear his
  name; a town decides whether to close its mine). Every scene should push or complicate it.
- **Questions**: audiences stay for questions, not information. Pose the central
  question in the first 10 minutes; answer small ones while raising bigger ones;
  withhold the biggest until it hurts.
- **Acts** still apply: set-up (world, people, question), complication (the process,
  reversals, the point of no return), resolution (the outcome and what it meant).
  **Character change** - even in an issue film, track one person's change.
- **Chronology vs thematic**: start chronological; break it only when a flashback or
  a thematic grouping earns more tension than the timeline.
- **Openings**: a scene that embodies the film's conflict (not a montage of the
  "beautiful"), then the set-up. Test three openings.
- **Endings**: the last image answers the first; avoid the summary interview.
- Cut long: 2.5 h assembly -> 100 min fine cut; features 75-100 min; series episodes
  per broadcaster (42/58 min), with episode cliffhangers planned from the structure doc.

## 4. Narration and voiceover
Decide the voice: none (verite), **narrator** (third-person), **director as narrator**
(first-person essay), or **presenter**. Narration should do what picture and
interviews cannot - time jumps, facts in a sentence, irony, the author's position -
never describe what we see or repeat what was just said.
- Write to picture, late: draft **scratch VO** (your own voice, or text-to-speech) in
  the timeline from the fine cut; rewrite until each line is shorter; read aloud.
- Lines are short (under 25 words), concrete, present tense where possible; leave air
  after the line for the picture to land.
- Record the final with the narrator watching picture, in a dead room, on a
  broadcast mic (U87/416), with multiple reads per line; the editor conforms line by
  line. Allow for ADR-style pick-ups after notes.
- **Title cards** carry what narration shouldn't (dates, places, an outcome in text);
  keep them consistent and legible.

## 5. Interviews and verite in the cut
- Interviews: cut on thought, hide joins with the other camera, a reaction or a
  cutaway; a jump cut is honest - a hidden one is fine if it doesn't change meaning.
- Verite scenes: let them run; cut for rhythm and clarity, not length. Keep the
  moment that's uncomfortable.
- Archive: motivated by what's said or by a time jump; don't wallpaper.
- Music: structural, not emotional glue; silence is a tool; temp music ruins judgement
  of scenes that work on their own - watch scenes mute.

## 6. Test and revise
Screen rough cuts to people who don't know the story; ask what they think the film is
about, where they were lost or bored, which person they cared about. Treat the
structure document as the master and re-build the timeline from it when the answer is
"move act two". Keep an **ethics pass**: has anyone been made to say what they didn't
mean; is every reordering defensible; does anyone need a right of reply.

## Gotchas
- Cutting from picture first and discovering the story doesn't work as audio.
- Opening with a montage of beauty and a sting; audiences wait 15 minutes for the film.
- Narration that describes the shot; interviews that repeat the narration.
- B-roll "wallpaper" over every interview cut instead of letting faces hold.
- Structure held only in the timeline; a restructure takes weeks.

## Verify
- [ ] Transcripts and verite logs complete; selects by theme/character.
- [ ] Paper edit and radio cut done before picture; story works with eyes closed.
- [ ] Spine stated; central question posed early; one character's change tracked; three openings tested.
- [ ] Narration drafted to picture and cut down; recorded to picture with pick-ups; ethics pass done.
