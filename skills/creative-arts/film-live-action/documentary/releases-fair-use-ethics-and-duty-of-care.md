# Releases, fair use, ethics and duty of care

**Goal:** every person, place and piece of material in the film is there with
documented, informed consent or a defensible legal basis; participants are protected
from harm you could foresee; and the E&O lawyer finds a folder, not a story.

## 1. Releases (the paperwork)
- **Appearance release**: grants the right to use the person's image, voice, name and
  story in the film and its promotion, all media, worldwide, in perpetuity; confirms no
  approval rights; waiver of claims (as far as law allows); signed and dated with
  contact details. Have a plain-language version and translations where needed.
- **On-camera consent**: when paper is impossible (a crowd, a street), record the
  person agreeing on camera after you've explained what the film is; log it. Broadcasters
  accept this if the explanation is adequate.
- **Minors**: a parent/guardian signs, and the child's assent is sought appropriately;
  schools need the school's and each parent's consent; some countries restrict filming
  children entirely.
- **Capacity / vulnerable participants**: if someone may lack capacity (illness, crisis,
  intoxication, dementia), consent must come from an authorised person and be revisited;
  broadcasters require a documented assessment.
- **Location release**: owner's permission to film and to depict the property.
- **Materials release**: for photos, letters, diaries, home movies supplied by participants.
- **Crowd notices** at public events ("filming is taking place; by entering...") plus
  a cut-away policy for anyone who objects.
- Keep a **release log** (name, role, scene/date, type of consent, document ref) that
  matches every face in the cut - the E&O underwriter's first request.

## 2. Informed consent (the substance)
A signature is not consent if the person didn't understand. Informed means they know:
what the film is about (honestly, including that it may be critical), where it may be
shown, that they won't control the edit, the likely effects of appearing (publicity,
reactions in their community, legal exposure), that they may stop filming, and what
happens to footage already shot. Revisit consent when things change (a conviction, a
relapse, a child's involvement) - **ongoing consent** is the broadcaster standard
(Ofcom section 7, BBC guidelines). Record the conversation or make a dated note.

## 3. Legal checks on content
- **Defamation**: anything that lowers a named/identifiable person in the eyes of
  others must be true (and provable), honest opinion on stated facts, or privileged.
  Keep evidence; take legal advice before lock; offer **right of reply** to anyone
  criticised (a written summary of the allegations with reasonable time to respond, and
  include their response or state that they declined).
- **Privacy**: filming in private spaces, medical/sexual/financial matters, and
  identifying people in distress need consent or a public-interest justification;
  in the EU/UK, GDPR applies to footage of identifiable people.
- **Fair use (US) / fair dealing (UK)**: the *Documentary Filmmakers' Statement of
  Best Practices* describes defensible uses (critique, illustration of a point,
  incidental capture, historical sequence); the use must be limited and transformative;
  obtain a **legal opinion letter** per instance for E&O. Music is rarely covered.
- **Anonymisation**: blur/obscure faces, pseudonyms, voice alteration, silhouettes;
  decide *before* filming for sources at risk; make sure metadata, locations and
  recognisable details don't undo it.
- E&O insurance for documentaries will ask for all of the above; start the list in prep.

## 4. Ethics beyond the law
- **Power imbalance**: you leave with a film; they stay with the consequences. Ask
  "would I be comfortable if they saw every frame and knew why I chose it?"
- **Accuracy and context**: no re-ordering that changes meaning, no lines pulled from
  one context into another, reconstructions labelled, archive not used to imply what
  it doesn't show.
- **Payment**: disclosed; paying for access can compromise credibility - decide a policy
  (expenses yes; fees to participants in some traditions, never to interviewees in
  journalism).
- **Showing the cut**: no approval, but a pre-release viewing lets participants prepare
  and flags factual errors; document what was changed and why.
- Conflicts: if the filmmaker's own interests (a brand, a funder with an agenda) shape
  the film, say so on screen or decline the money.

## 5. Duty of care plan
Written before the shoot for any film with vulnerable participants, trauma, or public
exposure (reality-style docs especially):
- Risk assessment per participant (mental health, legal, safety, online abuse).
- **Support**: access to an independent psychologist/counsellor before, during and
  after; a named production contact; clear information about broadcast dates and
  likely attention; social-media guidance.
- **Aftercare**: check-ins for months after release; a path to raise concerns.
- Safeguarding for minors and vulnerable adults (DBS/background checks for crew in
  contact with them, chaperones).
- Crew welfare too: trauma exposure for editors and shooters; debriefs.
Broadcasters/streamers now require this plan as a delivery item.

## Gotchas
- Releases signed before the person knew the film was critical of them - voidable, and unethical.
- A child's consent from a teacher instead of a parent.
- "We'll blur him" - but his tattoo and street name identify him.
- Right of reply offered the day before lock with 24 hours to respond.
- No aftercare; a participant harassed after release with nobody to call.

## Verify
- [ ] Release log complete and matched to every identifiable person, location and material in the cut.
- [ ] Informed, ongoing consent documented; minors/vulnerable participants handled with authorised consent.
- [ ] Defamation/privacy review done; right of reply offered properly; fair-use opinions obtained.
- [ ] Duty-of-care plan written and resourced, with aftercare after release.
