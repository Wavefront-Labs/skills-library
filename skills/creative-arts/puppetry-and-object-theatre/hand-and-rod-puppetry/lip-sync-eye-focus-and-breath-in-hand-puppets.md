# Lip sync, eye focus and breath in hand and rod puppets

**Goal:** make a hand, glove or mouth/rod puppet read as a thinking, breathing
character — the audience should forget your arm exists.

## 1. Lip sync (mouth puppets)
- **One movement per syllable.** Open the mouth on each syllable, close between. Not
  per word, not per letter.
- **Drop the jaw, not the head:** the *lower jaw* (your thumb) opens; the top of the
  head (fingers) stays mostly still. Beginners flip the top of the head back —
  "flapping" — which looks like the puppet is being hit in the chin.
- **Open on the sound**, closing on consonants like m/b/p. Match mouth size to
  volume: shouting = wide, whispering = small.
- Stop moving the mouth entirely when the character stops speaking. Stray movement
  confuses the audience about who's talking.
- Practise to recorded dialogue in a mirror or on camera; speak along slowly, then
  at pace.

## 2. Eye focus
- The puppet's **eyes must look at what it's talking to**: another puppet, an object,
  the audience. Most puppet heads have eyes angled so you must tilt the puppet
  *slightly down* toward the audience to make contact — check from the front.
- **Look, then act:** the puppet sees something, reacts (a beat), then moves or speaks.
  This "thought before action" sells life.
- When two puppets talk, they look at each other while speaking but cheat out toward
  the audience (a three-quarter angle).

## 3. Breath and fixed point
- **Breath:** a small rise/fall before speaking or on emotional beats. A puppet that
  never breathes looks dead between lines.
- **Fixed point:** when the puppet moves its head, keep a reference point in space
  steady (e.g. the eyes stay on a target while the body turns). Constant drift = no
  focus.
- **Stillness** is powerful; a puppet holding still while others move draws attention.

## 4. Height and the playboard
- Keep the puppet at a **consistent height** relative to the playboard (stage edge);
  the classic error is sinking as your arm tires. Pick a reference (e.g. the
  playboard at the puppet's waist) and hold it.
- **Entrances/exits:** "walk" the puppet up an imaginary staircase as it enters, down
  as it exits — popping straight up looks like an elevator.
- Train arm endurance; rest your elbow on a support where the set allows.

## 5. Arm rods and live hands
- **Arm rods** (thin rods to the wrists) are worked by your free hand: gesture follows
  thought, so the head initiates and the hand follows.
- Keep rods hidden in silhouette: move them close to the body, avoid crossing the face.
- **Live hands** (your own hand in a glove) give dexterity for props; match gloves to
  the puppet's colour and keep gestures in the puppet's scale.

## 6. Voice
Pick a voice you can sustain for the whole show without strain; place it close to
your speaking range and vary character with rhythm and attitude more than pitch.

## Gotchas
- Watching your own monitor or mirror too much freezes the puppet; rely on feel.
- Puppet looking at the ceiling (due to tired wrist) is the most common lost focus.
- Uncertain: training traditions differ (European glove puppetry vs. TV mouth-puppet);
  mechanics here are mainly for mouth/rod figures.

## Verify
- [ ] Filmed dialogue shows one jaw drop per syllable, no flapping.
- [ ] Eye contact reads from the audience position.
- [ ] Height consistent over a 5-minute scene.
- [ ] Breath visible before lines and in pauses.
- [ ] Entrances and exits use the staircase walk.
