# Monitor puppeteering and working to frame

**Goal:** give a puppet performance that works for the lens: in frame, on eyeline,
continuity-consistent, with nothing in shot that post-production can't remove.

## 1. Working from a monitor
- On camera, puppeteers usually perform **looking at a monitor**, not at the puppet,
  because the frame is what matters.
- **Flipped vs. unflipped:** some performers prefer a **mirror-flipped** feed (moves like
  a mirror, intuitive); others work with the true camera image. Choose one and keep
  it consistent across the production; switching mid-shoot causes direction errors.
- Position the monitor so you can see it without twisting your puppet arm; floor
  monitors, clip-on monitors or a monitor on a stand in the trench.
- Learn the camera's **frame lines** for each set-up (wide, mid, close-up): ask the
  DP/camera operator to show you the edges.

## 2. Height, eyeline and frame
- **Consistent height:** the puppet's body line in frame must not sink between takes.
  Mark your arm position or use set marks.
- **Eyeline:** the puppet must look at its scene partner or **into lens** when
  intended. Puppet eyes are fixed; tilt to make focus read on camera — check on the
  monitor.
- **Close-ups:** small movements are big on screen. Reduce gesture size and speed.
- Stay **inside frame** for gesture: hands drifting out of frame for no reason look
  odd.

## 3. Lip sync to playback
- Many productions pre-record dialogue and play it back on set; you sync to it.
- One mouth movement per syllable (same principle as live mouth-puppet technique,
  `creative-arts.puppetry-and-object-theatre.hand-and-rod-puppetry.lip-sync-eye-focus-and-breath-in-hand-puppets`).
- Listen for the playback's first syllable cue; anticipate slightly so the mouth opens
  *on* the sound — late sync is the most visible error.
- For live-voice performance, a boom or lav mic is placed on or near the performer,
  not the puppet.

## 4. Rigs, rods and removal
- **Below-frame performance** is the default: set floors are raised or the puppeteer
  works in a trench so the puppet appears at a natural height.
- **Arm rods** in frame: production may paint them out digitally. Minimise crossing
  the puppet's face or body — clean background makes removal cheaper.
- **Green/blue screen** gloves, sleeves or rods help keying; check colour spill on the
  puppet.
- Shoot a **clean plate** (the empty frame) of each set-up for rig removal.
- Coordinate with the VFX supervisor: what's removable cheaply vs. what must be hidden
  in camera.

## 5. Multiple puppeteers
- **Right-hand / assistant puppeteers** operate a second hand or props; they watch the
  same monitor and follow the lead performer's timing.
- Rehearse movement as **choreography** with counts; agree who leads entrances.
- Keep bodies clear of each other and of the camera's moves (dolly, crane).

## 6. Continuity
- Note puppet costume, prop positions, hand and head angles at the end of each take,
  like an actor. Take reference photos for pickups.

## Gotchas
- Long takes are physically hard — arm fatigue lowers height; schedule rest.
- Monitor latency (wireless links) desyncs your lip sync; ask for a wired or
  low-latency feed.
- Uncertain: flipped-monitor preference and rod-removal budgets vary widely by
  production.

## Verify
- [ ] Monitor orientation decided and consistent for the whole shoot.
- [ ] Puppet height and eyeline consistent across takes (playback check).
- [ ] Lip sync lands on syllables with playback audio.
- [ ] Clean plates shot for every set-up needing removal.
- [ ] Continuity notes and photos kept per scene.
