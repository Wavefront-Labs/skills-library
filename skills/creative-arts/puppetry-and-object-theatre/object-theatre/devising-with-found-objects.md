# Devising with found objects

**Goal:** make everyday objects — a teapot, a shoe, a pile of newspapers — into
performers, and build a short piece of theatre from what they suggest.

## 1. Choosing objects
- **Gather widely, then edit:** bring 20–30 objects; spend time handling each one.
- Look for objects with **a front** (a spout, a handle, a hole) — something the
  audience will read as a face or focus point.
- Notice the **object's own qualities**: weight, sound, how it falls, what it was
  used for. These suggest character and story more than a costume would.
- **Associations** count: a ruler suggests a teacher; a matchbox suggests danger or a
  tiny house. Object theatre often plays with what objects *mean*, not only how they
  move.

## 2. Investing an object with life
The same principles as any puppetry, applied to things that weren't designed to move:
- **Focus:** decide where the object's "eyes" are and make it look at things. A tilt
  toward a target reads as attention.
- **Breath:** a small, regular rise and fall (or expansion, for a soft object) when
  "at rest".
- **Thought before action:** look, pause, then move.
- **Weight:** respect the object's real weight and gravity; a heavy object moving
  weightlessly breaks belief.
- **Stillness:** life is clearest in contrast with stillness. Move less than you think.

## 3. The manipulator
- In object theatre the performer is usually visible. Decide the convention:
  - **Neutral manipulator** — eyes on the object, face quiet (as in multi-operator
    puppetry).
  - **Performer-as-character** — the performer narrates, plays alongside or against
    the objects. Many object-theatre pieces move between these deliberately.
- Make each switch clean so the audience knows who to watch.

## 4. Scale and metaphor
- **Scale shifts:** a sugar cube becomes a building; a hand becomes a giant. Combine
  objects at different scales to create landscapes.
- **Transformation:** one object turns into something else (a glove becomes a bird);
  these moments often get the strongest reactions.
- **Metaphor:** a story about war told with toy soldiers and kitchen knives; meaning
  comes from collision of object and subject.

## 5. Devising process
1. **Free play** (10 min per object): find 10 ways it can move, sound, open, fall.
2. **Character test:** give one object a single want (to reach the edge of the table);
   improvise its attempts.
3. **Encounters:** put two objects together and play a meeting — conflict, attraction,
   indifference.
4. **Record** every improvisation on video; review and keep the moments that worked.
5. **Structure:** choose 3–5 strongest moments; find the story arc (setup, complication,
   climax, resolution) that links them.
6. **Fix and rehearse** manipulation like choreography; add sound and light last.
7. Show early to an audience and watch where attention drifts.

## Gotchas
- Too many objects confuse the audience; keep a small "cast".
- Literal miming (making a cup "drink") is often weaker than an unexpected use.
- Fragile found objects break — source duplicates before you fix the show.
- Uncertain: object-theatre conventions vary strongly by company and country.

## Verify
- [ ] Each object has a clear focus point and audience reads where it "looks".
- [ ] Breath and stillness visible in a filmed run.
- [ ] Manipulator convention consistent or switched deliberately.
- [ ] Piece has a clear arc built from tested improvisation moments.
- [ ] Backup duplicates of every key object.
