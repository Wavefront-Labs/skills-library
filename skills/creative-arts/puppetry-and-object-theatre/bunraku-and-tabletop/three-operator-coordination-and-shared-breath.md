# Three-operator coordination and shared breath

**Goal:** several puppeteers move one figure as if one mind drives it, in full view of
the audience, without pulling focus from the puppet.

## 1. The tradition, briefly
In Japanese **bunraku** (ningyō jōruri), large figures are operated by three
puppeteers: the **omozukai** (head and right hand, the senior operator, sometimes
unhooded), the **hidarizukai** (left hand) and the **ashizukai** (feet/legs). The
voices and narration come from the tayū (chanter) with shamisen accompaniment.
Traditional apprenticeship takes many years per role. Western "bunraku-style" or
**tabletop** puppetry borrows the multi-operator principle at smaller scale.

## 2. Dividing roles (tabletop adaptation)
- **Head (lead) operator:** head and torso; decides focus, timing and intention. The
  others follow.
- **Second operator:** hands/arms (often the free hand of the lead operates one hand).
- **Third operator:** feet and hips — controls **weight and contact** with the table.
- Agree **who leads** each sequence; in some scenes the feet operator leads (e.g. a
  run).

## 3. Shared breath and cues
- **Breathe together:** the lead operator takes an audible or visible breath before a
  movement; all three move on the out-breath. Practise with eyes closed, hands on the
  puppet, feeling for the impulse.
- **Physical listening:** stay in light body contact (shoulder to shoulder) so weight
  shifts transmit without words.
- **Cue vocabulary:** tiny pressure signals through the puppet itself (a squeeze at the
  hip = "stop") rather than whispers.
- Warm-ups: mirror exercises, moving a stick between three people's fingertips,
  "group walk" stopping together without a leader.

## 4. Weight and contact
- The puppet must **sit on the table/floor**: feet operator lets weight settle;
  head operator must not lift the figure off.
- **Gravity sells life:** a sigh drops the shoulders, a step transfers weight onto the
  foot. Move from the centre of mass outward.
- Stillness: when the puppet stops, all operators stop — any one operator still moving
  creates jitter.

## 5. Visible-operator focus
- Operators wear neutral (often black) clothing; faces stay **neutral and directed at
  the puppet**. Audience looks where operators look.
- Don't "act" with your face unless it is a deliberate convention of the piece.
- Position bodies behind or beside the puppet; avoid blocking it from the audience.
- Operators move with the puppet's rhythm, not their own (no sudden adjustments of
  footing).

## 6. Tabletop staging
- Table height: around the operators' hip height, standing; operators often in a
  slight squat to keep low.
- Use the **table edge** as horizon and ground line; scale changes (puppet near edge vs.
  far) suggest distance.
- Props should be scaled to the puppet, and positioned so operators can reach.

## Gotchas
- Two leads = a puppet pulled in two directions. Settle leadership before each scene.
- Operators watching each other instead of the puppet breaks the illusion.
- Uncertain: traditional bunraku hierarchy and training details summarised from
  secondary sources.
