# Screen, light and figure setup for shadow theatre

**Goal:** produce crisp, controllable shadows at the scale you want, and manipulate
figures so they act rather than just slide across the screen.

## 1. The screen
- Material: white cotton sheeting or muslin for traditional looks; **rear-projection
  screen** fabric or tracing-paper-type sheets for brightest, most even image.
  Avoid fabrics with visible weave or seams.
- **Tension** it on a frame — wrinkles show as bands. Cotton screens can be dampened
  and stretched.
- Size: work back from audience size and distance; the image must read from the back
  row.

## 2. The light source decides the look
- **Point source** (small LED, halogen bulb, the traditional oil lamp): sharp shadows
  and **dramatic scale changes** — moving a figure toward the light enlarges it and
  softens the edge.
- **Broad source** (fluorescent, large LED panel): soft shadows, little scale change.
  Usually less useful.
- **Overhead projector / LED projector**: shadows plus projected scenery; good for
  layering and colour.
- Rule of thumb: **figure against the screen = sharp, actual size; figure near the
  light = big and fuzzy**. Use this distance as an expressive tool (a giant looming in).
- Place the light **behind and slightly above** the performers' heads so their
  bodies don't cast shadows; or use multiple lights for separate playing zones.
- Prevent spill: black out the backstage area; light leaks wash out contrast.

## 3. Figures
- Cut from card, thin plastic, leather or rawhide (traditional). Translucent dyed
  materials give **coloured shadows**; opaque materials black ones.
- **Articulation:** joints at shoulders, elbows, knees, jaw, using fine rivets or
  knotted thread; gravity does much of the movement.
- **Control rods:** one main rod (often perpendicular to the screen) holding the
  body, thinner rods for hands. Keep rods **perpendicular to the screen** where
  possible so their shadows shrink to a dot.
- Silhouette design: legible **profiles**; exaggerate nose, posture, costume outline.
  Cut-outs (perforations) add detail inside the silhouette.

## 4. Performance technique
- Keep the figure **flat against the screen** for sharp focus; move off it only on
  purpose.
- **Walk** by small bobbing steps along the bottom edge (a "ground line" of tape or a
  rail); the audience reads the bottom edge as the floor.
- Turning: flip the figure for a mirrored profile (needs a two-sided figure or a
  matched pair).
- Use your **hands and bodies** too — human shadows combined with figures are a
  hallmark of contemporary work.

## 5. Tradition: wayang kulit
Javanese and Balinese **wayang kulit** uses intricately cut, painted buffalo-hide
figures with horn rods, performed by a single master puppeteer (**dalang**) who voices
all characters and directs a gamelan ensemble, traditionally with an oil lamp
(**blencong**) and performances lasting through the night. Audiences may watch from
either side of the screen. Treat these traditions with respect: learn from
practitioners and credit sources before adapting.

## Gotchas
- LEDs with multiple emitters produce multiple overlapping edges — use a single-emitter
  "point" LED for sharp work.
- Heat from halogen lamps near paper figures is a fire risk; keep distance or use LED.
- Uncertain: traditional details (e.g. dalang practices) vary by region and lineage.

## Verify
- [ ] Screen taut, evenly lit, no seams or hot spots from audience view.
- [ ] Sharp edges with figures pressed to the screen; deliberate scale changes work.
- [ ] Control rod shadows minimised.
- [ ] No performer shadows or backstage light spill on the screen.
- [ ] Any traditional form used is credited and researched with practitioners.
