# Technical flats for tech packs — drawings a factory can build from

**Goal:** a flat is not an illustration. It is an unposed, proportionally accurate line
drawing of the garment laid flat, front and back, with every seam, stitch and detail
shown so a pattern maker and sample room can build it without guessing. A beautiful
croquis sketch sells the idea; the flat gets it made.

## 1. Set up
- **Tool:** vector (Adobe Illustrator is the industry default; Affinity Designer or
  Inkscape work) so lines stay crisp and edits are fast. Hand flats on a template are
  fine for development but redraw in vector for the tech pack.
- **Template:** draw over a *flat figure template* (a front-view body outline at a
  consistent scale) — not a fashion croquis, which is elongated to ~9 heads and will
  make every garment look longer than it is.
- **Symmetry:** draw half, reflect, join. Asymmetric details go on after the reflect.

## 2. Line-weight hierarchy
Use 3–4 consistent weights (example values, adjust to output size):
- **Outline / silhouette:** heaviest (e.g. 1–1.5 pt).
- **Seams and construction lines:** medium (e.g. 0.5–0.75 pt).
- **Topstitching:** thin *dashed* line set a consistent offset inside the seam
  (dash and gap small, e.g. 1–2 pt).
- **Folds/drape indication:** minimal and thin, or omit — flats show construction, not wrinkles.

## 3. What a complete flat shows
- Front **and** back view, same scale, side by side (add side or inside views when a
  detail cannot be seen otherwise: pocket bags, linings, waistband interiors).
- Every seam, dart, pleat, yoke, panel line.
- Closures: buttons (count and placement), zips (type and length shown), snaps, hooks.
- Topstitching (single/double needle), bartacks, coverstitch hems.
- Hems, cuffs, plackets, collar stand vs collar fall, pocket openings and flaps.
- Hardware and trims drawn to approximate size.
- Rib or knit structures indicated with fine parallel lines.

## 4. Callouts
Short leader lines to labels: "2× needle topstitch 6 mm", "invisible zip CB 55 cm",
"1×1 rib cuff". Keep exact measurements in the tech pack's spec sheet (points of
measure table) rather than cluttering the drawing — the flat says *what*, the spec
sheet says *how big*. Name the views ("FRONT", "BACK").

## Common errors (what sample rooms push back on)
- Proportions copied from the croquis: trouser legs too long, waist too narrow.
- Front and back that don't agree — side seams that land in different places, a back
  yoke with no matching front shoulder seam.
- Missing the back view's centre-back seam, vent or zip.
- Collars drawn as a single shape with no stand/fall break.
- Gathers drawn as a texture instead of a gathered seam with a stated ratio.
- Mixed scales between styles in one range — buyers compare flats side by side.

## Library habit
Keep a component library (collars, pockets, cuffs, plackets, zips, button sets) as
symbols or a swatch/brush set. Redrawing a pocket for every style is how errors creep
in. Version-name files with the style number and date.

Uncertainty: line-weight values above are a working convention rather than a published
standard; brands and factories have their own house templates — always use theirs when given.

## Verify
- [ ] Drawn on a flat template, not a croquis; proportions look like the garment on a table.
- [ ] Front and back present, consistent seam positions.
- [ ] Every closure, seam and stitch type is visible and called out.
- [ ] Line weights follow one hierarchy across the whole range.
- [ ] A colleague can say how each detail is constructed without asking you.
