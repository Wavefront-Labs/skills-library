# Using historical costume as design reference

**Goal:** take silhouette, cut or construction logic from historical dress and translate it
into a contemporary garment without making a costume.

## 1. Identify what you're looking at
- Place the garment by silhouette markers: waistline position, sleeve volume, skirt
  support (crinoline 1850s-60s, bustle 1870s-80s, S-bend corset c.1900, dropped waist 1920s,
  New Look 1947, shift 1960s).
- Date by construction: machine vs hand stitching, closures (hooks and eyes, zips post-1930s
  in mainstream dress), fabric (synthetics post-1940s).

## 2. Go to sources that show cut
- **Janet Arnold, *Patterns of Fashion*** (scaled patterns of surviving garments, 1560-1940).
- **Norah Waugh, *The Cut of Women's/Men's Clothes*** and *Corsets and Crinolines*.
- Museum catalogue records with interior photos; period tailoring manuals (digitised on
  archive.org).
- Fashion plates show the ideal, not the reality; use surviving garments for proportion.

## 3. Translate
Pick ONE historical element per garment and change everything else:
- Keep the **cut**, change fabric and fastening (a 1780s sack-back pleat on a nylon parka).
- Keep the **silhouette**, change construction (bustle volume from a padded technical knit).
- Keep the **detail**, change scale and placement (a 17th-c. slashed sleeve as a whole back).

## Gotchas
- Combining several period elements on one garment reads as costume.
- Many "historical" references online are film costume, not period dress; check provenance.
- Pattern Magic-style historical cuts may need modern ease; toile before committing.

## Verify
- [ ] The reference is dated and sourced from a surviving garment or reliable pattern book.
- [ ] Each design takes at most one or two historical elements.
- [ ] A stranger would read the result as current, not as a period piece.
