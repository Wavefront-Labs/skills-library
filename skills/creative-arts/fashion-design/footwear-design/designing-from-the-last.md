# Designing from the last

**Goal:** in footwear the **last** — the 3D foot-shaped form a shoe is built over —
fixes most of the design before you draw a line: heel height, toe shape, fit and
stance. Good footwear design starts by choosing or briefing the last, then draws the
style on it, not on a flat sketch alone.

## 1. Choose the last
A last is defined by (among others):
- **Heel height / heel pitch:** the last is made for one heel height. You cannot put a
  70 mm heel on a flat last — the foot angle and toe spring are wrong.
- **Toe shape:** round, almond, square, pointed. Pointed toes add length beyond the foot
  ("toe extension") — the last is longer than the size suggests.
- **Toe spring:** how far the toe lifts off the ground; affects roll-off and look.
- **Volume/fit:** instep girth, joint (ball) girth, width fitting.
Factories usually hold a library of lasts; using an existing one is far cheaper than
commissioning a new last (which also needs grading across sizes). Brief the factory with
heel height, toe shape and reference shoes.

## 2. Tape and draw on the last
1. Cover the last in masking tape (overlapping strips, both sides).
2. Mark **centre lines** front and back, the **topline** (opening edge), and key
   points: back height, throat position, joint line (widest part across the ball).
3. Draw style lines directly on the tape: vamp/quarter seam, eyelet facing, straps,
   decorative overlays. Look from every angle — lines that look straight on paper
   curve wildly over the instep.
4. Cut the tape off along centre lines and flatten it onto card: this is the start of
   the **upper pattern** (the "mean form" or shell). Pattern engineering beyond this is a
   specialist step, usually done by a pattern cutter at the factory.

## 3. Upper components (vocabulary to design with)
- **Vamp** (front covering toes/instep), **quarters** (sides and back), **counter**
  (stiffener at the heel), **toe puff** (stiffener in the toe), **tongue**, **eyestay/
  facing**, **lining**, **collar** (padded topline on sneakers), **foxing** and **overlays**.
Each seam is a cost and a potential pressure point; a seam across the flex line (ball of
foot) creases and can rub.

## 4. Sole and construction
Construction decides the look, price and repairability:
- **Cemented** — upper glued to sole. Light, sleek, most common for fashion shoes.
- **Goodyear welt** — upper stitched to a welt, which is stitched to the sole. Heavier,
  resoleable, visible welt edge.
- **Blake stitch** — stitched straight through insole to outsole; slim edge, flexible.
- **Vulcanised** — rubber sole bonded with heat (classic canvas sneakers).
- **Moulded/injected sole units** for sneakers; new unit tooling (moulds per size) is
  a major cost, so designers often adapt an existing open-tooling sole.
Match material and construction: thin, soft leathers need lining and reinforcement;
very stiff materials won't last (pull over the last) without wrinkling.

## Gotchas
- Drawing a beautiful side profile that no last in the factory can produce.
- High heels need a matching **shank** (stiffener under the arch) and correct heel
  placement under the heel bone, or the shoe tips and breaks — a structural issue,
  not just styling.
- Size grading changes proportions: a strap that sits perfectly on a sample size may
  cut across the ankle on the largest size. Check at least smallest/sample/largest.
- Lead time and minimums for new lasts and sole moulds are significant; costs and
  quantities vary by factory (not stated here because they vary too widely).

## Verify
- [ ] Heel height, toe shape and last source decided before detailed design.
- [ ] Style lines drawn on a taped last and viewed from all sides.
- [ ] Seams avoid the flex line or are deliberately reinforced.
- [ ] Construction method and sole unit chosen and suited to materials.
- [ ] Design checked at multiple sizes.
