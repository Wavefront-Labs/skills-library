# Oxford, derby, monk and loafer

**Goal:** design within the classic shoe families precisely: the lacing construction
defines the style, not the decoration.

## The families
| Style | Defining construction | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Oxford (Balmoral) | **Closed lacing**: quarters stitched **under** the vamp; eyelet facings meet | Sleek, formal |
| Derby (Blucher) | **Open lacing**: quarters stitched **on top of** the vamp; facings flap open | More adjustable (high insteps), casual-formal |
| Monk strap | No laces; strap(s) with buckle across the instep (single/double monk) | Between formal and casual |
| Loafer | Slip-on, low vamp, moccasin-style apron or plain; penny, tassel, horsebit | Casual to smart |
| Wholecut | One piece of leather for the upper | Very formal, demands flawless leather |

## Variants
- **Toe caps**: plain toe, cap toe, wingtip (brogue W-shaped cap).
- **Brogueing**: perforations along seams; full brogue (wingtip with medallion), semi-brogue
  (cap with medallion), quarter brogue.
- Formality decreases with more brogueing and with derby vs oxford, brown vs black.

## Design levers
Toe shape and last, vamp length, eyelet count (5 typical for oxfords), facing shape,
welt and sole thickness, heel height (~2.5-3 cm for men's dress).

## Gotchas
- Calling any lace-up "oxford": the closed lacing is the defining feature.
- Brogueing on a sleek last can look unbalanced; heavier lasts suit full brogues.
- High-instep customers struggle with oxfords; derby is the fit-friendly option.

## Verify
- [ ] Lacing construction stated correctly for each design.
- [ ] Toe cap and brogue variant specified.
- [ ] Last and heel height consistent with formality.
