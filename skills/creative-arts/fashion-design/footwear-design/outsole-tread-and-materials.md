# Outsole tread and materials

**Goal:** outsoles that grip, flex and wear well for the intended use, and that the factory can mould.

## Materials
| Material | Character | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural/synthetic rubber** | Best grip and durability; heavier | Sneakers, boots, work |
| **Blown rubber** | Lighter, softer, wears faster | Running forefoot |
| **EVA** (ethylene-vinyl acetate) | Very light, cushioned; poor abrasion as outsole | Midsoles, casual and slides |
| **TPU** | Tough, abrasion-resistant, can be clear | Heel tips, cleats, shanks |
| **PU** | Light, direct-injected | Work, fashion |
| **Leather** | Classic, breathable, slippery when new | Dress shoes |
| **Crepe** | Natural rubber sheet, soft | Desert boots |

- Rubber hardness quoted in **Shore A** (55-70A typical outsole).

## Tread design
- Lugs: depth 2-5 mm casual/sneakers, 5-8 mm+ hiking.
- Siping (thin slits) improves wet grip on smooth surfaces.
- Flex grooves under the ball (metatarsal) line let the sole bend where the foot does.
- Self-cleaning: lugs spaced so mud releases.
- Heel area: hard-wearing compound or zones (dual-density).

## Testing
- Slip resistance: **ISO 13287 / SATRA TM144** coefficient of friction on tiles/steel with
  water or glycerol; work footwear needs ratings (SRA, SRB, SRC).
- Abrasion: DIN/ISO 4649 (mm³ loss).

## Gotchas
- Gum rubber marks floors; non-marking compounds exist.
- Deep lugs on fashion soles add weight and cost.
- Leather soles need a rubber heel top-piece or they wear fast.

## Verify
- [ ] Compound and hardness specified.
- [ ] Flex groove at the ball line.
- [ ] Slip test results meet target.
