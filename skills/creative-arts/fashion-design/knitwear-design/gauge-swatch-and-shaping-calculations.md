# Gauge swatch and shaping calculations

**Goal:** a knitted garment is built stitch by stitch, so every measurement on the
design must become a stitch count and a row count. Those come only from a real,
finished swatch. Skip it and the garment comes out the wrong size, length or drape.

## 1. Knit a proper swatch
- Use the **exact** yarn, colour (dyes change yarn behaviour), stitch structure and
  machine/needle size of the final garment.
- Hand knit: at least ~15 × 15 cm so you can measure 10 cm well inside the edges.
  Machine: knit the swatch at the intended tension/stitch length, with a few rows of
  waste yarn and marked counts.
- **Finish it like the garment:** wash, dry and block (or steam) as the care label will
  say. Many yarns relax, bloom or shrink substantially; measuring a raw swatch is the
  classic error.
- Rest it flat (overnight if you can), then measure without stretching.

## 2. Measure and convert
Count stitches and rows across 10 cm in two or three places and average. Example:
22 sts and 30 rows per 10 cm = 2.2 sts/cm and 3.0 rows/cm.

- **Width:** stitches = finished width (cm) × sts/cm. A 50 cm front at 2.2 = 110 sts.
  Round to suit the stitch pattern repeat (e.g. a multiple of 4 for 2×2 rib) and add
  edge/selvedge stitches for seaming.
- **Length:** rows = length × rows/cm. A 60 cm body at 3.0 = 180 rows.

## 3. Space the shaping
To decrease from 110 sts to 90 sts over 20 cm (60 rows): 20 sts to lose = 10 decrease
rows if decreasing one stitch each side. 60 rows ÷ 10 = decrease every 6th row.
When it doesn't divide evenly, alternate (e.g. every 5th and 6th row) and write it out
row by row. **Fully fashioned** shaping (decreases a few stitches in from the edge) leaves
visible marks used as a design detail; edge decreases are cleaner for seaming.

## 4. Knit-specific design thinking
- **Ribs pull in.** A rib at the same stitch count as the body will be narrower; a
  welt or cuff is commonly knit on fewer stitches or tighter. Swatch the rib too.
- **Stitch structures change gauge:** cables pull in, lace opens out, tuck and
  intarsia change row count. Swatch each structure; a mixed garment needs a swatch per
  structure.
- **Knit drapes and grows.** Heavy or long garments lengthen when worn; dense
  structures resist it. Hang the swatch with a weight for a day for long pieces.
- **Negative ease** is normal for fitted knits (garment smaller than the body);
  woven intuition about ease does not apply.
- **Machine gauge** (needles per inch on the needle bed, e.g. 3gg chunky to 12gg+
  fine) decides the yarn weight range. Confirm the factory's machine gauges early;
  you can't design a 14gg fine-knit for a factory that runs only 7gg. (Industrial
  flatbed brands include Shima Seiki and Stoll; programming is a specialist step not
  covered here.)

## Gotchas
- One swatch for a multi-colour yarn batch — dye lots differ; swatch the production lot.
- Measuring on the needle or while stretched.
- Forgetting seam stitches — each seam eats about a stitch per side.
- Not recording swatch data; label every swatch with yarn, needle/machine, tension,
  finishing and the measured gauge.

## Verify
- [ ] Swatch in the final yarn/structure, washed and blocked as the garment will be.
- [ ] Gauge measured in several places and averaged; written on a swatch tag.
- [ ] Every key measurement converted to stitches/rows, rounded to the pattern repeat.
- [ ] Shaping spaced row-by-row, adding up exactly to the target count.
- [ ] Rib and each special structure swatched separately.
