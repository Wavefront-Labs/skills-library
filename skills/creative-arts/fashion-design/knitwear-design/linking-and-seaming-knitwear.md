# Linking and seaming knitwear

**Goal:** choose the make-up method that gives the quality, stretch and cost the knit
garment needs.

## Methods
| Method | How | Quality | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linking (point-to-point) | Each loop of one edge is placed on a linking machine point and chained to the matching loop of the other piece | Flat, elastic, invisible, premium | Fully fashioned seams, collars, trims, toe closing |
| Cup seaming | Edges butted between two cups and chain-stitched | Flat-ish, elastic | Side and sleeve seams in fully fashioned knits |
| Overlock (514) / safety stitch | Cut-and-sew seams | Bulkier, less elastic, cheap | Cut-and-sew knitwear |
| Mattress stitch (hand) | Hand seam | Invisible | Hand knits, samples |
| Wholegarment | No seams | Seamless | Knitted in one piece |

## Fully fashioned vs cut-and-sew make-up
- Fully fashioned panels have selvedge edges -> linking or cup seaming; minimal waste.
- Cut-and-sew panels are cut from knitted lengths -> overlock; edges must be secured
  against laddering.

## Quality checks
- Seams stretch with the fabric (pull test) without cracking threads.
- No dropped loops at linked seams; loops matched one-to-one (no puckers).
- Stripes and patterns match at seams.
- Ends tidied and secured (darned in, not knotted).

## Gotchas
- Linking needs skilled labour and is slower; budget it.
- Overlocking fine-gauge luxury knits looks cheap and bulky.
- Thread for linking must match yarn colour and have stretch.

## Verify
- [ ] Make-up method specified per seam.
- [ ] Pull test passed at seams.
- [ ] Stripe/pattern matching checked.
