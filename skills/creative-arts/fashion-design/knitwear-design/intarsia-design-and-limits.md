# Intarsia design and limits

**Goal:** large colour areas or motifs knitted as a single-layer fabric with no floats,
knowing the machine constraints.

## How it works
Each colour area has its own yarn (carrier). The yarn knits only within its area; at the
colour boundary the adjacent yarns **interlock** (tuck or knit together) to prevent holes.
Result: single-thickness fabric, same weight as plain, no floats - unlike jacquard.

## Good uses
Argyle, large motifs, colour blocks, logos, picture knits.

## Limits
- **Carriers per row**: each colour area crossing a row needs a carrier; machines have a
  finite number (often 8-16+ on intarsia-capable V-beds). Two areas of the same colour in one
  row need two carriers.
- Knitting is slower; cost rises with carrier count.
- Small dots or thin lines (1-2 stitches) are hard; use embroidery (Swiss darning) after
  knitting instead.
- Vertical edges are clean; diagonals step by stitch.

## Design steps
1. Draw at stitch resolution on a grid (correct aspect ratio).
2. Count colour areas per row; minimise carriers by merging areas or using jacquard
   for small repeating details.
3. Mark joins and specify join type with the programmer.

## Gotchas
- Holes or loose stitches at colour joins if interlock is not set correctly.
- Hand-finishing (tidying ends) adds cost for hand intarsia.
- Very many colours make the garment expensive; check against target cost.

## Verify
- [ ] Carriers per row within machine limits.
- [ ] Small details moved to embroidery or jacquard.
- [ ] Swatch shows clean joins without holes.
