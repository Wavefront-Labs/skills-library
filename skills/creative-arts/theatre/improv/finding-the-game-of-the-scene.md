# Finding the game of the scene

**Goal:** make a two-person improv scene funny on purpose rather than by luck,
by establishing a normal world, noticing what's unusual in it, and repeating and
escalating that unusual thing.

The terms here come mainly from the UCB school of long-form; other traditions
(Chicago "relationship-first", Keith Johnstone's status and storytelling work)
emphasise different things. This is one reliable engine, not the only one.

## 1. Base reality in the first three lines
Answer **who** (relationship), **what** (activity) and **where** fast, ideally
through specifics, not exposition:
- Weak: "Hi, I'm your brother. We're in a kitchen."
- Strong: "Danny, you've been stirring that risotto for forty minutes and Mum's
  plane lands at six."
Both players **yes-and**: accept every established fact and add one. Denial
("We're not brothers") kills the shared reality and the audience's trust.

## 2. Spot the first unusual thing
Listen for the first thing that breaks the expectation of this base reality —
a strange behaviour, an absurd belief, an overreaction. It's often unplanned:
a slip of the tongue or an odd choice. When you hear it, **point at it**: a
reaction from the "straight" character ("You're stirring it with your hand?")
tells the audience *this* is what the scene is about.

## 3. Name the game (to yourself)
Turn the unusual thing into a pattern you can repeat: "Danny treats risotto as
a sacred ritual." Ask:
- **"If this is true, what else is true?"** — explore. Why would he? What does it
  cost him? (He's skipped work, he has a risotto shrine.)
- **"If this happened, what happens next?"** — heighten. Each repetition goes
  further. (He won't let Mum in until it's done; he calls the fire brigade
  to keep the heat steady.)
Each **game move** should be bigger or more specific than the last, and grounded in
the same logic. Three to five beats is typical before an edit.

## 4. Roles in the scene
- The **straight man / grounded character** keeps base reality and reacts as a
  real person would; the contrast is where the comedy lives.
- The **unusual character** commits completely and justifies the behaviour.
Both are equally important. Two "weird" characters with no one grounding them
usually produce noise, not comedy.

## 5. Edits
End the scene on a laugh at the peak of the game. In long form, an off-stage
player **sweeps** across the front (sweep edit) or taps out a player (tag-out)
to jump to a new scene or a time-dash. Edit early rather than late: a scene that
"has nowhere to go" usually needed an edit two beats ago.

## Drills
- **First-line scenes:** start with one specific line; partner must yes-and with a
  fact about the relationship.
- **Point at it:** coach freezes the scene at the first unusual thing; players name it.
- **Game in three:** do the same game three times, escalating each time.

## Gotchas
- **Gagging / going for the joke** too early, before base reality exists — there's
  nothing to be unusual against.
- **Switching games** mid-scene ("and now he's also a vampire") — muddies the pattern.
  Find one; play it.
- **Asking questions instead of adding information.** "What are you doing?" makes
  your partner do all the work. Make a statement.
- **Transaction scenes** (buying, ordering) default to boring; the relationship
  is usually more interesting than the transaction.
- **Safety and consent:** physical contact and sensitive content are agreed in
  advance in most ensembles; respect the team's rules.

## Verify
- [ ] Who, what and where are established within the first few lines, by specifics.
- [ ] The first unusual thing is identified and reacted to.
- [ ] You can state the game in one sentence.
- [ ] Each repetition escalates within the game's logic.
- [ ] The scene edits on a laugh, not after the energy dies.
