# Costume renderings and the costume plot

**Goal:** design what every character wears in every scene, communicate it clearly
enough that the costume shop can build, buy or pull it, and make sure the actors
can actually get in and out of it in time. (Constructing garments is a
handcrafts/sewing skill.)

## 1. Script breakdown → costume plot
Read the play and build a grid: **characters down the side, scenes across the top**.
In each cell record: on stage or not, time/season/occasion, any action that
affects clothing (gets wet, fights, undresses on stage, ages ten years). Mark
every **change** and the time available for it (count the pages or ask stage
management for timings from runs).
This plot is the backbone of the budget and the wardrobe plan; update it as
blocking changes.

## 2. Research
- Period and place: primary sources (paintings, photographs, fashion plates,
  catalogues, museum collections) beat costume-movie memory.
- Social research: class, occupation, money — a character's clothes say how much
  they earn, care and conform.
- Collect research into a board per character and one for the world of the play.
Decide with the director how literal the period should be; "inspired by" gives
freedom but must be consistent across the cast.

## 3. Silhouette and palette first
- Design the **whole stage picture**: line up all characters' silhouettes in one
  row. Groups (families, factions, classes) should read at a glance from the back
  row through shape and colour.
- Build a colour plan with the scenic and lighting designers — test swatches under
  the show's light colours; strong gels and LED colours can shift fabric
  dramatically.
- Save the strongest contrast for the characters who need focus.

## 4. Renderings
- One rendering per costume (look), full figure, in a pose that suggests character.
- Media: traditional (pencil, watercolour, gouache, markers) or digital (Procreate,
  Photoshop). Readability matters more than polish.
- Annotate: fabrics, closures, details hidden in the drawing (back views),
  accessories, wigs/hair, shoes. Attach **fabric swatches** once chosen.
- Note *stage requirements*: pockets for props, mic pack placement, knee pads for
  falls, reinforced seams for fights, washable vs dry-clean for daily maintenance.

## 5. The quick-change plan
For each change shorter than a few minutes:
- Simplify fastenings (hook-and-loop, magnets, zips instead of buttons); pre-rig
  layers (wear the next costume under).
- Specify a **quick-change booth** location and dressers.
- Rehearse quick changes in tech with the real costumes; time them.

## 6. Fittings and the costume bible
At fittings, check movement (sit, kneel, reach overhead, fight), sightlines from a
distance, and comfort. Photograph each fitting. Keep a **costume bible**: renderings,
plot, swatches, measurements, sources and running/maintenance notes — essential
for understudies, replacements and remounts.

## Gotchas
- **Budget**: bought, pulled from stock, altered and built cost very differently.
  Mark each piece in the plot and get the shop's estimate early.
- **Actors' bodies** change and casting may change; design for the person cast, not
  the sketch.
- **Shoes** are often ordered last and arrive late; they affect posture, sound and
  safety (raked stages). Order early, rehearse in them.
- Fabric that looks great in hand may vanish or glow under stage light; always
  check swatches on stage.

## Verify
- [ ] Costume plot lists every character in every scene, with change times.
- [ ] A line-up of all silhouettes reads clearly by group from a distance.
- [ ] Each rendering has annotations, back views where needed and swatches.
- [ ] Every quick change is timed in tech and achievable.
- [ ] Swatches are checked under show lighting.
