# Period silhouette and costume research

## Silhouette first
The overall shape of the body in clothing is the strongest period signal from
the back of the auditorium. Examples (Western fashion, broad strokes):
- 1770s: wide hips via panniers, conical stays.
- 1795-1820: high (empire) waist, columnar skirts.
- 1830s: wide sleeves (gigot), low wide shoulders, bell skirts.
- 1850s-60s: crinoline (cage) skirts, dome shape.
- 1870s-80s: bustle (two phases), narrow fronts.
- 1890s: leg-of-mutton sleeves, hourglass.
- 1900s: S-bend corset, pouter-pigeon front.
- 1910s: hobble skirts, higher waist; 1920s: dropped waist, straight tubular;
  1930s: bias-cut, natural waist; 1940s: padded shoulders, rationing;
  1950s: New Look (cinched waist, full skirt) or pencil.
Men's tailoring changes too (collar heights, coat cut, trouser width, hats).

## Foundations create the shape
Corsets/stays, petticoats, crinolines, bustles, padding, hip rolls, girdles,
bullet bras — the silhouette comes from underneath. Budget and fit them first;
actors need them in rehearsal.

## Primary sources
- **Museum collections online**: V&A, Met Costume Institute, FIDM, Kyoto Costume
  Institute, national museums — extant garments with dates and details.
- **Photographs** (from 1840s on), including everyday people, not just fashion.
- **Paintings and portraits** (with caution: artists idealise).
- **Fashion plates and magazines** (aspirational, not average).
- Pattern books (Janet Arnold, *Patterns of Fashion*; Norah Waugh).
Prefer primary over secondary sources; record citations on the research board.

## Adapting for stage
- Decide the **accuracy level** with the director (exact period, period
  "flavour", modern reinterpretation).
- Consider movement (fights, dance), quick changes, budget, and actor comfort.
- Class, age, region and character choices: not everyone wore the latest fashion.

## Gotchas
- Mixing decades unintentionally (1850s skirt with 1870s bodice).
- Skipping foundations to save money — garments won't hang right.
- Using film costumes as research — they're designs, not sources.
