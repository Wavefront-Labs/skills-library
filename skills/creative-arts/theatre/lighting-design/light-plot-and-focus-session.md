# Light plot and focus session

**Goal:** get from an idea about how the show should look to a rig that is hung,
patched and focused so cueing can start on time.

## 1. Concept in terms of light
For each scene, write: time of day, source (sun, window, practical lamp, abstract),
mood, and what must be seen (faces, a prop, the whole stage). Use reference images.
Identify the **looks** the show needs; each look ends up as a cue or a cue state.

## 2. Area coverage (a starting system)
Divide the acting space into **areas** roughly 2.5–3.5 m across (depends on fixture
beam size and throw). For each area, the classic McCandless approach: two front lights
at about 45° above and 45° to each side of the actor, often one warm and one cool.
Common additions:
- **Backlight** to separate actors from the set (strong in dance and musicals).
- **Sidelight** from booms in the wings — sculpts bodies, essential for dance.
- **Top light**, specials (a single chair, a doorway), washes and texture (gobos).
Overlap areas so actors don't drop into dark gaps when they cross. Check face
visibility: front light below roughly 30° flattens and blinds; above about 60°
puts eyes in shadow.

## 3. Fixtures and colour
- Profiles/ellipsoidals (shaped, hard or soft edge; shutters and gobos), Fresnels
  (soft wash), PARs, and LED versions of each; moving lights for flexibility.
- Pick lens/beam angle by throw distance: beam diameter ≈ 2 × throw × tan(angle/2).
- Colour with gel (by manufacturer number) or LED mixing. Remember LED fixtures
  render skin and costume differently from tungsten — test with the costume team.

## 4. The light plot and paperwork
- Draw the plot over the venue plan (often 1:25 or 1/2"=1'-0") in Vectorworks
  Spotlight or similar. Each fixture symbol shows **unit number, channel, colour,
  gobo, focus purpose**.
- Produce a **channel hookup** (channel → units, position, purpose, colour) and an
  **instrument schedule** (position → units). Tools like Lightwright keep them in sync.
- Include circuits/DMX addresses and power. Check rig weight and power with the
  production electrician.

## 5. Running focus
- Prepare **focus charts**: for each unit, the hot-spot position (stage coordinates
  from centre line and setting line), beam edges, shutter cuts.
- Order the session by position so the crew moves efficiently (one bar, then the next).
- Stand in the hot spot facing the light (a **focus walker** helps); use the light
  on your face and shadow to judge, not the floor.
- Bring up one unit at a time; shutter off the set, proscenium and audience.
- Record final focus (photos per unit help) — someone will need to re-focus after
  touring or a bump.

## Gotchas
- **Time**: focus usually takes longer than scheduled. A common estimate is a few
  minutes per conventional unit (unconfirmed for your crew); plan accordingly.
- **Set changes** after focus mean re-focus. Lock set positions first.
- **Reflective floors** bounce light upward and into eyes; check floor finish early.
- Dimmer curves and LED fades differ — what fades smoothly on tungsten can step on
  some LEDs at low levels.
- Safety: working at height (ladders, MEWPs, catwalks) follows venue rules; safety
  bonds (secondary suspension) on every fixture.

## Verify
- [ ] Every acting area has front coverage from two sides plus separation from behind or the side.
- [ ] Plot, hookup and instrument schedule agree unit by unit.
- [ ] Every unit has a focus chart entry and a recorded final focus.
- [ ] No light spills onto the audience or proscenium unless intended.
- [ ] Faces are readable from the extreme seats in every look.
