# Reading First Folio clues

The **First Folio** (1623) is the first collected edition of Shakespeare's plays.
Some practitioners (Patrick Tucker's *Secrets of Acting Shakespeare*, Neil Freeman's
Folio editions) argue its punctuation, capitals and spellings preserve
theatrical clues that modern editors smooth away. Scholarly caveat: much of it
reflects compositors' habits, so treat clues as **options**, not instructions.

## Where to see it
Free facsimiles: Bodleian First Folio (firstfolio.bodleian.ox.ac.uk), Folger
Digital Collections; Applause First Folio or Freeman's editions in modern type.

## Procedure
1. Put your modern edition side by side with the Folio text for your speech.
2. Note differences:
   - **Punctuation**: Folio may have colons and full stops where modern editions
     have commas, or vice versa. Folio "heavy" punctuation (: ;) can suggest
     a shift in thought; long unpunctuated runs can suggest racing thought.
   - **Capitals** on unexpected words (*Honour*, *Blood*) — possibly
     emphasis or an important concept.
   - **Spellings**: variants (*mee* vs *me*, *doe* vs *do*) — some practitioners
     read extra letters as lengthening/emphasis.
   - **Line breaks**: different lineation may turn prose into verse or show
     shared lines.
   - **Speech prefixes and stage directions**.
3. **Prose-verse shifts**: note where a character moves between verse and
   prose (e.g. Hamlet with the players vs his soliloquies; Lear's madness).
   Typical conventions: prose for comic, low-status, informal or mad speech;
   verse for heightened or formal. A shift signals a change in state or
   relationship — play it.
4. Try the speech using Folio punctuation and observe what changes. Keep what
   illuminates; discuss with the director/voice coach.

## Gotchas
- Asserting Folio "is what Shakespeare meant" — uncertain.
- Mixing editions in a company without agreement — confusion over cues.
- Ignoring Quarto texts for plays with significant variants (*Hamlet*, *Lear*).
