# Projection and vocal health for the stage

**Goal:** be heard and understood at the back of the house for eight shows a week,
without hoarseness. Projection is mostly *breath + resonance + articulation*, not
volume pushed from the throat.

Not medical advice: persistent hoarseness (longer than about two weeks), pain,
or sudden voice loss needs an ENT / laryngologist, ideally one who sees performers.

## 1. Breath: low and wide, not high and tight
- Stand with weight balanced, knees unlocked, neck long. Tension in jaw, neck or
  shoulders transfers straight to the larynx.
- Inhale so the lower ribs and belly expand (front, sides and back); shoulders
  stay still. Exhale with a steady "sss" and time it — 20–30 s is a reasonable
  working target, more with practice.
- **Support** means a controlled, steady release of breath from the lower torso
  muscles, not tightening the belly. Think "steady stream", not "push".
- Breathe at thought changes (punctuation helps), not mid-phrase; running out of
  air is when people squeeze the throat.

## 2. Resonance carries further than volume
- Hum on "mm" and feel buzz in lips and face; move to "mm-ah", keeping the buzz
  forward. A forward, buzzy sound carries with less effort than a louder, throatier
  one.
- Open the throat space: a relaxed jaw drop, the feeling of the start of a yawn.
- **Speak to a point** at the back wall (or the last row), not to your scene
  partner's face — while keeping eye contact with them. Intention to reach is half
  of projection.

## 3. Articulation for distance
Consonants carry meaning; vowels carry tone. In a large house, finish final
consonants ("wan**t**", "han**d**") and avoid dropping the ends of lines in pitch and
energy — the most common note actors get. Drills: tongue twisters slowly then at
pace; whispered text so the consonants alone carry (a stage whisper must still be
supported).

## 4. Warm-up (15–20 minutes before every show)
1. Physical release: shoulder rolls, neck stretches, spine roll-down.
2. Breath: three or four long "sss" or "fff" releases.
3. Lip trills and tongue trills sliding through the range (low effort, high return).
4. Humming and sirening on "ng" low-to-high and back.
5. Articulators: tongue stretches, jaw massage, twisters.
6. Speak a passage of your own text into the empty house.
Cool down after: gentle sirens and hums at low volume.

## 5. Shouting, screaming and crying on stage
These are acted, not real. Reduce actual volume and keep support; let the
emotional intensity come from the release and the face. Rehearse them marked
(at reduced volume) and save full use for runs. If a role needs repeated screams,
work with a voice coach to set a safe version, as with any stunt.

## 6. Vocal health on a run
- Hydrate steadily through the day; steam inhalation (plain water) helps dry
  vocal folds. Theatre air is often dry.
- Don't whisper to "save" your voice — a forced whisper can strain it. Speak
  gently or rest.
- Avoid shouting over noise (bars, after-parties). It's the classic run-killer.
- Reflux, smoking and alcohol all irritate; dairy's effect is individual and
  disputed.
- Sleep. Fatigue shows in the voice first.
- If hoarse: reduce load, warm up gently, tell stage management early so an
  understudy can be ready.

## Gotchas
- "Speak from the diaphragm" is shorthand — you can't directly feel the diaphragm;
  the felt work is in the abdominal and rib muscles.
- **Mics change the job.** In amplified shows, over-projecting into a body mic
  distorts; work with the sound team on level.
- Coaching lineages (Linklater, Fitzmaurice, Berry, Rodenburg, Estill) disagree on
  details; pick one consistent approach rather than mixing contradictory cues.

## Verify
- [ ] Someone in the last row can follow your text, including line endings, in a run.
- [ ] You can sustain a supported "sss" for 20+ seconds without shoulder lift.
- [ ] You warm up before every show and cool down after.
- [ ] After a two-show day your voice is tired at worst, not hoarse or painful.
- [ ] Screams and shouts are set, marked in rehearsal and do not hurt.
