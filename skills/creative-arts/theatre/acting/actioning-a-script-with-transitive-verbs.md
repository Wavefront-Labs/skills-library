# Actioning a script with transitive verbs

**Goal:** give every thought you speak a playable intention aimed at your scene
partner, so each line *does* something rather than *describes* something.
Practice codified in UK rehearsal rooms by Max Stafford-Clark (Out of Joint) and
in Marina Caldarone and Maggie Lloyd-Williams' *Actions: The Actors' Thesaurus*.

## The rule
Each **thought** (not each sentence) gets one transitive verb that fits the frame
**"I ___ you"** (or "I ___ him/her/them" when talking to a third party).
- Transitive: it needs an object and changes them. *I reassure you, I needle you,
  I seduce you, I belittle you, I galvanise you.*
- Not states or descriptions: "I am angry", "I explain", "I tell" are not
  playable — they do nothing to the other person. "Explain" can become *I
  enlighten you*, *I patronise you*, *I educate you*.

## Procedure
1. **Mark thoughts.** Pencil a slash where the thought changes. A long speech may
   be 1 thought or 12. Full stops, dashes and change of subject are clues.
2. **Know the objective first.** The action is the tactic serving the scene's
   objective (see `creative-arts.theatre.acting.breaking-a-scene-into-units-and-beats`).
   "I want her to stay" → actions might run *I charm / I guilt / I threaten*.
3. **Write the verb above the thought**, in pencil. Use a thesaurus to sharpen:
   *encourage* → *embolden*, *buoy*, *spur* each play differently.
4. **Test it on your feet.** Say the line to your partner while playing *only* the
   verb, then try a contrasting verb on the same line. Keep the one that moves the
   partner most specifically and makes sense of their response.
5. **Watch the partner's line.** Your action should be readable in what they say
   next. If their reply makes no sense after your action, the action is wrong.
6. **Check variety.** Read down the column of verbs: if every thought is *I attack
   you*, the scene has no shape. Real people change tactics when one fails.
7. **Then let go.** In performance you play the partner, not the list. Actions are
   rehearsal scaffolding; they come back as notes ("you've lost the *needle* on
   that line").

## Group actioning (Stafford-Clark style)
Directors sometimes action the whole play round the table in week one: the
company agrees a verb for each thought before standing up. It is slow (often days)
but gives a shared vocabulary for notes. Agree in advance whether actors may
propose alternatives later.

## Gotchas
- **Adverbs creep in** ("I angrily dismiss you"): drop them; the verb should hold
  the colour.
- **Playing the verb at the audience** — the object is the partner, always.
- **Negative choices only**: insults and attacks read well on paper but flatten a
  scene. Look for the warmer verb that could still get the objective.
- **Over-actioning soliloquies**: the object may be the audience, yourself, or
  God; choose deliberately.
- Some directors dislike actioning; don't impose it in the room — do it as
  homework.

## Verify
- [ ] Every thought has exactly one verb that fits "I ___ you".
- [ ] No state, explain/tell/ask verbs remain.
- [ ] Each verb has been tried aloud against at least one alternative.
- [ ] The verb column shows tactic changes where the beats change.
