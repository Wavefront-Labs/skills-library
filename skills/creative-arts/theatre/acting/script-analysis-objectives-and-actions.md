# Script analysis: objectives, obstacles and actions

**Goal:** turn a printed role into a sequence of playable choices — what you want,
what's in the way, and what you *do* to the other person on each thought — so you
arrive at rehearsal with something to try rather than a mood to emote.

This is the common core of the Stanislavski-derived tradition (as taught through
Uta Hagen, Stella Adler and others, and "actioning" in UK
training). Vocabulary differs between teachers and schools; the mechanics below are
the shared part. Treat named terms as labels, not doctrine.

## 1. Read for facts before feelings
Read the whole play at least twice before marking anything. Then list **given
circumstances** — only what the text states or directly implies:
- Who/where/when: age, status, relationships, place, time of day, year, weather.
- What happened just before the scene (the "moment before").
- What others say about your character (often more reliable than what the character says).
Mark each fact with its page number. Separate **facts** from **your inventions**
(backstory you make up to fill gaps); inventions are fine but must never contradict
a fact. A common error is building a characterisation on something the script
never says.

## 2. Super-objective and scene objectives
- **Super-objective:** what the character wants across the whole play, phrased as
  an active want toward a person: "to make my father admit he was wrong",
  not "to be sad" or "to find myself".
- **Scene objective:** what you want *from the other person in this scene*. Test:
  can you tell, at the end of the scene, whether you got it? If not, rephrase.
- **Obstacle:** what stops you — usually the other character, sometimes yourself
  or circumstance. No obstacle, no scene.
- **Stakes:** what happens if you fail. Raise them until you care.

## 3. Beats (units)
A beat changes when the tactic or the topic changes — often signalled by a new
piece of information, an entrance, or a refusal. Draw a line across the script at
each shift. A two-page scene typically has 4–10 beats. Give each a title in your
own words ("the ambush", "she offers a way out").

## 4. Actioning: one transitive verb per thought
For each line (or each thought within a long speech), write a **transitive verb**
— something you can do *to* the other person: *I flatter you, I warn you, I corner
you, I soothe you, I dare you*.
Rules that keep verbs playable:
- Must take a direct object ("I ___ you"). "I worry" fails; "I alarm you" works.
- Avoid result verbs you can't control ("I convince you") — choose the tactic
  ("I reason with you", "I shame you").
- Vary them. Five "I persuade you" in a row plays as one note.
- Write them in pencil. The director and your partner will change them.

## 5. Subtext and the "why now"
For any line that seems to say less than it means, write the unsaid sentence in
the margin. Ask "why does she say this *now* and not earlier?" — the answer is
usually the beat change.

## 6. Taking it into rehearsal
- **Play the action on the partner, not the line.** Watch whether they change;
  their response tells you whether to keep the tactic or switch.
- **Don't play the end of the scene** (knowing it's tragic, you play doom from line one).
  Play the hope.
- Do a pass where you speak only the verb before each line (quietly, "I tease you —")
  to check every line has one; drop that in performance.
- If a choice feels false, check it against your facts list first, then try the
  opposite verb — contradiction often unlocks a scene.

## Gotchas
- **Generalising emotion.** "Angry" is a result; anger comes from a blocked objective
  pursued hard. Play the want.
- **Over-marked scripts.** If you're reading notes instead of hearing your partner,
  you've done too much homework for this stage. Learn lines clean, then layer.
- **Ignoring punctuation and verse.** In heightened text (Shakespeare, Restoration),
  line endings and antitheses are part of the given circumstances — see voice-and-speech
  skills.
- Methods conflict on emotional/sense memory; this skill stays with text-led
  analysis, which most rehearsal rooms accept regardless of school. Unconfirmed:
  how strictly a given director expects actioning in the room — ask.

## Verify
- [ ] A facts list with page references exists, separate from invented backstory.
- [ ] The super-objective and every scene objective are phrased as an active want from a person, with a testable win.
- [ ] Each scene has an obstacle and stakes you can name.
- [ ] Beat lines are drawn; each beat has a title.
- [ ] Every thought has a transitive verb, and consecutive verbs vary.
- [ ] In a run, you can tell from your partner's reaction whether each tactic landed.
