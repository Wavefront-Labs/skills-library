# Managing stage fright

Some arousal improves performance; stage fright is when it disrupts it (dry
mouth, shaking, blanking, nausea, avoidance). Common at every career stage.

## Before the show
1. **Fixed pre-show routine** at the same times each night: arrival, physical
   warm-up, vocal warm-up, a moment with your props and set, costume, a quiet
   minute. Predictability lowers anxiety.
2. **Preparation removes uncertainty**: lines rock solid, run tricky
   transitions, check props yourself.
3. **Breathing**: slow breathing with exhales longer than inhales (e.g. 4 in,
   6-8 out) for 2-3 minutes activates the parasympathetic response. Physical
   shake-out burns adrenaline.
4. **Reappraise**: tell yourself "I'm excited" rather than "calm down" —
   research by Alison Wood Brooks (2014) found reappraising anxiety as
   excitement improved performance more than trying to calm down.
5. Limit caffeine; eat 2-3 hours before; hydrate.

## On stage
- **Put attention on the task**: the partner, the objective, a specific object
  (small circle of attention). Self-monitoring ("how am I doing?") feeds fear.
- If you blank: stay in character, keep looking at the partner, go back to
  what you want — the words often return; partners can help; the DSM can
  prompt.
- Accept physical symptoms (shaky hands) without fighting them; they pass once
  absorbed in action.

## Longer term
- Exposure: perform more, in lower-stakes settings (readings, open mics).
- Debrief facts, not catastrophes, after a bad show.
- Persistent, severe anxiety: speak to a GP or a performing-arts health
  specialist (BAPAM in the UK, Performing Arts Medicine Association in the
  US). Beta-blockers are prescription-only — never take someone else's.

## Gotchas
- Avoiding the warm-up because nerves feel better "not thinking about it".
- Self-medicating with alcohol.
- Comparing to colleagues who appear calm; most aren't.
