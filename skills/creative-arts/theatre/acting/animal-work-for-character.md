# Animal work for character

A drama-school staple (taught at RADA, LAMDA, Juilliard and others since at least
the mid-20th century) for escaping the actor's own physical habits. It gives
tempo, centre, focus and rhythm grounded in observation.

## Procedure
1. **Choose the animal** from the text and circumstances: what is the
   character's energy? (Stanley in *Streetcar* → ape/bull; a nervous clerk →
   bird/rabbit; a predator → cat.) Try two or three before committing.
2. **Observe live** — zoo, farm, park, or high-quality video if necessary. Note:
   - Centre of gravity and where movement starts.
   - Tempo and rhythm: continuous or staccato, bursts and stillness.
   - Focus: how the eyes/head take in the world, how fast it reacts.
   - Breath and sound.
   - Status behaviours: approach, retreat, eating, grooming, threat.
3. **Embody at 100%** in a studio session: on all fours if needed, for
   20-30 minutes. Hunt, eat, rest, react to sound.
4. **Stand up gradually** — 75%, 50%, 25% — keeping the centre, tempo and
   focus while the shape becomes human.
5. **Arrive at ~10%**: a human walk and stillness with the animal's essence. Do
   a human task (make tea, read a paper) as the 10% animal.
6. **Bring to the scene**: use the animal as a note ("more cat in the
   entrance"), not a costume.

## Variations
- Mixed animals: one for public self, one under pressure.
- Group animal improvisation for ensemble worlds (e.g. a pack for a gang).

## Gotchas
- Visible animal mannerisms in a realist show (twitching nose): reduce further.
- Choosing the obvious cartoon animal; observation must be real, not
  stereotype.
- Physical safety on hard floors: knee pads for all-fours work, warm up first.

## Verify
- [ ] Observation notes cover centre, tempo, focus, breath and status.
- [ ] You can move smoothly from 100% to 10% and back.
- [ ] Observers can sense the energy at 10% without naming the animal.
