# Blocking and staging a scene

**Goal:** place and move actors so the audience always knows where to look and what
changed, without the movement looking arranged.

## 1. Prepare before the room
- Get the **ground plan** (scale drawing, commonly 1:25 metric or 1/2"=1'-0" US) from
  the scenic designer. Note entrances, furniture, levels, and the **sightline limits**
  — the lines from the extreme side seats past the proscenium/masking.
- Mark on a copy of the script where the **story turns** (each beat change). Moves
  belong at turns; movement mid-thought pulls focus off the line.
- Decide the scene's one key **stage picture** — the image you want at its climax —
  and work backwards to get the actors there.
- Ask stage management to **tape out** the floor (coloured tape per scene) and
  provide rehearsal furniture of the right size. Blocking on an untaped floor
  usually has to be redone on the set.

## 2. Stage geography (proscenium)
Directions are from the **actor's** viewpoint facing the audience: stage left =
audience's right. Upstage = away from the audience. A nine-area grid (UR, UC, UL,
R, C, L, DR, DC, DL) is the common shorthand. Rough strength conventions: downstage
and centre are strong; an actor standing beats one sitting; a full-front body
position beats profile beats full-back. These are tendencies, not laws — light
and contrast override them.

## 3. Tools for focus
- **Triangles:** three actors in a triangle, apex upstage, give the apex focus and
  keep all faces open. Straight lines across the stage flatten and hide people.
- **Contrast:** the one standing among the seated, the one still among the moving.
- **Eyelines:** the audience looks where the actors look.
- **Upstaging:** if A moves upstage of B, B must turn away from the audience to talk
  to A. Use it deliberately (to give A power) or level them ("share the scene" —
  both in quarter-profile on the same plane).
- **Distance = relationship.** Closing distance reads as intimacy or threat;
  opening it as retreat. Changes in distance are more legible than positions.

## 4. Motivating moves
Every move should have a reason the actor can play: to get closer, to escape, to
get an object, to block a door. Start by letting actors move when they feel the
impulse on a first walk, then **fix and clean**: keep what tells the story,
remove drift, and adjust for sightlines. Pre-blocking everything on paper is
faster for big ensembles, fight scenes and musicals; improvise-then-fix works
better for small dramatic scenes. Many directors mix both.

## 5. Notation
Stage management records blocking in the prompt book; actors record their own in
pencil. Standard shorthand: `X` = cross, `DSL` = down stage left, arrows for path,
`→ sofa`, `sit`, `ent.`/`exit`. Number moves and key them to the script line. Ask
for **blocking to be read back** at the start of the next rehearsal before
running it.

## 6. Other configurations
- **Thrust (three sides):** keep actors moving; avoid holding anyone static
  down-centre facing one side. Use diagonals; the "strong" spot is often near the
  centre or the upstage wall.
- **In-the-round:** someone always has a back to a section. Rotate focus so no
  section loses a key moment's face; sit in every section during runs. Use
  corners/vomitories for entrances.
- **Traverse:** play along the long axis; profile is the default.

## Gotchas
- Blocking from the front row only. Walk to the side and back seats (and the
  balcony) during each scene — tall furniture and upstage actors vanish.
- Too much movement because "it's a static scene". Stillness during an important
  speech often holds focus better than a cross.
- Choreographed fights, lifts and falls must be set by a qualified fight/movement
  director, not ad-libbed into blocking — see stage-movement-and-combat.
- Changes after tech: every change must go to stage management so cues and
  sightlines are rechecked.

## Verify
- [ ] Every move is tied to a script line and a playable reason.
- [ ] At each beat change, you can point to what visually changed.
- [ ] From the extreme seats, no key face or action is hidden.
- [ ] Stage management's prompt book matches what the actors actually do.
- [ ] The climax stage picture exists and is reached naturally.
