# Front elevations, detail drawings and set costing

## Front elevations (designer's elevations)
- A **front elevation** shows each scenic unit as seen from the front, flat,
  at scale (1/2" = 1'-0" or 1:25), with overall dimensions, openings (doors,
  windows), mouldings, and heights above stage.
- Group related units on one plate (e.g. all walls of the interior set) in
  sequence as they appear on the ground plan, keyed with unit letters/numbers.
- Note **finish/texture** callouts and refer to paint elevations.
- Show the **back** or edge if the unit is seen from more than one side.

## Detail and section drawings
- **Sections** cut through mouldings, cornices, door frames, stairs to show
  profiles and depths.
- **Details** at larger scale (1" = 1'-0", 1:10, 1:5): mouldings, ornaments,
  hardware, practical mechanisms (doors that must slam, trick furniture).
- Indicate what must be practical (weight-bearing, opening, breakaway).
The technical director/shop then produces **construction (shop) drawings**
(framing, materials, joinery) from the designer's drawings.

## Costing a set
1. Break the design into units; with the PM/TD estimate **materials** (timber,
   steel, sheet goods, fabric, paint, hardware) and **labour** (build, paint,
   install hours x rates) per unit.
2. Add props, furniture hire, flame-retardant treatment, transport, contingency
   (often 10-15%).
3. Compare against the budget; if over, **value engineer**: simplify
   construction, reuse stock, reduce units, cheaper finishes. Keep the
   design's essentials.
4. Update estimates after any design change; track actual spend.

## Gotchas
- Elevations without dimensions — the shop guesses.
- Details missing for practical elements (stairs, doors) — safety and delays.
- Costing late (after model sign-off) — expensive redesigns.

## Verify
- [ ] Every unit on the ground plan has an elevation with dimensions.
- [ ] Practical elements have details.
- [ ] Costed estimate within budget before sign-off.
