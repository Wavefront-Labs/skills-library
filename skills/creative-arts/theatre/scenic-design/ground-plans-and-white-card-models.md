# Ground plans and white-card models

**Goal:** turn a design idea into drawings and a model accurate enough that the
director can block on it, the production manager can cost it, and the workshop
can build it. (Building the set itself is a handcrafts/production skill.)

## 1. Survey the venue first
Get the theatre's technical pack: plan and section drawings, stage dimensions,
proscenium width and height, wing space, fly bars (number, spacing, weight limits),
trap positions, floor loading, get-in door size, rake, and seating plan.
If the drawings are old, visit with a tape measure — get-in door size is the
classic cause of a set that can't enter the building.

## 2. Ground plan (plan view)
- Scale: **1:25** is standard in metric countries; **1/2" = 1'-0"** (1:24) in the US.
  Smaller venues sometimes use 1:50 for overall plans.
- Draw the **setting line** (usually upstage edge of the proscenium) and the **centre
  line**; all dimensions run from these two datums.
- Show: set pieces, platforms with heights, steps, doors (with swing direction),
  masking, furniture, entrances, fly lines used, and the edge of the first row.
- Include **sightline lines** from the extreme seats (front-row ends, and the highest
  seats for vertical sightlines) to show what is visible.
- Title block: show, venue, scale, date, revision letter, designer.
CAD (Vectorworks Spotlight is common in theatre; AutoCAD, SketchUp also used) or
hand-drafted; keep the venue drawing as a locked base layer.

## 3. Section (side view)
Draw a centre-line section through the stage and auditorium showing heights:
platforms, flown pieces (in and out positions), borders, the lighting positions,
and the vertical sightline from the front row (what's hidden above) and the
highest seat (what's seen behind/over). Flown pieces need trim heights.

## 4. White-card model
A rough scale model in plain white card (or foam board), before colour or detail:
- Build the **model box** of the venue — stage floor, proscenium, side walls —
  at the same scale as the plan.
- Add pieces quickly; the point is proportion and space, not finish.
- Use a **scale figure** (a card person ~1.75 m at scale) in every photo and
  discussion; directors read scale from people, not numbers.
- Photograph from audience eye level (a phone held at the seat position in the
  model) to test the composition.
- Iterate with the director; changes are cheap now and expensive later.

## 5. Final model and presentation
After agreement, a **finished model** (painted, textured, often with scene changes)
and a full drawing set go to the production manager and workshop. Present at a
**design presentation** to cast and crew: walk through each scene change with
the model, explain the idea, show references.

## Gotchas
- **Wing space and storage.** Every piece used in one scene and not the next needs
  somewhere to go — draw storage positions.
- **Doors and actors.** A door in a flat needs offstage clearance for the actor
  waiting and for the door to swing.
- **Rakes and steps** must meet building/safety codes and be comfortable for dancers
  or actors in costume; check with the production manager. Exact rise/going limits
  vary by jurisdiction (unconfirmed for your venue).
- **Weight and cost** drive revisions. Get an early estimate from the workshop on a
  white-card version before falling in love with the finished model.
- Touring sets must fit every venue — design to the smallest stage.

## Verify
- [ ] Ground plan and section are to stated scale, with setting line and centre line.
- [ ] Sightlines from extreme seats are drawn and nothing essential is hidden.
- [ ] Every piece fits through the get-in door and has offstage storage.
- [ ] A white-card model with a scale figure has been reviewed with the director.
- [ ] The production manager has costed the design before the finished model.
