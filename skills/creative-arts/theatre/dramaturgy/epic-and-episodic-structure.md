# Epic and episodic structure

## Episodic structure
Scenes as relatively independent units, often spanning wide time and place
(Shakespeare's histories, Büchner's *Woyzeck*, Expressionist *Stationendrama*,
much contemporary new writing). Momentum comes from accumulation, juxtaposition
and theme rather than a single causal chain.

**Analysis steps**
1. Title each scene with its **gest** (the social action it shows): "Mother
   Courage sells her son's life by haggling too long".
2. Note time and place jumps; build a chronology.
3. Find the **organising principle**: a journey, a person's decline, a
   repeated question, a dialectic of opposites.
4. Check juxtapositions: what does each scene comment on in the one before?

## Brecht's epic theatre
From Brecht's writings (*A Short Organum for the Theatre*, 1948; the "Notes to
*Mahagonny*" table contrasting dramatic and epic theatre).
- **Each scene for itself** (not "one scene makes another").
- **Verfremdungseffekt** (estrangement/alienation/distancing): making the
  familiar strange so the audience judges rather than identifies. Devices:
  captions/placards announcing what will happen, songs that interrupt, visible
  lighting and stage machinery, actors addressing the audience, demonstrating
  rather than becoming the character ("the street scene" model).
- **Gestus**: the physical and vocal attitude revealing social relations.
- Narrative interest shifts from "what happens?" to "how and why?".

## Dramaturgical implications
- Scene captions and their wording are dramaturgical choices.
- Songs need clear placement and function (comment, not mood).
- Transitions should be quick and visible, not hidden in blackouts.
- Actor work: "not... but..." — showing the choice the character didn't make.

## Gotchas
- Equating Verfremdung with coldness: Brecht wanted entertaining, emotional
  theatre that also provokes thought.
- Treating episodic plays as "badly structured" well-made plays.
