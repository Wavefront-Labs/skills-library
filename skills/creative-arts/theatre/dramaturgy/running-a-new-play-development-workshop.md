# Running a new-play development workshop

**Goal:** give a playwright the information they need to write the next draft —
what the play is doing in the room — without the room rewriting the play for them.
The dramaturg's job here is protecting the writer's intentions while making sure
they hear the truth.

## 1. Before: agree what the workshop is for
Meet the playwright and ask:
- **What draft is this, and what are you working on?** ("Is the second act too long?",
  "Does the mother's turn land?")
- **What kind of event?** A table read (actors seated, one day), a workshop (several
  days with rewrites between sessions), or a staged reading with an audience.
- **What is off limits** for feedback right now (e.g. the ending is being rewritten —
  don't spend time on it).
Write the playwright's questions down; they set the agenda for every feedback session.

Practical setup: cast actors who can sight-read well; give them the script at least
a few days ahead; assign a reader for stage directions (keep them trimmed — reading
every direction aloud slows the play and distorts its rhythm). Number the pages and
date the draft on every page header.

## 2. During: listening for the play
Sit where you can see both the actors and the playwright. Take notes in two
columns: **what happened** (laughs, silences, restlessness, confusion — with page
numbers) and **questions** it raised. Note where actors stumble over the same line
twice — often a writing problem, not a reading one. Time each act.

## 3. Structuring feedback
Unstructured talkbacks drift into "here's the play I would have written". Use a
protocol. A widely used one is Liz Lerman's **Critical Response Process**, roughly:
1. **Statements of meaning** — responders say what was meaningful, striking or
   memorable. No judgements.
2. **Artist asks questions** — the playwright asks their own questions first.
3. **Neutral questions** from responders — questions without opinions buried in
   them ("What's the time gap between scenes 3 and 4?" not "Why is scene 4 so slow?").
4. **Opinion time**, by permission — "I have an opinion about the ending, would you
   like to hear it?" The playwright may say no.
In a private workshop, the dramaturg usually gives notes one-to-one afterwards
rather than in front of the company.

## 4. Giving dramaturgical notes
- Lead with **what the play is trying to do**, as you understand it. If your reading
  doesn't match the writer's intention, that gap is the note.
- Frame notes as **questions and observations**, not fixes: "I lost track of what
  Ana wants after her sister arrives" rather than "Cut the sister".
- Prioritise: three big notes beat thirty small ones. Line notes go in a separate
  document.
- Separate **structural** issues (stakes, through-line, act break) from **local**
  ones (a clunky line).

## 5. Tracking rewrites
- Every new page gets the date and a coloured page convention (or revision marks)
  so actors know what's new. Many companies follow screen industry colour order
  (white, blue, pink, yellow, green...), though theatre practice varies.
- Keep a change log: date, pages, what changed, why.
- Archive every draft; playwrights often restore cut material.

## Gotchas
- **Fixing by committee.** If actors start pitching jokes and cuts, stop and
  redirect to observations.
- **Directors versus dramaturgs.** When a director is also present, agree in advance
  who gives which notes, so the writer doesn't get contradictory instructions.
- **Audience talkbacks** after readings are noisy; weight them as data points,
  not votes.
- Rights and credit: in some jurisdictions, dramaturgical contribution has led to
  disputes over authorship. Agree in writing that the playwright owns the text.
  Unconfirmed: specific contract norms — check local writers' guild guidance.

## Verify
- [ ] The playwright's questions are written down and addressed by the end.
- [ ] Feedback followed a protocol; no one pitched rewrites unprompted.
- [ ] Your notes tie observations to page numbers.
- [ ] Draft and revision pages are dated and logged.
- [ ] The playwright leaves with a clear priority for the next draft.
