# Calling the show from the prompt book

**Goal:** give every lighting, sound, fly, automation and stage cue at the exact
moment the director and designers set, every night, in a way operators can
follow without doubt.

Who calls varies: in UK practice usually the **DSM** "on the book"; in US practice
usually the **PSM/SM** at the calling desk. The protocol is the same.

## 1. The calling script (prompt book)
- One script page per sheet with a wide margin (or a single-sided script with blank
  facing page). Cues go in the margin, with a line or bracket to the exact word or
  action where the GO lands.
- Label by department: common conventions are **LX** (lighting), **SQ**/**Sound**,
  **Fly**, **Auto**, **VQ** (video). Cue numbers are set by the designers;
  inserted cues use decimals or letters (LX 14.5, SQ 7A).
- Write the standby **half a page to a page before** the GO — enough time for operators
  to prepare and confirm, short enough not to wait forever.
- Pencil in rehearsal and tech; ink (or a clean typed version) once frozen. Keep a
  backup copy at the theatre.

## 2. Standby / go protocol
Exact wording varies by company; consistency matters more than the words. A
widespread form:
1. "**Standby** LX 14, Sound 7." → each operator replies "**LX standing by**",
   "**Sound standing by**". No reply = not on standby; ask again.
2. Cue lights (if used) switched on for standby.
3. "LX 14, Sound 7 … **GO**." The department and number come *before* GO; GO is
   the trigger word, said on the exact moment. Operators fire on "GO", never on
   the number.
4. Cue lights off = GO, in systems that use them.
- Group simultaneous cues in one standby and one go.
- Don't say "go" in casual speech on headset ("it'll go fine") — use "proceed",
  "carry on". Operators react to the word.

## 3. Timing the GO
- Call on the **action**, not the line, when the cue is visual (a door closes,
  a hand reaches the switch). Watch the stage, not just the book.
- For a cue landing on a word, say "GO" so the operator hits it *on* the word —
  you lead by the operator's reaction time (typically a fraction of a second).
- Musical cues: count bars with the musical director's marks in the book.

## 4. Headset etiquette
- Silence on channel during standbys except for the call and replies.
- Identify yourself and the recipient: "Fly, from SM — …"
- Problems on stage are reported in short form ("SM, from stage left: door jammed")
  and dealt with off-channel where possible.

## 5. When it goes wrong
- **Missed cue**: decide fast whether to call it late (if the moment is still live)
  or skip to the next cue. Tell the operator plainly: "LX, skip 15, standby 16."
- **Wrong cue fired**: don't panic-correct mid-scene unless the stage is unsafe
  (e.g. blackout on a moving set). Agree in tech what the recovery is for the
  biggest risk cues.
- **Show stop**: a safety issue (injury, fire, automation fault) — the SM stops the
  show using the venue's procedure (often a **show-stop announcement** with house lights
  up). Have the text in the book. Resume from an agreed point.
- Write everything in the **show report**: what happened, time, cause, fix.

## 6. Practice
Shadow-call a run before you take over: call into a dead channel while the
regular caller calls live, then compare timings. Understudy callers do the same.

## Gotchas
- Standby too early: operators lose focus; too late: they miss it.
- Turning pages: never have a standby on one page and its GO hidden on the next
  without a note. Tab big sequences.
- Lighting desks with "auto-follow" cues: know which cues fire themselves so you
  don't call them.
- Cue numbers change during tech; read back changes with each operator at the end
  of every session.

## Verify
- [ ] Every cue in the book has a standby, a placement mark and a department label.
- [ ] Operators confirm every standby before you go.
- [ ] Your book matches each operator's cue list (checked after each change).
- [ ] You have a written recovery plan for show stops and the riskiest cues.
- [ ] Show reports record every deviation.
