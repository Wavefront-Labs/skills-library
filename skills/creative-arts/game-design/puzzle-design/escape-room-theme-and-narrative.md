# Escape room theme and narrative wrapper

**Goal:** a room where every puzzle has a reason to exist in the world, players know who
they are and why they are there, and the ending pays off the story.

## Nicholson's "Ask Why"
For every puzzle, answer "why would this be here?" in-world. A combination lock on a
diary makes sense; a random 4-digit padlock on a cupboard in a pirate ship does not.
Rooms that answer "why" feel coherent; rooms that do not feel like puzzle collections.

## Building the wrapper
1. **Premise** (one sentence): "You are thieves with an hour to steal the ruby before the
   curator returns."
2. **Player role and goal:** the role defines what actions are natural (thieves pick
   locks, scientists run experiments).
3. **Story beats in the flow:** intro briefing, first reveal (opening a hidden room),
   midpoint twist, final puzzle, ending.
4. **Puzzles from the world:** derive puzzles from props and the setting. Lab: mixing
   chemicals by colour, a periodic-table cipher. Pirate ship: sextant reading, tide tables.
5. **Set dressing** supports, never misleads: decorative items that look interactive need
   "do not touch" markers or should be removed.
6. **Endings:** a physical climax (the vault opens, the ruby lifts), and a short
   in-character outro; consider multiple endings for teams that fail.

## Game master story role
- Briefing video or actor sets the premise and rules in under 3 minutes.
- Hints in the room's voice (the curator's ghost, the mission control radio).
- Debrief: show photos, time, and a story epilogue.

## Gotchas
- Lots of padlocks dilute theme; vary mechanisms (magnets, RFID, sensors).
- Story told only through texts in the room goes unread under time pressure; deliver key
  beats through events, sound and reveals.
- Horror or intense themes need content warnings and opt-out.

## Verify
- [ ] Every puzzle has a written in-world "why".
- [ ] Story beats mapped onto the puzzle flow chart.
- [ ] Ending has a physical or audiovisual payoff.
