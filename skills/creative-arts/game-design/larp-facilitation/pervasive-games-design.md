# Pervasive games design

**Goal:** games that extend beyond a bounded time, place or group into everyday life, without harming people who did not agree to play.

The standard academic reference is Markus Montola, Jaakko Stenros and Annika Waern, Pervasive Games: Theory and Design (2009). It describes pervasive games as expanding the traditional "magic circle" in space, time and social membership.

## Procedure
1. **Choose which dimensions to expand:** space (play in streets, buildings, online), time (play over days or weeks), and social (include non-players, strangers or the public).
2. **Define player consent and onboarding:** players must understand the game is on, and how to opt out. Provide a clear way to join and leave.
3. **Protect bystanders:** do not frighten or inconvenience the public; avoid props that look like real weapons, and do not require trespassing.
4. **Check laws and permissions:** public spaces, private property, data protection and local rules differ; consult authorities when needed.
5. **Create safety rules:** emergency contacts, a stop signal, curfews, and a way to confirm a situation is a game.
6. **Design the interface:** how do players receive clues (messages, websites, phone calls, physical objects)? Test each channel.
7. **Plan pacing and attrition:** a long game needs rhythm, reminders and a graceful ending.
8. **Test in the real setting** and have staff on call.
9. **Debrief players** and document learning.

## Gotchas
- Blurring game and reality can distress players; offer clear boundaries (see alternate-reality-game-design).
- Weather, public events, and shop hours disrupt plans; have fallbacks.
- Player privacy: avoid revealing personal information.

## Verify
- [ ] Expansion dimensions chosen deliberately.
- [ ] Bystander safety and legal checks done.
- [ ] Opt-out and emergency procedures documented.
