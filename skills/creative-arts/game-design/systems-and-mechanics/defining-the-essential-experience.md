# Defining the essential experience

**Goal:** a written statement of the experience the player must have, specific enough
that you can watch a playtest and say whether it happened.

Schell's core claim (Art of Game Design ch2): the designer creates an *experience*; the
game is only the artefact that produces it. You cannot directly build an experience, so
you must name it precisely and then design artefacts that reliably cause it.

## Procedure
1. **Answer Schell's three questions** (Lens of the Essential Experience):
   - What experience do I want the player to have?
   - What is essential to that experience?
   - How can the game capture that essence?
2. **Use a reference moment.** Write a paragraph about a real experience (yours or
   observed) that has the feeling you want: "Sledding: the run-up, the moment of no
   control, the crash, the walk back up laughing." Real experiences are richer than
   imagined ones.
3. **Extract the essentials.** Underline the 3-6 parts that make it that experience
   (loss of control, short cycle, social laughter). Everything else is incidental.
4. **Map essentials to mechanics.** For each, name the rule/feedback that produces it:
   | Essential | Mechanic that produces it | How we'll observe it |
   |---|---|---|
   | brief loss of control | momentum physics, no braking mid-run | player leans/flinches |
   | short cycle | 20-second runs, instant restart | > 10 runs in 5 minutes |
5. **Name the MDA aesthetic targets** (challenge, fellowship, discovery...) so the team
   shares vocabulary (see `creative-arts.game-design.systems-and-mechanics.eight-kinds-of-fun-aesthetic-targets`).
6. **Write the observable signs** into the playtest plan. If you cannot observe it, you
   cannot know you delivered it.

## Gotchas
- **Describing the game instead of the experience** ("a stealth game set in Venice") is
  the most common failure. Rewrite until no mechanic or setting is named.
- **Multiple essential experiences** dilute each other. If you have two, pick one as the
  core and make the other support it.
- Players rarely report the experience in words; watch faces, posture, repeat-play and
  what they talk about afterwards.
- Simulations of the real thing often miss the essence; you may need to abstract or
  exaggerate (Schell's example: a realistic ride simulation can feel duller than a
  stylised one).

## Verify
- [ ] One paragraph names the experience without naming mechanics or setting.
- [ ] Each essential element maps to at least one mechanic.
- [ ] Each has an observable sign written into the playtest plan.
- [ ] A playtest has been judged against those signs.
