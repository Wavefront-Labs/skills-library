# Ludonarrative harmony

**Goal:** what the player does and what the story says point in the same direction, so
the game's meaning is felt through play, not contradicted by it.

Clint Hocking coined "ludonarrative dissonance" (2007 blog post on BioShock): the game's
rules expressed self-interest while its story argued against it, creating a conflict
between the ludic and narrative layers.

## Audit procedure
1. **Write the story's claims:** what the narrative says about the character, world and
   theme ("Nathan is a reluctant everyman", "violence has a cost", "the town is starving").
2. **List what mechanics reward:** count verbs and rewards (kills per hour, looting,
   ignoring NPC needs, speed).
3. **Compare in a table:**
   | Story claims | Mechanics reward | Harmony? |
   |---|---|---|
   | town is starving | food plentiful in loot | dissonant |
   | violence is costly | combat is the only progression | dissonant |
   | trust is fragile | betraying allies has no system effect | weak |
4. **Fix either side:** change mechanics (food scarce, non-violent options, betrayal
   consequences) or change claims (the story embraces what play is).
5. **Use mechanics as expression:** let the rules say the theme (Schell: lenses of
   unification) - resource scarcity for survival themes, limited perspective for horror.

## Techniques for harmony
- **Narrative from verbs:** design the story around what the player does most.
- **Systemic consequences:** world state reflects player choices.
- **Diegetic justification** for game conventions (respawn explained by the fiction) -
  use sparingly.
- **Cutscene vs play parity:** characters shouldn't be incompetent in cutscenes after
  being superhuman in play.

## Gotchas
- Some dissonance is accepted convention (health regeneration); focus on dissonance with
  the game's *central* themes.
- Over-justifying every mechanic in fiction bloats exposition.

## Verify
- [ ] Story claims and mechanic rewards tabled side by side.
- [ ] Central-theme dissonances fixed or deliberately embraced.
- [ ] Core verbs reflected in the narrative premise.
