# Session prep and table pacing — ready to improvise, not scripted

**Goal:** prepare the material that actually gets used at the table, and keep a
three-to-four-hour session moving so every player gets moments that matter.
System-agnostic; examples assume D&D-style play.

## 1. Prep what's reusable, not a plot
Players will not follow a script. Prepare *ingredients* and let them choose the
order. A compact checklist (popularised as "lazy" prep among GMs):
1. **Review the characters** — their goals, recent events, and one hook for each.
2. **A strong start** — open in motion (an ambush, a messenger bursting in), not
   "you're in a tavern, what do you do?"
3. **Possible scenes** — 3–6 bullets of things that *might* happen. Not a sequence.
4. **Secrets and clues** — ~10 one-line facts the players could learn. Don't tie each to
   a location; drop them wherever players investigate. This is what prevents dead ends.
5. **Locations** — a few evocative places with 2–3 sensory details each.
6. **NPCs** — name, want, one mannerism or voice cue. A list of spare names for improvised NPCs.
7. **Monsters/challenges** and **rewards** relevant to the likely scenes.
Write on one page or index cards. If prep takes much longer than the session, cut.

## 2. Session zero (once per campaign)
Agree on tone, content lines (hard limits) and veils (fade-to-black topics), table
etiquette, scheduling and how much player-vs-player conflict is OK. Introduce a safety
tool (X-card, Lines & Veils, check-ins) — see
creative-arts.game-design.larp-facilitation.safety-and-calibration-tools.

## 3. Pacing at the table
- **Scene framing:** start scenes late, end them early. When a scene's question is
  answered, cut: "You get the map. That night…"
- **Watch energy, not the clock.** Signs of drag: side conversations, phones, the same
  plan debated twice. Respond by introducing a complication or a clock ("you hear
  boots on the stairs").
- **Combat:** keep turns fast — players plan on others' turns, roll attack and damage
  together, describe hits briefly. End fights when the outcome is clear (enemies flee
  or surrender) rather than grinding out hit points.
- **End on a hook** — a cliffhanger or decision gives momentum to next session.

## 4. Spotlight
- Keep a mental (or written) tally of who has had a meaningful moment. After a loud
  player acts, turn to a quiet one directly: "Kai, what is your character doing while this happens?"
- Quiet players may be content; ask outside the session rather than forcing them.

## 5. Rules adjudication
- When a rule question would take more than a minute, **make a ruling** in the
  player's reasonable favour, note it, and look it up after the session. Be consistent next time.
- "Yes, and" / "Yes, but" / "No, but" keeps improvised actions moving: grant the
  attempt and attach a cost or roll instead of refusing.
- Say the DC or the stakes before the roll when failure has consequences, so players
  choose with information.

## Gotchas
- **Over-prepped plot** makes GMs railroad players back onto it. Prep situations and NPC goals instead.
- **Clue bottlenecks:** if only one clue unlocks the next step, players will miss it.
  Use several routes to every essential conclusion (often given as "three clues").
- **Absent players** — decide at session zero how their characters are handled.
- **Burnout** is real; a shorter session or skipped week beats a tired GM.
- The prep checklist summarises widely shared GM advice; tailor it to your system and table.

## Verify
- [ ] Prep fits on one page: strong start, scenes, ~10 secrets, NPCs, locations.
- [ ] Every essential conclusion has multiple clues.
- [ ] Session zero covered content limits and a safety tool.
- [ ] Each player had at least one spotlight moment this session.
- [ ] Session ended on a hook; any rulings noted for lookup.
