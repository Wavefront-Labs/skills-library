# Jaquaying dungeons

**Goal:** a dungeon players navigate with real choices: routes, shortcuts, retreats,
alternative approaches, rather than a corridor of rooms.

Justin Alexander's essay "Jaquaying the Dungeon" (The Alexandrian) analyses dungeons by
designer Jennell Jaquays (e.g. *Caverns of Thracia*, *Dark Tower*) and lists techniques
for non-linear design.

## Techniques (from the essay)
1. **Multiple entrances** - players choose how to approach.
2. **Loops** - routes that connect back, allowing flanking, retreat and choice.
3. **Multiple level connections** - more than one way between levels (stairs, shafts,
   chasms).
4. **Discontinuous level connections** - a passage from level 1 that goes straight to
   level 3.
5. **Secret and unusual paths** - hidden doors, climbing routes, underwater passages.
6. **Sub-levels** - small areas between main levels.
7. **Divided levels** - parts of a level reachable only from other levels.
Also: **mix of elevations** within a level (stairs, ledges) to create vertical complexity.

## Procedure
1. Draw the dungeon as a **flowchart of rooms** first (nodes and edges), not a map.
2. Check: does every important area have at least two routes? Is there at least one loop
   per level? Are there several entrances?
3. Add secret paths that reward exploration and allow bypassing.
4. Populate with factions or inhabitants who use the loops (patrols, ambushes).
5. Then convert to a map with geography that explains the routes.

## Running it
- Let players retreat and come back another way; inhabitants react (reinforce, move).
- Track which doors are open, which routes are known.

## Gotchas
- Too many connections become a featureless maze; keep some chokepoints for drama.
- Loops are invisible to players without good description (sounds from adjacent
  passages, drafts, glimpses).

## Verify
- [ ] Flowchart shows loops on every level and multiple entrances.
- [ ] At least one discontinuous or secret connection.
- [ ] Inhabitants' behaviour uses the routes.
