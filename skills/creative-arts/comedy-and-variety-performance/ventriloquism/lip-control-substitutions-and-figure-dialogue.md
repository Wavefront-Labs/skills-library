# Ventriloquism: lip control, substitutions and figure dialogue

**Goal:** speak for a figure without visible lip movement, make the figure look alive,
and write a two-voice routine whose comedy comes from the relationship.

## 1. The position
- Teeth slightly apart (a few millimetres), lips relaxed and slightly parted, a faint
  natural smile. Tension is the giveaway; a relaxed face looks still.
- The **tongue** does the work: most sounds can be made with no lip involvement.
- Practise in front of a mirror, then on camera (closer and harsher than an audience).
- Breathe low; the figure's voice should not cost you obvious effort.

## 2. The problem sounds and substitutions
The labials need the lips: **B, P, M, F, V, W** (and sometimes Q). Common substitutions —
*exact choices vary between teachers; use what sounds cleanest to your ear in context*:
| Target | Substitute | How |
|---|---|---|
| B | a soft "D" / "G" | tongue tip on the ridge behind the top teeth, voiced |
| P | a soft "T" / "K" | same place, unvoiced, quick |
| M | "N" / "NG" | nasal hum with tongue placement |
| F | "TH" (unvoiced) | tongue gently against the top teeth |
| V | "TH" (voiced) | as above, voiced |
| W | "OO" glide | start with an "oo" vowel |
The ear hears the context; "Dig dad dear" in a flowing sentence reads as "Big bad bear".
Drill substitution words, then phrases, then the figure's real lines.
**Write the script to dodge labials** where possible; then the remaining ones get
practise.

## 3. The figure's voice
- Differ from your own voice in **pitch, placement or rhythm** (higher/lower, nasal/throaty,
  faster/slower). The more distinct, the more the audience believes two people.
- Keep it sustainable for a full show; a strained falsetto fails by the second set.

## 4. Manipulation: making it alive
- **Mouth sync:** the figure's mouth opens on each syllable — not every letter — and
  closes on the end of words. Over-flapping looks mechanical.
- **Focus:** the figure looks at whoever is speaking or being addressed. When you talk,
  it looks at you; when it talks to the audience, it looks at them.
- **Breath and life:** small head turns, blinks (if the figure has them), reactions
  while you speak. A frozen figure is dead.
- **You look at the figure** when it talks — the audience follows your eyes. This is the
  strongest misdirection in the act.

## 5. Writing the routine
- The figure is usually the one with the **bad behaviour** (rude, cheeky, smarter than you);
  the ventriloquist is the straight man who loses status.
- Classic structures: interview, argument, the figure embarrassing you, the figure
  interacting with an audience volunteer.
- Use the illusion itself for jokes (the figure drinks water while you "talk", the figure
  complains about your hand), but sparingly.
- Same comedy rules as stand-up: funniest word last, tags, a strong closer.

## 6. Figure care
- Hard-face figures: keep mouth mechanisms clean; carry spare control strings/levers.
- Soft puppets: transport in a padded case; check for loose eyes and seams.
- Travel with figures in a case, never crushed in luggage.

## Gotchas
- Speaking too fast in the figure's voice to hide substitutions — it muddies everything.
- Forgetting to animate the figure while you speak.
- Lips "twitching" on labial-heavy lines; re-write them.
- Mic placement: one mic picks up both voices; work at a consistent distance.

## Verify
- [ ] On video at close range, no lip movement on a labial-heavy test sentence.
- [ ] Figure voice clearly distinct and sustainable for 15+ minutes.
- [ ] Figure's mouth opens per syllable and its eyes follow the conversation.
- [ ] You look at the figure when it speaks.
- [ ] The script has a clear status game and a strong closer.
