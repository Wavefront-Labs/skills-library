# Guarding of moving parts and entanglement hazards

**Goal:** nobody can reach a moving part in normal operation, and servicing happens with energy isolated.

## Hazards
Rotating shafts, PTO drivelines, augers, chain and belt nip points, tines, knives and roll nips. People are caught while clearing blockages, lubricating, adjusting or cleaning with the machine running.

## Guard types
- Fixed guards (removed only with tools) for parts needing no access.
- Interlocked guards for periodic access: the machine stops before access and does not restart on closing.
- Distance and tunnel guards on feed openings, sized so a person cannot reach the hazard.
- Trip bars and emergency stops where a person could be drawn in, within reach.
- Standards such as the ISO 4254 series (agricultural machinery safety; part titles and editions not confirmed because iso.org blocks automated fetches (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)) and national rules specify designs; check the edition and local law.
- US example: 29 CFR 1928.57 (eCFR text re-fetched October 2026) requires master shields or guards on all PTO shafts, guarding of nip and meshing points of gears, belts, chains, sheaves, pulleys, sprockets and idlers, guarding of revolving shafts (except smooth shaft ends protruding less than half the shaft diameter), and rotation warnings where a part keeps turning after power is off. Guards must withstand a 250 lb person. Parts of it apply only to equipment made after 25 October 1976. This is a summary; ask the workplace safety authority.

## Practice
- Refit guards after every service.
- Lock out: stop the engine, remove the key, relieve hydraulic, spring and gravity energy, and verify zero energy before working.
- Train all workers, including family and seasonal staff.
