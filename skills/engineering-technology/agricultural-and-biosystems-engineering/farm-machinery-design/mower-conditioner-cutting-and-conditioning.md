# Mower-conditioner cutting and conditioning

**Goal:** a clean cut and enough crop surface damage to dry at a uniform rate, without leaf loss.

## Cutting
- Disc mowers cut by impact at high blade-tip speed and tolerate heavy, lodged crops; sickle (reciprocating) bars shear and need sharp, correctly set guards and sections. Take cutterbar speed from the operator's manual; do not guess it.
- Ragged stubble, streaks and plugging point to dull or damaged blades, worn guards, or ground speed too high for the crop density.
- Stubble height is a trade-off: lower gives more yield; higher leaves a drying cushion and, for some forages such as alfalfa, protects regrowth. Follow local extension guidance.

## Conditioning
- Roller (intermeshing rubber or steel) conditioners crush stems, which suits legumes; flail or impeller conditioners abrade the waxy cuticle, which suits grasses. The point is that stems dry far more slowly than leaves; conditioning narrows the gap.
- Roll gap and pressure are adjustable. Too tight breaks leaves and raises loss; too loose leaves stems intact. Start from the manual, then examine cut stems: legume stems should be cracked and flattened with little leaf shatter.
- Flail intensity is set by rotor speed, hood position and deflector; over-conditioning grass shreds it.

## Swath
A wide swath (most of the cut width) speeds drying; narrow windrows dry slowly but resist rain damage and rake easily. Decide from the forecast and target moisture (see `hay-moisture-and-drying-rate`).

## Verify
- [ ] Stubble even, no uncut streaks.
- [ ] Sample 10 stems after the pass: most visibly cracked (legumes) or abraded (grass).
- [ ] Compare leaf shatter against a comparison strip at baling.
- Unconfirmed: exact cutter speeds and roll gaps vary by make and model.
