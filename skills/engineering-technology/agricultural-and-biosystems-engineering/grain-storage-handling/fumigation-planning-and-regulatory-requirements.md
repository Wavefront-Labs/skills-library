# Fumigation planning and regulatory requirements

**This is planning guidance, not a procedure to carry out.** Fumigants are restricted-use or licensed-use pesticides; only trained, certified applicators may apply them, following the product label and local law. A mistake can kill people or contaminate food.

## Is fumigation needed
Prefer prevention and non-chemical options first: sanitation, cooling, drying, hermetic storage (`hermetic-storage-design-and-monitoring`), and monitoring (`insect-sampling-and-treatment-thresholds`). Fumigate only when a live infestation threatens the grain's market and other methods are not enough.

## Regulatory framework (confirm locally)
- The product label is the legal instrument; it sets the dose, exposure time by temperature, permitted commodities, structures, protective equipment, monitoring and placarding. Follow the label for your country; label requirements change, so read the current one.
- Phosphine (from aluminium or magnesium phosphide) is the common grain fumigant; methyl bromide production and import were phased out in the US on 1 January 2005 under the Montreal Protocol and Clean Air Act, with exemptions for critical uses and quarantine and pre-shipment (EPA); other fumigants exist in some markets. Residue limits in the importing country matter for export grain.
- Applicator certification and licensing are required in most countries; workplace regulators set exposure limits (for example US OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1000 Table Z-1 lists phosphine at 0.3 ppm as the 8-hour time-weighted average and 0.4 ppm as the short-term limit; confirm the current limit locally) and require personal and area monitoring and respiratory protection, and that two people be present as the label may require.
- Notification of neighbours or emergency services, warning placards, and secure access are normally required.

US grain-handling rule 29 CFR 1910.272 also requires training on pesticide and fumigant usage, and its appendix advises that when a fumigant was recently applied and entry is needed, aeration fans should run continuously and toxic levels be monitored with direct-reading instruments.

## Planning elements
1. Fumigation management plan: structure, grain amount and condition, temperature, exposure period, dose, application method, monitoring points, ventilation, emergency steps, and people responsible.
2. Gas-tightness: the structure must be sealed; test by a pressure decay test (half-life) against the label and the standard used in your jurisdiction (the required half-life varies by label and country (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)); poorly sealed structures lose gas and fail to kill resistant insects, and leak gas to workers and neighbours.
3. Temperature: low temperature extends the exposure; very cold grain may need extra time or deferral.
4. Application, monitoring of the concentration, confirming the target concentration-time product, then venting and clearance testing before re-entry; handle spent residues as the label instructs.
5. Resistance management: under-dosing and leaky structures promote phosphine-resistant insects; follow the label and keep records.
