# Nickel release and sensitivity

**Goal:** show nickel exposure from the device is acceptable for the patient population, including sensitised patients.

## Facts
- Nickel is present in austenitic stainless steels (around 10 to 14 percent), in nitinol (about half the composition) and in trace amounts in cobalt-chromium alloys. Nickel allergy is common in the general population (reported for roughly one in ten or more people, mostly women, from jewellery exposure; confirm epidemiology), though allergic reactions to implants are less common.
- Release depends on corrosion resistance of the surface, not alloy content: a stable passive oxide limits release (see passivation-astm-f86). Nitinol with a good titanium oxide layer (electropolish and passivation) releases little, whereas heat-tinted or damaged surfaces release more.

## Assessment
1. **Chemical characterisation and ion release testing:** immerse finished devices in a physiological solution for a defined time, analyse for nickel (ICP-MS), and extrapolate to daily release; test with surface condition as manufactured and after simulated damage.
2. **Toxicological risk assessment:** compare predicted daily exposure with tolerable intake limits; consider the sensitised patient.
3. **Corrosion testing** (see cyclic-polarization-astm-f2129) shows breakdown susceptibility.
4. **Surface treatments:** electropolishing, passivation, oxide thickening, coatings.
5. **Labelling:** state nickel content where the rules or practice require, for patients with known nickel allergy.
6. Clinical follow-up: complaints of dermatitis or hypersensitivity are recorded.

## Verify
- [ ] Ion release data on finished, sterilised devices.
- [ ] Toxicological assessment documented.
- [ ] Nickel labelling and complaint monitoring aligned with regulatory expectations for each market.
