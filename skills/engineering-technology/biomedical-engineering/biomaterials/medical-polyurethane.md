# Medical polyurethanes

**Goal:** choose a polyurethane whose biostability and mechanics suit the implant duration and environment.

## Chemistry
- Segmented block copolymers of hard segments (diisocyanate and chain extender) and soft segments (polyether, polyester, polycarbonate, silicone-modified). Hard-to-soft ratio sets hardness (Shore A to Shore D) and modulus.
- **Polyether urethanes** are hydrolytically stable but can oxidise in vivo (metal ion oxidation and environmental stress cracking), as seen in early pacemaker lead insulation failures.
- **Polyester urethanes** hydrolyse, so are poor for long-term implants.
- **Polycarbonate urethanes (PCU)** resist hydrolysis and oxidation better and are used in longer-term implants (for example spinal discs and cartilage replacements, lead insulation) with biostability data; confirm per grade and application.
- **Aromatic versus aliphatic:** aromatic types can degrade to aromatic amines under light or in vivo; aliphatic types avoid this.

## Selection and testing
1. Contact duration and environment (stress, metal contact, fluids).
2. Mechanical: tensile, tear, creep, flex fatigue, hardness, abrasion; correlate to application.
3. Biostability: in vitro oxidative (hydrogen peroxide with cobalt chloride) and hydrolytic ageing, stressed samples for environmental stress cracking, and real-time in vivo data where available.
4. Biological evaluation of the finished device (see biocompatibility-evaluation-iso-10993) and extractables.
5. Processing: dry resin before extrusion or moulding; moisture causes degradation; thermal history changes properties.

## Verify
- [ ] Biostability data for the grade in the relevant environment.
- [ ] Stress-cracking tests under strain with oxidative media.
- [ ] Processing controls for moisture and temperature.
