# Magnesium alloy implants

**Goal:** judge when a bioresorbable magnesium implant is appropriate and how to test it.

## Features
- Magnesium corrodes in body fluid: Mg plus 2 H2O gives magnesium hydroxide plus hydrogen gas. Gas can accumulate as subcutaneous bubbles if the rate is too high, and the corrosion products raise local pH.
- Mechanical properties (modulus about 40 to 45 GPa, close to bone) reduce stress shielding, but strength falls as the implant corrodes; the design must keep strength through the healing period.
- Alloys: rare-earth containing alloys (WE43 type), calcium and zinc alloys, with elements chosen for biocompatibility; alloy composition and impurities (iron, nickel, copper) strongly increase corrosion.
- Some CE-marked Mg screws and a Mg coronary scaffold have been used clinically; confirm current regulatory status and evidence for any device.

## Testing
1. In vitro immersion in simulated body fluid or cell culture medium at 37 C, measuring mass loss, hydrogen evolution and pH; the in vitro rate often differs from in vivo, so use correlation with animal data.
2. Electrochemical tests (polarisation, impedance) for corrosion behaviour.
3. Mechanical retention over time in the corroding state.
4. Degradation products analysis (see ISO 10993-15) and toxicology of released ions.
5. Animal implantation with imaging for gas and bone response.

## Control
- Coatings (conversion, polymer), alloy purification, and surface treatments slow the initial degradation.

## Verify
- [ ] Degradation rate in vivo correlated with in vitro model.
- [ ] Mechanical strength retention meets the healing timeline.
- [ ] Gas evolution and local pH effects evaluated.
