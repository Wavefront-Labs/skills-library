# Bioresorbable scaffold lessons

**Goal:** capture design and evidence lessons from the first generation of coronary bioresorbable scaffolds.

## What happened (summary; consult primary sources)
- A poly-L-lactic acid scaffold eluting everolimus was developed to restore vessel function after resorption. Early trials looked promising, but larger randomised trials and registries later showed higher rates of target lesion failure and scaffold thrombosis versus a metallic drug-eluting stent, particularly in the first years and in small vessels. The manufacturer stopped global sales in 2017, and regulators issued safety communications.

## Contributing factors identified
- **Thick struts** (around 150 micrometres) compared with metallic stents (roughly 80 to 100 micrometres or less), increasing flow disturbance and thrombogenicity, especially if the scaffold was undersized or malapposed.
- **Mechanical differences:** lower radial strength and limited over-expansion tolerance (risk of strut fracture), so deployment technique (vessel sizing, pre-dilatation, post-dilatation) mattered.
- **Resorption timeline** leaves a period when the scaffold is neither fully supportive nor gone.

## Design lessons
1. Material strength per unit thickness is the limit: polymer scaffolds need thicker struts for equal radial force; evaluate metallic bioresorbable alternatives and thinner polymers.
2. Test over-expansion, strut fracture and acute recoil during development, plus longer clinical follow-up before broad adoption.
3. Define deployment instructions and patient selection, and train users; make this part of the usability and risk file.
4. Post-market surveillance and registries detect late events; define stopping rules.

## Verify
- [ ] Strut thickness and radial force compared to the metallic benchmark.
- [ ] Over-expansion limits tested and labelled.
- [ ] Follow-up and surveillance plan covers late events.
