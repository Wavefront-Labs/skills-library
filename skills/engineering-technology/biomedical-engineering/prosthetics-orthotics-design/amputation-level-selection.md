# Amputation level and its effect on prosthetic design

**Goal:** give the design engineer the level-to-function relationships that drive socket, suspension and component requirements.

The choice of amputation level is made by surgeons based on perfusion, disease, healing and function. This skill is design context only, not clinical advice.

## Main levels (lower limb)
- **Partial foot, Syme:** short lever loss; high-load sockets and low-profile feet are the design problems.
- **Transtibial:** knee preserved; suitable residual length gives lever arm and area for load transfer. Energy cost of walking is increased, and the Waters 1976 study (J Bone Joint Surg Am, 70 patients) found performance better the lower the level of amputation; the percentage energy-cost figures per level and cause are not restated here (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- **Knee disarticulation:** end-bearing, long femoral lever, but bulky knee mechanisms need clearance.
- **Transfemoral:** knee function must be replaced; energy cost rises more than transtibial, which is higher than transtibial in that study (percentages gated). Residual femur length controls the lever for socket control; very short limbs behave like hip disarticulation.
- **Hip disarticulation and hemipelvectomy:** pelvic socket and hip joint design.
- **Upper limb:** transradial retains elbow; transhumeral needs elbow and often shoulder-driven control; shoulder disarticulation limits control sources.

## Design consequences
- Shorter residual limb means lower lever arm for suspension, more reliance on seal or osseointegration.
- Bilateral loss can need shorter, lighter "stubbies" for training.
- Cause (vascular, trauma, tumour, congenital) influences age, activity target and skin condition.
