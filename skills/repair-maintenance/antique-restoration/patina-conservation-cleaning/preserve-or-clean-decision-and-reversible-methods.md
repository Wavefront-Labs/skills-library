# Preserve-or-clean decision and reversible methods

**Goal:** remove what harms or obscures an antique (dirt, grime, active corrosion, failed
coatings) while keeping what gives it value and history (original finish, patina, wear
from use, marks). The default answer is "do less"; every step must be justified and,
where possible, reversible.

## 1. Look before acting: the condition report
Write a short report with photos (raking light and normal light, scale bar):
- Object, materials (wood, finish type, metal, paint, textile), approximate date.
- **Original surfaces vs later additions**: overpaint, later varnish, replaced parts.
  A UV torch (365 nm) helps: shellac fluoresces orange, modern varnishes often dull/blue,
  retouching shows as dark patches.
- **Active vs stable damage:** flaking paint, powdery corrosion, woodworm frass (active)
  need stabilising; old stable wear does not.
- Owner's aim: display, use, sale. Record what the owner agreed to.

## 2. Patina or dirt?
- **Patina** — the slow change of the surface itself: oxidised and polished wood finish,
  toned metal, worn edges, hand-darkening on handles. It is part of the object.
- **Dirt/grime** — material sitting *on* the surface: soot, dust, nicotine, wax build-up,
  fly-specks. Usually removable without touching the surface.
- Test: dirt comes off on a slightly damp swab; patina doesn't, and shouldn't be chased.
- Over-cleaned brown furniture and over-polished bronze lose value at auction;
  "untouched original condition" is a selling point.

## 3. The gentlest-first cleaning ladder
Always test on a small, inconspicuous area (≈ 1 cm²) and watch the swab.
1. **Dry:** soft brush (pony/goat hair) into a vacuum nozzle covered with net/stocking;
   smoke sponge (vulcanised rubber) for matte surfaces.
2. **Saliva/deionised water**, swab barely damp, rolled not rubbed. (Conservators really do
   use saliva — its enzymes lift grime gently. Deionised water with a trace of non-ionic
   surfactant is the polite alternative.)
3. **Mild non-polar solvent:** white spirit/mineral spirits (odourless) — removes wax and
   greasy grime from wood finishes without dissolving shellac or most old varnishes.
4. **More polar solvents** (ethanol, acetone) only after testing: they dissolve shellac
   (ethanol) and many varnishes/lacquers (acetone) — i.e. they can remove original finish.
5. Stop at the first level that works. If the swab picks up colour from the finish/paint
   itself (not dirt), stop.

## 4. Stabilise rather than restore
- Flaking paint: consolidate with a dilute conservation adhesive (e.g. a few percent
  Paraloid B-72 in solvent, or sturgeon glue on gesso), laid down with a warm spatula
  through silicone paper — no repainting needed.
- Active corrosion on metal: remove loose products mechanically, dry, and protect with
  microcrystalline wax or a reversible lacquer.
- Woodworm: confirm activity (fresh pale frass, clean exit holes), then freezing (e.g.
  ≥ 1–2 weeks at about −20 to −30 °C, bagged) or targeted treatment — not blanket chemicals
  on finished surfaces.
- Environment fixes everything slowly: stable RH (about 40–60 %), no direct sun, no radiators.

## 5. Reversibility and records
- Use materials that can later be removed without harming the original (B-72, wax,
  animal glue in wood joints), and record what was used where.
- Keep removed original parts in a labelled bag with the object.
- Final report: before/after photos, methods, materials (with product names), dates.

## Gotchas
- Silicone-containing furniture polishes contaminate surfaces and complicate future
  finishing — avoid entirely.
- "Feeding" wood with oil is a myth; oil darkens and attracts dirt over time.
- Uncertain: consolidant concentrations and freezing parameters are typical guideline
  values; check a conservator for significant pieces.

## Verify
- [ ] Condition report with photos and owner's agreed aim exists before cleaning.
- [ ] Patina and original finish identified and left in place.
- [ ] Every method spot-tested; ladder stopped at the gentlest effective step.
- [ ] Active damage stabilised; materials reversible where possible.
- [ ] Before/after record lists methods and materials used.
