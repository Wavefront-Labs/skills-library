# Veneer patching, blisters and inlay repair

**Goal:** make lifted, blistered or missing veneer and inlay sound and nearly invisible,
keeping as much original material as possible.

## 1. Assess
- Tap the surface with a fingernail: a hollow, papery sound marks a blister or loose
  area; outline it in chalk or low-tack tape.
- Find the glue: almost all pre-1950s veneer is **hide glue**, which reactivates with
  heat and moisture — good news, most relaying needs no new glue at all.
- Keep every loose fragment in a labelled envelope; original veneer is always better than
  a patch.

## 2. Relaying blisters and lifted edges
1. **Lifted edge:** slide a thin palette knife under, scrape out dust and old glue
   crumbs, work in fresh glue (hot hide or liquid hide glue) with the knife or a
   syringe with a blunt needle.
2. **Closed blister:** slit along the grain with a fresh scalpel (No. 10A blade) —
   along the grain the cut disappears. Inject glue through the slit, press out excess.
3. **Heat reactivation (hide glue only):** cover with brown paper or a damp cloth and
   apply a household iron at medium (wool setting, ~150 °C) for 10–20 s, then press
   down with a veneer hammer or block until it cools. Test the finish first: heat can
   bloom shellac or lacquer — lift the iron sooner and refinish locally if needed.
4. Clamp with a **caul**: a flat block, covered in cling film or waxed paper, with a
   cork or rubber pad, under a G-clamp or weight. Several hours for liquid hide glue.
   Where clamps won't reach, use go-bars or bags of sand.
5. Wipe squeeze-out with a warm damp cloth before it gels.

## 3. Patching missing veneer
1. **Match** species, figure, colour and thickness. Old veneer is often 1–1.5 mm saw-cut;
   modern veneer is ~0.6 mm and will need a backing layer or two to build up. Salvage
   from old furniture backs or drawer bottoms of the same period where possible.
2. Cut the patch to a **boat or diamond shape** with ends pointing along the grain —
   straight lines across the grain show; a long lozenge along the grain hides.
3. Tape the patch over the damage with the grain aligned, cut through both layers with a
   scalpel against a steel rule, remove the patch, chisel out the damaged veneer to the
   line and scrape the groundwork clean and flat.
4. Glue in with hide glue, caul and clamp. Level when dry with a card scraper, not
   sandpaper on the surrounding finish.
5. Colour with alcohol dyes or pigment in shellac with a fine brush, then pad over (see
   `repair-maintenance.furniture-restoration-upholstery.french-polishing.french-polishing-and-shellac-repair`).

## 4. Inlay, stringing and banding
- Stringing (1–2 mm lines of boxwood, ebony or holly) and crossbanding: buy ready-made
  from marquetry suppliers or cut with a cutting gauge from veneer.
- Clean the groove with a scraper or a filed-down small screwdriver. Glue, press in with
  a hammer face or the back of a gouge, tape across to hold. Mitre corners with a chisel.
- Missing marquetry pieces: take a rubbing (pencil on tracing paper), transfer to veneer,
  cut with a fretsaw or scalpel, shade if needed in hot sand (dip edge for a few seconds).

## 5. Small losses: burn-in and wax
- **Burn-in sticks** (shellac/resin): heat a burn-in knife (electric or spirit-heated),
  melt the stick into the chip, level with the knife; finish flush with a fine abrasive.
  Several colours can be blended, and grain lines painted on afterwards.
- **Hard wax fill sticks:** softer, good for small holes in less used areas; not durable
  on table tops.

## Gotchas
- Too much moisture or heat lifts surrounding veneer and destroys finish — localised
  heat only, short bursts.
- PVA or contact cement under veneer creeps and can't be reheated; avoid on antiques.
- Lifted veneer around keyholes and drawer fronts is often from wood movement: don't trim
  the overhang until relaid — it often fits back.
- Spirit burners for burn-in knives: keep away from solvent fumes.
