# Sympathetic restoration of vintage timepieces

**Goal:** get an old watch or clock working reliably while keeping as much of
what makes it valuable and authentic — original parts, finishes, dial and case — and
leaving a clear record of what was done.

> Draft: practice varies between conservator and restorer traditions; for museum
> objects, follow conservation ethics (minimum intervention, reversibility) and
> involve a conservator. Radium regulations differ by country.

## 1. Before touching it: the condition report
- Photograph everything: dial (straight-on and angled), hands, case inside and out,
  movement both sides, any marks and serial numbers, case-back hallmarks.
- Record: maker, calibre, serial numbers (movement and case — do they belong
  together?), previous repairer's marks scratched inside the case-back (they date
  past services), replaced parts you can identify, damage.
- Test what works before strip-down.
- Agree **the owner's goal**: a reliable daily wearer, a collector's piece kept as
  original as possible, or a display object. This decides most later questions.

## 2. Conserve, repair, or replace — in that order
1. **Conserve** (clean, stabilise) original parts whenever possible.
2. **Repair** an original part (re-pivot a wheel, re-tooth, weld/braze a broken lever,
   re-pin) before replacing it.
3. **Replace** with an original/new-old-stock part, then a period part from a donor,
   then a newly made part — in that order of preference.
4. Keep replaced original parts and return them to the owner in a labelled bag.
Ask of every intervention: could a future restorer reverse it? Is it distinguishable
on close inspection (for museum work)?

## 3. Dials: the biggest value decision
- **Never refinish an original dial without explicit owner consent** — a
  refinished (redialled) dial usually cuts collector value substantially, even if it
  looks better.
- Clean dials with the gentlest method: a soft blower, dry soft brush; anything wetter
  is tested on an edge first — printing on many vintage dials dissolves in water or
  solvent. Enamel dials: clean only; hairline cracks are stable — leave them.
- **Patina** (even ageing, "tropical" browning) is often prized; preserve it.
- **Radium luminous paint** (common pre-~1960s): radioactive. Don't scrape, sand or
  brush it; work over a tray with a damp wipe for any dust, wear gloves, no eating,
  dispose of debris per local rules; keep hands/dials in a sealed container. Re-luming
  with modern non-radioactive compounds is a disclosure item.

## 4. Movements and obsolete parts
- Sourcing: material houses and dealers, NOS (new old stock), donor movements of the
  same calibre (check variants — "same calibre" can differ in balance staffs, stems,
  setting parts), collector forums, parts books (Bestfit/Flume-type references for
  stems, staffs, crystals).
- **Making parts** (lathe and bench): staffs, stems, wheels, clicks and springs, screws
  — match the period's finish (blued screws, black-polished steel, gilt plates) and
  material. See the balance-staff turning skill.
- Old fusee chains, verge escapements, cylinder escapements and enamel need specialist
  knowledge — refer if you don't have it.
- Use period-sympathetic but **modern-reliable** lubrication; synthetic oils are now
  common practice in restoration.

## 5. Cases and finishes
- Light cleaning over polishing. Preserve hallmarks, maker's marks and engravings;
  never polish them away.
- Plating wear on base-metal cases: replating is irreversible and should be the owner's
  explicit choice.
- Wooden clock cases: clean and wax; don't strip original finishes — refer to a
  furniture conservator for veneer and finish work.

## 6. Document the work
A restoration record (kept with the piece):
- Before and after photos.
- Every part replaced: what, why, source (original/NOS/donor/made).
- Treatments applied (cleaning methods, lubricants, any refinishing).
- Timing results (timegrapher numbers for watches; rate over a week for clocks).
- Date and restorer's mark — scratched lightly inside a case-back is traditional;
  ask the owner first.

## Gotchas
- "Improving" a dial or case can destroy most of the piece's value.
- Mixing case and movement from different watches to "complete" one is
  misrepresentation if not disclosed.
- Donor parts from a slightly different calibre variant cause odd faults later.
- Radium dust in your workspace is a lasting hazard; contain it from the start.

## Verify
- [ ] Condition report and photos before work; owner's goal agreed in writing.
- [ ] Each intervention followed conserve → repair → replace and is recorded.
- [ ] Dial and case finishes left original unless owner explicitly consented.
- [ ] Radium handled with containment if present.
- [ ] Original removed parts returned; restoration record handed over with the piece.
