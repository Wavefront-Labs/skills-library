# Disinfectant selection, dwell times and touchpoints

**Goal:** actually kill the target pathogens on the surfaces that spread them. Most
"disinfection" fails for one of three reasons: the surface wasn't cleaned first, the
product was wrong for the organism, or it didn't stay wet for the label **contact time**.

## 1. Words that matter
- **Cleaning:** removes soil and many microbes — always first (organic matter shields
  microbes and inactivates many disinfectants). Some "one-step" products clean and
  disinfect lightly soiled surfaces; heavy soil still needs a separate clean.
- **Sanitising:** reduces bacteria to safe levels (e.g. food-contact surfaces).
- **Disinfecting:** kills listed bacteria/viruses (claims on the label).
- **Sterilising:** all microbial life — not a cleaning-services activity.

## 2. Choose by claim, then by surface
Check the label/registration: in the US, **EPA registration** and EPA lists such as List G
(norovirus) and List K (C. difficile spores). Find the product's EPA Reg. No. on the label and
check that the label has directions for the pathogen under "Use Directions for Disinfection".
In the UK/EU, look for tests to the relevant EN standards.
| Active | Strengths | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| Quats (quaternary ammonium) | Low odour, residual, surface-friendly | Weak on non-enveloped viruses (norovirus) and spores; cotton/microfibre can bind quats ("quat binding") |
| Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) | Broad, includes norovirus; sporicidal at high strength | Corrodes metals, bleaches fabric, inactivated by soil, irritant; never mix with acids/ammonia |
| Hydrogen peroxide / accelerated H₂O₂ | Fast, broad, breaks down to water/oxygen | Can damage some metals/finishes at high strength |
| Alcohol | Fast on small items/electronics | Evaporates before contact time on large areas; flammable |
| Peracetic acid | Sporicidal, used in healthcare/food | Pungent, corrosive |
Bleach for norovirus (CDC): 1,000 to 5,000 ppm chlorine, which is 5 to 25 tablespoons of
household bleach (5% to 8%) per gallon of water, left on the surface at least 5 minutes, or
use an EPA-registered product against norovirus. Check the product label and local guidance
for other organisms.

## 3. Contact (dwell) time
- The label time (EPA List G shows 0.5 to 10 minutes) is how long the surface must **stay visibly wet**. If it
  dries early, re-apply or use a product with a shorter time for the use.
- Pre-moistened wipes: use enough wipes to keep surfaces wet; don't wipe a whole room with
  one wipe.

## 4. Touchpoint programme
1. Map high-touch surfaces per area: door handles and push plates, light switches, lift
   buttons, handrails, taps, toilet flush handles, desks/keyboards (if in scope), shared
   phones, vending/kiosk screens, chair arms, reception counters.
2. Set frequencies by traffic and risk, higher during outbreaks.
3. Method: one cloth per area (use a fresh face per surface), clean to
   dirty, top to bottom. Electronics: maker-approved products sprayed on the cloth.
4. Record completion (sign sheet or app).

## 5. Electrostatic sprayers and fogging
- Electrostatic sprayers charge droplets so they wrap around surfaces — useful for large
  areas and complex shapes, **only with a product whose label allows that application
  method**, on surfaces cleaned first, and still achieving contact time.
- Fogging/misting whole rooms with general disinfectants isn't a substitute for cleaning;
  room decontamination systems (hydrogen peroxide vapour, UV-C) are specialist and need
  sealed, unoccupied rooms.
- PPE per the SDS; keep people out until the label's re-entry interval.

## 6. Outbreak response clean
Identify the organism (norovirus? C. diff? flu?), pick a product with that claim, clean
then disinfect all surfaces in affected areas (with a plan for soft furnishings), increase
touchpoint frequency, separate equipment for affected areas, launder hot (CDC: detergent and hot water at the maximum cycle length, then dry on the highest heat), and brief staff.
ATP swabs indicate organic residue, not specific pathogens.

## Gotchas
- Spray-and-wipe-immediately = cleaning, not disinfecting.
- Follow the label dilution; stronger than labelled can damage surfaces. Use dispensers.
- Disinfectants on food-contact surfaces may need a potable rinse — check label.
