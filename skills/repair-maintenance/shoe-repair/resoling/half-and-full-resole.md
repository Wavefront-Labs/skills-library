# Half-sole and full resole

**Goal:** give a pair of shoes a new walking surface that bonds or stitches
permanently, matches the original look, and doesn't change how the shoe fits or flexes.

> Draft: adhesive brands and open times vary (polychloroprene contact cements like
> Renia Colle de Cologne or Barge; PU adhesives for some rubbers). Always read the
> adhesive's data sheet and ventilate — solvent cements are flammable and harmful.

## 1. Assess the shoe first
- **Construction:** look at the sole edge and inside.
  - **Goodyear welt:** a strip (welt) around the edge, stitched to the sole; insole not
    pierced by the outsole stitch. Fully resoleable many times.
  - **Blake stitch:** stitch line visible inside the shoe on the insole. Needs a
    Blake/McKay machine to re-stitch through, or cement + a stitched-on half sole.
  - **Cemented:** no stitches — glued. Resole by cementing.
  - Moulded/injected soles (trainers, many boots) — often not economically resoleable.
- **Wear:** worn only at the forepart → **half sole**. Worn through to the midsole/
  welt, cracked across, or heel-to-toe wear → **full resole**.
- **Damage to the welt or insole?** Holes through the insole mean a bigger job
  (welt/insole repair first) — quote it now.

## 2. Half sole (leather or rubber)
1. Mark a cut line across the waist, just behind the ball, where the sole still has
   thickness — usually a gentle curve or a straight skived line.
2. Cut and remove the worn forepart; keep the welt/stitching intact if Goodyear.
3. **Skive** (feather) the remaining sole at the joint so the new piece overlaps on a
   long bevel (~15–20 mm), not a butt joint.
4. Rough both surfaces (bench roughing wheel or 40–60 grit), remove dust.
5. Cement both surfaces thinly; wait the open time (until touch-dry, typically
   10–20 min); a second coat on porous leather. Reactivate with heat if the cement
   requires it.
6. Position carefully (contact cement grabs on contact), press — sole press or
   hammer from centre outward on a last/anvil.
7. On Goodyear shoes, re-stitch through the welt with a sole stitcher (or hand
   stitch) along the original channel; or a Blake re-stitch where applicable.
   Cemented half soles on rubber protectors don't need stitching.

## 3. Full resole
1. Remove heel (pry off top lifts, extract nails), then the old sole: cut the
   stitches along the welt with a knife or stitch cutter, peel the sole.
2. Clean the welt and cork filler; replace crumbled cork with new cork filler/sheet.
   Fit a new shank if broken.
3. Cut the new sole oversize (~5 mm all round), rough, cement, lay, press.
4. Cut a stitch channel (channel opener) on leather soles, stitch through the welt
   (6–8 stitches per inch is typical for dress shoes), close the channel by cementing
   and rubbing the flap down so stitches are hidden — or leave open on a work boot.
5. Rebuild heel (see heel repair skill).

## 4. Edge finishing
- Trim the sole flush with the welt using a knife then the finishing machine's
  trimmer; sand edges progressively (80 → 180 → 320 grit).
- Apply edge ink/dye in matching colour (two thin coats), dry, then **burnish** with a
  heated edge iron or wax on the finishing machine's brushes.
- Sole bottom: scour, stain if leather, and polish/wax; add a waist dye line if the
  original had one.

## 5. Rubber protectors (topy) on leather soles
Thin rubber sheet over the forepart of a new leather sole: rough the leather lightly,
cement, press, trim flush, bevel the edge. Adds grip and life; slightly reduces breathability.

## Gotchas
- Cement too wet when joined → weak bond and edges lift within weeks.
- Changing sole thickness changes the toe spring and fit — match the original.
- Never re-stitch a new channel next to an old one on a thin welt; reuse the holes
  or the welt splits.
- Glue on uppers is near impossible to remove from suede: mask with tape.

## Verify
- [ ] Construction identified and the job type (half/full) quoted before starting.
- [ ] Bond surfaces roughed, cemented to data sheet, pressed; no lifting at edges after flexing.
- [ ] Stitching through welt even, in channel, closed or neat.
- [ ] Edge flush, inked and burnished; colours matched.
- [ ] Shoe flexes at the ball and sits level on a flat surface.
