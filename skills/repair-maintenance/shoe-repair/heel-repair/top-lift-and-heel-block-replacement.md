# Top lift, heel tip and heel block replacement

**Goal:** the most common cobbler job done properly — fresh heel wear surface,
solid fixing, original height and angle, and a clean edge — plus rebuilding heels
that wore past the lift.

> Draft: tip pin diameters and lift sizes vary by manufacturer; keep an assortment and
> measure the old part. Use eye protection on finishing machines.

## 1. Assess
- Worn only into the **top lift** (the replaceable bottom layer)? → lift/tip replacement.
- Worn into the **heel block/stack** (layers above the lift eroded at the back corner)?
  → rebuild with a heel base/lift layer before the new top lift.
- Heel **loose** or wobbling → reattach from inside (nails/screws through insole into
  heel) before anything else.
- **Broken heel** (ladies', plastic core) → replacement heel block or refuse if the
  shank or heel seat is damaged.
- Check heel height with a ruler before you start; you'll match it at the end.

## 2. Men's top lift (rubber or leather/rubber combination)
1. Pry the old lift off with a lift remover or chisel, working from the back.
2. Pull or cut flush any nails; file the heel base flat. Fill hollows with cut leather
   or a heel base layer so the seat is flat and level.
3. Rough the heel bottom and the new lift (grinding wheel/sandpaper), dust off.
4. Cement both, wait open time, place lift slightly oversize and centred, press.
5. Nail: 4–6 heel nails (ring-shank/brass) around the perimeter about 8–10 mm from
   the edge, angled slightly inward, into solid heel. Check inside the shoe for
   protruding points — **clinch** them on an iron last if they come through.
6. Trim on the finishing machine flush with the heel, following the existing angle
   (pitch); sand, ink the edge, burnish.

## 3. Ladies' heel tips (pin tips)
1. Pull the old tip with pliers (or tip puller). Inspect the hole and the plastic heel
   core: worn into the plastic? → build up first.
2. Measure the old pin diameter (commonly ~2.5–3.5 mm) and choose a tip with the
   matching pin and tip size/shape (round, square, stiletto).
3. Put a drop of cyanoacrylate or cement in the hole, tap the tip pin home with a
   hammer on a last/heel anvil.
4. Trim to the heel outline on the finishing wheel, angle to match the original.
5. If the plastic core is worn down and exposed: cut back cleanly, glue and pin a
   small piece of heel-tip material or a solid block to restore height, then fit the tip.

## 4. Rebuilding a heel block / stacked heel
1. Remove top lift; cut back damaged layers to sound, flat material.
2. Glue in new layers of stacking leather (or leatherboard/rubber) to bring height back,
   each layer cemented and pressed, slightly oversize.
3. Nail through layers into the heel base where construction allows.
4. Top with the new lift; check height with a ruler and level on a flat surface.
5. Trim the whole stack to the heel's profile; sand through grits; stain/ink
   layers to match and burnish so the stack lines show as on the original.

## 5. Height and pitch
- Original heel height should be restored within ~1 mm; a lower heel changes the
  shoe's pitch, overloads the shank and can make the toe lift off the ground.
- Stand the shoe on a flat surface: the heel tread should sit flat, not on its back edge.
- Pairs must match — measure both heels and finish both at the same time.

## Gotchas
- Nails too near the edge split leather stacks; too long come through the heel seat.
- A wobbly heel re-lifted without fixing the inside attachment fails quickly.
- Plastic heels get hot on the finishing wheel and melt; light passes.
- Trimming square instead of following the heel's taper looks obviously "repaired".

## Verify
- [ ] Heel attachment checked solid before replacing the lift.
- [ ] Seat flat and filled; lift cemented and nailed; no nail points inside.
- [ ] Tip pins seated fully, matching original size and shape.
- [ ] Height restored and matched on both shoes; heel sits flat.
- [ ] Edge trimmed to profile, inked and burnished.
