# Shoe stretching and fit adjustment

**Goal:** make a specific pair comfortable for a specific foot — relieve pressure
where it pinches, take up volume where it slips — without damaging the shoe or
promising what leather can't do.

> Draft: stretch limits below are practical rules of thumb; results depend heavily on
> leather type, lining and construction. Patent, synthetics and heavily lined shoes
> stretch little or crack.

## 1. Assess the fit with the wearer present
- Have them wear the shoes standing and walking. Ask where it hurts; mark with chalk
  or a wax pencil on the outside while they point: little toe, bunion (1st MTP joint),
  across the instep, toe box height, heel cup.
- Check **length:** about a thumb's width (~10–15 mm) beyond the longest toe when
  standing. **Length cannot really be stretched** — at most a few mm of toe box relief.
  Too short is a "don't buy/return" answer, not a repair.
- **Width** in leather uppers: roughly up to half a width size (~3–6 mm girth) is
  realistic in soft leather; less in stiff or lined leather.
- **Slipping heel / too much volume:** padding, not stretching.

## 2. Width and spot stretching
**Tools:** two-way shoe stretcher (adjusts width and toe-box height) with screw-in
**bunion/corn plugs**, ball-and-ring pliers for spot stretching, boot shaft stretcher,
leather stretch spray (alcohol-water mix based).
**Method:**
1. Apply stretch spray (or 50:50 isopropyl/water, test first) to the inside lining
   and outside at the target area — damp, not soaked.
2. Insert stretcher; fit bunion/corn plugs at the marked points.
3. Turn the handle until the leather is snug, then **a quarter to half turn more**.
   Don't crank — a sudden crack or seam stress means stop.
4. Leave **24–48 hours**. Check; repeat in small increments.
5. For a precise pressure point: ball-and-ring pliers (ball inside, ring outside)
   pressed on the mark, after spraying; a little heat from a heat gun on low, kept
   moving, helps on stiff leather (careful — overheating shrinks and cracks leather).
6. Condition the leather afterwards (cream) — alcohol dries it.
**Boot calves:** shaft stretcher, same principle; zips limit how much a zipped boot gains.

## 3. Taking up space (too big / heel slip)
- **Heel grips** (self-adhesive suede/foam pads) inside the back of the heel counter;
  place where the heel lifts, slightly high. Reduces slip ~2–4 mm.
- **Tongue pads** under the tongue push the foot back into the heel cup — often
  better than heel grips for laced shoes with heel slip.
- **Half insoles / ball-of-foot pads** fill forefoot volume and relieve pressure on
  the metatarsals.
- **Full insoles** reduce volume overall (~2–5 mm); remove existing removable insole
  if the new one is thicker.
- Lacing changes (heel-lock/runner's loop) for laced shoes cost nothing — try first.

## 4. Fitting insoles
- Trace the original insole or the shoe's last outline on the new insole; cut slightly
  oversize and trim to fit without curling at the edges.
- Orthotic/medical insoles need room: check heel counter depth and toe-box height.
- Arch supports placed too far forward cause pain at the ball — the arch peak should sit
  just behind the ball of the foot.

## Gotchas
- Stretching patent or painted finishes cracks them; synthetic uppers barely move.
- Overstretching one side makes the shoe twist; stretch both shoes only if both need it.
- Wet leather on a stretcher for too long can stain (water marks) — spray evenly over
  a whole panel, not a blotch.
- Promise ranges, not guarantees.

## Verify
- [ ] Pressure points and slip marked with the wearer, length checked first.
- [ ] Leather type suitable; product tested; stretched in small increments.
- [ ] Leather conditioned after stretching; no cracks or seam stress.
- [ ] Wearer re-tries; marked areas relieved, heel slip reduced.
- [ ] Inserts sit flat without curling or crowding toes.
