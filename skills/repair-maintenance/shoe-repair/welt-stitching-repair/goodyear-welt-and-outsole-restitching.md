# Goodyear welt and outsole re-stitching

**Goal:** restore the stitched structure of welted footwear — outsole stitch, welt
and upper seams — so the shoe can be resoled again and won't separate.

> Draft: machine names (Landis/Rapid outsole stitchers, Adler/Singer post-bed and
> patcher machines) are examples; settings depend on the model. Hand-welting
> details vary between makers' traditions.

## 1. Understand the stitches
A Goodyear-welted shoe has two seams:
- **Welt seam (inseam):** joins the welt, upper and lining to the **insole rib**
  (gemming/canvas rib or a carved holdfast). Hidden inside the shoe.
- **Outsole stitch:** joins the welt to the outsole, visible on the welt top and
  the sole bottom (often hidden in a closed channel).
Diagnosing which is broken decides the job: outsole stitch failure = sole flapping
from the welt; welt seam failure = the welt and upper pulling away from the insole —
far more work.

## 2. Outsole re-stitching (common)
Typical when a sole is coming loose or during a resole.
1. Open the channel on leather soles (or work in the old stitch line).
2. Machine stitch on an outsole stitcher: lock-stitch with waxed thread through welt
   and sole, **following the original holes in the welt** wherever possible.
   Stitch density for dress shoes is often 7–10 per inch (SPI), work boots 5–7.
3. Hand alternative: waxed linen or polyester thread (e.g. 0.8–1.0 mm), two needles,
   **saddle stitch** through awl holes from the welt side, angling the awl to come out
   in the sole channel. Keep tension even and pull each stitch the same.
4. Close the channel (cement + rub down) or leave open; burnish.

## 3. Welt replacement (partial or full)
When the welt is cracked, rotted, torn or has too many holes from previous re-stitches.
1. Remove the outsole. Cut out the damaged welt section back to sound welt; skive the
   ends at an angle for a scarf joint.
2. Inspect the insole rib: if the gemming has torn away from the insole, re-cement
   new canvas/gemming or carve a new holdfast on leather insoles.
3. New welt strip (pre-grooved leather welt), skived to join; cement the scarf.
4. **Hand-welt** (or welt machine) through welt, upper and lining into the rib with a
   curved awl and waxed thread, stitches ~5–6 per inch, pulling the upper tight to the
   last. A last must be in the shoe for this.
5. Refill cork, re-sole, outsole-stitch through the new welt.
Partial replacement at the toe or at a damaged section is common; full re-welting is
a major job priced accordingly.

## 4. Upper seam repair
- Split seams on uppers (back seam, vamp/quarter seam, boot shafts):
  - **Post-bed machine** for reaching into the shoe; **patcher (cylinder arm)** for
    boots. Match thread colour and stitch length; go back into original holes.
  - Reinforce from inside with a thin leather or tape strip cemented behind the seam if
    the leather edge is tired.
  - Where no machine can reach: hand stitch with a curved needle, baseball/saddle stitch.
- Stitch lines that pass through the original holes are invisible; new holes next to
  old ones perforate and tear the leather.

## Gotchas
- Re-stitching a rotten welt: the new stitches tear out on first wear. Test welt
  strength by flexing — replace if it cracks.
- Machine stitch on a dry, brittle sole edge — condition or replace the sole.
- Thread too thick for old holes cracks the welt; too thin rots/abrades — match.
- Stitching without a last in a soft upper pulls the shape out.

## Verify
- [ ] Correct seam diagnosed (outsole stitch vs welt seam vs upper seam).
- [ ] Original holes reused; stitch count per inch matches original.
- [ ] Welt sound after flex test; scarf joints tight and invisible from above.
- [ ] Stitch tension even; no puckering or thread breaks.
- [ ] Shoe re-lasted shape correct; channel closed or edge finished.
