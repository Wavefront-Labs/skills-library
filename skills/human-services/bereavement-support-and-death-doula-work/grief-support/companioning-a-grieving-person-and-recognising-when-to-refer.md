# Companioning a grieving person and recognising when to refer

**Goal:** be a steady, non-judging presence for someone who is grieving, help them see their
reactions as human rather than broken, and notice the minority who need more than you can offer.

This is non-clinical support. It is not counselling or therapy unless you are qualified and
contracted to provide that. Work under your organisation's supervision and risk procedures.
For self-guided exercises a griever can use between sessions, see
mind-self.grief-and-stress-coping-technique.grief-processing-exercises.self-guided-exercises-for-moving-through-loss.

## 1. Companioning, not fixing
- **Listen more than you speak.** Ask about the person who died — their name, what they were
  like, how they died, if the griever wants to tell it. Use the name.
- Reflect back, don't interpret: "It sounds like evenings are the hardest."
- Tolerate silence and tears; don't rush to comfort them away.
- **Avoid:** "They're in a better place", "Everything happens for a reason", "At least…",
  "I know how you feel", comparing losses, or timelines ("You should be over it by now").
- Follow their beliefs and language about death, not yours.

## 2. Normalise the range
Grief commonly includes: sadness, yearning, anger, guilt, relief (especially after long
illness), numbness, poor sleep, appetite change, forgetfulness, sensing the person's
presence, dreams. Many people swing between grieving and getting on with life — the **dual
process model** describes this oscillation as healthy, not avoidance. Ongoing connection to the
person who died (**continuing bonds**) is normal; "letting go" is not required.

Grief has no fixed stages; the popular five-stage model was not intended as a sequence
grievers must pass through.

## 3. Companion over time
- Support often drops away after the funeral, just as reality arrives. Regular, predictable
  contact over months matters.
- **Anniversaries, birthdays, holidays, the first of everything** are spikes; mark them in
  your diary and check in beforehand.
- Practical life (finances, paperwork, cooking for one) adds load — link to practical help.
- Mind your boundaries: agreed contact times, the scope of your role, an ending planned with them.

## 4. When grief needs clinical help
Refer (with consent) to a GP, grief counsellor or psychologist when you notice:
- **Intense yearning or preoccupation that remains severe and disabling long after the death**
  — prolonged grief disorder is defined as lasting at least 6 months (ICD-11) or 12 months
  (DSM-5-TR) after the loss, with significant impairment.
- Traumatic circumstances (suicide, homicide, sudden violent death, disaster) with intrusive
  images, nightmares or avoidance.
- Depression symptoms beyond grief: pervasive hopelessness, worthlessness not tied to the loss.
- Heavy alcohol or drug use, inability to function at work or with dependants over time.

## 5. Urgent: suicide risk
Bereaved people — especially after suicide loss or the death of a partner or child — can be
at increased risk. If someone speaks of wanting to die or join the person, **ask directly**
about suicidal thoughts and plans, don't leave them alone if risk seems immediate, and follow
your organisation's risk procedure (crisis line, emergency services, GP). Tell your
supervisor the same day.

## 6. Look after yourself
Grief work draws on your own losses. Use supervision, debrief after hard sessions, and step
back from a client whose loss mirrors a raw one of yours.

## Gotchas
- Cultural and faith differences shape grief and mourning practices deeply; ask, don't assume.
- "They seem fine" can be numbness; "they cry every session" can be healthy.
- Don't diagnose — describe concerns and refer.
- The diagnostic time thresholds above are as published at time of writing (check current editions).

## Verify
- [ ] You talked about the person who died, by name, more than you advised.
- [ ] Clichés and timelines avoided; reactions normalised.
- [ ] Anniversaries and key dates diarised for check-ins.
- [ ] Referral indicators reviewed; referrals made with consent.
- [ ] Any suicide-risk talk asked about directly and escalated per procedure.
- [ ] You took the case to supervision.
