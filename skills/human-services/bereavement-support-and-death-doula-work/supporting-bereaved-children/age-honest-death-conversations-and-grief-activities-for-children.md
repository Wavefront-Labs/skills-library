# Age-honest death conversations and grief activities for children

**Goal:** children get truthful information they can understand, chances to express and
remember, and adults around them who know what to expect and when to seek more help.

Non-clinical support. Follow child-safeguarding procedures (see
human-services.childcare-and-early-childhood-care.child-safeguarding.recognising-indicators-handling-disclosures-and-reporting)
and refer to child-specialist bereavement or mental-health services when indicated. Work
with parents/guardians' consent and alongside them, not around them.

## 1. Use concrete, honest words
- Say **"died"** and **"dead"**. Explain what it means: "Their body stopped working. They
  can't breathe, eat, or feel pain anymore. They won't come back."
- **Avoid euphemisms:** "gone to sleep" (fear of sleeping), "lost" (why don't we look for
  them?), "went away" (abandonment), "God took them because they were good" (fear of being good).
- Give the cause simply and truthfully at the child's level. Children usually find out
  eventually; discovering a lie later damages trust more than the truth now.
- Families' faith beliefs can be shared *alongside* the concrete facts.
- Suicide: children can be told honestly in age-appropriate terms (e.g. "they died by
  suicide — they killed themselves; their mind was very unwell") — specialist guidance
  exists; involve a specialist service where possible.

## 2. What children typically understand (rough guide)
| Age | Typical understanding | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Under 2–3 | Senses absence and others' distress | Routine, comfort, familiar carers |
| ~3–5 | May think death is reversible; magical thinking ("I caused it") | Repeat the facts; reassure it wasn't their fault |
| ~5–9 | Grasp that death is permanent and universal; curious about details | Answer questions plainly, including about bodies |
| ~9–12 | Fuller understanding; worry about who'll look after them | Honest information, involvement, routine |
| Teens | Adult-like understanding; may grieve with peers, privately, or through risk-taking | Respect privacy, stay available, watch for risk |

Ages vary widely with development and experience (uncertain at the edges).

## 3. How children grieve
Children often **"puddle-jump"**: intensely sad one moment, playing the next. That's normal,
not uncaring. Grief also shows in behaviour: clinginess, regression (bedwetting, baby talk),
anger, sleep problems, stomach aches, school difficulties. It may resurface at later
developmental stages or milestones.

## 4. Grief activities
- **Memory box:** objects, photos, letters, a scent — something the child curates.
- Drawing or writing to the person who died; a feelings jar; a memory jar of written moments.
- Stories and picture books about death (choose ones that use honest language).
- Rituals: lighting a candle on birthdays, visiting a place, planting something.
- Teens: playlists, journals, online memorials, peer groups.

Offer; never force. Let the child lead what they share.

## 5. Funerals and rituals
Offer the choice to attend, **with preparation**: what they'll see (a coffin, people crying),
how long, what they can do (place a drawing, choose a song). Assign a trusted adult to stay
with them and leave if needed. Children excluded from rituals often regret it later.

## 6. Guiding the surrounding adults
- Children watch adults for cues; **adults crying in front of children is OK** — it models grief.
- Keep routines and boundaries; predictability is reassuring.
- Tell the school/childcare (with family permission) so staff can support and prepare for anniversaries.
- Reassure about who will look after them.

## When to refer
Persistent, worsening distress months on; talk of wanting to die or join the person;
self-harm; traumatic circumstances with nightmares/flashbacks; prolonged withdrawal, school
refusal or risky behaviour → child mental-health or specialist bereavement service; urgent
risk → emergency/crisis procedure.

## Verify
- [ ] Child told the truth in concrete words, without euphemism.
- [ ] Explanation pitched to the child's developmental understanding; "not your fault" said.
- [ ] Child offered (not forced) activities and a prepared choice about the funeral.
- [ ] Adults guided on routine, modelling grief and informing school.
- [ ] Referral indicators checked; any risk escalated per procedure.
