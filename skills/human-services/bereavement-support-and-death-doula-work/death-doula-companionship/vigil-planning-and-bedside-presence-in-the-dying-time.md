# Vigil planning and bedside presence in the dying time

**Goal:** the person dies as close to their wishes as circumstances allow, surrounded the way
they wanted, and the people with them feel accompanied rather than alone and frightened.

Death doulas provide **non-medical** support. You do not assess, diagnose, administer or
adjust medication, or give clinical advice. Symptom management belongs to the palliative or
hospice team, GP or nurses — your role includes noticing and prompting the family to call
them. Training standards and regulation of doulas vary by country; work within your
training, any code of practice you subscribe to, and your agreement with the family.

## 1. Plan the vigil early (while the person can say)
A vigil plan records how the person wants their last days and hours to feel:
- **Who** should be there, who should not, and whether they want people present at the
  moment of death (some prefer privacy; some die when loved ones step out).
- **Environment:** light, music or silence, scents, objects, photos, a view of the window,
  pets.
- **Touch:** hand-holding, massage of hands/feet (light, if comfortable), or no touch.
- **Words:** readings, prayers, poems, stories, things they want said or not said.
- **Spiritual/cultural practices** and who leads them (faith leader, family).
- **After death:** how long the body stays before moving, washing/dressing wishes, rituals.
- Copies to family and — with consent — the care team, so it's honoured on any shift.

## 2. Organise the vigil
- A rota so someone is present but family can rest, eat and sleep — exhausted watchers
  can't be present.
- A notebook at the bedside: visits, what was observed, questions for the nurse.
- Contact list: palliative team (day and after-hours), GP, faith leader, funeral director,
  family to call.
- Know what the family should do when death occurs and whom to call (expected-death procedures
  vary by place and setting — confirm with the care team in advance).

## 3. Bedside presence
- Be calm and unhurried; your steadiness sets the tone.
- Speak to the person as if they can hear — **hearing is widely believed to persist late**,
  so encourage family to say what they need to say. Avoid talking *about* them over the bed.
- Simple comfort within non-medical scope (as agreed with the care team): moistening lips,
  adjusting pillows, gentle touch, quiet music, reading.
- Hold silence. Not everything needs words.

## 4. Supporting the family through what they see
Common changes as death nears include: longer sleep, less eating and drinking, confusion
or restlessness, cool or mottled hands and feet, changes in breathing pattern with pauses, and
noisy breathing from secretions. Explain in plain terms that these are commonly part of
dying, while **prompting them to contact the nurse about any distress** — restlessness,
apparent pain, breathlessness, noisy secretions — because the care team can often ease it.

Families may ask "How long?" — be honest that no one knows; the nurse can give the best view.

## 5. Advocacy
If the person's documented wishes aren't being followed (an unwanted transfer, music turned
off, a visitor they refused), raise it calmly with the family and care team, pointing to the
plan. You advocate; you don't override clinical or legal decision-makers.

## 6. The moment of death and after
Stay with the family; there's no rush. Let them sit, touch, talk, pray. Help them follow the
after-death plan and the care team's procedure for an expected death. See after-death-practical-support.

## Gotchas
- **Scope creep** into nursing tasks — even when asked — exposes you and the family to harm.
- Family conflict surfaces at deathbeds; stay neutral and keep returning to the person's wishes.
- People sometimes rally briefly before dying; families may misread it — gently prepare them.
- Your own grief accumulates; debrief with a peer or supervisor after each death.

## Verify
- [ ] Vigil plan recorded with the person (or from their known wishes) and shared with consent.
- [ ] Rota, bedside notebook and contact list in place, including after-hours clinical contact.
- [ ] You stayed within non-medical scope and prompted clinical calls for distress symptoms.
- [ ] Family knew what to expect and what to do at the time of death.
- [ ] You debriefed afterwards.
