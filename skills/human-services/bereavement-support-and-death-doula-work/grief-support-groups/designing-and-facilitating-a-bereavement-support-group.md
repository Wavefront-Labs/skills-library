# Designing and facilitating a bereavement support group

**Goal:** a group where bereaved people feel less alone, can speak and listen safely, and
leave each session no worse than they arrived.

Non-clinical peer/support-group practice, not group therapy. Facilitators should be trained,
supervised, and backed by a referral pathway to clinical services.

## 1. Design choices
- **Loss-specific groups** work better than mixed ones when numbers allow: partner loss,
  death of a child, suicide loss, pregnancy/infant loss, sudden/traumatic death. People feel
  understood faster and fewer comparisons arise.
- **Closed** (same members, fixed series, e.g. 6–10 sessions): deeper trust, clear ending.
  **Open/drop-in** (ongoing, anyone can come): accessible, but trust resets each time.
- Size: roughly 6–12; small enough for everyone to speak.
- **Two co-facilitators** where possible — one leads, one watches the room and can step out
  with a distressed member.
- Timing since loss: many services suggest waiting some weeks to months after the death
  before group joining (practice varies — uncertain; follow your service).

## 2. Screening (a short pre-group conversation)
- Explain what the group is and isn't.
- Check readiness to hear others' stories, not just tell their own.
- Screen for acute suicide risk, active crisis or trauma symptoms that a group might worsen —
  these people need individual or clinical support first (or alongside).
- Ask about practical needs (access, interpreter, timing).

## 3. Group agreements (set in session one, revisited)
- Confidentiality: what's said here stays here (state its limits: risk to life).
- One person speaks at a time; it's OK to pass.
- No advice-giving unless asked; speak from your own experience ("I" statements).
- No comparing whose loss is worse.
- Phones off; start and end on time.

## 4. Session structure (example, 90 min)
1. **Opening** (10 min): welcome, agreements reminder, a grounding moment or candle lighting.
2. **Check-in round** (20–30 min): name, who they're grieving, how this week has been.
3. **Theme** (30–40 min): a topic (sleep and the body, anniversaries, guilt, telling the
   story, changed identity, continuing bonds) with discussion or a gentle activity (writing,
   photo sharing).
4. **Closing round** (10–15 min): one word or one thing they're taking away; brief closing ritual.

End on time and **stay afterwards** for anyone who needs a quiet word.

## 5. Holding shared sorrow safely
- Pace: someone telling a traumatic death in vivid detail can flood others. Gently
  acknowledge and invite them to share "what's most important for the group to know".
- Balance airtime: "Let's hear from someone we haven't heard from yet."
- If someone becomes very distressed, co-facilitator offers to step out with them; check on
  them before they leave.
- Follow up any disclosure of suicidal thinking individually and per procedure, the same day.
- Suicide-loss groups: expect guilt, "why" questions and stigma; avoid detailed method
  discussion; know postvention resources.

## 6. Closing the series
Signal the ending from midway. Final session: reflect on change, a closing ritual (naming the
people who died, a shared reading, planting), information about ongoing supports and alumni
or open groups. Endings can stir the original loss; acknowledge that.

## Gotchas
- One dominant member can make the group unsafe for others — address it privately early.
- Mixing very recent and long-ago losses can discourage both.
- Facilitators' own grief surfaces; debrief after every session.

## Verify
- [ ] Group type and loss focus chosen deliberately.
- [ ] Every member screened; those at acute risk directed to individual help.
- [ ] Agreements set and revisited, with confidentiality limits stated.
- [ ] Sessions open and close with structure; everyone had the chance to speak.
- [ ] Distress and risk followed up the same day; facilitators debriefed.
