# Advance-wishes conversations, legacy projects and getting affairs in order

**Goal:** the person's values and wishes are known to the people who'll need them, their
legacy is shaped the way they want, and the practical loose ends are listed and on their way
to being handled by the right professionals.

Scope: you **facilitate and organise; you don't give legal, financial or medical advice or
draft legal instruments.** Wills, powers of attorney and formal advance directives must follow
jurisdiction-specific law — refer to a lawyer, the official advance-care-planning form for the
area, and the person's doctor. Check capacity concerns with the care team.

## 1. Advance-wishes conversations: values first
Start with what matters, not forms:
- "What does a good day look like for you now?"
- "What worries you most about the future?"
- "What would be unacceptable to you — what would make life not worth living?"
- "If you couldn't speak for yourself, who should speak for you? Do they know what you'd want?"
- "Where would you prefer to be cared for, and to die, if you had a choice?"

Then: encourage them to discuss specific medical choices (resuscitation, ventilation,
artificial feeding, hospital transfer) **with their doctor**, who can explain what each
means for them, and to complete the jurisdiction's formal documents. Help them prepare
questions for that appointment.

Make sure the chosen decision-maker is in the conversation; wishes known only to you are useless.

## 2. Legacy projects
Offer, don't push. Options:
- **Letters** for loved ones, including for future milestones (birthdays, graduations, weddings).
- **Ethical will:** values, life lessons, hopes for others.
- Audio or video recordings telling stories; recipe collections; memory books; annotated photos.
- Handing on objects with their stories attached.

Energy fades — start with the most important item, keep sessions short, and record rather
than write if fatigue is high. Agree who will keep the material and when it's to be given.

## 3. Funeral and after-death preferences
Burial/cremation/other options legal locally, body or organ donation (needs advance
registration in many places), religious or secular ceremony, who leads, music, readings,
what to wear, where ashes go, budget, any prepaid plan. Record them where the family can find
them — **wills are often read after the funeral**, so funeral wishes put only in a will may be missed.

## 4. Affairs-in-order checklist (organise, then refer)
- Will current? (refer to a lawyer/legal service to make or update)
- Financial and healthcare decision-maker documents in place? (formal process per jurisdiction)
- Where key documents are kept: ID, will, insurance, pension, property, bank details
- List of accounts, bills and subscriptions; who to notify
- **Digital legacy:** devices, password manager access via legitimate means, social-media
  memorialisation preferences — never collect passwords yourself
- Dependants and pets: who will care for them
- Any messages to deliver, or people to reconcile with

## Gotchas
- People often want to talk about death more than their families allow. You may need to
  facilitate a family conversation, gently.
- Don't impose a "good death" ideal; some people choose not to plan, and that's their right.
- Capacity can change; prioritise the decision-maker conversation early.
- Cultural and faith contexts affect who decides and how death is spoken of — ask.

## Verify
- [ ] Values and preferences recorded in the person's words.
- [ ] Decision-maker named and has heard the wishes directly.
- [ ] Medical choices referred to the doctor; legal documents referred to the proper process.
- [ ] Legacy items prioritised, with custody and delivery agreed.
- [ ] Funeral wishes stored somewhere the family will see before the funeral.
- [ ] Affairs checklist completed and referrals made.
