# Guiding a family through the first days after a death

**Goal:** the family knows what has to happen, what can wait, and what they get to choose —
so decisions made in shock are ones they won't regret.

General educational reference. **Legal steps, deadlines, terminology and who certifies death
vary by country and state** — confirm with the doctor, funeral director or registry office.
You don't give legal or estate advice; refer to the funeral director, registry, a lawyer or
the executor.

## 1. Immediately after the death
- **Expected death at home:** there is usually no emergency. Follow the plan agreed with the
  palliative team — typically call the hospice/GP/nurse line to arrange verification of death.
  The family can take time with the person before anyone is called to move them.
- **Unexpected death at home:** call emergency services; don't move or wash the body; police
  and possibly the coroner/medical examiner will be involved. Families need gentle explanation
  that this is routine, not suspicion.
- **In hospital or a care home:** staff guide the immediate steps; ask about time with the
  body, and belongings.

## 2. What has to happen (typical order)
1. **Verification/pronouncement of death** by an authorised clinician.
2. **Medical certificate of cause of death** from a doctor — or referral to the
   coroner/medical examiner if the death was sudden, unexplained, violent, or the doctor can't
   certify. Coroner referral can delay registration and the funeral; explain the family has
   no control over the timing.
3. **Registration** of the death with the registry within the legal deadline (often days to
   weeks), producing the death certificate. Order several certified copies — banks,
   insurers and pension providers each want one.
4. **Funeral arrangements** (can usually start before registration, but the burial/cremation
   needs the paperwork).

## 3. Funeral choices — slow it down
In shock, families may sign the first package offered. Help them:
- Check the person's recorded wishes (see end-of-life-planning-support) and any prepaid plan.
- Know they can compare funeral directors, ask for itemised prices, and choose simpler options.
- Know home funerals, family-led ceremonies, or direct cremation may be legal options (check locally).
- Decide who will pay; estate funds may be released for funerals in some systems; ask about
  government help for people on low incomes.
- Separate decisions that are urgent (who collects the body) from ones that can wait a day.

## 4. Notifications (a shared checklist)
- Close family and friends (delegate a "phone tree")
- Employer, school, faith community
- Doctor/hospital appointments to cancel; equipment and medication returns
- Government agencies (benefits, pensions, tax, passport, licence) — some countries offer
  a single notification service
- Banks, insurers, utilities, landlord/mortgage, subscriptions, social media
- **Executor or next of kin** handles the estate; probate/estate administration needs legal advice.

## 5. Decision support in shock
- Shock affects memory: **write everything down** — names, numbers, next steps — and give
  the family the list.
- One task at a time; offer to make calls with them on speaker.
- Check they are eating, sleeping, and have someone with them.
- Watch for vulnerable people (a frail partner, children, someone with a disability) who may
  now lack a carer — this can be urgent.
- Look out for scams targeting the recently bereaved.

## Gotchas
- Don't promise timelines that depend on the coroner or registry.
- Bills in the deceased's sole name usually shouldn't be paid from survivors' own money
  without advice.
- Family disagreement over arrangements: the legally responsible person decides — help them
  find out who that is.
- Terms and steps above are generalised across jurisdictions (uncertain for any specific one).

## Verify
- [ ] Family knows whether the death is expected or reportable and who to call.
- [ ] Certification, registration and certified-copy steps listed with local deadlines.
- [ ] Funeral decisions slowed down; prices and wishes checked.
- [ ] Notification checklist shared and divided among family.
- [ ] Written next-steps list left with the family; vulnerable survivors' care covered.
