# Settlement orientation, interpreters and cultural bridging

**Goal:** help newcomers become able to run their own lives in a new country — housing,
income, health, school, work, language, belonging — as fast as they want to, without
the worker becoming a permanent go-between. General professional reference; eligibility
for services depends heavily on visa/immigration status, which varies by country and changes often.

## 1. A key boundary: immigration advice
In many countries, giving immigration or asylum *advice* is a regulated activity (e.g.
requires accreditation). Unless you're accredited, don't advise on visa or asylum
cases — refer to a qualified adviser or legal service, and help the person keep appointments
and documents organised. Check the rules in your jurisdiction.

## 2. Settlement assessment and plan
Cover, in the person's priority order: safety and immediate needs, housing, income
and benefits entitlement, health registration (GP, dental, immunisations), children's
school enrolment, English/local-language classes, recognition of qualifications, employment,
legal/document needs, family reunion questions (refer), social connection. Set goals that
build independence: "I can book a GP appointment myself."

## 3. Orientation to systems
- Teach how things work, not only what to do: why the GP is the first stop, how renting
  and deposits work, what a bill looks like, how buses are paid, emergency numbers,
  rights at work, how to spot scams.
- **Do it with them, then let them do it:** first call together, next call they lead.
- Use translated materials and visual guides; check understanding by asking them to explain back.

## 4. Working with interpreters
- **Use professional interpreters** — not children, family or friends, especially for
  health, legal, safeguarding or sensitive topics.
- Check language *and dialect*; consider the interpreter's gender and community ties
  (a small community may mean they know each other — offer telephone interpreting from elsewhere).
- **Brief** the interpreter beforehand (purpose, sensitive content); **debrief** after.
- Speak directly to the client in the first person, short sentences, pause for interpretation.
- Allow double time. The interpreter interprets everything; ask them not to add or omit.

## 5. Cultural bridging
- **Cultural humility over cultural "knowledge":** ask the person what matters to them;
  don't assume based on nationality or religion. Communities are diverse internally.
- Explain local norms without judgement (e.g. expectations of school contact, punctuality,
  child discipline law) — framed as information that protects them.
- Connect to community associations and faith or diaspora groups *if they want that*;
  some people avoid their community for good reasons.

## 6. Trauma and wellbeing
Many refugees have experienced torture, war, loss and dangerous journeys; post-arrival
stress (uncertainty, poverty, isolation) adds to it. Don't press for trauma stories
unless needed and with consent. Watch for signs that warrant a mental-health referral
(specialist torture/trauma services exist in many countries).

## Gotchas
- Doing everything for clients and creating dependency.
- Asking about status in ways that feel like interrogation; explain why you ask.
- Assuming literacy in the first language.
- Over-promising timelines for housing or family reunion.
- Child protection, family violence or exploitation concerns follow standard safeguarding
  procedure; involve your supervisor and specialist services.

## Verify
- [ ] Immigration-advice boundary respected; legal referrals made.
- [ ] Settlement plan in the person's priority order with independence goals.
- [ ] Professional interpreter used, briefed, and the right language/dialect.
- [ ] Orientation checked by explain-back; client did the second task themselves.
- [ ] Wellbeing considered and specialist referral offered where indicated.
