# Request-offer matching and distribution logistics

**Goal:** neighbours helping neighbours reliably — requests get met, offers get used,
nobody is put at risk and the coordinators are still standing in month six. General
reference; informal groups still have legal duties around personal data, food safety
and money, which differ by country.

## 1. Principles worth deciding early
- **Solidarity, not charity:** people who ask for help are members, not cases. Minimise
  means-testing and proof requirements.
- Decide your scope (shopping, prescriptions, food, phone check-ins, lifts?) and what you
  won't do (personal care, money handling, medical advice) — write it down.
- Decision-making: who decides what, and how (a weekly open meeting, a small rotating
  coordinating group).

## 2. Intake
- One or two **simple channels**: a phone line or number with set hours, plus an online form.
  Many people asking for help aren't online.
- **Minimum data:** first name, contact, area, what's needed, by when, any access needs.
  Don't collect what you won't use.
- Offers form: availability, area, what they can do, transport, any checks held.
- Acknowledge every request within a set time, even if just "we're working on it".

## 3. Matching and dispatch
- A shared tracker (spreadsheet or simple tool) with **status columns**: new → matched →
  in progress → done / couldn't help. Assign a dispatcher per shift.
- Match by area, timing and fit. Avoid sending the same volunteer to many vulnerable
  people without the group knowing who they are.
- **Close the loop:** volunteer reports done; dispatcher confirms with the requester
  occasionally; mark unmet requests and refer on.
- Recurring needs (weekly shop) → consistent pairing, with a backup.

## 4. Distribution logistics
- **Food:** follow basic food-safety rules (cold chain for chilled items, use-by dates,
  allergen info, clean storage); local regulators often publish guidance for community food
  projects.
- Hub layout: intake, sorting, packing, collection. A one-way flow reduces chaos.
- Deliveries: doorstep drop with a call/knock, no entering homes unless agreed and safe.
- Track stock in/out roughly so you know what to ask donors for.

## 5. Safety basics
- **Money:** avoid handling cash for others; if unavoidable, receipts and two-person checks.
- Simple safeguarding: volunteers know how to raise a concern (someone in danger, signs of
  abuse, someone very unwell) and who to call; emergencies → emergency services.
- Share personal data only on need-to-know; delete it when no longer needed; secure
  shared documents (no public spreadsheets of addresses).

## 6. Keeping the network running
- Rotate coordinator roles, cap shift lengths, and name the risk of a single point of failure.
- Regular check-ins for volunteers; celebrate wins; let people step back without guilt.
- Link with local councils, food banks and services for referrals and surplus food —
  without losing independence.

## Gotchas
- A public sign-up sheet exposing vulnerable people's addresses.
- Requests lost in group chats — always move them to the tracker.
- Founders doing everything until they collapse.
- Mission creep into tasks needing training (personal care, debt advice).
- Concerns about a vulnerable person's safety: escalate to services; the group can't hold it alone.

## Verify
- [ ] Scope and limits written down and shared with volunteers.
- [ ] Phone and online intake; every request acknowledged and tracked to a status.
- [ ] Personal data minimal, access-restricted, deleted when done.
- [ ] Food-safety and money rules written and followed.
- [ ] At least two people can run every coordination role.
