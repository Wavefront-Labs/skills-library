# Preparing and speaking for someone at meetings and hearings

**Goal:** make sure the person's voice is heard — preferably in their own words — in
rooms where professionals decide about their life: care reviews, case conferences,
school exclusion meetings, housing panels, benefit appeal hearings and other
**non-court** tribunals. You keep them in control of what's said. For formal court
representation, they need a lawyer; some tribunals allow lay representatives and
others only a supporter — *check the specific body's rules before you go.*

## 1. Before the meeting
- **Find out:** purpose, who's attending and why, the decision being made, the agenda,
  papers (ask for them in advance; you're entitled to them in many processes), length,
  venue access.
- **Ask for adjustments** the person needs: a quiet room, breaks, an interpreter,
  easy-read papers, attending by video, fewer people in the room, a later start.
- **Prepare with the person (key step, often skipped):**
  1. Go through the papers together. Mark anything wrong or missing.
  2. Agree what they want out of the meeting — their top two or three points.
  3. Write them down in their words; they can read them, or you can.
  4. Agree roles: what they'll say, what you'll say, what you'll only raise if they want.
  5. Agree **signals:** to pause, to take a break, "say this for me", "I'm finished".
  6. Practise likely hard questions.
- **Prepare evidence** for hearings: a short written statement or submission, key
  documents indexed and paginated, the rule or policy you rely on.

## 2. In the room
- Arrive early; sit next to the person, not with the professionals.
- At the start, ask the chair to introduce everyone and their roles, and explain yours:
  "I'm an independent advocate. I'm here to support X to give their views and to
  speak on their behalf where they ask me to."
- Make sure the person is addressed directly, not talked about.
- Let them speak first if they want to; don't jump in to fill pauses.
- When speaking on instruction, flag it: "X has asked me to say…". Never add your own
  opinion as theirs. If you have an independent concern (e.g. a procedural failure), say
  that it's yours.
- **Check understanding** at decision points — ask for jargon to be explained, ask for a
  break to talk privately if something unexpected comes up.
- Before the end, summarise what's been decided, who will do what, by when; ask for
  their objection to be recorded if they disagree.

## 3. Hearings (benefits, school admissions/exclusions, housing panels)
- Know the test the panel applies; organise your points around it.
- A short opening: what the person is asking for and the two or three reasons.
- Let the person give evidence; prepare them that questions can feel intrusive
  (e.g. detailed questions about daily living in disability benefit hearings).
- Close by linking evidence to the test. Ask when and how the decision will arrive.

## 4. After
- Debrief with the person: how did it feel, what happened, what's next.
- **Check the minutes/decision** against your notes. Request corrections in writing
  promptly — minutes become the record later relied on.
- Diarise actions promised and review dates; chase if missed. Note appeal rights and deadlines.

## Gotchas
- Over-speaking for someone who can speak for themselves, because it's quicker.
- Being drawn into side conversations with professionals before or after.
- Surprises in the room — new reports, unannounced attendees. Ask to adjourn if the
  person hasn't had a fair chance to see new material.
- People agreeing in the moment to please the room. Check privately before final agreement.

## Verify
- [ ] Papers read with the person in advance; their points written in their words.
- [ ] Adjustments requested; roles and signals agreed.
- [ ] Every statement on their behalf flagged as on instruction.
- [ ] Decisions, actions and any disagreement recorded at the end.
- [ ] Minutes checked and corrected; appeal deadlines noted.
