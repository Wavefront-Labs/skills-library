# Funding-scheme advocacy and challenging restrictive practice

**Goal:** help a disabled person get the support, access and respect they're entitled
to — and challenge services that exclude, over-restrict or decide for them. Builds on
`human-services.advocacy-for-others.individual-advocacy-casework.taking-instructions-and-pursuing-an-advocacy-case`.
*Schemes and laws named here are examples (Australia's NDIS, England's Care Act, US
ADA/Medicaid waivers); rules, names and deadlines change — always check the current
official guidance for the person's jurisdiction.*

## 1. Frame it as rights
- **Social model:** the barrier is the inaccessible environment or service, not the
  impairment. Frame asks as removing barriers ("the stairs exclude her") rather than
  special favours.
- **CRPD** (ratified by most countries) sets principles: autonomy, inclusion,
  equal recognition before the law, independent living, freedom from restraint and
  exploitation. Useful language even where not directly enforceable.
- **Anti-discrimination law** usually requires *reasonable adjustments/accommodations*
  in work, education, services and housing.

## 2. Funding-scheme plans and assessments
1. **Prepare before the assessment/planning meeting.** Help the person describe a
   typical *bad* day, not their best day — people under-report out of pride or habit.
2. **Map needs to goals:** what support is needed to do what (personal care, getting to
   work, community participation, respite for carers).
3. **Evidence:** reports from therapists or doctors that state functional impact and
   recommend specific supports and amounts. Ask professionals to use the scheme's own
   criteria language (e.g. "reasonable and necessary" for the NDIS).
4. **Check the draft plan/decision** against what was asked; note gaps.
5. **Challenge:** internal review within the time limit (short in many schemes), then
   external appeal/tribunal. Lead with the eligibility criteria and the evidence.

## 3. Reasonable adjustments
Ask in writing: the barrier, the adjustment requested, how it removes the barrier. Offer
alternatives. If refused, ask for reasons in writing; cost or practicality defences have
limits that vary by law. Escalate via complaint, then a human-rights/equality body.

## 4. Supported decision-making
The person makes the decision with support, not someone else for them.
- Give information in accessible form (easy read, pictures, time, trusted people).
- Explore options and consequences in their terms.
- Record what they decided and how they were supported.
- Challenge "lacks capacity" assumptions: capacity is decision-specific, time-specific,
  and must be assessed with all practicable support first. Unwise ≠ incapable.
- Substitute decision-makers (guardians, deputies) should still seek and weigh the person's will and preferences.

## 5. Challenging restrictive practices
Restraint (physical, mechanical, chemical — e.g. sedation used for behaviour rather than
a diagnosed condition), seclusion, environmental restrictions (locked kitchens), and
denial of rights. Ask:
- Is there a **behaviour support plan**, authorised as local rules require, with a
  plan to reduce restriction?
- Is it the **least restrictive** option, used as a last resort, for the shortest time?
- Is it **reported and reviewed** as required?
- What is the behaviour communicating (pain, boredom, distress, unmet need)?
Report unauthorised restraint as a safeguarding matter and to the relevant regulator.

## 6. Exclusion
School exclusions, being moved into institutional settings, segregated work, inaccessible
transport: challenge through education processes, community-living rights, discrimination complaints.

## Gotchas
- Talking to carers or staff instead of the person; use their communication method.
- Accepting "the system won't fund that" without asking for the written decision and criteria.
- Missing short review windows.
- Restrictive practice normalised as "how we manage him" — name it as restraint.

## Verify
- [ ] Asks framed as barrier-removal with rights language.
- [ ] Assessment prep captured a typical bad day; evidence uses the scheme's criteria.
- [ ] Review/appeal deadlines diarised.
- [ ] Decisions made via supported decision-making and recorded.
- [ ] Any restrictive practice checked for authorisation, least-restriction and reporting.
