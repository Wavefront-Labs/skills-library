# Taking instructions and pursuing an advocacy case

**Goal:** help a person get what *they* want from a service or institution — a
benefit decision, a care package, a repair, a school place — by representing their
view faithfully and pursuing it until there's an outcome. The advocate is on the
person's side, independent of the service, and does not decide what's best for them.
Advocacy is not legal advice; refer to a lawyer when legal rights need asserting in
court or a statutory deadline needs legal action.

## 1. Principles that shape every step
- **Instructed:** you act on what the person tells you, even if you'd choose differently.
- **Independence:** declare any link to the service you're challenging; if you work for
  it, you aren't independent.
- **Empowerment:** do *with*, not *for*, where they're able; build their own self-advocacy
  (see `human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy` skills).
- **Confidentiality** with stated limits (safeguarding, serious risk).
- **Non-instructed advocacy** applies when someone lacks capacity to instruct (e.g. advanced
  dementia): you then use recognised approaches — observing, asking people who know
  them, checking rights are respected — and say clearly you're non-instructed. *Statutory
  forms of this vary by jurisdiction.*

## 2. First meeting: taking instructions
1. Explain your role: what you do, what you don't (decide for them, give legal advice),
   independence, confidentiality and its limits, how to complain about you.
2. Hear the story. Then pin down: **"What do you want to happen?"** Separate the issue
   ("my care hours were cut") from the outcome ("hours restored to 20 a week, or a proper
   reassessment").
3. Check priorities if there are several issues; take one or two at a time.
4. Agree **consent** in writing: what you can share, with whom, and authority to request
   records on their behalf.
5. Agree how and when you'll update them, and accessible formats they need.
6. Read back your notes of their instructions. Their words, not yours.

## 3. Gathering evidence
- The decision letter, assessments, care plans, correspondence.
- **Request records** when needed (in many jurisdictions a subject access / freedom-of-
  information-style request; often a statutory response time). Keep copies of everything.
- Know the **rules** the service must follow: its policy, eligibility criteria, relevant
  law, timeframes for review. Decisions that ignore their own policy are the strongest to challenge.
- Note deadlines immediately — appeal windows can be short.

## 4. Representing the person
- Start with the lowest effective step: a call or letter to the decision-maker
  asking for an explanation or reconsideration.
- Letters: who you are and that you act with consent; the decision; what the person
  wants; why (facts, policy, impact on them); what you're asking for and by when.
- In meetings, the person speaks if they want to; you back them up, clarify, keep it on
  track (see `human-services.advocacy-for-others.meeting-and-tribunal-representation.preparing-and-speaking-for-someone-at-meetings-and-hearings`).
- Check back after every contact: "They've offered X — what do you want to do?"
- Escalate through complaints and appeals if needed
  (`human-services.advocacy-for-others.complaints-and-appeals-advocacy.drafting-complaints-and-escalating-to-review-and-ombudsman`).

## 5. Case notes
Dated, factual, what was done and agreed, what the person instructed. Assume the person
(and possibly a tribunal) will read them. Separate fact from your opinion.

## 6. Closing
Close when the issue is resolved, the person asks, or every route is exhausted.
Write to confirm the outcome, what they can do if it recurs, and how to come back.
Ask for feedback.

## Gotchas
- Substituting your view: "I know you said X, but I think Y is better for you" — stop.
- Families and staff often want to instruct you; politely, you act for the person.
- Taking on everything at once dilutes progress and burns out advocates.
- Missing an appeal deadline while waiting for records — lodge a holding appeal if allowed.

## Verify
- [ ] Role, independence, confidentiality limits explained; written consent obtained.
- [ ] The person's desired outcome recorded in their own words and read back.
- [ ] Relevant policy/law and all deadlines identified.
- [ ] Every offer or development checked back with the person before responding.
- [ ] Case closed in writing with outcome and how to return.
