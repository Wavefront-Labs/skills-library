# Bedside presence in the last weeks

**Goal:** help someone die comfortably and not alone, in the way they would want, and
support the people around them. This is the **non-clinical** role. Symptom management
(pain, breathlessness, agitation, medication) belongs to the palliative or nursing
team; your job is presence, comfort within your training, and noticing.

## 1. Before you sit with someone
- Read the care plan and **advance care plan** if there is one: wishes about place of
  death, who should be present, religious or cultural practices, resuscitation status
  (so you know what *not* to do and who to call).
- Ask the nurse what to expect today and what to call them for.
- Find out the person's name preference, faith, music, what they loved.

## 2. Being there
- **Presence is the intervention.** Sit where they can see you if their eyes open; say
  your name; speak normally. Hearing is thought to persist late, so talk *to* them,
  not over them, and nothing at the bedside you wouldn't want them to hear.
- Gentle touch (hand-holding) if they or family welcome it.
- Silence is fine. Reading aloud, prayer (if wanted), or familiar music at low volume.
- Keep the room calm: soft light, limited visitors at once if they tire, phone off.

## 3. Small comforts (within your role and the plan)
- **Mouth care:** moist swabs and lip balm as directed — dry mouth is very common and
  uncomfortable. Don't put fluids into the mouth of someone who can't swallow.
- Repositioning, pillows, warm or cool cloths, a light blanket — as the plan allows.
- Keep them clean and dry; preserve dignity in front of visitors.
- Don't offer food or drink unless the plan says it's safe; reduced appetite near
  the end is normal and families often need reassurance on this.

## 4. Changes to report to the nurse
Signs of distress (grimacing, moaning, restlessness), noisy breathing, breathing that
changes pattern, skin colour changes, fever, apparent pain, new agitation. Families
often find changing breathing frightening; the nurse can explain and assess. Report;
don't reassure that "it's nothing" yourself.

## 5. Supporting family in the room
- Introduce yourself and your role; ask what would help.
- Offer practical things: tea, a blanket, a chair that reclines, where the toilet is,
  how to call the nurse.
- Encourage them to talk to and touch their relative, and to take breaks — offer to
  sit while they rest so the person isn't alone.
- Don't fill silences with platitudes ("they're in a better place"). "This is so hard"
  is usually more honest.
- Questions about prognosis or medication → the nurse or doctor.
- Differences between family members: stay neutral; involve the team.

## 6. At and after the death
Note the time, stay calm, call the nurse — death is confirmed by authorised staff
according to local law. Follow cultural/religious wishes about the body and the room
if known. Give family time. Then follow your service's procedure.

## 7. Afterwards: you
Grief in care workers is normal and cumulative. Debrief with your supervisor, use
employee support programmes, attend memorials if your service does. Persistent
intrusive memories or numbness are worth raising.

## Gotchas
- Moving from "doing" to "being" is hard for task-focused workers — resist busyness.
- Don't impose your own beliefs about death.
- "No one dies alone" vigil volunteers need a clear handover and a call-for-help route.

## Verify
- [ ] Advance care plan, cultural wishes and escalation contacts known.
- [ ] Comfort measures given only within your training and plan.
- [ ] Distress and changes reported to the nurse promptly.
- [ ] Family offered practical support and respite at the bedside.
- [ ] Debrief taken after a death.
