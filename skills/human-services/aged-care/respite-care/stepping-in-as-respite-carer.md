# Stepping in as a respite carer

**Goal:** give the regular carer (usually a spouse or adult child) a genuine break by
providing care that feels familiar to the older person, then hand back with no
surprises. Respite fails when the carer can't relax because they don't trust it, or
comes home to a disrupted, distressed person.

## 1. Rapid familiarisation (the most important 30 minutes)
Ideally a meet-and-greet happens **before** the first session with both carer and
person present. Capture, in their words:
- **The day's shape:** wake, meals, naps, TV programmes, bedtime ritual.
- **How they like things done:** tea strength, which chair, shower vs wash, the
  phrase that settles them.
- **Health and safety:** mobility and transfer method, continence routine, diet and
  swallowing needs, allergies, falls history, anything the person does that worries
  the carer (wandering at night, turning the stove on).
- **Medication:** who gives it while the carer is away and under what authority —
  only administer if trained and authorised by your service; otherwise confirm the
  arrangement explicitly.
- **Contacts:** carer's number, GP, a backup family member, what counts as "call me".
- **Triggers and comforts:** topics, people, music, objects.
Use the service's respite intake form if it has one; a single page on the fridge works
too. Read any existing care plan.

## 2. Keeping routines intact
Do it their way, even if it's not how you'd do it. Changes in routine are a major
source of distress, especially with dementia (see
`human-services.aged-care.dementia-support.responding-to-confusion-and-distress-in-dementia`).
Introduce yourself each time; explain where the carer is in a reassuring, consistent
way ("Jean's at the shops, she'll be back after lunch").

## 3. Centre-based or residential respite
- Bring the familiar into the unfamiliar: own pillow, photos, clothes labelled.
- Expect a settling period; the first night is often the hardest.
- Share the "about me" profile with every shift, not just the first.

## 4. When something goes wrong
- Follow your service's procedures for falls, illness and emergencies as in any
  care role (see
  `human-services.aged-care.mobility-and-falls-support.safe-transfers-and-falls-prevention`).
- Agree beforehand which events trigger a call to the carer during their break —
  calling for every small thing defeats the purpose; not calling about a fall is a
  breach of trust.
- The person asking repeatedly for the carer: reassure, distract, don't lie about
  the carer having left forever.

## 5. Handing back
Give a short, factual account: what they ate and drank, toileting, sleep, mood,
activities, anything unusual and what you did. Write it down too. Highlight positives
— carers are often anxious that respite will unsettle things.

## 6. Notice the carer
Carer strain is common: exhaustion, irritability, tears, "I can't do this
anymore", health neglect. Respite workers often see it first. You are not the carer's
counsellor, but report concerns to your coordinator so carer-support services can be
offered. If you see signs the older person may be at risk because the carer is not
coping, follow safeguarding procedures (see
`human-services.aged-care.elder-safeguarding.recognising-and-escalating-elder-abuse`).

## Gotchas
- Short-notice emergency respite often comes with no familiarisation; prioritise
  safety items (mobility, swallowing, medication, contacts) and ask the person
  directly.
- Don't rearrange the house, throw things out or "improve" routines.
- Confidentiality applies to what you see in the family home.

## Verify
- [ ] Routine, safety, medication and contact details captured before the carer left.
- [ ] Call-the-carer triggers agreed.
- [ ] Routine kept as described; changes only for safety.
- [ ] Written and verbal handback given.
- [ ] Signs of carer strain or risk reported to the coordinator.
