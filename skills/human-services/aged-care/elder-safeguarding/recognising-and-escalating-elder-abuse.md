# Recognising and escalating elder abuse

**Goal:** notice possible abuse or neglect, respond safely to the person, and get the
concern to the people who can act, without investigating it yourself. Legal duties
(mandatory reporting, serious-incident schemes, adult-protection agencies) **vary by
country and state** and by setting; learn your organisation's safeguarding policy and
who your safeguarding lead is. This is general training reference only.

## 1. Forms and indicators
No single sign proves abuse; patterns and unexplained changes matter.
- **Physical:** unexplained bruises (especially upper arms, inner thighs, face, in
  different healing stages), burns, fractures, explanations that don't fit the injury,
  delays in seeking care.
- **Psychological/emotional:** fearfulness around a particular person, withdrawal,
  being belittled, threatened or isolated from visitors.
- **Financial:** unpaid bills despite adequate income, missing possessions or cash,
  sudden changes to wills or bank accounts, new "best friend", a relative controlling
  all money while the person goes without, misuse of power of attorney.
- **Sexual:** bruising around genitals or breasts, unexplained infections, distress
  during intimate care, a disclosure.
- **Neglect (by others):** poor hygiene, weight loss, untreated wounds, lack of aids,
  glasses or dentures, left alone in an unsafe state.
- **Self-neglect:** hoarding, refusing essential care, squalor — usually handled
  through the same safeguarding route, with extra weight on the person's choices.
- **Organisational/institutional:** rigid routines, rough handling, chronic
  understaffing, residents unable to complain.
Abusers can be family, other residents, visitors, or staff (including colleagues).

## 2. If someone tells you
1. Stay calm; listen; let them talk in their own words.
2. Don't ask leading questions ("Did your son hit you?") — use "Tell me what
   happened" if you need to clarify.
3. Don't promise secrecy. Say you need to tell someone whose job is to help.
4. Reassure: "Thank you for telling me. You did the right thing."
5. Write down **their exact words**, date, time, what you saw, as soon as possible.

## 3. Act
- **Immediate danger:** call emergency services, then your manager.
- Otherwise report **the same shift** to your supervisor / safeguarding lead using the
  organisation's procedure. If the concern is about your manager, go above them or to
  the external regulator/adult-protection body.
- **Preserve evidence:** don't wash, clean up, or move items if there's a possible
  assault, unless needed for the person's health.
- Don't confront the alleged abuser or investigate — it can increase risk and
  compromise any later process.
- Mandatory reporting timelines can be short (sometimes within 24 hours for serious
  incidents in residential care — **verify for your jurisdiction**).

## 4. Autonomy and capacity
Adults with decision-making capacity can make choices others disagree with, including
staying in a risky relationship. Safeguarding is about **making safety personal**:
what does the person want to happen? Still report — the team, not you alone, weighs
consent, capacity, risk to others, and whether a crime has occurred. Where capacity
is in doubt, a formal assessment is a professional task, not a care-worker judgement.

## 5. Record
Facts, not conclusions: "Bruise approx. 3 cm on left upper arm, purple; Mrs X said 'he
grabbed me'", not "she's being abused". Include who you reported to and when.

## Gotchas
- Assuming bruises are "just old age" or blood thinners without reporting.
- Telling a colleague instead of using the formal route — informal chats are not reports.
- Fear of being wrong: you report a **concern**, not an accusation. Most jurisdictions
  protect good-faith whistleblowers.

## Verify
- [ ] You know your safeguarding lead and reporting route, internal and external.
- [ ] Disclosure handled without leading questions or promises of secrecy.
- [ ] Concern reported the same shift (immediately if danger) and recorded factually.
- [ ] Evidence preserved; no self-investigation or confrontation.
- [ ] The person's own wishes recorded.
