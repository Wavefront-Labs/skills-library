# Working a residential care shift and handover

**Goal:** get every resident in your group the care they need this shift, in an order
that is safe rather than merely fast, and pass on what the next shift must know.
Routines differ hugely between homes; this is the transferable skeleton. Clinical
decisions belong to the registered nurse (RN) or equivalent.

## 1. Taking handover
Arrive early enough to hear it all. For each resident in your group, capture on your
handover sheet (which stays in the building and is destroyed securely):
- **changes** since last shift — new falls, illness, behaviour, family concerns;
- tasks due (weights, fluid charts, repositioning schedule, appointments);
- risks: falls risk, pressure areas, swallowing, behaviours, infection precautions;
- anything **not done** last shift and why.
Ask if something is vague ("unsettled" — how? when?). Structured formats like
**ISBAR** (Identify, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) help.

## 2. Plan the shift
Before starting, sort your group:
1. **Time-critical:** residents with diabetes needing breakfast at a set time,
   medication-dependent routines, appointments, people at high falls risk who wake
   early.
2. **Two-person assists:** coordinate with a colleague now, not mid-shower.
3. **Preferences:** early risers vs late sleepers. Waking everyone by 7 am for the
   roster's convenience is task-centred care.
Tell the team lead what you can't fit in **early**, not at the end of shift.

## 3. During the shift
- **Call bells:** answer promptly even if busy — acknowledge, check safety, say when
  you'll be back, and come back. An unanswered bell is how falls happen.
- Do regular **rounding** (comfort, toilet, drink, position, call bell in reach).
- Keep charts current as you go (food/fluid, repositioning, bowel charts) — late
  entries from memory are unreliable.
- Treat each resident's room as their home: knock, wait, introduce yourself.
- Infection control: hand hygiene between residents; PPE per signage.

## 4. Escalate to the nurse — immediately
New pain, fall, change in breathing, colour, consciousness or confusion, suspected
infection, skin breakdown, refusal of food/fluids, chest pain, distress you can't
settle, any allegation or sign of abuse. Report what you **saw and heard**, not a
diagnosis. If the RN isn't available and it's an emergency, follow the emergency
procedure.

## 5. Progress notes
- Objective and specific: "Ate 1/4 of lunch, declined dessert, said 'my tooth hurts'"
  beats "poor appetite, unsettled".
- Record care given, care declined, observations, who you reported to and when.
- Date, time, your name and role; follow the home's system (paper or electronic).
- No opinions about personality, no abbreviations the home hasn't approved.
- Late entries marked as late. Never alter an earlier entry (see
  `human-services.disability-support.support-planning-and-documentation.writing-shift-notes-and-incident-reports`).

## 6. Handing over
Same structure you received: changes, outstanding tasks, risks, what you reported to
whom. Walk-round handovers at the bedside are good if the resident is included and
privacy kept — don't discuss one resident in front of another.

## Gotchas
- "Routine creep" — doing it the way the last shift did it rather than the plan says.
  Re-read plans when a resident changes.
- Short staffing: report it via the proper channel; never skip safety tasks silently.
- Speaking about residents in corridors and lifts breaches privacy.

## Verify
- [ ] Handover captured changes, risks and outstanding tasks for every resident.
- [ ] Shift prioritised by safety and preference; gaps flagged early.
- [ ] Call bells answered; rounding done.
- [ ] Changes escalated to the RN immediately and documented.
- [ ] Notes objective and complete; handover given in the same structure.
