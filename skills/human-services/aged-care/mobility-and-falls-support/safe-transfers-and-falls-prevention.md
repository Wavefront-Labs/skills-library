# Safe transfers and falls prevention

**Goal:** keep the person moving (immobility is itself a hazard) while protecting them
and you from injury. Transfer methods are set by an assessment, usually by a
physiotherapist or occupational therapist; this reference is about carrying that plan
out well, not choosing it.

## 1. The rule that prevents most injuries
**Use the method in the mobility/manual-handling plan, every time.** If the person
seems weaker or stronger than the plan assumes, do not "just help them up" — use the
safer method you're trained in and ask for a reassessment. Never lift a person's full
weight by hand; most services ban the "underarm drag" lift outright.

## 2. Before any transfer
- Explain the move and agree on a cue word ("ready, steady, stand").
- Brakes on (bed, wheelchair, commode); footplates swung away.
- Footwear: closed-heel, non-slip shoes or grip socks — not loose slippers.
- Walking aid within reach, right height (handles at wrist crease with arms hanging).
- Clear path; check for dizziness after sitting up — let them sit on the edge of the
  bed for a minute before standing (blood pressure can drop on standing).

## 3. Assisted sit-to-stand (if the plan says the person can weight-bear)
1. Shuffle forward to the front of the seat; feet hip-width, slightly behind knees.
2. "Nose over toes" — lean forward.
3. Hands push up from the armrests, **not** pulling on the frame (frames tip).
4. You stand to the side, supporting at the hip/belt as directed; your knees bent,
   back straight, close to the person.
5. Once standing, pause for balance before walking; turn with small steps, not a pivot.

## 4. Hoists and aids
- Check the hoist's service tag, battery and the **sling size and type** listed on the
  plan. Wrong sling size is a classic cause of falls from hoists.
- Number of workers: follow the plan (many services require two for hoist transfers —
  local policy varies).
- Check loops/clips attached on both sides before lifting; lift a few centimetres and
  re-check before moving.
- Slide sheets are for repositioning in bed, not for leaving under the person.

## 5. Reducing fall risk at home
Walk through with fresh eyes and report or fix:
- loose rugs, cords, clutter on routes; poor lighting (night-lights to the toilet);
- missing grab rails at toilet and shower; low chairs or beds;
- frequently used items stored too high or low;
- pets underfoot; spills.
Also report: new medication with drowsiness, new confusion, poor eyesight or glasses
not worn, fear of falling (people who fear falling often stop moving and weaken).

## 6. If the person falls
1. **Don't rush to lift them.** Stay calm, reassure, keep them still.
2. Check for response, breathing, pain, bleeding, head strike, odd limb position.
3. Head injury, suspected fracture, can't get up, on blood thinners, or unwell →
   call emergency services / follow your service's post-fall protocol.
4. If uninjured and assessed safe to get up, use the approved method (often a
   lifting cushion or hoist, or guided self-rise) — not a manual lift.
5. Report and document: time, how found, what they said, actions, observations.
   Many services require observation for 24–48 h after a fall (check local protocol).

## Gotchas
- A person grabbing you as they fall can injure you — don't try to catch; guide them
  down against your body and slide.
- Falls often rise with infections or medication changes — report a cluster.
- Bed rails can be a restrictive practice and an entrapment risk; only as planned.

## Verify
- [ ] Transfer done exactly per the current plan, with equipment checked.
- [ ] Footwear, brakes, path and aid checked before moving.
- [ ] Home hazards noted and reported.
- [ ] After a fall: assessed before moving, protocol followed, incident documented.
- [ ] Change in mobility reported for reassessment.
