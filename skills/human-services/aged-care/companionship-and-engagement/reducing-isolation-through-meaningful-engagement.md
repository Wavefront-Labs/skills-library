# Reducing isolation through meaningful engagement

**Goal:** time spent with an older person that they experience as company and purpose,
not as being "entertained" or processed. Loneliness is linked to poorer health and low
mood; the fix is relationships and roles, not just a busier activity calendar.

## 1. Start with the person, not the activity list
Build (or read) a short **life story / "about me"** profile: where they grew up, work,
family, faith, music, sport, pets, routines, what a good day looks like, topics to
avoid. Ask the person first; family and the care plan fill gaps. One page is enough
and should be shared with the team (with consent).

## 2. Conversation that works
- Sit at eye level, good light on your face, background noise off. Check hearing aids
  and glasses — untreated hearing loss looks like disinterest.
- Open questions about **their expertise**: "How did you get the bread to rise in a
  wood oven?" puts them in the teacher role.
- Use prompts: photos, objects, music from their teens and twenties, maps of home town.
- Tolerate silence; sitting together quietly is companionship too.
- Avoid "elderspeak" (sing-song tone, "sweetie", over-simple sentences). Speak as to
  any adult.

## 3. Reminiscence, carefully
Reminiscence can be enjoyable and affirming, but memories can include war, loss,
abuse or migration trauma. If someone becomes distressed: acknowledge, don't probe,
offer to change topic, stay with them, and tell your supervisor afterwards. Never push
for details. Group reminiscence works best with a theme (school days, first job) and a
facilitator who includes quieter members.

## 4. Matching activity to person
Ask: does it give a **role**, a **choice**, a **connection** or **pleasure**?
- Roles: folding laundry, setting tables, gardening, helping a newer resident — "being
  useful" beats passive watching. Offer, don't impose.
- Adapt rather than exclude: large-print cards, raised garden beds, seated exercise,
  shorter sessions, one-step tasks for people with dementia (see
  `human-services.aged-care.dementia-support.responding-to-confusion-and-distress-in-dementia`).
- Respect "no thanks" — preferring solitude is not the same as being isolated. Note
  it and offer again another day.
- Mix group and one-to-one; some people never join groups but light up one-to-one.

## 5. Outings
- Plan: accessibility (toilets, ramps, seating, parking), weather, medication times,
  fatigue, mobility aids, what to do if unwell. Follow your service's outing risk
  assessment and sign-out procedure.
- Take: contact numbers, ID, any medication the plan specifies, water, money.
- Shorter and successful beats long and exhausting.

## 6. Connecting beyond you
Paid companionship ends when the shift ends. Help keep **lasting links**: video calls
with family (set up and stay for the first few), letters, faith communities, clubs,
befriending services, intergenerational programmes. Record which links matter.

## 7. Notice and report
Withdrawal, tearfulness, loss of interest, sleeping much more, saying life isn't worth
living, or giving things away — report to your supervisor/clinical lead that shift.
Any talk of suicide or self-harm follows your service's urgent procedure; don't keep
it confidential.

## Gotchas
- Activities chosen to fill a calendar, not by residents, get low turnout and blame
  the residents.
- Infantilising activities (colouring for adults without asking) damage dignity.
- Boundaries: no personal gifts, lending money, or private contact outside your role.

## Verify
- [ ] You know three things about the person's life and interests.
- [ ] Hearing/vision aids checked before conversation.
- [ ] Activity offered gave choice or role; refusal respected.
- [ ] At least one lasting external connection supported.
- [ ] Changes in mood reported; risk statements escalated immediately.
