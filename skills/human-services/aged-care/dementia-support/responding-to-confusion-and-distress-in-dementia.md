# Responding to confusion and distress in dementia

**Goal:** reduce distress and keep the person engaged and safe by reading what
confusion or agitation is telling you, instead of arguing with it. This is non-clinical
care-worker reference; the person's care plan, behaviour support plan and the clinical
team decide treatment.

## 1. Core stance: behaviour is communication
"Responsive behaviours" (agitation, calling out, resisting care, walking, repeated
questions) usually respond to *something*: pain, need for the toilet, hunger, thirst,
boredom, fear, noise, being rushed, or a misread situation. Before anything else run a
quick **needs check**: pain? toilet? hungry/thirsty? too hot/cold? tired? too much
noise or too many people? Fix what you can first.

## 2. Communicating
- Approach from the **front**, at eye level; say your name and theirs every time.
- One idea per sentence; give time (count to ten silently) before repeating.
- Offer **two choices**, not open questions ("tea or juice?", not "what would you like?").
- Use gesture and objects — hold up the cardigan while you say "cardigan".
- Match their pace and tone; a calm, low voice is contagious, so is irritation.
- Don't quiz memory ("Do you remember who I am?") — it exposes the loss.

## 3. Validation over correction
If someone says they must collect the children from school, correcting ("your kids are
60") usually causes fresh grief or anger, and will need repeating. Instead:
1. **Acknowledge the feeling**: "You're worried about the children."
2. **Explore**: "Tell me about them. What are they like?"
3. **Redirect** gently to something connected: a photo album, a cup of tea, a task.
Avoid elaborate lies that you must keep up; honest-but-kind redirection is the usual
aim. How far to go with "therapeutic fibbing" is contested — follow your service's
policy and discuss with your supervisor.

## 4. Routine and environment
- Keep the **same order** each day for waking, meals, personal care; consistency
  lowers anxiety. Use the person's life story (work, hobbies, routines) to shape it.
- Good lighting, especially late afternoon; reduce background TV/radio.
- Clear signs with words *and* pictures on toilet and bedroom doors; contrasting
  toilet seat colour helps find it.
- Remove mirror/reflection triggers if they cause distress; avoid patterned floors that
  read as steps or holes.

## 5. Common situations
- **Sundowning** (late-day restlessness): schedule busy tasks in the morning, offer a
  calm afternoon activity, lights on before dusk, limit caffeine after midday.
- **Walking/wandering:** treat as purposeful (looking for someone, exercise, needing
  the toilet). Provide safe walking routes and company; follow the service's
  missing-person procedure if someone is unaccounted for — act immediately, don't wait.
- **Repeated questions:** answer as if for the first time; a written note or
  whiteboard ("Mary visits at 2pm") can help.
- **Refusing personal care:** stop, leave, come back in 10–15 minutes or with a
  different worker; see
  `human-services.aged-care.personal-care-assistance.dignified-personal-care-routine`.

## 6. Red flag: delirium is not "the dementia getting worse"
A **sudden** change over hours or days — more confused, drowsier, more agitated, or
fluctuating — may be delirium (infection, dehydration, constipation, medication,
pain). It is a medical problem: report it to the nurse/GP the same shift.

## Gotchas
- Restraint, locked doors, sedation and covert medication are **restrictive
  practices** with legal rules in most jurisdictions; never improvise them.
- Distress at the same time each day is a pattern — log it (time, before, after) so
  the team can find the trigger.
- Family know the person best; ask them for the story behind recurring themes.

## Verify
- [ ] Needs check done before treating behaviour as "the dementia".
- [ ] Short sentences, two-choice questions, waited for a response.
- [ ] Feelings validated rather than facts corrected.
- [ ] Patterns and sudden changes recorded and reported to the clinical team.
- [ ] No restrictive practice used outside an authorised plan.
