# Conducting an intake and psychosocial assessment

**Goal:** understand a person's situation well enough to make a defensible, shared
judgement about what is going on, what the risks are, what is already working, and
what should happen next. This is professional reference material, not advice about a
particular person; agency procedure and local law always take precedence.

## 1. Before you start
- Read what already exists (referral, previous files) but hold it loosely: referral
  information is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- Check for **immediate safety** issues first. If the referral suggests imminent risk
  (to the person, a child, or others), the assessment waits; follow crisis or
  safeguarding procedure (see `human-services.social-work.crisis-intervention.immediate-safety-de-escalation-and-suicide-risk-screening`).
- Book a professional **interpreter** if needed, never a family member or child,
  especially where abuse might be disclosed.

## 2. Open the interview properly
1. Introduce yourself, your role and your agency, and why you are meeting.
2. Explain **confidentiality and its limits** in plain words: what is recorded, who may
   see it, and that risk of serious harm (and child-protection concerns) may have to be
   shared. Say this *before* disclosures, not after.
3. Get informed consent for the assessment and any information-sharing, and record it.
   Note when consent is not required under statute (e.g. some statutory child-protection
   work) — that varies by jurisdiction; check your legislation.
4. Ask what *they* want from the contact. Their stated goal anchors everything else.

## 3. Cover the domains (a guide, not a script)
Use open questions and follow the person's story; tick domains off afterwards.
- **Presenting issue:** in their words; why now?
- **Safety and risk:** to self, to others, from others; children or dependants in the household.
- **Health:** physical, mental health, substance use, disability, medication.
- **Family and relationships:** who's who, who helps, who harms.
- **Housing, income, work/education, legal issues.**
- **Culture, identity, faith, language** — and what these mean to the person.
- **History:** relevant trauma, losses, previous service contact (and how it went).
- **Strengths and protective factors:** coping that has worked, people, skills, values.

## 4. Visual tools
- **Genogram:** three generations; standard symbols (square male, circle female,
  others as your agency uses), lines for relationships, notes on conflict, loss, illness.
  Draw it *with* the person — it often surfaces what questions miss.
- **Eco-map:** the person/household in the centre, systems around (school, work,
  church, GP, services, friends); line thickness/style for strength and stress, arrows for
  direction of energy or resource flow.

## 5. Form a judgement
Write a short **formulation**: what is happening, why (your working explanation),
what helps, what the risks are and how likely/severe, and what is uncertain. Then a plan:
needs, goals, referrals, review date. Where risk is significant or the picture is
unclear, **take it to supervision** or a multidisciplinary meeting before deciding.

## Gotchas
- **Fact vs opinion:** record "Ms X said..." / "I observed..." separately from "In my
  assessment...". Courts and complaints panels read the difference.
- **Confirmation bias:** the referral's framing sticks. Actively look for evidence
  against your first hypothesis.
- **Deficit drift:** a list of problems is not an assessment. If you can't name three
  strengths, you haven't finished.
- **One-off snapshot:** assessment is continuous; set a review point.
- Standardised tools (e.g. agency risk matrices, validated screening scales) support but
  don't replace judgement; use only those your agency has approved and you're trained in.

## Verify
- [ ] Limits of confidentiality explained and consent recorded before disclosures.
- [ ] Immediate safety considered and acted on first.
- [ ] All domains touched, including strengths and the person's own goal.
- [ ] Genogram/eco-map done or a reason recorded why not.
- [ ] Written formulation separates fact, report and professional judgement.
- [ ] Significant risk or uncertainty discussed in supervision; review date set.
