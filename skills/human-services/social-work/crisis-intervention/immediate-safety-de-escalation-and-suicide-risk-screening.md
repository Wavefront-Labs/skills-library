# Immediate safety, de-escalation and suicide-risk screening

**Goal:** get a person (or family) through the acute phase safely, then back to a
level where ordinary support can work. This is a professional training reference, not
a substitute for your agency's crisis protocol, clinical mental-health assessment, or
emergency services. If life is in immediate danger, call emergency services first.

## 1. Safety first — everyone's
- Is anyone in immediate danger (medical emergency, weapon, overdose taken, violence
  in progress)? → emergency services now. Don't attempt to manage it alone.
- Position yourself near an exit; remove obvious hazards if safe to do so; tell a colleague
  where you are. Lone working in crisis situations should follow agency rules.

## 2. De-escalation
- **Lower your own arousal:** slower, quieter voice; relaxed open posture; side-on rather
  than square; respect personal space.
- **One voice:** only one worker talks.
- **Listen and name the feeling:** "You're furious that nobody's called you back." Validation
  is not agreement.
- **Short sentences, simple choices:** "Would you rather sit here or by the window?"
- **Avoid:** arguing, ordering, threatening consequences, touching without consent,
  crowding, sudden movements, "calm down".
- If escalation continues, withdraw and call for help. Your job is not to "win".

## 3. Ask about suicide — directly
Asking does not plant the idea; evidence consistently shows it does not increase risk.
Use plain words: *"Sometimes when people feel like this they think about suicide. Are you
thinking about killing yourself?"* A structured screener such as the C-SSRS covers:
- wish to be dead; active thoughts of killing oneself;
- thoughts of method; intent to act; a plan with details;
- any preparatory behaviour or attempt (recent and lifetime).
Also ask about access to means, substance use, recent losses, previous attempts,
hopelessness, and protective factors (reasons for living, people, responsibilities).

## 4. Escalate by level (check your protocol for exact thresholds)
- **Thoughts, no plan or intent:** safety plan, increased contact, involve supports,
  link to mental-health services, consult supervisor.
- **Plan or intent, or recent preparatory behaviour:** same-day referral to an urgent
  mental-health/crisis team; don't leave the person alone if acute; involve supervisor.
- **Imminent risk / attempt in progress:** emergency services.
Record exactly what was asked, what was said, and what you did.

## 5. Safety plan (Stanley–Brown structure)
Written *with* the person, in their words:
1. Personal warning signs. 2. Things I can do alone to cope. 3. People and places that
distract me. 4. People I can ask for help. 5. Professionals and crisis lines (local
numbers). 6. **Making the environment safer** — reducing access to lethal means
(medication, firearms, ligature points) with the person's agreement and supports' help.
Give them a copy; photograph it on their phone.

## 6. Stabilise and follow up
Roberts' stages after safety: build rapport, identify the main problems, explore feelings,
generate alternatives, make an action plan, follow up. Follow-up contact after a crisis
(phone call within 24–72 h) is itself protective.

## Gotchas
- Accepting "I'd never do it" without asking about plan and means.
- No-suicide "contracts" — not evidence-based; use a safety plan instead.
- Overlooking risk to others, or children in the home, during an adult crisis.
- Debrief yourself afterwards; crisis work accumulates. Every significant risk decision
  goes to supervision.

## Verify
- [ ] Immediate danger ruled out or emergency services called.
- [ ] Suicide asked about directly, including plan, intent, means and history.
- [ ] Response matches the risk level per agency protocol; supervisor informed.
- [ ] Written safety plan with means restriction; person holds a copy.
- [ ] Follow-up booked and documented.
