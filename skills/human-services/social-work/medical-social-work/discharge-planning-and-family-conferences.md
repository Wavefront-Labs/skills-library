# Discharge planning and family conferences

**Goal:** get a patient home (or to the right next place) safely, on time, and with their
wishes respected — while helping them and their family adjust to what has changed.
General professional reference; your hospital's discharge policy, capacity law and funding
rules apply and vary widely by country.

## 1. Discharge planning starts at admission
- Screen early: who lives with the patient, who helped before, stairs/access, previous
  services, carers' own health, finances, any safeguarding concern.
- Ask the **multidisciplinary team (MDT)** for the expected date of discharge and the
  likely functional picture (OT, physio, nursing, medical).
- Identify early the cases that will take longest: new care needs, homelessness, no next
  of kin, disputed decision-making, safeguarding, out-of-area funding.

## 2. Assess for the next setting
- The patient's own goals and preferences first — not the family's, not the bed manager's.
- Supports available vs. supports needed (personal care, meals, medications, transport,
  supervision overnight?).
- Carer capacity and willingness. A daughter who says "of course" may be exhausted; ask
  privately and offer a carer's assessment or support.
- **Capacity:** if there is doubt about whether the patient can decide where to live,
  that is a formal, decision-specific assessment under your law (often led by the
  medical team, with social-work input). Don't assume incapacity from diagnosis or age.
- Options: home with no change, home with services, step-down/rehab, respite,
  residential care. Know your local referral routes and wait times.

## 3. Running a family conference
1. **Prepare:** agree purpose with the treating team; check who the patient wants
   present (and whether they will attend); book a private room, interpreter, enough time.
2. **Pre-meet the team** (5 minutes): agree the medical message and who says it.
3. **Open:** introductions, purpose, timeframe. Ask what the family already understands.
4. **Information:** medical update in plain language, then pause. Silence is fine.
5. **Explore:** values, what the patient would want, worries, practical constraints.
6. **Plan:** options, recommendation, next steps, who does what by when.
7. **Close and document:** summary, decisions, disagreements, follow-up. Send the
   summary to those entitled to it.
Useful acknowledgement prompts: value what they say, acknowledge emotion, listen, ask
questions to understand the person, elicit questions.

## 4. Adjustment and linkage
New disability, diagnosis or bereavement: normalise reactions, give information in small
pieces, link to peer groups, condition-specific charities, financial and legal help
(powers of attorney, advance care planning), and community services before discharge.

## Gotchas
- **"Medically fit" ≠ safe to discharge.** Name the gap and escalate; don't be rushed into
  an unsafe plan by bed pressure. Record your professional view.
- **Self-discharge / against advice:** a capacitous adult can leave; document risks
  explained, support offered, and inform the GP/community services where consented.
- Family speaking for a patient who can speak for themselves.
- Referrals made on the day of discharge — community services need lead time.
- Ethically tricky or disputed cases (capacity, safeguarding, end of life): bring to
  supervision and the MDT, and use ethics consultation where available.

## Verify
- [ ] Discharge screen completed within the first days of admission.
- [ ] Patient's own wishes recorded; capacity assessed formally where in doubt.
- [ ] Carer asked privately about willingness and ability.
- [ ] Family conference has a documented summary, decisions and owners.
- [ ] Services confirmed in place (not just referred) before the patient leaves.
