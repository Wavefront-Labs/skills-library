# Whole-family assessment and family-violence response

**Goal:** help a family become safe and stable enough to stay (or come back) together,
by understanding how the family works as a system, supporting parenting where it is
struggling, and responding to family violence without making anyone less safe.
General professional reference only; local risk tools, referral pathways and legal duties apply.

## 1. Whole-family assessment
- Map the family: everyone in the household *and* anyone with significant influence
  (non-resident parents, grandparents, new partners). A genogram helps.
- For each child: development, needs, and the child's own account of family life.
- For each carer: their history, health, substance use, supports, and how they understand
  the child's needs (**parenting capacity** — can they recognise and meet the need,
  and sustain change?).
- **Patterns over incidents:** when do things go well? What triggers crises? What has
  been tried before and why did it stop?
- Ask what each member wants to be different. Shared goals beat imposed goals.

## 2. Supporting parenting capacity
Practical, specific, observable: routines, boundaries, play, managing behaviour,
school attendance, budgeting. Model, coach, then step back. Use evidence-based
parenting programmes where your service has them. Measure progress against the
child's experience ("Is she getting to school fed?") not attendance at sessions.

## 3. Family violence: do no harm first
- **Ask routinely and safely:** see each adult alone. Never ask about abuse in front of
  the suspected abuser or children. No family members as interpreters.
- **Coercive control** (monitoring, isolation, financial control, threats) can be more
  dangerous than visible injury. Ask about it explicitly.
- **Risk assess** with your agency's tool (e.g. DASH in the UK, or state/provincial
  equivalents). High-risk indicators include strangulation, escalation, separation,
  threats to kill, weapons, pregnancy, stalking. High risk triggers multi-agency
  referral (e.g. MARAC) and safety planning *now*.
- **Partner with the protective parent** — don't blame them for "failing to protect".
  Focus accountability on the person using violence (Safe & Together principle).
- **Couples counselling and joint mediation are generally contraindicated** where there is
  ongoing coercive control; they can increase danger.
- **Safety plan** with the survivor: safe contacts, a code word, escape bag, phone
  security, children's plan, specialist DV service referral.
- Engaging the person using violence: only through appropriate behaviour-change
  programmes and never in a way that reveals what the survivor disclosed.

## 4. Reunification and family meetings
- **Family group conferences / family group decision-making:** an independent
  coordinator, private family time, the family drafts the plan, professionals agree it.
  Powerful for widening the support network.
- Reunification: graded contact, clear conditions, network in place, review dates, and a
  plan for what happens if things go backwards.

## Gotchas
- Working only with the parent who answers the door (often the mother) and missing others.
- Treating family violence as "a relationship issue" or "mutual conflict".
- Disclosures leaking: what one adult tells you must not reach the other adult unless it
  is safe and agreed (or legally required and risk-managed).
- Closing too early after a calm patch. Change is proven over time.
- High-risk family violence and any child-protection concern: involve your supervisor
  and the multi-agency system; don't hold it alone.

## Verify
- [ ] Every household member and significant adult identified; children's voices recorded.
- [ ] Adults seen alone; abuse asked about safely.
- [ ] Risk tool completed; high risk referred on the same day.
- [ ] Safety plan exists and the survivor has a copy only if safe to hold.
- [ ] Goals are shared, observable and reviewed on dates.
