# Safety assessment, home visits and safety planning

**Goal:** answer one question well — *is this child safe enough, today and over time, and
what has to change?* — and act on the answer in a way the family can understand.
This is a general professional reference. Thresholds, timeframes, powers and terminology
(notification, report, section 47 inquiry, investigation) are set by your jurisdiction's
law and your agency's procedures; follow those first.

## 1. Frame the assessment: harm, danger, safety
Many agencies use a Signs of Safety–style structure. Keep three things separate:
- **Past harm:** what has actually happened to the child, how often, how bad. Evidence-based.
- **Future danger:** what you are worried will happen if nothing changes — written as a
  specific statement ("We are worried that Jay (4) will be hit again when Dad drinks").
- **Existing safety:** protection *demonstrated over time* — not promises, not intentions.
Plus **complicating factors** (substance use, mental illness, family violence,
isolation) which raise worry but are not harm in themselves.
Score or rate (e.g. 0–10 safety scale) with the family and the network; the gap between
your number and theirs is a conversation, not a contest.

## 2. Home visits
- **Plan:** purpose, who you need to see (every child — seen, and where age allows,
  spoken to alone), known risks to you, whether to go joint (with police or a colleague).
  Tell your office where you are and when you'll check in.
- **Announced vs unannounced:** unannounced visits show the ordinary day but cost
  trust; use them for a reason you can state.
- **Observe:** the child's presentation, interaction with carers, sleeping arrangements,
  food, hygiene hazards, who else is in the house. Ask to see where the child sleeps.
- **Talk to the child** in age-appropriate ways (drawings, three-houses tool, "words and
  pictures"). Don't lead; don't promise secrecy.
- **Leave** if you feel unsafe. Your safety is not negotiable.

## 3. Safety planning with the network
1. Explain the worry statement in plain language — no jargon.
2. Identify a **safety network**: relatives, friends, neighbours who know the worry
   and agree to act.
3. Write specific rules: who supervises, what happens when Dad has been drinking,
   who the child can call, how the network checks in.
4. Agree how you will know it is working (monitoring, visits, child's view).
5. Review on a set date. A plan that is never reviewed isn't a plan.

## 4. Out-of-home-care decisions
Removal is a last resort and almost always a legal process (court order, emergency
power) under statute. It is never a lone-worker decision: involve your supervisor and
legal team, and follow your agency's delegation. Prefer **kinship** placements where
safe, keep siblings together, maintain family contact, and plan for reunification or
permanency from day one.

## Gotchas
- **Disguised compliance:** parents agreeing with everything while nothing changes. Judge
  on changed behaviour seen over time.
- **Cumulative harm / neglect:** no single incident crosses the line; look at the
  chronology (see `human-services.social-work.case-recording-and-court-work.defensible-case-notes-chronologies-and-court-reports`).
- **Start-again syndrome:** each new worker resets the history. Read the file.
- **Invisible men / other adults:** assess every adult with access to the child.
- **Optimism bias** when the family is likeable or you're overloaded — supervision exists
  to catch this.

## Verify
- [ ] Every child seen; age-appropriate child voice recorded.
- [ ] Harm, danger, safety and complicating factors written separately.
- [ ] Worry statement and safety goals are in words the family understands.
- [ ] Safety network named, plan specific, review date set.
- [ ] Decisions with significant risk made with supervisor/legal input and recorded.
- [ ] Your own visit safety (check-in, joint visit) planned.
