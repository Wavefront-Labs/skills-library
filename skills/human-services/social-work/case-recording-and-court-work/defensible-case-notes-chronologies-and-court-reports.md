# Defensible case notes, chronologies and court reports

**Goal:** a record that a stranger — a new worker, a judge, the person themselves —
can read and understand what happened, what you thought and why you acted.
General professional reference; court rules, report templates and record-retention law
vary by jurisdiction and court. Use your agency's templates and legal team.

## 1. Case notes
Write as if the person will read it (they often can, via records-access requests).
- **Contemporaneous:** same day where possible; date and time of contact *and* of writing.
- **Who, what, where, when:** people present, type of contact, location, duration.
- **Separate fact, report and opinion:**
  - Observed: "Kitchen had no food in the fridge or cupboards."
  - Reported: "Mr B said he had not eaten since Tuesday."
  - Analysis: "In my view this indicates..." — with the evidence it rests on.
- Quote key words verbatim, especially disclosures.
- **Record decisions and rationale**, including what you decided *not* to do and why.
- Plain language, no jargon, no judgemental adjectives ("aggressive", "uncooperative") —
  describe the behaviour instead.
- Never alter an entry; add a dated addendum.

## 2. Chronologies
A table of significant events: date, event, source, impact on the person/child, and
action taken. Significant means it changes the picture (incidents, moves, births, deaths,
referrals, missed appointments with impact) — not every phone call. Chronologies reveal
**cumulative harm** and patterns that single notes hide. Keep it updated, not rebuilt.

## 3. Court reports and affidavits
Typical structure (check your court's template):
1. Author, qualifications, role, period of involvement.
2. Sources of information relied on.
3. Background and chronology summary.
4. Assessment: each issue the court must decide, evidence for and against.
5. The person's/child's wishes and feelings.
6. Options considered, with pros and cons.
7. Recommendation and reasons.
Rules of thumb: answer the legal question asked; every opinion linked to evidence;
acknowledge contrary evidence; numbered paragraphs; no new allegations sprung in the
report. Have it reviewed by your supervisor and lawyer before filing.

## 4. Giving evidence
- **Prepare:** reread your report and key notes; know your chronology; meet your lawyer.
- **On the stand:** listen to the whole question, pause, answer to the judge/magistrate.
  Short, factual answers. "I don't know" and "I don't recall — may I refer to my notes?"
  are acceptable.
- **Cross-examination:** it tests evidence, it isn't personal. Don't argue, don't
  speculate outside your expertise, concede points that are fair. Ask for a question to
  be repeated or broken down if compound.
- Your duty is to the court, not to "winning" for your agency.

## Gotchas
- Writing "client refused" without recording what was offered and why they declined.
- Copy-pasted assessments carrying old errors forward.
- Opinion dressed as fact — the first thing cross-examination finds.
- Notes written days later from memory, undated.
- Late reports. Court deadlines are hard; tell your supervisor early if you'll miss one.

## Verify
- [ ] Notes dated, timed, contemporaneous, with fact/report/opinion distinguished.
- [ ] Decisions and rationale (including non-actions) recorded.
- [ ] Chronology current and limited to significant events.
- [ ] Report answers the court's question; every opinion cites evidence.
- [ ] Supervisor/legal review done before filing.
