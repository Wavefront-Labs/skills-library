# Using supervision, reflective models and managing vicarious trauma

**Goal:** keep your judgement sound and yourself well in work that is complex, emotionally
heavy and high-stakes. General professional reference; regulator and employer
requirements for supervision frequency and CPD differ — check yours.

## 1. What supervision is for
Kadushin's three functions, plus one often added:
- **Administrative / managerial:** workload, case decisions, accountability, procedure.
- **Educational / developmental:** skills, knowledge, learning from cases.
- **Supportive:** the emotional impact of the work.
- **Mediative:** representing staff to the organisation and vice versa.
If your supervision is only caseload management, the other functions are missing — say so.

## 2. Getting the most from it (supervisee)
- Agree a **supervision agreement**: frequency, length, cancellation, what's recorded,
  confidentiality limits, how disagreements are handled.
- **Bring an agenda:** cases needing decisions, cases you feel stuck or uneasy about,
  your learning goals, how you are coping.
- Bring the case you're *avoiding*. That's usually the one that matters.
- Ask for the decision and rationale to be recorded on the case file.
- Morrison's 4x4x4 cycle is a useful prompt: **experience** (what happened) →
  **reflection** (how did it feel?) → **analysis** (what does it mean, what theory/evidence
  applies?) → **action plan**.

## 3. Giving supervision (supervisor)
Make it safe to say "I don't know" or "I got it wrong". Ask before telling. Test
hypotheses ("What else could explain this?"). Notice patterns in a worker's cases and
emotions. Record decisions. Check wellbeing every time, not only when someone looks unwell.

## 4. Reflective models for your own use
- **Gibbs cycle:** description → feelings → evaluation → analysis → conclusion → action plan.
- **Kolb:** concrete experience → reflective observation → abstract conceptualisation →
  active experimentation.
- **Schön:** reflection-*in*-action (adjusting in the moment) and reflection-*on*-action
  (afterwards). Keep a brief reflective log after significant contacts.
**Critical reflection** adds: whose interests does this decision serve? What assumptions
(about class, culture, gender, disability) am I bringing?

## 5. Ethical dilemmas
1. Name the conflicting values (autonomy vs protection, confidentiality vs duty to warn).
2. Check law and policy — some questions are already answered.
3. Check your code of ethics (NASW, BASW, AASW, or your national code).
4. List options and consequences for each stakeholder.
5. **Consult** — supervision, an ethics forum, a professional association advice line.
6. Decide, act, and record the reasoning.

## 6. Vicarious trauma and burnout
- **Signs:** intrusive images from clients' stories, hypervigilance, numbness, cynicism,
  dread before work, sleep problems, withdrawal from friends, irritability, more errors.
- **Burnout** (exhaustion, depersonalisation, reduced efficacy) is often about workload and
  organisation; **vicarious trauma** is about exposure to trauma content. Both matter.
- **Actions:** name it in supervision; ask for caseload mix review; use employee assistance
  or your own therapy; protect rest and boundaries; peer support; critical-incident debrief
  after a death or serious event. If symptoms persist or affect functioning, see a health
  professional.

## Verify
- [ ] Written supervision agreement exists and supervision happens at the agreed rate.
- [ ] You bring an agenda including at least one stuck or worrying case.
- [ ] Key case decisions from supervision appear on the case file.
- [ ] You use a reflective model after significant events.
- [ ] You can name your own early warning signs and what you'd do about them.
