# Safeguarding inquiries, capacity-aware practice and self-neglect

**Goal:** protect an adult at risk from abuse or neglect in a way that follows *their*
wishes as far as possible — protection done *with* people, not *to* them. Examples below
lean on the England framework (Care Act 2014, Mental Capacity Act 2005) as one worked
example; other jurisdictions (US Adult Protective Services, Australian state schemes)
have different thresholds, names and powers. Check your local law and policy.

## 1. Recognise the categories
Physical, sexual, psychological/emotional, **financial or material**, neglect and acts of
omission, **self-neglect**, domestic abuse, modern slavery, discriminatory and
organisational (institutional) abuse. Financial abuse is common and under-reported —
watch for sudden changes to wills, bank activity, new "friends", unpaid bills despite income.

## 2. When does a duty to inquire arise?
Under the English model: the adult has care and support needs, is experiencing or at
risk of abuse or neglect, and because of those needs cannot protect themselves. Other
systems use different eligibility tests (age, "vulnerable adult" definitions). If in
doubt, raise the concern and consult — don't sit on it.

## 3. Running a person-led inquiry
1. **Immediate safety** — emergency services, medical care, police where a crime may
   have occurred (preserve evidence).
2. **Ask the adult what they want** ("Making Safeguarding Personal"): what outcome
   would make them feel safer?
3. Gather information proportionately: the adult, carers, providers, health, police.
4. Consider an **advocate** if the person has substantial difficulty taking part.
5. Agree a safeguarding plan; review whether the outcomes they wanted were achieved.

## 4. Capacity, decision by decision
Core principles (MCA as the example): presume capacity; take all practicable steps to
support the decision; an **unwise decision is not proof of incapacity**. The functional
test: can the person **understand, retain, use or weigh** the relevant information, and
**communicate** a decision — for *this* decision, at *this* time — and is any inability
because of an impairment of mind or brain? If they lack capacity, act in their **best
interests** (or the local equivalent) and choose the least restrictive option.
Capacity assessments for significant decisions should be documented and are often shared
with health colleagues.

## 5. Self-neglect
The hardest balance. A capacitous adult may refuse help. Good practice:
- Build a relationship over time; small practical wins (heating, food, a repair).
- Assess capacity specifically, including **executive capacity** — can they *act* on
  what they say?
- Consider risk to others (fire risk, children, neighbours).
- Hold a multi-agency risk meeting for high-risk refusals; document options offered.
- Never close a high-risk case solely because the person declined; agree a review plan.

## 6. Multi-agency work and information-sharing
Share what is necessary, proportionate, relevant, accurate, timely and secure. Consent is
preferred, but can be overridden where there is serious risk or a legal basis — record the
reasoning. Use safeguarding meetings with clear chairs, actions and owners.

## Gotchas
- Assuming capacity is lacking because of age, diagnosis, or disagreement with you.
- Treating the alleged abuser's account as the family view.
- Organisational abuse hiding in "how this home does things".
- Big decisions (removal, deprivation of liberty, overriding consent): supervisor,
  legal advice and multi-agency involvement — never alone.

## Verify
- [ ] Immediate safety secured; police informed where a crime is suspected.
- [ ] Adult's desired outcomes recorded in their words.
- [ ] Capacity considered for the specific decision, with reasoning recorded.
- [ ] Advocate considered; information-sharing basis documented.
- [ ] Plan reviewed against the person's outcomes, not just risk closure.
