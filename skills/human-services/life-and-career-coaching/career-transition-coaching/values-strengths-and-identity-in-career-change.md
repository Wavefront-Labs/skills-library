# Values, strengths and identity in career change

**Goal:** help someone who is changing career — by choice, redundancy, burnout, a
return after a break, or a health change — get clear on *what they want work to give
them*, generate realistic directions, and adjust to becoming a different working self.
This is the direction-and-identity side; CV writing, job search and interview tactics
are a separate craft (often a careers adviser's), and coaching doesn't replace
financial, legal or employment-rights advice.

## 1. Start where they are
Transitions have an emotional shape (Bridges): an **ending** (loss of role, status,
colleagues — even if chosen), a **neutral zone** (confusing, unproductive-feeling, but
where new options form), then a **new beginning**. Clients pushed straight to "what's
next?" after redundancy often stall because the ending hasn't been acknowledged.
Ask: "What are you losing? What are you glad to leave?"

## 2. Values: what work must give them
- **Card sort:** 30–50 value cards (security, autonomy, creativity, status, helping
  others, variety, location, learning…). Sort into *essential / important / nice to have*;
  narrow essential to five. Then define each in their words — "autonomy" means different
  things to different people.
- **Peak experiences:** "Tell me about three times work felt really right." Pull out
  what was present each time.
- **Frustrations:** what drained them in past roles is equally informative.
- Turn the result into **criteria** to test any option against.

## 3. Strengths
- Pattern-spot from stories: what did they do well that energised them? (Skills they're
  good at but hate are not strengths to build a career on.)
- Optional tools: VIA character strengths survey (free), CliftonStrengths (paid), or
  360 feedback from friends/colleagues ("When have you seen me at my best?").
- Separate **transferable** skills from sector-specific ones; career changers usually
  undervalue the former.

## 4. Options: widen, then test
1. **Generate** without judgement: aim for 10+ directions, including "unrealistic" ones —
   they reveal values.
2. **Cluster and shortlist** three to five against the criteria.
3. **Test with small experiments before big leaps** (Ibarra's core point: you discover
   who you could be by trying, not by introspection alone):
   - Informational conversations with people doing the work (prepare 5 questions;
     ask what a bad day looks like).
   - Short courses, volunteering, shadowing, a side project, a freelance gig.
4. **Debrief each experiment:** energy, fit with values, what surprised them.

## 5. The identity shift
"I've been an accountant for 20 years — who am I if I'm not that?" Help them:
- Build a narrative that connects past to future ("I've always been the person who
  untangles complicated problems; now I want to do it with people, not ledgers").
- Practise saying the new identity out loud ("I'm moving into…").
- Find new peer groups in the target field; old networks often pull them back.
- Expect ambivalence and false starts; that's normal in the neutral zone.

## 6. Practical realities
Check runway (savings, income needs), retraining time and cost, family impact.
Encourage specialist advice where needed (financial planning, redundancy rights,
benefits). *Uncertain:* labour-market facts change fast; point clients to current
local sources rather than relying on your impressions.

## Gotchas
- Leaping to retraining (an expensive course) before any small experiment.
- Coaching the "should" career — the one parents or status suggest.
- Burnout looks like "I hate my career"; sometimes it's the job, workload or manager.
  Explore before they abandon a field.
- Grief after involuntary job loss can include depression; refer if it becomes disabling.

## Verify
- [ ] The ending was acknowledged before option-generation.
- [ ] Five essential values defined in the client's words and used as criteria.
- [ ] At least ten options generated, three to five shortlisted.
- [ ] Each shortlisted option tested with at least one low-cost experiment.
- [ ] Client can tell a coherent past-to-future story of the change.
