# Active supervision and risky-play judgement

**Goal:** children play freely and challenge themselves, while you see trouble coming —
distinguishing healthy risk from real hazard.

Educational reference for carers; your setting's ratios, policies and risk assessments
take precedence.

## 1. Active supervision — what it actually means
Being in the room is not supervising. Active supervision is:
- **Positioning:** stand or sit where you can see the whole area, back to a wall or fence,
  not with your back to children. In a team, spread out and cover zones.
- **Scanning:** look up and around every few seconds, even while engaged with one child.
- **Counting:** know how many children you have and recount at every transition.
- **Listening:** silence or sudden noise are both signals.
- **Anticipating:** know which children are climbing beyond their skill, which pairs clash,
  where crowding happens.
- **Being close enough to reach:** under-3s near water, height or food need you within arm's reach.

Phones away. A carer on their phone is the commonest supervision failure.

## 2. Joining play
- Follow the child's lead; describe what they're doing ("You built it really tall!") rather
  than directing.
- Ask open questions; add a material to extend play rather than taking over.
- Balance: enough engagement to support learning, enough distance to keep scanning everyone.

## 3. Age-appropriate choices (rough guide)
| Age | Typical play | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | Floor play, treasure baskets, tummy time | Small objects (choking), mouthing |
| 1–3 | Climbing low structures, sand, water, push toys | Falls, water, choking, biting |
| 3–5 | Imaginary play, bikes, ball games, construction | Collisions, climbing heights, exclusion |
| 5–12 | Rules games, sport, building, creative projects | Rough play escalating, unsupervised wandering |

Small-parts rule of thumb: anything that fits through a toilet-roll tube is a choking risk for
under-3s (uncertain as a precise standard — check your local toy-safety guidance).

## 4. Risky play: risk-benefit, not risk-elimination
Climbing, balancing, speed, using real tools, rough-and-tumble — children build judgement,
confidence and physical skill from manageable risk. Ask:
1. **What's the benefit?** (skill, confidence, fun)
2. **What's the realistic worst outcome?** A scraped knee vs. a serious fall.
3. **Can the child see and judge the risk themselves?** That's a *risk*. A hidden danger they
   can't judge (rotten branch, broken equipment) is a *hazard* — remove it.
4. **What would reduce harm without killing the benefit?** Softer landing, adult nearby,
   agreed limits ("only as high as you can climb by yourself").

Coach rather than warn: "Where will you put your foot next?" beats "Be careful!"

## 5. Never acceptable
- Unsupervised access to water of any depth for young children — they can drown in a few
  centimetres, silently.
- Cords, blind cords, strings around necks (including bike helmets on play equipment).
- Broken equipment, sharp edges, hot surfaces, chemicals, unfenced roads.
- Children out of sight in public places or on excursions.

## Gotchas
- **"Supervision by proximity"** — standing in a group of adults chatting. Spread out.
- Over-protection creates its own harm (anxiety, poor risk judgement). Families vary in
  their comfort with risky play; discuss your setting's approach with them.
- Outdoor surfaces, weather and sun: hot slides, ice, UV — check before play.
- Record and report injuries per policy, even minor ones.

## Verify
- [ ] Positioned to see all children; headcount known at all times.
- [ ] Phone away while supervising.
- [ ] Under-3s within arm's reach near water, height or food.
- [ ] Hazards checked and removed before play; risky-play limits agreed with children.
- [ ] Injuries recorded and families informed.
