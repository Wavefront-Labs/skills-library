# Tantrums, biting and conflict: calm limits and redirection

**Goal:** keep everyone safe, help the child regulate, and teach the skill they're missing —
consistently with the family and your setting's behaviour-guidance policy.

Educational reference for carers. **Physical punishment, shaming, threats and withholding
food or rest are never acceptable in a care role** (and are prohibited in many jurisdictions
and regulated settings).

## 1. Start with your own state
Children borrow your calm. Lower your voice, slow down, get down to their eye level. If
you're flooded, swap with a colleague if you can.

## 2. Prevention beats reaction
Most "behaviour" is a predictable response to:
- Hunger, tiredness, overstimulation, illness
- Waiting too long, crowding, too few resources (three children, one red truck)
- Transitions without warning (see daily-routines-and-transitions)
- Not having words for a feeling or need

Fix the environment before blaming the child: duplicate popular toys, shorten waits, offer
quiet spaces.

## 3. Tantrums / meltdowns
1. **Keep them and others safe** — move hazards, give space, stay nearby.
2. **Few words.** Name the feeling: "You're really cross that it's time to go."
3. **Don't reason or bargain mid-storm** — the thinking brain is offline.
4. **Hold the limit** calmly: "I won't let you throw the blocks." Don't give in to end it
   (that teaches tantrums work), but don't punish it either.
5. When calm: reconnect warmly, then briefly problem-solve if age-appropriate.

## 4. Biting (common in 1–3-year-olds)
- **Attend to the bitten child first** — comfort, wash the area, first aid per policy;
  broken skin needs the parent informed and may need medical advice.
- To the child who bit: calm, short and firm: "Biting hurts. I won't let you bite."
- Look for the pattern: teething, frustration, crowding, a particular time of day — shadow
  that child at high-risk times and offer alternatives (teething ring, words, "say 'stop'").
- Inform both families per policy; **don't name the other child** to either family (privacy).

## 5. Redirection and choices
- Redirect to an acceptable version: "Balls are for throwing outside — let's go throw them there."
- Offer two acceptable choices: "Do you want to put your shoes on or shall I help?"
- Tell children what *to* do rather than what not to do: "Walk inside" over "Don't run".

## 6. Peer and sibling conflict
- Step in early; describe, don't judge: "You both want the bike."
- Help them generate solutions (turns with a timer, finding another bike); support the
  quieter child to speak.
- Avoid forced apologies; coach repair ("How can we help her feel better?").

## 7. Consistency
Agree with families on language and approaches; the same limit, said the same way, by every
adult. Record recurring incidents factually (time, trigger, what happened, what helped).

## When to involve others
Behaviour that is persistent, intense, sudden-onset, self-injurious, or that comes with
developmental concerns, sexualised behaviour beyond what's typical for age, or signs of
distress at home → talk with your supervisor, record observations, and discuss with the family
whether a health or early-intervention assessment would help. Some of these may also be
safeguarding indicators (see child-safeguarding).

## Gotchas
- Time-out used punitively and isolatingly tends to escalate young children; many settings
  prefer "time-in" (staying close while the child calms).
- Labelling a child ("the biter", "naughty") sticks — to staff, families and the child.
- Praise effort and specific behaviour, not just "good girl/boy".

## Verify
- [ ] You stayed calm and kept everyone safe first.
- [ ] Limits stated briefly and held consistently, with no shaming or physical punishment.
- [ ] Biting: bitten child cared for, both families informed without naming the other child.
- [ ] Recurring behaviour recorded with triggers; pattern discussed with supervisor/family.
