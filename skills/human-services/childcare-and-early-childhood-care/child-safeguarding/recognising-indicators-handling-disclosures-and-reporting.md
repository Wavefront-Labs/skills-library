# Recognising indicators, handling disclosures and reporting

**Goal:** notice when a child may be harmed, respond in a way that protects the child and any
future investigation, and get the concern to the people whose job it is to act.

Educational reference only. **Reporting duties, thresholds and who to call are set by your
jurisdiction's law and your organisation's safeguarding procedure — know them before you
need them.** Your role is to notice, record and report; it is *not* to investigate.
**If a child is in immediate danger, call emergency services first.**

## 1. Indicators (signs that raise concern, not proof)
- **Physical:** injuries that don't match the explanation; bruises on soft or unusual areas
  (ears, neck, cheeks, torso, buttocks) — especially in **non-mobile babies, who rarely bruise
  accidentally**; patterned marks; burns; repeated injuries.
- **Neglect:** persistent hunger, poor hygiene, unsuitable clothing for weather, untreated medical
  or dental needs, frequent unexplained absence, a child left unsupervised.
- **Emotional:** extreme withdrawal or anxiety, flinching, excessive compliance, low self-worth,
  being frightened of going home or of a particular adult.
- **Sexual:** sexual knowledge or behaviour unusual for age, genital soreness or injury,
  sudden fear of particular people or places, gifts or secrets from an adult.
- **Adult behaviour:** a carer who is hostile, rejecting, describes the child very negatively,
  delays seeking medical help, or gives changing explanations.

One sign alone rarely proves anything; patterns over time matter, which is why **written
observations** are essential.

## 2. When a child tells you (a disclosure)
1. **Stay calm** and listen. Your reaction shapes whether they keep talking.
2. Let them speak in their own words. **Don't ask leading questions** ("Did Daddy hit you?").
   If you need to, use open prompts only: "Tell me more", "What happened next?"
3. **Don't promise to keep it secret.** Say: "I'm glad you told me. I need to tell someone whose
   job is to help keep you safe."
4. Reassure: they did the right thing; it isn't their fault.
5. **Don't examine** the child for injuries or take photos (unless your procedure specifically
   directs it), and don't confront the alleged person.
6. Record **as soon as possible**, using the child's exact words (see below).
7. Report the same day — immediately if the child may be at risk tonight.

## 3. Recording
- Date, time, place, who was present.
- **Child's exact words** in quotes; your questions too.
- What you observed (injury location/size drawn on a body map if your procedure uses one).
- Facts, not interpretation. Sign and date. Keep it secure, not in a personal notebook.

## 4. Reporting
- Tell your **designated safeguarding lead/supervisor** per procedure — and, where the law
  makes *you* personally a mandatory reporter, make sure the statutory report is made; telling
  your manager may not discharge your personal duty in every jurisdiction (check).
- In-home carers (nannies, babysitters) without an organisation: contact the local
  child-protection authority or police directly.
- Don't tell the family you're reporting if that could put the child at more risk — follow
  advice from the authority.

## 5. Safe practice for yourself
- Avoid being alone with a child out of sight where possible; use open doors, visible spaces.
- Follow intimate-care policies (toileting, changing) exactly.
- No private messaging, social-media contact or gifts outside policy.
- **Allegations against staff or concerns about a colleague** go to the designated person or,
  if it involves them, the next level or the external authority. Whistleblowing protections
  often apply.

## Gotchas
- "It's probably nothing" and "I don't want to get the family in trouble" are the classic
  reasons concerns go unreported. Reporting a concern in good faith is not an accusation.
- Cultural practices should be understood respectfully, but never override a child's safety.
- Your own distress after a disclosure is normal — use supervision or an employee assistance programme.

## Verify
- [ ] You know your jurisdiction's reporting duty and your organisation's designated lead.
- [ ] Disclosures met with calm listening, no leading questions, no secrecy promise.
- [ ] Record made promptly with the child's exact words and signed.
- [ ] Report made the same day (immediately if risk is immediate).
- [ ] You followed safe-practice and intimate-care policies.
