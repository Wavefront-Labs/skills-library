# Preparing for and speaking up in appointments

**Goal:** leave an appointment (health, welfare, housing, disability, school) with a
clear answer or a clear next step — not with "we'll see". Services are busy and
appointments short; the person who arrives with one precise request and evidence gets
further. This is self-help reference; for serious disputes an independent advocate or
advice service can help.

## 1. Prepare (15 minutes beforehand)
Write a one-page sheet:
- **My main ask:** one sentence. "I need a reassessment of my care hours because I can
  no longer shower alone." Put it first.
- **Why:** 2–3 facts with dates/examples. Bring evidence (letters, a symptom diary,
  photos of a hazard).
- **Questions** (max 3–5), most important first.
- **What I'll accept:** your ideal outcome and your acceptable minimum.
- Practical: reference numbers, medication list, previous decisions.
Ask in advance for **adjustments** you need: interpreter, longer appointment,
accessible room, phone/video option, easy-read information.

## 2. In the room
- Say your main ask **in the first two minutes** — not after the small talk runs the
  clock down.
- Use "I" statements and facts: "I've fallen twice since March" rather than "nobody
  helps me".
- Take notes or ask your support person to. Ask if you can record (some services
  allow it; ask first).
- If you don't understand: "Can you explain that another way?" Ask what jargon means.
- Before leaving: "So what happens next, who does it, and by when?"

## 3. When you're fobbed off
Common lines and calm responses:
- "There's nothing we can do." → "Who *can* do something? Can you refer me or tell me
  in writing why not?"
- "You'll have to wait." → "How long, and what do I do if things get worse before then?"
- "That's policy." → "Could I see the policy, please? Is there discretion or an exception process?"
- "You don't meet the criteria." → "Which criterion? Can I have that decision in writing
  with reasons?"
**Broken record:** repeat your main ask calmly in the same words. Stay polite and
specific; anger is understandable but often lets a service focus on your tone, not
your request. If it's going nowhere, ask to speak to a senior or supervisor, or end the
meeting and put it in writing.

## 4. Bring a support person
A friend, family member, peer or advocate can take notes, remind you of your points,
and witness what was said. Agree their role beforehand (notes only? speak if I
signal?). You decide what they share. Independent advocacy services exist for many
groups (disability, mental health, older people, care leavers); see
`human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy.peer-navigation.walking-alongside-as-a-peer-navigator`.

## 5. After
Within a day, send a short **follow-up email/letter**: "Thank you for meeting today.
My understanding is that you will [action] by [date]. Please let me know if I've
misunderstood." This creates a record. Diary the date and chase if it passes. Log it in
your file (see
`human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy.own-case-paperwork.keeping-your-own-case-file-and-contact-log`).

## Gotchas
- Downplaying difficulties to seem capable — describe typical and worst days.
- Arriving with ten issues; the main one gets lost. Book another appointment for the rest.
- Accepting a verbal promise — always confirm in writing.
- Self-advocacy is tiring; it's fine to ask for help with it.

## Verify
- [ ] One-sentence main ask, evidence and questions on one page.
- [ ] Adjustments requested in advance.
- [ ] Main ask stated early; next steps, owner and date confirmed.
- [ ] Refusals asked for in writing with reasons.
- [ ] Follow-up written within a day and logged.
