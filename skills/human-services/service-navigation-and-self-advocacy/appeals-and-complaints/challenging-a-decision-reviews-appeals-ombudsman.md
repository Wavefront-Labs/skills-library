# Challenging a decision: reviews, appeals and the ombudsman

**Goal:** turn "I've been refused / cut / treated badly" into a structured challenge
that a decision-maker must answer. Routes and deadlines vary by country and service;
this is general reference. Free advice services (welfare rights, legal aid, community
legal centres, disability advocacy) can tell you the exact route and often help write
it. See also the applicant's view in
`law-government.public-administration-and-civil-service.applying-to-the-state.preparing-applications-and-challenging-refusals`.

## 1. Two different things
- **Review/appeal:** you disagree with a **decision** (refused payment, reduced care
  package, eligibility). Aim: change the decision.
- **Complaint:** you're unhappy with **how you were treated** (rudeness, delay, lost
  paperwork, unsafe care). Aim: apology, fix, change in practice, sometimes
  compensation.
Often you do both — but use the right channel for each.

## 2. First steps (do these this week)
1. **Find the deadline.** It is usually on the decision letter. Review windows can be
   short (weeks, not months). If you're near it, lodge a short request now and add
   detail later; ask about late-request rules if you've missed it.
2. **Ask for written reasons** if you don't have them: which rule, what evidence, why.
3. **Request your records** — your file, assessment notes, call logs. Most countries
   give a legal right of access to your personal information (e.g., subject access
   requests under UK/EU GDPR; FOI or privacy-act access elsewhere). There's often a
   free route and a statutory response time.

## 3. Internal review / reconsideration
- Write to the address on the letter. Say: the decision (date, reference), that you
  ask for it to be reviewed, **why it's wrong** — point to criteria and evidence,
  what's been missed or misunderstood — and what decision you want.
- Add new evidence that addresses the reasons given.
- Keep proof of sending.
Many systems require this step before you can go to a tribunal.

## 4. External appeal
If internal review fails, an independent tribunal or appeal body may hear it. Expect
forms, a bundle of documents, possibly a hearing (often informal). Representation
from an advice service makes a real difference; ask early because they get booked
up.

## 5. Complaints and the ombudsman
1. Use the organisation's **complaints procedure** first; ask for it in writing.
   State what happened, the impact, and what you want.
2. If unresolved after their final response (or a set time), complain to the
   **ombudsman** or sector **regulator** (care quality regulators, disability
   commissions, health complaints bodies). Ombudsmen generally look at maladministration
   — unfair process, delay, failure to follow rules — rather than re-making the
   decision.
3. Safety issues (abuse, neglect, dangerous care) should go to the regulator or
   safeguarding body straight away, not wait for internal processes.

## 6. Keeping going through delays
- Diary every deadline and promised response date; chase the day after it passes.
- One reference number per process; quote it always.
- Keep a contact log (see
  `human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy.own-case-paperwork.keeping-your-own-case-file-and-contact-log`).
- Ask your elected representative's office to make an enquiry if you're stuck —
  often effective with government agencies.
- Look after yourself: these processes are slow and draining; share the load with a
  supporter.

## Gotchas
- Missing the first deadline is the most common way strong cases are lost.
- Writing emotionally at length — facts, criteria and evidence persuade.
- Assuming an appeal can't make things worse; in some systems a full review can
  reconsider everything — ask an adviser.

## Verify
- [ ] Deadline found and request lodged within it.
- [ ] Written reasons and your records requested.
- [ ] Review letter names the decision, the error, the evidence and the outcome wanted.
- [ ] Complaint separated from appeal; escalation body identified.
- [ ] Every step logged with dates and references.
