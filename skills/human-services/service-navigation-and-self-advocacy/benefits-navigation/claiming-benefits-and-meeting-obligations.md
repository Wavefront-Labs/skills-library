# Claiming benefits and meeting obligations

**Goal:** get the payments you're entitled to, as early as possible, and keep them
without overpayment debts or sanctions. Rules, payment names and deadlines differ by
country and change often — confirm every specific with the agency or a free
**welfare rights / benefits advice** service. This is general self-help reference.

## 1. Check what you might get
- Use an official or reputable independent **benefits calculator** (many countries
  have them) with real numbers: income, savings, housing costs, household members,
  health/disability, caring.
- Look beyond the obvious payment: housing assistance, carer payments,
  disability-related supplements, concession cards, utility discounts, health-cost
  help. Many people miss entitlements because one claim doesn't trigger the others.
- Not sure? **Apply** — only a decision tells you, and some payments start from the
  claim date, so delay costs money.

## 2. Apply well
1. Note the **claim date** (and keep proof — screenshot, receipt number). Some systems
   let you register intent to claim now and complete later; ask.
2. Read the actual **criteria** and answer the question asked, with examples.
3. Describe your **worst and typical days**, not your best. For disability/health
   claims, explain how often, how long, and what happens if you try (pain, fatigue
   the next day, needing help). Many people understate because of pride or habit.
4. Be truthful and consistent across forms — inconsistencies cause refusals.
5. Keep a copy of everything you submit.
For how government applications are assessed generally, see
`law-government.public-administration-and-civil-service.applying-to-the-state.preparing-applications-and-challenging-refusals`.

## 3. Evidence
- Map each criterion to a piece of evidence: doctor's letter, specialist report,
  carer or support worker statement, payslips, rent agreement, bank statements.
- Ask professionals for letters that describe **functional impact** ("cannot walk
  more than 50 m without stopping") rather than just a diagnosis.
- Send copies, not originals, unless originals are demanded.

## 4. Assessments and interviews
Take a support person if allowed; bring notes; ask for breaks; ask for adjustments
(interpreter, accessible venue, phone instead of face to face). Write down afterwards
what was asked and said (see
`human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy.self-advocacy.preparing-for-and-speaking-up-in-appointments`).

## 5. Keep it: reporting obligations
- Know your **reporting cycle** and what counts as a **change of circumstances**
  (income, work hours, partner, address, household, savings, going overseas, going
  into hospital). Report within the required time and keep proof.
- Attend required appointments or activities; if you can't, contact them **before**
  — and ask for an exemption if illness or caring makes them unreasonable.
- Open every letter; set calendar reminders.

## 6. When it goes wrong
- **Refusal or reduced amount:** ask for written reasons; ask for a review within the
  deadline (see `human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy.appeals-and-complaints.challenging-a-decision-reviews-appeals-ombudsman`).
- **Overpayment/debt notice:** don't ignore it; ask how it was calculated; you may be
  able to challenge it or arrange affordable repayments. Get advice.
- **Hardship now:** ask about advance payments, crisis payments, and local emergency
  relief (food banks, charities).

## Gotchas
- Assuming you're ineligible because you work, own a home or have a partner.
- Submitting without evidence and planning to "send it later" — decisions get made
  without it.
- Phone advice from the agency isn't binding; ask for it in writing.
