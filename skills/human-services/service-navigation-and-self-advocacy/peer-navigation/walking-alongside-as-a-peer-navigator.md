# Walking alongside as a peer navigator

**Goal:** use what you learned surviving a system (benefits, housing, disability or
aged-care services, mental-health or justice systems) to help someone else get through
it — while they stay in charge and you stay a peer, not a caseworker, clinician or
rescuer. Peer roles can be paid, volunteer or informal; formal ones should come with
training, supervision and a clear role description.

## 1. What a peer adds
- **Credibility and hope:** "I've been where you are and it got better."
- **Insider know-how:** which form is the real one, what the assessor actually asks,
  how long it really takes, where the queue is shorter.
- **Equality:** a relationship without the power imbalance of professional services.
Protect that equality — it's the whole point.

## 2. Sharing know-how without taking over
- Ask what they want help with and what they already know.
- **Show, then step back:** do the first form together, then let them do the next
  one while you're there, then just check it (see
  `human-services.disability-support.daily-living-skill-building.task-analysis-prompting-and-fading`
  for fading).
- Offer options ("Some people do X, I did Y"), not instructions.
- Share tools: the contact log and case file
  (`human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy.own-case-paperwork.keeping-your-own-case-file-and-contact-log`),
  appointment prep sheets
  (`human-services.service-navigation-and-self-advocacy.self-advocacy.preparing-for-and-speaking-up-in-appointments`).
- Check your information is current — systems change, and what worked for you five
  years ago may not now.

## 3. Accompanying to appointments
Agree beforehand: what's my role (company, notes, prompts, speak only if asked)? What
shouldn't I mention? In the room, the person speaks for themselves; you support. Sit
beside them, not between them and the worker. Debrief afterwards: what happened, what
next, how do you feel?

## 4. Using your story purposefully
- Share **briefly and with a reason**: to build trust, offer hope, or illustrate an
  option. Then bring it back to them.
- Share what you're comfortable having repeated; you can't take it back.
- Not every detail helps — graphic trauma details can harm both of you.

## 5. Staying in the peer lane
You are **not**: a lawyer, a clinician, a case manager, a crisis service, or a lender.
- Advice-like questions (legal, medical, money decisions) → "I can tell you what I did,
  but let's get you to [advice service] for your situation."
- Risk (suicidal thoughts, abuse, violence, someone in danger) → follow your
  organisation's procedure and involve your supervisor or emergency services
  immediately. Explain confidentiality limits **at the start** so this isn't a
  betrayal later.
- No money lending, no taking people home, no 2 am phone availability. Clear
  boundaries keep the relationship safe and sustainable.

## 6. Looking after yourself
Hearing stories like yours can reopen your own. Signs to watch: dreading sessions,
sleep problems, anger at "the system" spilling over, feeling responsible for outcomes.
Use **supervision** (peer supervision is ideal), debrief after hard sessions, keep your
own support network, and take breaks. Stepping back for a while is part of the role.

## Gotchas
- Pushing your route as the only right one.
- Becoming the person's only support — help them build others.
- Advocating so hard you speak over them (that's advocacy for others, a different role).
- Informal peers have no organisational backing; know where to refer.

## Verify
- [ ] Person sets the goals; you offered options, not orders.
- [ ] Role agreed before each appointment; they spoke for themselves.
- [ ] Story shared briefly and purposefully.
- [ ] Confidentiality limits explained; risks escalated immediately.
- [ ] You have supervision or a debrief route and are using it.
