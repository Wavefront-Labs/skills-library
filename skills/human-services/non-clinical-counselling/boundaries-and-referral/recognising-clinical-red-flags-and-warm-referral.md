# Recognising clinical red flags and warm referral

**Goal:** stay helpful *inside* the non-clinical role — listeners, peer supporters,
befrienders, coaches, pastoral carers — by spotting when someone needs clinical or
emergency help, getting them there in a way that actually connects, and being honest
about confidentiality. You are not expected to diagnose; you are expected to notice and act.

## 1. Red flags: act the same day or immediately
**Emergency (call emergency services / follow emergency protocol now):**
- Suicide attempt in progress, or intent plus a plan plus access to means now.
- Serious injury, overdose, or medical symptoms (chest pain, collapse, confusion).
- Immediate danger to a child or another person.
- Severe confusion or psychosis where the person can't keep themselves safe.

**Urgent clinical referral (same day / next day — GP, crisis team, mental-health line):**
- Suicidal thoughts, even without a plan; recent self-harm.
- New hearing of voices, beliefs that others find bizarre, or strong paranoia.
- Not eating or sleeping for days; rapid weight loss; signs of an eating disorder.
- Mania: very little sleep without tiredness, racing speech, reckless spending or risk.
- Heavy drinking or drug use with withdrawal risk (alcohol and benzodiazepine
  withdrawal can be medically dangerous — never advise stopping abruptly).
- Disclosed abuse or domestic violence (specialist services).

**Routine referral (encourage the person to see a professional):**
- Low mood or anxiety lasting more than about two weeks and affecting work, sleep or relationships.
- Trauma memories, flashbacks or nightmares that dominate their life.
- Grief that isn't easing at all over many months *and* is disabling.
- Anything where you notice yourself out of your depth after a few conversations.

## 2. Warm referral vs signposting
**Signposting** is handing over a number. **Warm referral** makes the connection happen:
1. **Explain why**, in their terms: "What you're describing sounds really hard to carry,
   and I think a doctor could help with the sleep side in ways I can't."
2. **Explore barriers:** cost, waiting lists, fear of being "sectioned", previous bad
   experiences, transport, language. Address the ones you can.
3. **Make it concrete:** look up the service together, help them book or make the call
   with them there (with consent), offer to write down what they want to say.
4. **Handover** (with consent): brief the receiving service so the person doesn't
   repeat their whole story.
5. **Follow up:** "Did you manage to get the appointment?" Keep supporting alongside —
   referral is not abandonment. Say so explicitly.

## 3. Confidentiality limits
State them early, in plain words: "What you tell me stays between us, unless I'm worried
you or someone else — especially a child — is at serious risk. Then I'd need to involve
someone, and I'd try to tell you first." Then keep that promise both ways: don't gossip,
don't overreact, and don't sit on genuine risk. Record decisions and reasons per your
organisation's policy. Mandatory reporting duties differ by role and jurisdiction —
*know yours before you start; this skill can't tell you them.*

## 4. Boundaries that protect both of you
- Contact through agreed channels and hours only; no personal social media.
- No gifts of significance, loans or business with people you support.
- No touch without clear consent and organisational norms.
- If you feel special, needed or rescuing, bring it to supervision — it usually
  means the relationship is drifting.

## 5. Helper self-care
Warning signs of compassion fatigue or vicarious trauma: dread before shifts, numbness,
intrusive images from others' stories, irritability, cynicism, sleep problems. Use
regular supervision or debriefing (not only after crises), cap your load, keep a life
outside helping, and step back for a while when your own life is heavy.

## Gotchas
- Fear of overreacting delays action; when risk is real, a clumsy call is better than none.
- "Just call this number" referrals often go nowhere, especially for people with past bad experiences of services.
- Confidentiality limits stated *after* a disclosure feel like betrayal.
