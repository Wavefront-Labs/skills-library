# Intentional story sharing and peer-role boundaries

**Goal:** use your own lived experience (of mental distress, addiction, disability,
homelessness, a diagnosis, bereavement…) to offer hope and connection that a
professional can't — while keeping the focus on the other person and staying a
*peer*, not a mini-clinician. Peer support is not treatment; it often sits beside it.

## 1. What makes peer support different
- **Mutuality:** both people learn; the relationship is less hierarchical than
  worker–client. You're "someone who's been there", not an expert on their life.
- **Hope made visible:** your presence says recovery or a liveable life is possible.
- **Their language, their goals:** recovery is defined by the person, not by symptom
  reduction. Avoid clinical labels unless they use them.
- **Curiosity over assessment:** "How do you make sense of what's happening?" rather
  than "What are your symptoms?"

## 2. Sharing your story intentionally
Before any disclosure, run three checks:
1. **Purpose:** *who is this for?* It should open a door for them — normalise,
   offer hope, show a possible path — not relieve you.
2. **Timing:** after you've listened enough to know what's relevant. Early on, a
   one-line "I've been in hospital too" can build trust; a long story cannot.
3. **Dose:** short (30–60 seconds), then hand it back: "…that's what helped me —
   I'm curious what fits for you?"

Share the **arc**, not the graphic detail: what it was like, what helped, what life is
like now. Avoid method details of self-harm or substance use, war stories, and
anything that sets your path up as *the* way ("I stopped meds and I'm fine").

Know your **no-go zones** in advance: parts of your story you won't share at work,
and an exit phrase if asked ("That's a bit personal for me — but I can tell you what
helped").

## 3. Staying in the peer role
Common drift and what to do:
| Drift | Sign | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Becoming a clinician | Giving diagnoses, medication advice, "you should" | Share experience; refer clinical questions to the treating team |
| Becoming a friend | Personal phone number, socialising, lending money | Keep within service channels; warmth inside a structure |
| Becoming a rescuer | Feeling responsible for their recovery, overwork | Mutual responsibility; they lead |
| Becoming staff-identified | Defending the service against the person | Hold the lived-experience view in team meetings |

Dual relationships (same recovery group, same small town, same support network) are
common in peer work — talk them through with your supervisor rather than pretending
they won't arise.

## 4. Within teams
- You may be the only lived-experience voice in a clinical meeting. Translate:
  "From the person's side, that meeting felt like being judged."
- Document in plain, respectful language — the person may read it.
- You are still a mandated worker: confidentiality has the same limits as for any
  staff member (risk to self, others, children).

## 5. Looking after your own recovery
Peer work brushes against your own history. Have a personal wellness plan (e.g. a
WRAP-style plan), know your early warning signs, and use supervision. Taking time out
is part of modelling recovery, not failing at it.

## Gotchas
- "I know exactly how you feel" — you don't; similar experience isn't the same experience.
- Comparing ("Mine was worse") silences people.
- Being asked to do non-peer tasks (covering a support-worker shift, enforcing rules)
  erodes the role; raise it with the manager.
- Lived-experience workforce titles and training differ widely by country and sector.
  *Uncertain:* certification requirements vary; check your jurisdiction.
