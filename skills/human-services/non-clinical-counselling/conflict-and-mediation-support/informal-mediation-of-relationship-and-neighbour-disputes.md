# Informal mediation of relationship and neighbour disputes

**Goal:** help two people — neighbours, housemates, adult siblings, a parent and adult
child, members of a community group — talk about a conflict and reach something they
can both live with, while you stay impartial. This is informal, voluntary and
non-binding; for legal family mediation, workplace grievances or court-linked
processes see `law-government.mediation-arbitration-and-adr` skills.

## 1. Is mediation suitable? (screen first, separately)
Mediate only if:
- Both are **willing** and here voluntarily (not pressured by a landlord or family).
- There's **no violence, intimidation or coercive control** between them. Ask each
  person alone: "Have you ever felt afraid of them? Has anything physical happened?"
  If yes, don't bring them into a room together; refer to specialist support.
- Both have **capacity** to take part (not intoxicated, acutely unwell or in crisis).
- The issue is **theirs to settle** (not, say, a crime or a child-protection matter).
If you know one party well (same congregation, friend), say so and consider whether
someone else should do it.

## 2. Separate meetings (each 30–60 minutes)
- Explain the process: voluntary, confidential (with safety limits), you don't take
  sides or decide, either can stop at any point.
- Listen to their story fully. Reflect feelings and what matters to them.
- Ask: "What would need to happen for this to feel better?" "What do you think they'd
  say about this?" "What's your part in it, if any?"
- Agree what they're happy for you to share and what stays private.

## 3. The joint meeting
1. **Opening:** welcome, purpose, ground rules (one at a time, no insults, breaks allowed).
2. **Uninterrupted time:** each speaks for a few minutes; the other just listens.
   You summarise each, neutrally.
3. **Identify issues:** list them as shared problems, neutral wording ("noise in the
   evenings", "use of the shared drive"), not accusations ("his loud parties").
4. **Explore interests:** behind "they must cut the hedge" might be "I want light in
   my kitchen". Interests usually overlap more than positions.
5. **Generate options:** brainstorm without judging, then test each: is it specific,
   realistic, fair to both?
6. **Agree and write it down.**

### Reframing
Turn attack into need: "She's totally selfish about the bins" → "So having the bins
sorted fairly matters to you." Turn past blame into future: "So for the future you'd
want…". Do this for both parties equally — impartiality is visible in your language.

## 4. Writing a simple agreement
Who will do what, by when, and how they'll handle it if it goes wrong (e.g. "knock
and mention it within a day rather than leaving notes"). Plain words, both sign or
agree by message, each gets a copy. Set a review point (a call in four weeks).
Partial agreement is still a result — note what's agreed and what isn't.

## 5. When to stop
- Threats, abuse or escalating hostility in the room → call a break; separate; end if needed.
- One person agrees to everything just to end it (a power imbalance) → slow down, check
  privately, or stop.
- Disclosure of risk or a crime → follow safeguarding procedure.
- Going round in circles for over an hour → summarise progress, offer another meeting
  or a referral to a formal service.

## Gotchas
- Giving your opinion of who's right, even with body language, ends your usefulness.
- Pushing for apology or forgiveness backfires; let it come or not.
- Settlements that require a third party (landlord, council) need that party involved.
- Cultural norms around direct confrontation differ; shuttle (staying separate while you
  carry messages) may suit some disputes better than a joint meeting.

## Verify
- [ ] Each party screened separately for safety, willingness and capacity.
- [ ] You declared any connection to either party.
- [ ] Both had uninterrupted time and a neutral summary.
- [ ] Agreement is specific (who, what, when) with a fallback and review date.
- [ ] You stopped or referred when safety or power imbalance appeared.
