# Handling a crisis call: from rapport to a safe ending

**Goal:** in one contact, help a distressed caller or texter feel less alone, find out
whether they are at risk, increase their safety, and end with a plan — all inside your
service's protocol. **Your service's own risk framework, escalation rules and
emergency procedure override anything here.** This is non-clinical support; you are
not diagnosing or treating, and many lines are explicitly "listening, not advice" services.

## 1. Open and build rapport fast (first 1–3 minutes)
- Greet with the service name and your first name (or service alias). Slow, warm voice.
- Let them talk. Callers often test the line with silence, small talk or a hang-up first.
- Use the listening core (`human-services.non-clinical-counselling.supportive-listening.active-listening-reflection-and-holding-silence`):
  reflect feeling early — "It sounds like tonight has been really hard."
- **Text/chat:** shorter messages, one question at a time, acknowledge delays ("Take
  your time, I'm here"). No tone of voice, so name feelings more explicitly.

## 2. Ask about suicide — directly
If there is *any* sign of distress, ask in plain words: **"Are you thinking about
suicide?"** or "Are you thinking of killing yourself?" Evidence and training consensus
is that asking directly does not put the idea in someone's head; it gives permission to
talk. Avoid "You're not thinking of doing anything silly, are you?" — it invites "no".

If yes, gauge **immediacy** using your service's framework. Common elements:
- **Plan:** have they thought about how and when?
- **Means:** do they have access to what they'd use — is it with them now?
- **Timeframe / intent:** tonight, or a thought that comes and goes?
- **History:** previous attempts; recent loss, crisis or substance use right now.
- **Protective factors:** people, pets, faith, reasons for living, past coping that worked.
- **Already in progress?** Have they taken anything or harmed themselves already?
  If yes, this is a medical emergency — follow emergency protocol immediately.

## 3. Work towards safety
- **Distance from means:** "Would you be willing to put that in another room / give it
  to someone / lock it away while we talk?" Means restriction is one of the most
  effective interventions; ask for it specifically.
- **Safety plan** (collaborative, their words): warning signs, things they can do to get
  through the next hours, people and places that help, who they'll contact, crisis
  numbers, making the environment safer. Read it back.
- **Connect onward:** GP, community mental-health team, emergency department, a trusted person.
- **Escalate when your protocol says** — typically imminent risk with means and
  intent, a suicide in progress, or a child at risk. Know in advance whether your line
  traces calls or contacts emergency services with or without consent, and tell the caller
  honestly what you're doing if your protocol allows.

## 4. Ending safely
- Signal the ending; summarise what they've said and the plan.
- Confirm the next step ("So after this you'll text your sister and put the tablets in
  the car boot?") and that they can call back.
- Don't end abruptly because the queue is long — but do end; open-ended calls can
  become unhelpful for both of you.

## 5. Difficult contacts
- **Frequent callers:** follow the service's call plan (time limits, a consistent
  approach). Stay warm; repetition doesn't mean low risk.
- **Abusive or sexual callers:** you can end the call — one warning, then end, per policy.
- **Third-party callers** worried about someone else: support them and help them
  plan how to ask and get help; give emergency guidance if danger is immediate.
- **Silent calls:** say you're still there, invite them to talk, and keep the line open
  for the time your service allows.

## Gotchas
- Calm callers can be at higher risk: someone who has decided may sound peaceful.
- Don't promise confidentiality you can't keep.
- Don't argue with their reasons; explore ambivalence — part of them made this call.
- Record-keeping is often required within minutes of the call; do it before the next one.
- **Debrief** after hard calls with a shift supervisor. Vicarious trauma is the main
  reason volunteers leave; it is not weakness.

## Verify
- [ ] Asked about suicide in direct words when there was any distress.
- [ ] Gauged plan, means, timeframe, and whether harm was already under way.
- [ ] Explored protective factors and worked on distance from means.
- [ ] Safety plan read back, or escalation done exactly per protocol.
- [ ] Ended with a confirmed next step; call logged; debrief taken if needed.

*Uncertainty: escalation thresholds, tracing and consent rules differ by country and
service; this outlines common training content, not any one line's protocol.*
