# Active listening, reflection and holding silence

**Goal:** help a person feel heard and think more clearly about their own difficulty,
so that *they* find their next step — without you diagnosing, fixing or lecturing.
This is the base skill under helpline work, peer support, befriending and pastoral
visiting. It is **not therapy**: you are not assessing or treating a mental-health
condition. When the conversation reveals something beyond this role, see
`human-services.non-clinical-counselling.boundaries-and-referral.recognising-clinical-red-flags-and-warm-referral`.

## 1. Set up the conversation
- **Space and time:** private enough to talk, phone on silent, and say how long you
  have ("We've got about half an hour"). An open-ended chat with a hidden deadline
  ends with you glancing at the clock mid-disclosure.
- **Say what you can and can't offer** in one line if it's a formal setting: "I'm
  here to listen; I'm not a counsellor, and if you tell me someone's at risk I'd
  need to act on that." Confidentiality limits stated up front avoid a sense of betrayal later.
- **Posture:** face them at a slight angle, relaxed, eye contact that's natural for
  the person's culture (direct gaze is uncomfortable for some people — follow their lead).

## 2. The four core moves (OARS)
| Move | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Open question** | Invites the story | "What's been happening?" "How has that been for you?" |
| **Affirmation** | Recognises effort or strength (genuine, specific) | "You kept going to work through all of that." |
| **Reflection** | Shows you heard; deepens | "You sound exhausted, and a bit angry that no one noticed." |
| **Summary** | Gathers threads; lets them correct you | "So there's the money worry, your mum's health, and feeling alone with both — have I got that right?" |

Aim for **roughly two reflections per question.** Question-heavy conversations feel
like an interview; reflection-heavy ones feel like being understood.

### Levels of reflection
1. **Repeat/paraphrase** content — safe, but stays on the surface.
2. **Reflect feeling** — name the emotion you hear: "That sounds frightening."
3. **Reflect meaning** — the thing underneath: "It's not just the job — it's that
   you'd always seen yourself as the one who provides."
Offer deeper reflections tentatively ("I wonder if…", "It sounds like…"). If you're
wrong, they'll correct you, and the correction is useful.

## 3. Silence
Silence after a hard disclosure is usually the person *thinking or feeling*, not a
gap you must fill. Count to ten in your head before speaking. If it stretches and
feels stuck rather than reflective, a soft prompt works: "Take your time," or reflect
the last thing they said. On the phone, a quiet "mm" lets them know you're still there.

## 4. The traps (the "righting reflex")
- **Advice too soon** ("Have you tried…?"). Even good advice lands as "you haven't
  thought of the obvious". If they ask for ideas, ask first what they've considered.
- **Reassurance that dismisses** ("I'm sure it'll be fine", "Everything happens for a reason").
- **Making it about you** ("The same thing happened to me…") — peer workers do share
  deliberately; see `human-services.non-clinical-counselling.peer-support.intentional-story-sharing-and-peer-role-boundaries`.
- **"Why" questions** — "Why did you do that?" sounds like blame; try "What led up to that?"
- **Stacked questions** — ask one, then wait.
- **Interpreting or labelling** ("Sounds like you're depressed") — that's diagnosis.
- **Rushing to the problem-solving phase** because *you* are uncomfortable with their distress.

## 5. Ending
Signal the end five minutes before ("We've got a few minutes left"). Summarise, ask
what they'll take away or do next, and check what support they have before the next
contact. Don't open new heavy topics in the last minutes — note them for next time.

## Gotchas
- People often lead with a safer "presenting" issue; the real one comes later, after
  they've tested whether you'll judge.
- Your own discomfort is information about the conversation, not a cue to fix it.
- If someone mentions harm to self or others — even obliquely — don't reflect past it.
  Ask directly; that's covered in the crisis-line and referral skills.
- Cultural norms about emotion-talk vary; some people prefer practical framing ("what
  would help this week?") and that's fine.

## Verify
- [ ] Time and confidentiality limits were stated at the start.
- [ ] Reflections outnumbered questions; at least one reflection of feeling or meaning.
- [ ] No unsolicited advice, diagnosis or dismissive reassurance.
- [ ] You summarised and they confirmed or corrected it.
- [ ] You checked for risk if anything hinted at it, and ended with their next step.
