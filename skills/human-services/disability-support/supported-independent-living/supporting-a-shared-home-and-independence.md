# Supporting a shared home and independence

**Goal:** help residents in supported living run **their** home and lives — routines,
money, relationships with housemates — with as much control as possible, and less
support over time where that's what they want. "SIL" is NDIS (Australia) terminology;
similar models exist as supported living or group homes elsewhere.

## 1. It's their home, not your workplace
- Knock before entering bedrooms; don't use residents' food, TV or bathroom without
  invitation (follow the house's arrangements for staff).
- Residents decide décor, menus, visitors, routines — within tenancy and safety limits.
- Staff areas and paperwork should be unobtrusive. Avoid "institutional" signs like
  rosters taped to walls or locked kitchens unless an authorised plan requires them.
- Never refer to residents as "clients on the unit" or "beds".

## 2. Household routines
- Build a routine **with** each person: cleaning, laundry, shopping, cooking, bins.
  Visual schedules or apps help some people.
- Support at the right level: some need prompts, some need step-by-step coaching,
  some need you to do part (see
  `human-services.disability-support.daily-living-skill-building.task-analysis-prompting-and-fading`).
- Record what the person did independently — this is the evidence of progress.

## 3. Money
- Follow the organisation's **financial support policy** exactly: receipts for every
  purchase, signed records, counts at handover. Money handling is a common source of
  allegations and actual abuse.
- Support people to budget and choose, not to be told what they can afford. If a
  substitute decision-maker or financial administrator is appointed, know their scope.
- Never use residents' money for staff items or "house" items without agreement.

## 4. Shared-living dynamics
- Housemates usually didn't choose each other. Expect friction over noise,
  cleanliness, the TV, food.
- **House meetings** (accessible format, regular, residents chairing where possible)
  let people set shared rules themselves.
- Support conflict resolution; don't take sides or impose. Persistent conflict, or one
  resident harming another, is a safeguarding and compatibility issue to escalate —
  people have the right to be safe in their home.
- Respect each person's right to relationships, including intimate relationships, and
  privacy to have them.

## 5. Overnight support
Know which kind of night shift you're on:
- **Sleepover:** you sleep but respond if needed; know how you'll be woken (call
  system, monitor).
- **Active/awake night:** checks and support throughout; do checks as the plan
  states, not more intrusively.
Know the emergency plan: evacuation (residents' evacuation needs differ), on-call
manager, medical emergencies, seizure plans.

## 6. Building independence
Set goals the **person** chooses (cook a meal, catch a bus, manage a bank card) and
fade support as skills grow. Fading means less of your time — talk honestly with your
manager when support levels can reduce, even though it may affect funding hours;
over-servicing is a quiet harm.

## Gotchas
- "Duty of care" is often over-used to block choices; balance it with dignity of risk
  (see `human-services.disability-support.disability-safeguarding.dignity-of-risk-and-abuse-reporting`).
- Group outings by default ("everyone goes bowling Thursday") ignore individual
  choice.
- Talking about one resident with another breaches privacy.

## Verify
- [ ] You behave as a guest in their home.
- [ ] Routines co-designed; independence recorded.
- [ ] Every financial transaction receipted and logged.
- [ ] House meetings held; conflicts escalated when safety is affected.
- [ ] Night-shift type, checks and emergency plan known.
