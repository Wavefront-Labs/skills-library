# Building community participation and natural supports

**Goal:** the person belongs somewhere — known by name at the café, a regular at the
club, with relationships that don't depend on a paid worker. "Community access" that
means driving around or sitting in a shopping centre with staff is presence, not
participation.

## 1. Start from interests and roles
Ask (with the person, family, and the plan): what do they enjoy, what are they good at,
what did they used to do, what are they curious about? Then look for **valued roles**
connected to it: team member, volunteer, customer, member, regular. A love of trains →
model railway club; caring about animals → shelter volunteering.

## 2. Map the community
Find the places where people with that interest already gather: clubs, classes,
churches/faith groups, sports teams, libraries, men's sheds, community gardens, choirs.
Prefer **regular, small, mainstream** settings over one-off events or disability-only
groups (which have their place, but rarely build community ties by themselves).

## 3. Plan the outing
- Accessibility check: venue access, toilets (including changing places if needed),
  quiet space, seating, lighting/noise for sensory needs.
- Transport: the person's travel plan, cost, timetable, backup if a service is
  cancelled. Build towards independent travel where wanted (see
  `human-services.disability-support.daily-living-skill-building.task-analysis-prompting-and-fading`).
- Money, medication, ID, communication device, phone.
- Risk assessment as your service requires — proportionate, not a reason to avoid
  going.

## 4. How to be there: in the background
- Position yourself **beside or behind**, not between the person and others.
- Introduce the person, then step back. Direct conversation to them, not you.
- Avoid staff uniforms or lanyards in community settings unless required.
- Don't do things for the person in public that they can do — it shapes how others see them.
- Groups of residents with staff ("the bus outing") draw stares and block connection;
  aim for one-to-one or the person's own friends.

## 5. Build natural supports
A **natural support** is an unpaid community member who helps: the club member who
saves a seat, the coach who explains drills, the barista who knows the order.
- Spot people who are friendly and introduce them properly.
- Share **only what the person agrees to** about their support needs — confidentiality
  still applies.
- Model how to communicate with the person, then let others do it.
- Thank them; don't over-burden them.

## 6. Fade
Plan the fade from the start: arrive with the person, then leave for parts of the
session, then drop off and collect, then the person attends alone or with a friend.
Record progress and connections in notes so the next worker builds on them.

## Gotchas
- Worker preferences quietly shape outings — whose idea was this activity?
- Repetition is how belonging happens; changing activities every week prevents it.
- Rejection happens; debrief with the person and try another setting rather than
  abandoning participation.
- Respect choice: some people value quiet routines at home.

## Verify
- [ ] Activity grounded in the person's interests and a valued role.
- [ ] Accessibility and transport planned; backup known.
- [ ] Worker stayed in the background and directed others to the person.
- [ ] At least one named natural support developing, with consent about disclosure.
- [ ] Fading plan recorded and progressing.
