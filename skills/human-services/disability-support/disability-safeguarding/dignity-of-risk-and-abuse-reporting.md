# Dignity of risk and abuse reporting

**Goal:** protect people with disability from abuse and neglect **and** from the
over-protection that strips them of choices. Both are safeguarding failures. Reporting
schemes and capacity law differ by jurisdiction (e.g., Australia's NDIS Commission
reportable incidents, England's Care Act and Mental Capacity Act); learn yours and your
organisation's procedure. Complex decisions belong with the person, their supporters,
and the team — not with one worker.

## 1. Dignity of risk
Everyone learns and lives through risk: cooking, dating, travelling alone, spending
money unwisely. Denying people with disability those risks "for their safety" is
disabling. Use a **risk-enablement** approach:
1. What does the person want to do and why does it matter to them?
2. What specifically could go wrong, how likely, how serious?
3. What would reduce the risk while keeping the activity (skills, a phone, a buddy,
   a plan B)?
4. Who decides? Record the person's informed choice and the plan.
Escalate to your supervisor/team when the risk is serious or capacity is in question.

## 2. Supported decision-making
The CRPD (Article 12) frames legal capacity as a right; the shift is from
**substitute** decisions ("we decided for her") to **supported** decisions ("we helped
her decide"). In practice:
- Give information in the person's format (easy-read, visuals, AAC).
- Break decisions into parts; offer real options; allow time and trial.
- Involve people the person trusts — on their terms.
- Check your influence: are you steering toward what's convenient for staff?
- Where a guardian or substitute decision-maker exists, know their legal scope; they
  should still seek the person's will and preferences.

## 3. Recognising abuse and neglect
People with disability, especially intellectual disability or communication
differences, experience abuse at higher rates and are less often believed. Watch for:
- unexplained injuries; fear of a particular person; changes in behaviour, sleep,
  continence;
- missing money or possessions, unexplained purchases, controlled bank cards;
- sexualised behaviour new for the person, genital injury or infection;
- neglect: poor hygiene, missed meals, unattended health needs, isolation;
- unauthorised restrictive practices: locked rooms, sedation, restraint without a plan;
- peer-to-peer harm in shared homes.
Behaviour change may be the **only** disclosure some people can make.

## 4. Responding and reporting
- Immediate danger → emergency services.
- Disclosure: listen, don't lead, don't promise secrecy, record their exact words
  (see `human-services.aged-care.elder-safeguarding.recognising-and-escalating-elder-abuse`
  for disclosure technique).
- Report to your supervisor the same shift; follow time-limited external reporting if
  your role requires it (reportable-incident deadlines can be **24 hours** for serious
  incidents in some schemes — verify locally).
- Preserve evidence; don't investigate or confront.
- If the concern involves your organisation or manager, go to the regulator.

## 5. Supporting complaints
People have the right to complain without fear. Make the complaints process accessible
(easy-read, independent advocate, support to write). Never discourage a complaint
because it reflects on you or the service.

## Gotchas
- "Duty of care" used as a blanket reason to refuse choices.
- Assuming someone "lacks capacity" generally — capacity is decision-specific and can
  fluctuate.
- Dismissing allegations because the person "often makes things up".

## Verify
- [ ] Risky choices handled through a recorded enablement plan, not a refusal.
- [ ] Decisions supported with accessible information and trusted people.
- [ ] Abuse indicators known; behaviour change treated as possible disclosure.
- [ ] Reports made same shift and to external bodies within required timelines.
- [ ] The person can access complaints and advocacy independently.
