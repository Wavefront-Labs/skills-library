# Supporting AAC users and different communicators

**Goal:** make sure the person can say what they want, to whoever they want, whenever
they want — and that you understand them. Communication access underpins consent,
choice and safeguarding. AAC systems are set up by a **speech pathologist / speech and
language therapist**; support workers make them work day to day.

## 1. Know the person's communication profile
Read the **communication passport/profile**: how they say yes and no, show pain,
discomfort, like and dislike; their AAC system(s); signs they use; how they best
understand you; what to avoid. If there isn't one, ask the person, family and SLP —
and start one.

## 2. AAC is their voice: keep it available
- The device or board goes **everywhere**: meals, bathroom (a laminated board),
  outings, bed. An AAC system left charging in the bedroom is a person without words.
- Charge devices overnight; check volume; carry the low-tech backup.
- Don't edit vocabulary, move buttons or delete pages without the person/SLP —
  motor memory depends on stable locations.
- Never remove a device as a consequence or to stop "repetitive" use.

## 3. Aided language modelling
People learn AAC by seeing it used, like children learn speech by hearing it.
- **Model on their system as you talk:** say "Let's go *out*" while pressing GO and
  OUT. A few key words per sentence is enough.
- Model without demanding: no "Show me 'drink' on your talker" tests.
- Respond to what they say on the device as real communication, even if it seems
  random — explore it ("You said 'bus'. Are you thinking about the bus?").

## 4. Key word sign
Key word sign systems (e.g., Makaton in the UK, Key Word Sign Australia) sign only the
key words while speaking the full sentence. Learn the person's core signs; use them
consistently across the team. Note: these are not the same as national sign languages
(BSL, Auslan, ASL); Deaf people who use a sign language may need a qualified
interpreter, not a worker's basic signs.

## 5. Giving time
- Ask one question, then **wait** — 10 seconds or more. Count in your head. Many
  people need time to process and to compose on a device.
- Don't finish sentences unless the person wants that.
- Reduce background noise; face the person; use their name to gain attention.

## 6. Easy-read and plain language
For letters, plans, consent forms, rosters:
- Short sentences, one idea each, common words, active voice.
- A supporting image beside each point; large font (14pt+); lots of white space.
- Test with the person: can they tell you back what it means?
Easy-read doesn't replace explaining in person.

## 7. When you don't understand
Be honest: "I'm not understanding yet. Can you show me?" Offer options, go to the
place or object, check the board, ask someone who knows them — but always check back
with the person. Don't pretend to understand.

## Gotchas
- Yes/no questions are quick but can be leading; people may say yes to please.
- Challenging behaviour often drops when communication works (see
  `human-services.disability-support.positive-behaviour-support.implementing-a-behaviour-support-plan-and-de-escalation`).
- Talking about the person in front of them because "they don't understand" — assume
  competence.

## Verify
- [ ] Communication profile read; yes/no/pain signals known.
- [ ] AAC with the person, charged, unchanged without consent.
- [ ] You modelled on their system during the shift.
- [ ] Waited long enough for responses; didn't finish sentences.
- [ ] Written information given in a format they understood.
