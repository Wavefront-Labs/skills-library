# Person-led personal support

**Goal:** provide personal care, transfers and daily routines **the way the person
directs**, safely and in line with their support plan. In disability support the person
is often the expert on their own body and care; many have directed workers for years.
Your employer's procedures and the person's plan override this general reference.

## 1. The stance: you are their hands, not their manager
- The **social model** of disability: barriers come from environments and attitudes,
  not just impairments. Your job is to remove barriers, not "fix" the person.
- **Ask, then follow:** "How do you like this done?" Then do it that way, unless it's
  unsafe or outside your training — in which case say so and escalate.
- Speak to the person, never over them to a family member or co-worker.
- Choice applies to small things too: clothes, order of tasks, music, what to eat.

## 2. Consent and intimate care
- Explain each step; check before touching. Consent can be spoken, signed, via AAC,
  or through gesture and expression the plan describes (see
  `human-services.disability-support.communication-support.supporting-aac-and-different-communicators`).
- Watch for non-verbal "no": turning away, tensing, distress. Stop and check.
- Preferences about worker gender for intimate care are legitimate; they should be in
  the plan and rostered for.
- Keep doors closed and the person covered; never have a third person present for
  intimate care unless it's planned and agreed.

## 3. Transfers and hoisting
Follow the **manual-handling plan** exactly: method, equipment, sling type and size,
number of workers. The person may tell you their technique — combine their knowledge
with the plan; if they conflict, use the plan's safer method and raise it for review.
Check equipment before use (hoist service date, battery, sling wear). Never lift the
full body weight manually. See
`human-services.aged-care.mobility-and-falls-support.safe-transfers-and-falls-prevention`
for pre-transfer checks.

## 4. Mealtime support
Follow the mealtime management plan (texture, fluid thickness, position, pace) set by
a speech pathologist/dietitian. Choking risk is a leading safety concern for some
people with intellectual disability or cerebral palsy. Details in
`human-services.aged-care.mealtime-and-nutrition-support.assisted-eating-hydration-and-texture-modified-meals`.

## 5. Know your scope
Common limits (they vary by employer and jurisdiction — confirm yours):
- Medication: only if trained and authorised, from a dose administration aid, with
  recording; never decide doses.
- Complex/clinical care (PEG feeding, catheters, bowel care, seizure medication,
  ventilation) needs specific training and delegation — often by a nurse — per person.
- **Restrictive practices** (restraint, locked doors, removing items) only under an
  authorised behaviour support plan; never improvised.

## 6. Observe, record, report
Note changes in skin, health, mood, mobility, continence, appetite, and anything the
person tells you about their wellbeing or safety. Record factually in shift notes;
escalate the same shift. See
`human-services.disability-support.support-planning-and-documentation.writing-shift-notes-and-incident-reports`.

## Gotchas
- "Doing for" to save time erodes skills and control — budget time for the person to
  do what they can.
- Workers sometimes copy a colleague's shortcut; the plan wins.
- Boundaries: friendly is good; becoming a "friend" with gifts, social media and
  money handling is not.
- Don't comment on people's bodies, weight or choices.

## Verify
- [ ] Asked how the person wants it done and did it that way.
- [ ] Consent checked at each step, including non-verbal signals.
- [ ] Transfers and meals per current plans; equipment checked.
- [ ] Stayed within trained and delegated scope.
- [ ] Changes recorded and reported the same shift.
