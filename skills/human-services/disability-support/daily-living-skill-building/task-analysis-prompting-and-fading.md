# Task analysis, prompting and fading

**Goal:** teach a skill the person wants (make toast, catch the bus, use an ATM) so
they end up doing it **without you**. The method is borrowed from applied behaviour
and occupational therapy practice; complex programmes should be designed with an OT,
behaviour practitioner or the person's team — this is the support-worker version.

## 1. Pick the goal with the person
Motivation is everything. A skill the person wants (making their own coffee) is learned
far faster than one chosen for them. Write it as an observable outcome: "Makes instant
coffee independently, from getting the cup to sitting down with it."

## 2. Write a task analysis
Break the task into small, observable steps, in the order **this person** will do them,
in **their** kitchen. Example (instant coffee):
1. Fill kettle to the line. 2. Switch on. 3. Get cup. 4. Spoon in coffee. 5. Add
sugar if wanted. 6. Wait for click. 7. Pour water to the mark on the cup. 8. Add milk.
9. Stir. 10. Carry to table.
Do the task yourself following your list to check nothing's missing. Steps should be
small enough that the person can succeed at each.

## 3. Choose a teaching order
- **Forward chaining:** teach step 1 first; you do the rest.
- **Backward chaining:** you do everything except the **last** step, which they do
  and get the satisfaction of finishing; then the last two, and so on. Often good for
  people who need motivation from completion.
- **Whole task:** do every step every time with prompts as needed — common for
  people who can do many steps already.

## 4. Prompting hierarchy
From most to least intrusive:
**Physical** (hand-over-hand or hand-under-hand) → **Model** (you show) →
**Gesture** (point) → **Verbal** ("kettle next") → **Visual** (picture card,
checklist) → **Independent**.
- **Least-to-most:** wait (5–10 s), then give the least prompt that works. Good when
  the person already knows some steps.
- **Most-to-least / errorless:** start with enough help to succeed, then reduce. Good
  for new or safety-critical steps (hot water, roads).
Hand-under-hand (your hand under theirs) keeps the person in control better than
hand-over-hand. Physical prompting needs consent.

## 5. Fade, deliberately
Prompt dependence is the main failure — the person waits for "kettle next" forever.
- Plan the fade: drop to the next-less-intrusive prompt after a set number of
  successes (e.g., three in a row).
- Move yourself physically further away over sessions.
- Replace your prompts with **natural cues** or a visual checklist they keep.
- Praise the achievement, then fade praise to natural reward (the coffee).

## 6. Record progress
For each session, record each step: I (independent), V, G, M, P (prompt level). A
simple grid shows which steps are stuck. Share in shift notes so every worker prompts
the **same way** — inconsistent prompting stalls learning.

## 7. Travel training (special case)
Use the same structure for a route; start with the full trip together, then shadow
from a distance, then meet at the destination. Plan for what goes wrong (missed stop,
cancelled service, lost phone) and practise it. Agree a risk plan with the person and
team.

## Gotchas
- Talking constantly is a prompt too — be quiet and let them think.
- Steps that are too big look like "can't learn".
- Generalisation isn't automatic: a skill learned at the day centre may need re-teaching at home.

## Verify
- [ ] Goal chosen by the person and written as observable outcome.
- [ ] Task analysis tested by doing it yourself.
- [ ] Prompt level recorded per step, per session.
- [ ] Fading plan exists and is shared with all workers.
- [ ] Progress reviewed; support reduces as skills grow.
