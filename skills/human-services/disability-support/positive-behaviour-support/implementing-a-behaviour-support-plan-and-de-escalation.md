# Implementing a behaviour support plan and de-escalation

**Goal:** reduce behaviours of concern by meeting the needs behind them, and keep
everyone safe when things escalate. Behaviour support plans (BSPs) are written by a
qualified behaviour support practitioner; restrictive practices are legally regulated
in many places (e.g., authorisation and reporting under Australia's NDIS rules). As a
support worker you **implement and give feedback** — you don't design or change the
plan alone.

## 1. Think in functions
Behaviour usually serves a purpose. Common functions:
- **Get something** — attention, an item, an activity.
- **Escape/avoid** — a demand, noise, a person, pain.
- **Sensory** — it feels good or blocks something that feels bad.
- **Communicate** — when other ways aren't available or aren't being heard.
Also check **setting events**: poor sleep, pain, illness, constipation, medication
changes, missed routine, a change of staff. Many "behaviour" problems are unrecognised
pain or communication breakdown.

## 2. Record ABC data
When an incident happens, record:
- **A — Antecedent:** what happened just before (where, who, what was asked, noise).
- **B — Behaviour:** what the person did, observably ("threw cup at wall", not "lost it").
- **C — Consequence:** what happened right after (what you did, what the person got
  or avoided).
Plus time, duration, intensity. Patterns across ABC charts let the practitioner find
the function. Vague records ("aggressive again") are useless.

## 3. Prevention (most of a good plan)
- Follow the plan's **proactive strategies**: predictable routines, visual schedules,
  warnings before transitions, choices, reduced demands on bad days, sensory
  breaks, meaningful activity.
- Teach and honour **replacement behaviours** — if a break card gets a break every
  time, the person needn't hit to escape.
- Know the person's early warning signs (pacing, rocking, louder voice, a certain
  phrase) from the plan.

## 4. The crisis cycle and de-escalation
Typical phases: **calm → trigger → agitation → escalation → peak → de-escalation →
recovery.**
- **Agitation:** use the plan's early strategies — reduce demands, offer the
  preferred activity, lower your voice, give space, fewer words.
- **Escalation/peak:** safety first. Remove others and hazards rather than the
  person; keep a safe distance and exit; one calm worker speaks; don't argue, threaten
  or touch unless the plan authorises it. Call for help per procedure; emergency
  services if there's serious risk.
- **Recovery:** the person may be exhausted, embarrassed or still fragile. Don't
  lecture or impose consequences; offer water, quiet, reconnection. Re-introduce
  demands slowly.

## 5. Restrictive practices
Physical restraint, mechanical restraint, chemical restraint (medication to control
behaviour), seclusion, environmental restrictions (locked fridges). In many
jurisdictions these require **authorisation, a plan, and reporting** each use.
Unauthorised use can be a reportable incident and potentially assault. If you believe
one is needed in an emergency, follow your organisation's emergency procedure and
report immediately.

## 6. After an incident
Complete the incident report the same shift; debrief with your supervisor; check the
person (and housemates) for injury and distress; feed observations to the practitioner.
Workers can be affected too — use support offered.

## Gotchas
- Different workers responding differently teaches the person that behaviour sometimes works — consistency matters more than any single strategy.
- Reacting with lots of attention can reinforce attention-maintained behaviour; follow the plan.
- Never use punishment, withholding of food, or loss of outings as consequences.

## Verify
- [ ] BSP read and early warning signs known.
- [ ] ABC data objective and complete for every incident.
- [ ] Proactive strategies used daily, not only in crises.
- [ ] No restrictive practice outside authorisation; any use reported.
- [ ] Incident reported and debriefed the same shift.
