# Short-notice respite familiarisation

**Goal:** give families of people with disability a real break — often the only one
they get — while the person has a safe and ideally enjoyable stay. Respite in
disability often involves children, complex health needs and group settings, so the
familiarisation must be fast **and** thorough on safety-critical items. Compare with
the aged-care version,
`human-services.aged-care.respite-care.stepping-in-as-respite-carer`.

## 1. Safety-critical first (never skip, even in an emergency placement)
Confirm and **physically see** the plans for:
- **Seizure management** (seizure types, emergency medication, when to call an
  ambulance) — only trained, authorised workers give emergency medication.
- **Mealtime management** (texture, choking risk) and allergies/anaphylaxis plan.
- **Medication** list and administration authority; meds in original labelled
  packaging or pharmacy-packed aids — don't accept loose tablets.
- **Manual handling/transfers** and equipment.
- **Behaviour support plan** and any authorised restrictive practices.
- Health conditions needing monitoring (diabetes, PEG feeding, continence, sleep).
- Emergency contacts; who has legal authority for medical decisions.
If a critical plan is missing, tell your coordinator before accepting care.

## 2. The one-page profile (ten minutes with the family)
- What people like and admire about the person.
- What's important **to** them (favourite things, people, routines, comforters).
- How best to support them (communication, triggers, what calms them, bedtime
  ritual, how they show pain/fear).
Write it in the person's voice where possible; share it with every shift.

## 3. Arrival and settling
- Let the person explore; show their room, the toilet, where their things are.
- Familiar items from home: blanket, toy, music, device.
- Visual schedule for the stay: days, activities, when family returns. Uncertainty
  about "when do I go home?" drives distress.
- Allow time; avoid scheduling a big outing on day one.

## 4. Group respite and camps
- Compatibility matters: age, needs, behaviours, noise tolerance. Raise concerns
  before the stay.
- Supervision ratios as set; know who you are responsible for at all times —
  especially at transitions (bus, pool, meals) where people go missing.
- Water activities: the higher-risk setting — follow the service's water safety
  plan, and note that people with epilepsy often need closer supervision in water.
- Night: sleep routines, bedwetting dignity, monitoring per plan.

## 5. Keep family informed — at the agreed level
Some families want a daily text and photo; some want silence unless it's urgent.
Agree in advance; respect photo/social-media consent.

## 6. Handback
Written and verbal: sleep, food, toileting, mood, activities, any incidents, medication
given (with the record), what went well. Return all belongings and medication; count
meds together if that's your service's process.

## Gotchas
- "He's always fine" from a tired parent isn't a plan — ask specific questions.
- Children's respite brings child-safeguarding requirements (checks, two-adult rules
  for some activities, no private messaging).
- Regression in toileting or sleep during respite is common; report without blame.

## Verify
- [ ] All safety-critical plans seen, current and understood.
- [ ] One-page profile shared with every shift.
- [ ] Visual schedule given; familiar items with the person.
- [ ] Supervision and water-safety rules followed in group settings.
- [ ] Handback documented; belongings and meds returned.
