# Ward and hospice visiting alongside clinical teams

**Goal:** add comfort, company and practical help to patients' days without getting in the
way of their care — and know when to step back. Volunteer reference only; your hospital or
hospice's induction and the nurse in charge decide what you may do on each ward.

## 1. Arriving on a ward
- Sign in where required; wear your ID badge visibly.
- **Check in with the nurse in charge** every shift: who'd like a visitor, who shouldn't be
  disturbed, who is in isolation, anyone with special communication needs or risks.
- Things change hour to hour. If staff are busy with an emergency, wait or come back.

## 2. Infection control (non-negotiable)
- **Hand hygiene** before and after every patient contact (gel or wash per local policy).
- Respect isolation signs: don't enter barrier-nursed rooms unless trained and permitted.
- Don't volunteer if you have diarrhoea/vomiting (commonly within 48 hours of last
  symptoms — check local rule), a fever, cold or flu symptoms.
- Don't sit on beds; don't touch drips, drains, oxygen or equipment.

## 3. Visiting a patient
- Knock or say hello at the curtain; introduce yourself and your role ("I'm a volunteer,
  not a nurse"). Ask if they'd like company.
- Sit at eye level; listen; follow their lead. Short visits are fine.
- **Practical comforts** within your role: fetching water (only if staff confirm they can
  drink — some patients are nil by mouth or need thickened fluids), reading, newspapers,
  helping with menus, charging a phone, a walk to the window if staff agree.
- **Never** give food, drink or medication without checking; never lift or move patients;
  never give medical information or opinions.
- If a patient asks for a nurse, is in pain, distressed or unwell — find a staff member
  straight away.

## 4. Wayfinding and front-of-house
Walk people to where they need to go rather than pointing, if you can. Know key locations,
accessible routes and the chaplaincy/patient advice service. Anxious visitors often need a
calm person more than directions.

## 5. In a hospice and at the end of life
- **Presence matters more than words.** Sitting quietly, holding a hand (if welcome),
  reading or playing music they like.
- Let the patient lead conversations about dying; don't change the subject, don't offer
  false reassurance, don't push religious views. Spiritual needs → offer chaplaincy.
- Some schemes (e.g. "no one dies alone" sitting services) have specific training; only
  sit vigil if trained for it.
- Families: offer tea, a quiet word, space. Ask staff before sharing anything about the patient.

## 6. Confidentiality and boundaries
What you see and hear stays on the ward: no names on social media, no talking about patients
outside. Don't look at notes or screens. Don't give contact details or continue contact
after discharge unless your scheme allows it.

## 7. After the shift
Use debriefs and volunteer support, especially after a death or difficult encounter. Concerns
about a patient's safety or treatment go to the nurse in charge or your volunteer manager —
the same shift.

## Verify
- [ ] Checked in with the nurse in charge before visiting.
- [ ] Hand hygiene before and after every patient; isolation signs respected.
- [ ] Checked with staff before giving any food or drink.
- [ ] Clinical needs passed to staff immediately, not handled yourself.
- [ ] Confidentiality kept; debrief used after hard shifts.
