# Listening on a helpline and following escalation protocols

**Goal:** give callers a safe space to be heard, within your service's model, and act
correctly and promptly when a call involves risk. Volunteer reference only: every helpline
has its own model (non-directive listening, information-giving, signposting) and its own
risk protocol — your training and shift supervisor override anything here.

## 1. Know your service model
Before your first shift be able to answer: Do we give advice or only listen? Do we signpost?
Are calls confidential, and what are the exceptions? Can we call emergency services, and
when? Is there a time limit? Is there always a supervisor on shift, and how do I reach them
mid-call? Listening services can be powerful *because* they don't fix — resist the urge to.

## 2. A typical call shape
1. **Open:** service greeting; let the caller start.
2. **Explore:** open questions ("What's been happening?"), reflections ("It sounds like
   you're exhausted"), summaries. Allow silence.
3. **Focus:** what feels most important to them right now?
4. **Close:** summarise; check how they feel now; signpost if your model allows; let them
   know they can call again.
Avoid: "I know how you feel", judging, problem-solving the caller didn't ask for, sharing
your own story.

## 3. When there's risk
- **Ask directly about suicide** if there's any hint — your service's training will give
  wording. Asking does not increase risk.
- Follow the protocol exactly: typically, assess immediacy (thoughts, plan, means, timing,
  already acted?), alert your supervisor, and — where your service does this — escalate to
  emergency services for imminent risk or an attempt in progress.
- Children or adults at risk of abuse: follow the safeguarding steps your service trains
  you in. Some services are fully confidential except in defined cases; know which.
- Write what was said and what you did, immediately after the call.

## 4. Difficult calls
- **Silent callers:** stay present, speak gently at intervals ("I'm still here; take your
  time"), follow your service's time guide before closing kindly.
- **Abusive or sexual calls:** you don't have to tolerate abuse. Use the scripted boundary
  statement and end the call per policy; tell your supervisor.
- **Repeat/frequent callers:** many services have care plans for them (call length, focus);
  use them consistently across volunteers.
- **Calls about a third party** ("my friend is suicidal"): support the caller and follow the
  protocol for third-party risk.
- **Hoax or testing calls:** treat seriously unless clearly not; it's often a caller checking you're safe.

## 5. Look after yourself
- **Debrief after every shift**, and immediately after a hard call — with the shift
  supervisor or debrief line.
- Don't take calls home: no searching for callers, no contact outside the service.
- Notice signs that the work is getting to you (intrusive thoughts, dread, numbness) and
  use the service's support. Taking a break is responsible, not weak.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state your service's model and confidentiality exceptions.
- [ ] You know how to reach your supervisor mid-call.
- [ ] You can ask about suicide in your service's words and know the escalation steps.
- [ ] You've practised the boundary script for abusive calls.
- [ ] You debrief after every shift.
