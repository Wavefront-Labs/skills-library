# Boundaries, confidentiality and handing over to staff

**Goal:** be the volunteer people can rely on — warm, dependable, discreet, and quick to
pass on what needs passing on. These basics apply to almost any care-related volunteering
(befriending, hospitals, helplines, home visiting, mentoring). Your organisation's own
policies and training take precedence.

## 1. Know your role, and its edges
- Read your **role description**. Know what you do, what you don't, and who you report to.
- The limits protect you and the people you support. Typical no's: personal care,
  medication, money handling, giving professional advice (medical, legal, financial),
  decisions on someone's behalf.
- When asked to go beyond it: "That's not something I can do, but I'll find out who can."
  Then actually tell your coordinator.

## 2. Reliability
Turn up when you said, for as long as you said. If you can't, tell the organisation as early
as possible. If your circumstances change, renegotiate your commitment rather than drift away.
For people who are isolated or in crisis, a no-show can hurt more than you'd think.

## 3. Boundaries in relationships
- **Friendly, not a friend** — within the frame of the scheme.
- No personal phone numbers, social media connections, home address sharing, or meeting
  outside arranged times unless the policy allows.
- Gifts: follow the gifts policy (usually only small tokens, declared).
- No romantic or financial relationships with people you support.
- Self-disclosure: brief, and only when it helps them.
- Watch for **dependency** or for you becoming over-involved ("I'm the only one who
  understands her"). Name it to your coordinator early.

## 4. Confidentiality
- What you learn stays inside the organisation, shared only on a **need-to-know** basis with
  the people who need it to help.
- No identifiable stories to friends or on social media — even "nice" ones.
- Keep notes and devices secure per policy; don't take records home unless permitted.
- **Confidentiality is not secrecy.** You can't promise to keep a secret if it involves
  someone being at risk. Say early on: "Most things stay between us, but if I'm worried
  you or someone else might be harmed, I'll need to tell [staff role]."

## 5. Safeguarding: the four Rs
- **Recognise:** signs of abuse or neglect (physical, emotional, sexual, financial, neglect,
  self-neglect, exploitation), or someone seriously unwell or at risk of suicide.
- **Respond:** stay calm, listen, don't ask leading questions, don't investigate, don't
  confront anyone, reassure them they've done the right thing.
- **Report:** to your designated safeguarding person/coordinator **the same day**; immediate
  danger → emergency services first, then the organisation.
- **Record:** what you saw/heard, in their words, with date and time; factual, no opinion.
- If you're worried about a staff member or volunteer's behaviour, report that too —
  your organisation should have a whistleblowing route.

## 6. Handing over well
Give staff: who, what you noticed or were told (exact words), when, what you've done, and
anything urgent. Then let staff take it — you're not expected to resolve it, and you
shouldn't try.

## 7. Look after yourself
Use supervision, debriefs and volunteer support. Saying no and taking breaks keeps you
volunteering longer.

## Verify
- [ ] You can describe your role's limits and who you report to.
- [ ] You've told the organisation of any change in availability promptly.
- [ ] You know your designated safeguarding contact and how to reach them today.
- [ ] You've never promised to keep a secret involving risk.
- [ ] Concerns are handed over the same day, with facts recorded.
