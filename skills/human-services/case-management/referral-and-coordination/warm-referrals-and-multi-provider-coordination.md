# Warm referrals and multi-provider coordination

**Goal:** the person actually reaches the service, the services don't contradict each other,
and someone — usually you — holds the single thread.

General professional reference; information-sharing law and agency protocols vary by
jurisdiction and override this.

## 1. Cold vs warm referral
- **Cold:** "Here's their number, give them a call." Drop-off is high, especially for people in
  crisis, with low literacy, anxiety or past bad experiences of services.
- **Warm:** you make contact *with* the client — a three-way call, a joint first appointment,
  or a phone call to the receiving worker while the client is with you — so they meet a
  named person and know what happens next.

Use warm by default for anyone with high need or low confidence. Cold is fine for a
confident client and a simple service.

## 2. A good referral contains
1. Client consent to refer and to share *specific* information (record what was agreed).
2. Why you are referring and what you are asking the service to do.
3. Relevant history, current risk, and what's already been tried — enough that the client
   doesn't have to retell everything.
4. Practical needs: interpreter, accessibility, childcare, safe contact method (vital in
   family-violence situations — never leave messages on a shared phone without checking).
5. Your contact details and when you'll follow up.

## 3. Track every referral
A simple tracker (in your case system or a sheet) per client:

| Service | Date sent | Contact name | Status | Next check | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Statuses: sent → acknowledged → appointment booked → attended → engaged / declined / closed.
**Follow up within a set time (e.g. 5 working days)** if not acknowledged. Unanswered
referrals are the most common way clients fall through gaps.

## 4. Coordinating several providers
- **Agree a lead worker.** One person coordinates, holds the plan and is the client's first call.
  Without one, everyone assumes someone else is following up.
- **Case conference** when 3+ services are active or plans conflict: client invited (and
  ideally present for at least part), agenda, shared actions, minutes circulated with consent.
- Resolve contradictions openly — e.g. one service requiring daily attendance while another
  requires job-search hours — and put the agreed compromise in the plan.
- Keep a shared contact list for the care team, with consent.

## 5. Information sharing
- Share the minimum needed for the purpose.
- Consent is the default basis. Most jurisdictions allow or require sharing *without* consent
  where there is serious risk to someone's safety or a child-protection concern — know your
  agency's rules, consult your supervisor, and document the reasoning.

## Gotchas
- **Referring is not handing off.** You remain responsible until the receiving service has
  actually picked up and the client is engaged.
- Waitlists: check capacity before referring; tell the client honestly how long it may be
  and what to do meanwhile.
- **Referral fatigue:** five new services in a week overwhelms. Sequence them.
- Services' names for themselves change; keep your directory current.
- Coordination meetings without the client become about the client, not for them.

## Verify
- [ ] Consent to refer and to share specific information recorded.
- [ ] High-need clients got a warm handover with a named contact.
- [ ] Every referral in the tracker with a follow-up date; none older than your threshold unchecked.
- [ ] Lead worker named and known to the client and all services.
- [ ] Conflicting requirements between services surfaced and resolved in the plan.
