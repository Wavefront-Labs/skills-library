# Supporting patients, families and staff through dying and death

**Goal:** be a steady, compassionate presence in hospitals and hospices for patients
facing serious illness, families around the dying, and staff carrying it all. This covers
the emotional and spiritual support side; prayers, last rites, commendation and other
rituals sit in `religion-philosophy.chaplaincy-and-pastoral-ministry` skills (e.g. the
end-of-life ministry skill). Chaplains don't give medical information — redirect
clinical questions to the team. *Policies differ by hospital; follow yours.*

## 1. Working the wards
- **Prioritise:** referrals from staff, patients/families who asked, end-of-life and
  ICU patients, long stays, those with no visitors, recently bad news.
- **Be part of the team:** attend handovers or MDT meetings if invited; let the nurse in
  charge know you're on the ward; feed back (with consent) things that affect care, e.g.
  fears about death, religious needs around food or treatment.
- Visit skills: `human-services.pastoral-and-chaplaincy-support.pastoral-visiting.arriving-being-present-and-leaving-a-pastoral-visit`.

## 2. After a hard diagnosis or bad news
Chaplains are often called *after* doctors have broken news.
- Don't repeat or reinterpret the medical information. Ask: "What have you been told?
  What's it like hearing that?"
- Expect shock, numbness, anger, practical questions. Allow all of it.
- Help them think about who to tell and how, and what support they have tonight.
- If they misunderstood something important, encourage them to ask the team again; offer to be present.

## 3. Families at end of life
- **Orient:** introduce yourself to the family group; learn names and relationships;
  notice who's struggling or excluded.
- **Name what's happening** gently, in line with what the team has said: "It sounds like
  the doctors think he may be dying."
- **Help them be present:** encourage talking to the patient (hearing may persist), touch,
  music, saying what needs saying — "thank you", "I love you", "I forgive you",
  "forgive me", "goodbye".
- **Cultural and religious needs:** ask early about practices around dying and after
  death (who should be present, washing, prayers, body handling, timing of burial). Tell
  the team so they can be planned for.
- **Long vigils:** suggest families take turns resting and eating; reassure that death
  sometimes happens when they step out — that's not failure.
- **Family conflict** about treatment or presence: listen to each side; help them hear
  each other; involve the clinical team for treatment decisions.

## 4. Immediately after a death
- Be with the family; allow time; don't rush to fill silence.
- Offer to accompany them to see the person, explain what they'll see if needed.
- Practical gentle guidance: what happens next (death certificate, mortuary/funeral
  director) as the hospital's bereavement office explains it.
- Perinatal loss (stillbirth, neonatal death): memory-making (photos, hand/footprints,
  naming, blessing), following local policy and parents' wishes.
- Point to bereavement support (see
  `human-services.bereavement-support-and-death-doula-work` skills) and offer follow-up if policy allows.

## 5. Supporting staff
- Be visible and available after deaths, especially sudden, child or traumatic ones.
- Offer brief check-ins ("That was a hard shift — how are you doing?"), not forced debriefs.
- Facilitate or join structured reflective spaces (e.g. Schwartz Rounds, team reflections).
- Recognise **moral distress** (staff forced to act against their values) and burnout; signpost to occupational health or employee assistance.
- Confidentiality applies to staff conversations too.

## Gotchas
- Offering religious explanations for suffering ("God's will") — rarely comforting, often wounding.
- Siding in family disputes.
- Your own grief accumulating; use supervision and rituals of your own.
- On-call: keep a quick-reference list of faith contacts, interpreters, bereavement office hours.

## Verify
- [ ] Ward visits prioritised and team informed; relevant needs fed back with consent.
- [ ] No medical information given or reinterpreted.
- [ ] Families' cultural and religious end-of-life needs asked about and communicated.
- [ ] Family accompanied after death and pointed to next steps and bereavement support.
- [ ] Staff offered support after difficult deaths; chaplain used own supervision.
