# Six-way cane, rush and Danish cord seats on new frames

**Goal:** a woven seat in a newly made frame that's tight, square and comfortable,
with the frame designed for it from the start. (Restoring an existing caned or rushed
seat is covered in
`repair-maintenance.furniture-restoration-upholstery.caning-rush-seating.hand-caning-pressed-cane-and-rush-seats`.)

## 1. Design the frame for the seat
- **Hand cane:** seat rails drilled with holes, typically ~8–10 mm apart
  centre-to-centre, about 10–12 mm in from the inside edge; hole size and spacing set
  the cane size. Chamfer hole edges lightly so cane doesn't cut. Round over the inner
  rail edges.
- **Rush / cord:** rails round or rounded-rectangular with no sharp edges; front rail
  often longer than back (trapezoid seat) — that's why rush needs corner fills.
- **Danish cord:** **L-shaped nails** on the inside of the side rails (spaced to the
  pattern), cord wrapped front/back rails.
- **Strong joints:** seat weaving pulls rails inward with real force; glue and pin.

## 2. Six-way standard hand caning
Strand cane sized to holes (e.g. "fine", "medium" — check a supplier's hole-size
chart). Soak a few minutes; keep the **glossy side up** always.
1. **Step 1:** verticals front-to-back, peg the first hole, take the cane down a hole
   and up the next. Not too tight — later steps tighten it.
2. **Step 2:** horizontals side-to-side, on top.
3. **Step 3:** second verticals, on top, slightly offset from step 1.
4. **Step 4:** second horizontals, **woven** over-under through steps 1 and 3,
   below step 2. Straighten pairs as you go.
5. **Step 5:** first diagonal, woven under verticals and over horizontals (or the
   reverse — be consistent), from back corner to front.
6. **Step 6:** second diagonal at the opposite angle, opposite weave.
7. **Binding:** a wider binding cane laid over the holes, couched down through each
   (or alternate) hole with a strand.
Tie off ends on the underside by looping under adjacent loops; peg holes as needed.
Damp cane shrinks as it dries — the seat tightens.

## 3. Rush (natural or fibre/paper)
- **Fill the corners** first on a trapezoid seat: work extra turns around the front
  corners only, until the remaining opening is rectangular.
- Then wrap continuously around all four corners in sequence: over the rail, up through
  the centre, over the adjacent rail at right angles. Turns square on the corners.
- **Pad** the seat pockets with cardboard or rush offcuts as you go (after the first
  few cm) to give a firm, domed seat.
- If the seat is rectangular (not square), the short sides fill first; finish the
  remaining strip by **figure-eighting** across the long rails.
- Paper rush is uniform and easiest; natural rush twisted from damp leaves as you go.

## 4. Danish cord
- Paper-based **Danish cord** wraps the front and back rails in groups (e.g. 4–5
  wraps), each group led around the L-nails on the side rails; then the side-to-side
  weave over-under in a pattern.
- Pull cord firmly and evenly; it doesn't shrink much like cane.
- Hide ends with tacks under the rails.

## Gotchas
- Cane **glossy side down** = a dull, weak seat. Check every pass.
- Over-tight first steps make step 4 impossible to weave.
- Rush seats sag if under-padded.
- Danish cord can loosen if wraps aren't packed; push each group tight.

## Verify
- [ ] Frame drilled/ nailed for the method; joints strong.
- [ ] Cane steps in correct order and alignment; diagonals straight; binding neat.
- [ ] Rush corners square; seat padded and firm.
- [ ] Danish cord groups even; pattern symmetrical.
- [ ] Seat tight when dry; no loose strands underneath.
