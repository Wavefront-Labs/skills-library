# Stranded colourwork, floats and steeking

**Goal:** flat, even colourwork fabric with no puckers — and the nerve (and method)
to cut a steek in a finished sweater without it unravelling.

## 1. Choose the technique
- **Stranded (Fair Isle / Nordic):** two colours per round, the unused one carried
  behind as a **float**. Best in the round. Fabric is thicker, warmer, less stretchy.
- **Intarsia:** separate bobbins for blocks of colour, twisted at colour changes;
  worked flat. For large isolated motifs where floats would be too long.
- **Mosaic / slip-stitch:** one colour per row, slipped stitches carry the other;
  easiest way to get colour patterns.
- **Double knitting:** two-layer reversible fabric; both sides patterned.

## 2. Floats
- **Float length:** catch (trap) floats longer than about **5 stitches** or ~2.5 cm,
  shorter for garments that fingers catch on (mitten insides). Stagger catches so
  they don't line up and show through.
- **Spread** the stitches on the right needle to their full width before working the
  next colour — this sets float length. Floats too tight = **puckering**, the #1
  stranded fault.
- Knit colourwork **inside out** (work on the far side of the circle) if your floats
  are chronically tight — the floats travel around the larger outer circumference.
- Go up a needle size for colourwork sections vs plain stockinette; stranded gauge
  is usually tighter. Swatch in the round.

## 3. Yarn dominance
The colour carried **below** (from the left in two-handed knitting, or held lower)
makes slightly larger stitches and stands out. Choose a **pattern colour** as dominant
and keep it in the same position the whole project; switching hands mid-yoke
changes the look.

## 4. Reading charts
Read right to left on every round (in the round). One square = one stitch. Mark
your round with a sticky note **above** the current row so you can see the rows
already worked align.

## 5. Steeking
A steek is a bridge of extra stitches (usually 5–9, often alternating colours as a
checkerboard) knitted in the round where you'll later cut — cardigan fronts,
armholes.
1. **Fibre:** grippy, slightly hairy wool (traditional Shetland) holds a cut edge on
   its own. Superwash, cotton, alpaca and slippery yarns **must** be reinforced, and
   even then many knitters avoid steeking them.
2. **Reinforce:** crochet reinforcement (single crochet joining half of the centre
   stitch with half of its neighbour, on both sides of the centre line), or two lines
   of small machine stitching each side of the cut line.
3. **Cut** straight up the centre between reinforcements with sharp scissors.
4. Pick up the button band or sleeve from the edge, then fold the steek to the
   inside and tack or cover it with a band facing.

## Gotchas
- **Long floats on the inside of mittens and sleeves** catch fingers — trap them.
- Colour value matters more than hue: check contrast with a phone camera's
  greyscale filter before starting. Two colours of equal value vanish together.
- **Jog** at the start of rounds: unavoidable in stripes; hide it under the arm or
  use jogless techniques.
- Intarsia holes at colour changes = you forgot to twist the yarns.

## Verify
- [ ] Swatch in the round, washed, gauge matched.
- [ ] Fabric lies flat; no puckers when stretched gently.
- [ ] Floats caught at planned intervals and not visible from the right side.
- [ ] Dominant colour consistent throughout.
- [ ] Steek reinforced on both sides before cutting; cut edge not running.
