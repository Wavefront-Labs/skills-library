# Ceramic-shell lost-wax sequence

**Goal:** a sound shell mould around a wax positive that survives dewaxing and
receives molten bronze without cracks, misruns or gas defects. Craft-foundry
practice summarised; slurry recipes, coat counts and drying times come from your
shell-material supplier and vary — treat numbers here as orientation.

## 1. Wax preparation
- Pull a hollow wax from the rubber mould (slush or brush coats to an even wall,
  typically a few millimetres for sculpture-scale bronze).
- **Chase** the wax: remove mould seams, fix bubbles, re-sharpen detail. Every flaw
  in the wax becomes a flaw in metal, and chasing wax is far easier than chasing bronze.

## 2. Spruing and gating
- Attach a **pouring cup** and wax rods (**sprues/gates**) so metal enters the thickest
  section and flows **downhill**, without turbulence, filling the piece bottom-up or
  evenly.
- Add **vents** from high points and pockets where air would trap.
- Rule of thumb: gates thicker than the section they feed, so the piece solidifies
  before the gate (directional solidification) — otherwise shrinkage pulls metal out of
  the piece and leaves porosity.

## 3. Building the shell
- Degrease and dry the wax. Dip in a **primary slurry** (colloidal silica binder with
  fine refractory flour, often with a wetting agent), drain, then sprinkle fine
  **stucco** sand (fused silica or zircon).
- Dry each coat fully (hours, controlled humidity and air movement) before the next.
- Back-up coats: coarser stucco. A typical sculpture shell is several to around ten
  coats; thicker for larger pours. Seal coat last (slurry only).
- Reinforce sharp edges and thin projections; they're where shells crack.

## 4. Dewaxing
- **Flash fire:** shell inverted into a hot kiln (often around 700-900 °C) so the outer
  wax melts out fast before the bulk expands and cracks the shell.
- **Steam autoclave:** the industrial standard, gentler on shells.
- Inspect and patch small cracks with slurry and fibre. Store dewaxed shells dry.

## 5. Firing and pouring
- Fire the shell hot (commonly ~900-1000 °C) to burn out residual wax and sinter it.
  Pour into **hot shells**; cold shells cause misruns and cold shuts.
- Support shells in a sand bed for the pour. See
  `handcrafts.bronze-casting.foundry-melt-pour.melting-and-pouring-bronze` for melt
  and pour.
- Knock out shell once cool enough, cut off gates and fettle
  (`handcrafts.bronze-casting.chasing-fettling.cutting-gates-and-chasing-a-casting`).

## Alternative: solid investment
Plaster-silica investment poured in a flask is the older studio method, slower to
burn out (often a multi-day cycle) and heavier, but needs no slurry tank. Jewellery
casting uses the same principle at small scale with a vacuum or centrifugal caster.

## Gotchas
- Dust: fused silica and investments are respirable crystalline silica hazards in
  some forms — use a respirator and wet methods.
- Shell coats dried too fast crack; too humid and they never cure between coats.
- Trapped air pockets from missing vents show as rounded voids on the casting.

## Verify
- [ ] Wax chased clean; walls even.
- [ ] Gating feeds thickest section, flows downhill, vents at high points.
- [ ] Each coat fully dry; shell thickness appropriate and edges reinforced.
- [ ] Shell intact after dewax and firing; poured while hot.
