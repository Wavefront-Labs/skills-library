# Compounding, macerating and bottling

**Goal:** go from a finished formula to clear, stable, labelled bottles that smell
the same every batch. Draft: craft-scale practice; regulatory requirements depend
on your market.

## 1. Compound the concentrate
- Weigh each material by the formula onto a tared beaker on a scale reading 0.01 g
  (0.001 g for small trials), in the formula's order. Many perfumers add solids
  and bases first, then heart, then tops, to limit evaporation of volatiles.
- Tick each line as you add it. Record actual weights (you'll overshoot sometimes —
  log it; for small overshoots scale everything else up proportionally).
- Note **lot numbers** for every material in the batch record.

## 2. Dilute
Typical concentrate ranges (by weight in the finished product; houses vary):
- **Extrait / parfum:** ~15-30%
- **Eau de parfum:** ~10-20%
- **Eau de toilette:** ~5-15%
- **Eau de cologne:** ~2-5%
Use **perfumer's alcohol** (ethanol, denatured appropriately for cosmetics where
required). A small proportion of water (a few %) is common in EDTs for a softer
opening, but adds cloudiness risk. Oil-based perfumes use a stable carrier
(jojoba, fractionated coconut) — no maceration chemistry, different feel.

## 3. Macerate (mature)
- Seal in a full glass bottle (minimal headspace) in the dark at stable room
  temperature for typically **2-6 weeks**; some leave longer. The blend rounds
  out, harsh alcohol edge softens, and some reactions (e.g. Schiff bases between
  aldehydes and certain amines) develop colour and smell. Smell weekly.
- Agitate occasionally.

## 4. Chill and filter
- Chill the macerated perfume (fridge or freezer, often a day or more) so waxes
  and insolubles precipitate; then filter cold through filter paper (lab grade, or
  coffee filters for a first pass). This prevents cloudiness when customers keep
  bottles cool.
- Don't use paper towels — they shed fibres.

## 5. Bottle
- Clean, dry glass bottles; fill with a funnel or pipette leaving room for the
  dip tube. Crimp sprayers need a crimping tool; screw sprayers are simpler.
- Test spray each bottle once into a blotter.

## 6. Label and record
- Label: product name, concentration type, volume, ingredients (INCI, e.g.
  "Alcohol Denat., Parfum (Fragrance), Aqua", plus required allergens), batch/lot,
  and producer details as your market requires. Selling cosmetics usually needs a
  safety assessment and notification (e.g. EU/UK responsible person, CPNP/SCPN) —
  check, I can't confirm every jurisdiction.
- Keep a retain sample of every batch.

## Gotchas
- Cloudiness after bottling: water too high, insufficient chill-filtering, or
  incompatible materials.
- Colour change over weeks is often normal (vanillin, Schiff bases) but discolours
  clear bottles and fabrics.
- Spray flammability: keep away from heat during filling.

## Verify
- [ ] Batch record with actual weights and lot numbers.
- [ ] Concentration matches the product type on the label.
- [ ] Macerated and chill-filtered; bottle stays clear when cold.
- [ ] Label includes INCI list and required allergens; retain sample kept.
