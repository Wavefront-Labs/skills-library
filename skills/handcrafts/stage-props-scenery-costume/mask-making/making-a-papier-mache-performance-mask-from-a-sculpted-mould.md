# Making a papier-mâché performance mask from a sculpted mould

**Goal:** a light, strong mask fitted to one actor's face, sculpted to the designer's
character, with sightlines, breathing and voice that work for a full performance.

## 1. Face cast (the base)
- Take a **plaster bandage face cast** of the actor: hair back under a cap, skin
  greased with petroleum jelly (brows and lashes especially), nostrils left clear.
  Never cover the nostrils; talk the actor through each step; stop if they're
  distressed. Remove after 5–10 min as it warms and sets.
- Fill the cast with plaster to make a **positive** face. This is the fit reference.

## 2. Sculpt
- Model the character in **water-based clay** directly on the plaster face.
  Keep the clay at least 3–5 mm over the face everywhere so the mask stands off the
  skin.
- Exaggerate: masks read at distance, so planes are bolder, brows and cheekbones
  sharper than real faces.
- Mark the actor's **pupil positions** on the sculpt; the eye holes must align with
  them, not with the character's sculpted eyes if they differ.
- For a half mask (e.g. commedia), stop at the upper lip so the actor can speak.

## 3. Mould
- Option A (fast): laminate directly over the clay with a cling-film separator.
  Simpler, but you lose the sculpt if it's damaged.
- Option B (repeatable): cast a **plaster negative** over the clay sculpt
  (1–2 cm thick, reinforced with scrim), remove the clay, seal the mould with
  shellac and grease lightly with petroleum jelly.

## 4. Laminate the papier-mâché
- Paper: torn (not cut) **kraft/brown paper** pieces 2–4 cm, plus thinner tissue
  for detail. Alternate colours per layer to see coverage.
- Paste: **wheat paste** or methyl cellulose with a little PVA for strength.
- Lay **6–10 layers** inside the negative (or over the positive), pressing into all
  detail, overlapping by half each time. Dry between every 2–3 layers to avoid
  mildew and shrinkage.
- Final layers of finer paper or tissue give a smooth surface.
- Dry fully (24–48 h), release, trim edges with scissors, bind edges with a strip of
  paper turned over.

## 5. Fit
- **Eyes**: cut holes at the pupil marks, larger than you think (at least 15–20 mm
  across); check peripheral vision with the actor standing and turning. Bevel the
  inside edges so the hole is thin where it meets the eye.
- **Nostrils and mouth**: open nostril holes under the nose; ventilation slots
  hidden in the shapes where possible.
- **Padding**: craft foam or felt at brow, cheekbones and nose bridge to set the
  stand-off and stop slipping.
- **Strap**: elastic (12–20 mm) or ties through reinforced holes at temple height;
  a second strap over the crown for heavy masks.

## 6. Finish
- Seal with gesso or acrylic primer, sand, then paint in acrylics; matt varnish so
  it doesn't flare under light.
- **Leather masks** (traditional commedia) are wet-moulded on a carved wooden form;
  **thermoplastic** (Worbla, or Wonderflex) masks are heat-formed over the positive.

## Gotchas
- **Actor can't see**: eye holes cut on the sculpt's eyes, not the pupil marks.
- **Mask fogs or stifles**: no ventilation; add slots, hollow the cheeks away.
- **Warps or goes soft**: too few layers or not dried between layers.
- **Rubs**: no padding at pressure points.
- **Voice muffled**: full mask over the mouth; consider a half mask.

## Safety
Plaster bandage warms as it sets; never cover nose or mouth; keep the actor
upright and have a helper. Do a skin-patch check for the release agent.

## Verify
- [ ] Face cast taken with nostrils clear and the actor comfortable throughout.
- [ ] Eye holes centred on the actor's pupils; peripheral vision checked moving.
- [ ] Actor can breathe and be heard after 15 minutes in the mask.
- [ ] Mask doesn't slip during movement; no pressure points.
- [ ] Finish matt and holds up under handling.
