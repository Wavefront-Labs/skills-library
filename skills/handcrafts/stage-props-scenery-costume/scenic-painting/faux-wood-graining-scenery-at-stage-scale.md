# Faux wood graining scenery at stage scale

**Goal:** flats, doors, panelling or a deck that read as real wood from the seats,
painted fast enough for a production schedule and matched to the designer's
paint elevation.

## 1. Read the elevation
- The designer's **paint elevation** (usually at 1:25 or ½" = 1') shows species,
  colour and board layout. Note board widths, direction of grain and where light
  hits in the design.
- Make a **sample board** about 60 × 60 cm and get it approved under show-like
  light before painting the set.

## 2. Paints and tools
- **Scenic paint** (e.g. concentrated scenic colours thinned with water and binder)
  or interior latex/acrylic house paint for the base, with acrylic glaze medium
  for transparent layers.
- Large brushes (10–15 cm), a **lining stick** (a straightedge on a handle) with a
  liner brush, graining combs or a rubber rocker, a **flogger** or dry chip brush,
  pump sprayer, sea sponges.

## 3. Method (a generic oak or pine)
1. **Prime** with a size or primer suited to the surface (muslin flats need a size
   coat; hard flats need a primer).
2. **Base coat** the lightest wood colour, usually a warm yellow-ochre/light tan.
   Solid, even, dry.
3. **Lay out boards**: snap chalk lines for each plank at the scale on the
   elevation (real boards 10–20 cm wide). Paint each plank individually so the
   grain stops at the joints.
4. **Glaze and drag**: brush a mid-tone glaze (raw sienna + burnt umber thinned
   1:3 or so with glaze medium and water) over one plank, then drag a dry brush or
   comb along the length for straight grain. Vary pressure; real grain wanders.
5. **Heart grain**: with a liner brush or the corner of a dry brush, draw the
   cathedral arches (nested, elongated V or U shapes) on some planks — not every
   plank. Use a darker glaze. Soften with a dry brush.
6. **Overgrain**: a transparent darker glaze dragged across alternate planks or
   areas to vary tone, as real timber varies board to board.
7. **Board lines**: with the lining stick, paint a thin dark line on one side of
   each joint and a thin highlight on the other (consistent with the design's
   light direction).
8. **Spatter and glaze**: light spatter of dark and light to break up flatness,
   then an overall toning glaze to pull everything together.

## 4. Scale and distance
- Everything must be **bigger and more contrasted** than real wood: a grain line
  that looks right at 30 cm vanishes at 15 m.
- Stand back regularly — at least 6–10 m, ideally from the house — and check under
  stage or similar light. Saturated colours shift under coloured light; check gel
  colours with the lighting designer.

## 5. Floors
- Use a hard-wearing floor paint or seal with a water-based floor polyurethane,
  matt or satin. Anti-slip additive if dancers or wet effects are on it.

## Gotchas
- **Too uniform**: identical planks look like wallpaper; vary every third plank.
- **Glaze dries too fast**: add retarder, work one plank at a time, wet edge.
- **Muddy**: overworked glaze, or too many opaque layers. Keep glazes transparent.
- **Grain crosses board joints**: tape or mask joints when glazing.
- **Gloss flare**: shiny finishes catch light; keep matt unless designed.

## Verify
- [ ] Sample approved by the designer under representative light.
- [ ] Board widths and grain direction match the elevation.
- [ ] Every plank varies in tone and pattern; grain stops at joints.
- [ ] Reads as wood from the furthest seat.
- [ ] Floor treatments sealed, non-slip where needed.
