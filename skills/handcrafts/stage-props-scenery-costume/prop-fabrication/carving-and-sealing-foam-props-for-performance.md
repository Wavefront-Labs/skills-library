# Carving and sealing foam props for performance

**Goal:** a prop that reads right from the audience, survives eight shows a week
of handling, and can't hurt anyone if it's swung, dropped or fallen on.

## 1. Pick the foam
- **EVA foam** (floor-mat foam, 2–10 mm sheet, also blocks): tough, flexible,
  heat-formable. Best for hand props, weapons, armour bits.
- **Polyurethane upholstery foam**: soft; for squashy/stunt props (food, rocks
  thrown on stage).
- **XPS** (blue/pink insulation board): carves crisply, rigid, light; for set
  dressing and large static props, not anything hit or swung.
- **EPS** (bead polystyrene): crumbly; mainly for hot-wire shapes coated hard.

## 2. Shape
- Draw templates full-size, check scale against the actor and the set.
- Cut with a fresh **snap-off knife** blade — change blades often; dull blades tear
  EVA. Bevel cuts at 45 degrees for seams.
- **Rotary tool** with sanding drums for EVA detail; low speed avoids melting.
- **Hot-wire cutter** for XPS/EPS: ventilate — hot-wire fumes from polystyrene are
  irritating; wear a respirator rated for organic vapours.
- Laminate blocks with **contact cement** (both surfaces, let tack ~10–15 min,
  press once — no repositioning). Hot glue is quick but fails in heat and flex.

## 3. Core for handled and swung props
- Swords, staffs, clubs: run a **fibreglass rod** or **PVC/CPVC tube** core
  (8–12 mm) through the centre, stopping **at least 5 cm short of the tip**, with
  foam all round. Never a metal or wooden core in a combat-safe prop: it becomes
  the weapon.
- Check with the fight director: they set what is acceptable to hit with.

## 4. Seal
- **Heat seal** EVA with a heat gun briefly — closes the pores and sharpens edges.
- Then a **flexible sealer**: 3–5 coats of Plasti Dip spray, Flexbond, or
  watered PVA mixed with latex for flex. Rigid sealers (wood filler, plain PVA)
  crack on EVA as it flexes.
- XPS/EPS: coat with a hard skin — **Foam Coat**, acrylic-modified plaster or
  fibreglass/epoxy over a barrier, because solvent paints dissolve polystyrene.
  Test every paint on scrap first.

## 5. Paint and finish
- Acrylic paints over the sealer; add a flexible acrylic medium for props that
  bend.
- Dry-brush highlights and dark washes so details read at 10+ m; stage lighting
  washes out low-contrast work.
- Top coat matt or satin: gloss flares under lights unless it's meant to.
- Add weight where the actor needs realism (a "heavy" bag with a hidden sandbag),
  but keep it within what the actor can safely handle.

## Gotchas
- **Contact cement seams split**: applied too thick or closed before tacky.
- **Paint cracking**: rigid sealer on flexible foam.
- **Prop looks like foam on stage**: no texture or highlight; foam's pores show
  under light if unsealed.
- **Melting**: solvent spray paint on XPS/EPS.
- **Unsafe in combat**: hard core near the tip, or a stiff edge. Always get the
  fight director's sign-off.

## Safety
Contact cement and Plasti Dip are solvent-based: use outdoors or with extraction
and an organic-vapour respirator. Heat guns ignite foam if held still. Hot-wire
fumes need ventilation.

## Verify
- [ ] Foam type matches the prop's use (handled, thrown, static).
- [ ] Any swung prop has a flexible core well short of the tip, signed off by the
      fight director.
- [ ] Sealer flexes without cracking when the prop is bent.
- [ ] Paint reads from the back row under show lighting.
- [ ] Prop survives a full rehearsal of handling without damage.
