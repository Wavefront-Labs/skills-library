# Designing and cutting connected papercuts

**Goal:** a single sheet papercut where every part holds together, cut cleanly with
no torn corners, then mounted so it reads well.

## 1. Design for connectivity
The rule of single-sheet papercutting: **everything you keep must connect to everything
else** (a positive design) — usually to an outer border.
- Shade in pencil what will be **kept**. Any shaded "island" not touching other shaded
  areas will fall out. Fix islands with bridges (stems, lines, a branch, a ribbon).
- Letters: the counters of O, A, B, D, P, R, Q fall out unless joined to the letter
  (stencil style) or the letters sit on a baseline bar that links them all.
- Keep minimum line widths at about **1 mm** for paper around 120–160 gsm; thinner
  lines are possible but fragile and slow (personal-practice figure, adjust to paper).
- Symmetrical work (jianzhi, wycinanki, scherenschnitte hearts): fold the paper and cut
  both halves at once; scissors are traditional here. Check the fold side is the side
  that must stay connected.

## 2. Transfer the design — in reverse
- Draw or print the design **mirrored** on the back of the sheet; you cut from the back,
  so pencil lines and any burr stay hidden, and the front shows clean edges.
- Text especially: mirror it, or the finished piece reads backwards.

## 3. Tools
- **Scalpel or craft knife**: a Swann-Morton handle with 10A or 11 blades, or an X-Acto
  #11. Replace blades often — when the blade starts to drag or tear rather than slice,
  change it. Many cutters change after every few small pieces in fine work.
- **Self-healing mat**, plain backing paper, good light.
- Paper: smooth, dense, sized paper in 100–160 gsm (cartridge, Canson, Fabriano, coloured
  card stocks). Fibrous or very soft paper tears.
- Scissors (small, sharp, pointed) for folded/scissor traditions.

## 4. Cutting technique
1. Cut **from the inside out**: smallest, most detailed areas first while the sheet is
   strong; borders last.
2. Hold the knife like a pen, blade at a low, consistent angle; pull toward you. Rotate
   the paper, not your wrist, to follow curves.
3. Cut **into corners**, never through them: start each cut at a corner point and draw away,
   so the cuts meet at clean points with no overcut. Overcuts weaken joins.
4. Lift each piece out only when it's free; if it sticks, recut the corner — never tear.
5. Light, repeated passes beat one hard pass in thick card.
6. Brace with the non-cutting hand behind the blade's path, never in front of it.

## 5. Kirigami and layered work
- **Kirigami** combines cuts and folds for pop-up structures and 3D forms — see
  handcrafts.paper-crafts.paper-engineering.pop-up-mechanisms-and-v-folds.
- **Layered papercuts** stack several cut layers with foam spacers in a shadow box for
  depth; design each layer's connectivity separately.

## 6. Mounting
- Flip over so the clean side faces up. Mount on a contrasting background, floated with
  a few dots of PVA or with foam pads for shadow, in a box frame so glass doesn't press.
- Use acid-free materials and UV glazing if the work is coloured — paper fades.

## Gotchas
- Dull blades are the cause of almost all torn corners.
- Hand oils and pencil smudge show on dark paper — cut from the back and wear a cotton glove
  under the steadying hand if needed.
- Long thin bridges flex and distort on mounting; add extra ties in large open areas.
- Scalpels are extremely sharp: store capped, dispose of blades in a sharps tin.

## Verify
- [ ] Before cutting, no shaded island is unconnected.
- [ ] Mirrored design on the back; text reads correctly from the front.
- [ ] Corners are crisp with no overcuts or tears.
- [ ] The piece lifts off the mat in one sheet without parts falling out.
