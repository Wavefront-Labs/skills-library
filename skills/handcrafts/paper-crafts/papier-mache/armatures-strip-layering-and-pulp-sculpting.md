# Armatures, strip layering and pulp sculpting

**Goal:** a papier-mâché piece that's light, rigid, doesn't warp or go mouldy, and takes a
smooth painted finish.

## 1. Armature
The armature gives shape; the paper gives strength.
- **Card and masking tape**: cut, fold and tape cardboard into the form; crumpled newspaper
  taped tight for bulk. Fast, light, stays inside.
- **Wire** (aluminium armature wire, 2–4 mm, or chicken wire) for limbs, thin projections
  and large open forms. Wrap with masking tape so paste has something to grip.
- **Removable moulds**: a balloon, a bowl or a clay form covered first with cling film or
  petroleum jelly as a release, then strip-layered and removed once dry (cut the shell
  and rejoin with more strips if needed).
Make the armature slightly smaller than the finished piece to allow for layers.

## 2. Paste
- **Wheat/flour paste**: cook ~1 part flour to ~5 parts water, simmered until it thickens
  and goes translucent; cool. Cheap, strong, but feeds mould — add a little salt or a
  few drops of clove oil, use within days, and dry thoroughly. (Uncooked flour-water works
  but is weaker.)
- **Methyl cellulose / wallpaper paste**: clean, slow to spoil, flexible; some wallpaper
  pastes contain fungicide — check the label and wear gloves or avoid for children.
- **PVA diluted** roughly 1:1 with water: hard, water-resistant shell, more expensive.
  Often used for the last layers.

## 3. Strip layering
1. **Tear** (don't cut) newspaper or brown kraft paper into strips 2–3 cm wide — torn
   feathered edges blend and lie flatter. Smaller pieces for tight curves.
2. Dip or brush paste on; squeegee off the excess between two fingers. Too much paste =
   soggy, slow, warped, mouldy.
3. Lay strips overlapping, smoothing out bubbles, each layer in a **different direction**
   to the last (a woven, plywood-like strength).
4. **Alternate paper colours** per layer (newsprint, then plain or brown) so you can see
   where a layer is complete.
5. 2–3 layers per session, then **dry fully** before the next. Most pieces want 6–8
   layers; masks and props that get handled want more.
6. Dry in warm moving air, turning the piece; trapped moisture under thick layers rots.

## 4. Pulp clay for detail
- Soak shredded paper (egg cartons, toilet paper, newsprint) in hot water, blend, and
  squeeze out water. Mix with glue (PVA), a little joint compound or plaster/whiting, and
  sometimes linseed oil, into a clay. Recipes vary widely; test small batches.
- Apply thinly (≤ 5–10 mm at a time) over the dried strip shell, model features, and dry
  between applications. Thick pulp cracks and takes days to dry through.

## 5. Finishing
1. Sand lightly once bone dry.
2. Prime with **gesso** or acrylic primer, 2–3 thin coats, sanding between, for a smooth paintable surface.
3. Paint with acrylics.
4. Seal with acrylic varnish (matte or gloss) for handling and moisture resistance —
  papier-mâché is never waterproof; keep it indoors.

## Gotchas
- Mould spots mean damp: you added layers before the last one dried. Throw away
  mouldy flour paste; don't use it.
- Balloons shrink as temperature drops and as they leak — the shell can cave. Do the first
  layers in one sitting, then remove the balloon as soon as it's rigid.
- Wire armatures can rust through paste stains; use aluminium or tape well.
- Pinatas need fewer layers (they must break) — 3–4 is typical.

## Verify
- [ ] Each layer was fully dry (no cool, damp feel) before the next.
- [ ] Shell is rigid when pressed lightly; no soft spots.
- [ ] No mould spots or sour smell.
- [ ] Primed surface is smooth enough that strip edges don't show through paint.
- [ ] Finished piece is sealed with varnish.
