# Western sheet forming at the vat

**Goal:** consistent, even, flat handmade sheets of a chosen thickness from prepared
pulp — the Western (tamezuki, "still sheet") method. Notes on Japanese nagashizuki at the end.

## 1. Pulp
- **Cotton linters** (sold in sheets/half-stuff) are the easy start: tear into stamp-sized
  pieces, soak overnight, blend in a kitchen blender (lots of water, short bursts) or beat
  in a Hollander beater. **Abaca** gives crisp, translucent, strong paper and shrinks more.
- Beating time controls the paper: short beating = soft, opaque, absorbent; long beating =
  hard, dense, rattly, translucent, more shrinkage. Keep notes.
- Recycled paper pulp works but contains fillers, inks and short fibres: weaker, greyer sheets.
- Kitchen blenders cut fibres rather than fibrillate them — fine for craft sheets, but a
  beater makes stronger paper.

## 2. The vat and the mould
- Vat: a tub wider than the mould by a hand's width each side, filled with water and
  pulp. Pulp concentration sets sheet thickness — start at roughly a handful of wet pulp
  per several litres and adjust by test sheets (no universal number; uncertain).
- **Mould** = the screened frame; **deckle** = the open frame on top that contains the pulp
  and makes the deckle edge. Hold them together firmly, deckle on top.

## 3. Forming a sheet
1. Stir the vat (fibres settle within seconds; stir before *every* sheet).
2. Hold mould and deckle vertically at the far side, slide them in at an angle, rotate to
   horizontal under the surface, and **lift straight up level** in one steady motion.
3. As water drains, give a gentle **shake front-to-back and side-to-side** to interlock the
   fibres (the "shake" sets formation — even fibre distribution, seen by holding a sheet to light).
4. Tilt to drain off the corner, then lift off the deckle carefully, without dripping on the sheet.
- A bad sheet (thin spot, drip mark, bubble)? Kiss it back into the vat face-down and reform.

## 4. Couching
1. Have a pile ("post") of damp felts — wool felts, interfacing or cotton sheeting — on a board.
2. Stand the mould on one long edge on the felt, **roll it down** firmly in one motion
   onto the felt, press, and **roll it up** off the far edge. The sheet transfers.
3. Lay another damp felt on top, couch the next sheet. Keep the stack aligned.
- Air bubbles mean the felt was too dry or the roll too quick.

## 5. Press and dry
- Press the post — a bookbinding press, a hydraulic press or a car-jack press — gradually,
  squeezing out water. Firm pressing makes denser, smoother paper.
- Separate sheets on their felts or transfer onto boards/blotters.
- **Restraint drying** (between blotters under weight, or stuck to glass/board) keeps
  sheets flat; air-dried loose sheets cockle.

## 6. Sizing
Unsized paper is absorbent (waterleaf) — fine for printmaking, poor for ink and
watercolour. Size internally (add a commercial internal size such as AKD to the vat) or
externally by dipping dry sheets in warm dilute gelatin and drying again.

## Washi contrast (nagashizuki)
Japanese papermaking uses long kozo fibres with a **formation aid** (neri, traditionally from
tororo-aoi root) that slows drainage. The papermaker scoops repeatedly and sloshes the
pulp back and forth, building thin layers, then couches sheets directly onto each other
without felts. It's a different motion and needs different fibre — don't expect Western
technique to transfer.

## Verify
- [ ] Held to light, the sheet's fibre distribution is even with no thin spots.
- [ ] Deckle edges are present and clean; no drip marks.
- [ ] Sheets in a batch are similar thickness (weigh or calliper a few).
- [ ] Dry sheets are flat, not cockled.
- [ ] A test ink line doesn't feather if the sheet was meant to be sized.
