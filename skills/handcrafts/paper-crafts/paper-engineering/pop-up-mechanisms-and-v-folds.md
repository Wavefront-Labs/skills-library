# Pop-up mechanisms and V-folds

**Goal:** pop-up cards and spreads that rise reliably when opened and fold completely
flat when closed, without tearing or protruding past the page edges.

## 1. Two families
- **90° mechanisms** (the card opens to an L): parallel folds and box folds. Easy, strong;
  most cards. Pieces are usually cut from the base sheet itself (kirigami style) or glued on.
- **180° mechanisms** (the spread opens flat, like a book): V-folds, floating layers and
  most pop-up book spreads. The pieces stand up out of a flat page.

## 2. Parallel / box fold (90°)
- Two parallel cuts from the spine fold into the card, then fold the strip between them the
  **opposite way** (it pops forward). The rise = cut length; the depth = distance of the
  fold from the spine.
- Rule for fold-flat: the piece's fold lines must be **parallel to the spine**, and the
  front (height) and depth segments set by the cut must keep it within the card when closed.
- Steps: nest box folds on box folds; each one is anchored to the previous's surface.

## 3. V-fold (180°)
- A piece folded in half, its two glue tabs fixed to the two pages, **with its fold line on
  the spine (or a fold parallel to it)**, each tab angled the same amount away from the spine.
- **Symmetry rule**: both tabs must make equal angles to the gutter, or it won't fold flat.
- **Angle rule**: the angle at which the tabs are set to the gutter controls how far the
  piece leans toward or away from the reader and how tall it stands; narrower and wider
  angles lean it in opposite directions. Which way each goes depends on the tab geometry,
  so treat this as a variable to mock up rather than a formula (unconfirmed as a general rule).
- **Fold-flat check**: when closed, the piece must lie inside the page. If the tip
  overhangs the page edge, it'll stick out when closed — shorten it.

## 4. Floating layers and multi-level
- A **floating layer** is a flat panel held parallel to the page above it, supported by
  two parallel "legs" glued between page and panel, with fold lines parallel to the spine.
- Add V-folds on top of V-folds or on floating layers for layered scenes; each upper piece
  hinges on the one beneath.

## 5. Moving parts
- **Pull-tab slider**: a strip running between two layers through a slot, with a stop at
  each end so it can't pull out. Linked lever pivots (brads/paper rivets) can make arms move.
- **Rotating wheel/volvelle**: a disc on a central paper rivet behind a window cut in the page.
- Keep moving parts under a cover layer so they don't catch.

## 6. Build practice
1. **Mock up** every mechanism in cheap card first; geometry is found by test, not formula.
2. **Score** every fold with a bone folder or embossing stylus against a ruler before folding —
   cleaner, and paper doesn't crack.
3. **Grain**: fold parallel to the paper grain where possible; cross-grain folds crack in heavier card.
4. Card weight: around 200–300 gsm for bases and standing pieces; lighter for layers that bend.
5. Glue tabs with a thin, even layer of PVA or a double-sided tape; spray adhesive is messy.
6. Test the open–close cycle ten times; the weakest joint shows itself.

## Gotchas
- A piece glued over the spine across both pages is the most common fold-flat failure.
- Tabs glued even a few millimetres asymmetrically make the piece twist and crush.
- Glue squeezed onto the hinge fold locks it — keep glue on tabs only.
- The angle-lean relationship is a mental model to sketch with; confirm by mock-up.

## Verify
- [ ] Every fold line in a mechanism is parallel to the spine or its parent fold.
- [ ] The card closes completely flat with nothing protruding past the edges.
- [ ] Mechanisms rise fully at the intended opening angle (90° or 180°).
- [ ] Ten open–close cycles: no tearing, no sticking, no twisting.
