# Wood and soda firing: placement and wadding

**Goal:** use flame, ash and vapour to glaze and mark pots, and get them off the
shelves afterwards. In atmospheric firing the kiln, not the glaze bucket, does much
of the surface work — so **where** a pot sits and **what** it sits on decide the result.

## How the atmosphere works
- **Wood firing**: wood ash carried by the flame lands on pots and melts into a natural
  ash glaze at stoneware temperatures (commonly around cone 10–12 and beyond on long
  firings). Long firings (days in anagama-type kilns) deposit more ash.
- **Salt firing**: salt thrown into the firebox vaporises; sodium reacts with silica in
  the clay surface to form glaze (orange-peel texture). Releases hydrochloric acid
  fumes — a reason many studios moved to soda.
- **Soda firing**: sodium carbonate/bicarbonate solution sprayed or introduced as
  wet packets near peak temperature; similar effects, fewer corrosive fumes.

## Reading flame paths
- Flame and ash travel from firebox to flue. Pots facing the flame front get heavy
  ash or soda; pots in the lee get flashing (colour gradients) and lighter deposits.
- Tall pots shadow those behind them; leave channels so the flame can move through.
- Keep a loading diagram for every firing and photograph results against it — this is
  how a crew learns its kiln.

## Wadding
Wads are small balls or coils of refractory material that lift pots off shelves and
off each other so glaze and ash don't weld them down.
- Common recipe family: roughly equal parts **alumina hydrate** and **kaolin** by weight,
  sometimes with some fine sawdust or flour so wads break away more easily (recipes vary
  widely between kilns — test).
- Mix stiff, roll into balls or pinch from coils; dip the contact face in wax resist or
  coat in alumina so they release from the pot.
- Typically three or four wads per pot, evenly spaced, under the foot. Pots can be
  stacked on wads or placed on their side ("on-side" placement gives dramatic flashing
  marks; wads hold them in place).
- After firing, knock wads off with a gentle tap and grind the residue smooth.
- Shelves: heavy kiln wash or alumina; wood-firing shelves often need regrinding.

## Stoking and reduction (wood)
- Steady, small stokes keep the temperature climbing; big stokes cool the firebox and
  smother. Black smoke at the flue and back-pressure at spy holes = heavy reduction.
- Crews work in shifts; the firing logs temperature, stoke times and atmosphere.
- Cool **slowly** (close dampers and spy holes) for ash glazes to develop.

## Soda introduction
- Near peak (once the target cone is softening), introduce soda over several passes —
  spraying solution into ports or dropping packets onto embers. Typical totals are
  stated per cubic foot of kiln and vary by kiln; record yours.
- Watch **draw rings** — small clay rings pulled out with a steel rod through a spy
  hole — to see glaze build-up progressively.

## Safety
- Long firings: fatigue is a hazard; never fire alone. Gloves, eye protection with IR
  filtering when watching inside, natural-fibre clothing.
- Salt fumes are corrosive and toxic; soda is less so but still causes eye and skin
  irritation. Respiratory protection near the ports.
- Clearances, fire breaks and local regulations for open combustion.

## Gotchas
- Glazes that work in electric kilns can run in ash-heavy zones; test glazed pots in
  several positions.
- Wad recipes and soda quantities above are general patterns, not specific to your kiln.
