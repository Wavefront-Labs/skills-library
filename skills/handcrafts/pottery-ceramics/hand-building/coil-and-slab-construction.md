# Coil and slab construction

**Goal:** hand-built forms whose joins survive drying and firing. Nearly every failure
in hand-building is a join that opened or a piece that dried unevenly, so the method
below is mostly about those two things.

## Choose the clay
- A **grogged** body (contains fired, ground clay) is more forgiving: it shrinks less,
  dries more evenly and resists cracking in thick or large work. Smooth throwing
  bodies work but crack more readily at joins.
- All pieces of one pot should come from the same bag and be at the same moisture.
  Joining wet clay to drier clay is the classic cause of cracked seams: the two
  shrink different amounts.

## Joining: score, slip, press, compress
1. **Score** both surfaces with a fork, serrated rib or needle tool in crosshatch.
2. Apply **slip** (the same clay mixed to thick cream with water; some potters add a
   little vinegar — anecdotal, unconfirmed benefit).
3. Press the parts together firmly and wiggle slightly until slip squeezes out.
4. **Blend and compress** the seam — smear clay across the join with a wooden tool or
   thumb on both sides where reachable, then compress with a rib. A seam that is
   only stuck, not blended, is a crack waiting to happen.
5. Optionally add a thin coil into inside corners and blend it in for strength.

## Coil building
- Roll coils on a canvas or wooden surface, using the whole hand from the centre
  outward; roughly finger-thick for small pots. Keep coils under plastic.
- Lay each coil on the scored-and-slipped top of the one below. For walls that go
  outward, place the coil on the outer edge; for walls that come in, the inner edge.
- Blend each coil down into the one below on the inside, and up on the outside (or
  vice versa) — at least one face fully blended every row.
- After a few rows the wall softens; let it **firm up** before adding more (minutes
  with a heat gun or fan held at a distance, or longer under loose plastic).
- Scrape and paddle the walls to refine shape and compress.

## Slab building
- Roll slabs evenly, about 6–10 mm for functional ware, between guide sticks or with
  a slab roller. **Roll in both directions** (or turn the slab) — clay rolled one way
  only tends to warp along that axis in drying.
- **Soft-slab** work: bend and drape while plastic (around forms, into hump/slump moulds).
- **Stiff-slab** work: let slabs reach soft **leather-hard** so walls stand; cut with
  paper or card templates, bevel edges at 45° for box corners.
- Compress both faces of each slab with a rib before building.

## Drying (where hand-built work cracks)
- Dry **slowly and evenly**: loosely under plastic for the first day or several,
  uncovering progressively. Thin parts (handles, rims, attached elements) dry first —
  wrap them with a strip of plastic or wax them.
- Flip or rotate pieces so the base dries; set flat slabs on a ware board, ideally
  sandwiched between boards or drywall to prevent warping.
- Hollow out or pierce enclosed or thick sections: trapped air and thick walls can
  burst in the kiln. Many studios cap solid thickness around 2–2.5 cm for safe firing
  — a rule of thumb, slow bisque helps more.

## Gotchas
- Cracks at joins discovered at leather-hard can sometimes be repaired with slip
  made from the same clay plus a little vinegar, or paper-clay slip. Cracks found
  bone-dry rarely heal reliably.
- Recycled clay must be fully re-wedged; lumps of drier clay act like joins.

## Verify
- [ ] All pieces at the same moisture when joined.
- [ ] Every join scored, slipped, blended and compressed.
- [ ] Slabs rolled multi-directionally and compressed.
- [ ] No enclosed air; thick sections hollowed or pierced.
- [ ] Dried slowly under plastic with thin parts protected.
