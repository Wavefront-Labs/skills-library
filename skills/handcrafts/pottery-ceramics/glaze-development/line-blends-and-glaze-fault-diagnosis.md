# Line blends and glaze fault diagnosis

**Goal:** change one variable at a time, record everything, and read a fault back to
its cause. Glaze development is controlled experiment, not recipe collecting.

## Basic chemistry, enough to reason with
A glaze is three roles:
- **Glass former** — silica (from flint/quartz, also in feldspars and clays).
- **Flux** — lowers melting point (calcium from whiting, sodium/potassium from
  feldspars, magnesium from talc/dolomite, boron from frits, zinc, lithium...).
- **Stabiliser** — alumina (from clays and feldspars) stiffens the melt so it stays on
  the pot.
Colourants (iron, cobalt, copper, chrome, manganese, rutile...) are added on top,
typically 0.5–10%. **Unity molecular formula (UMF)** expresses a recipe in oxide ratios;
free calculators such as Glazy or desktop glaze software compute it. Silica:alumina
ratio broadly controls gloss vs matte.

## Test tiles
- Same clay body as the real work; vertical tiles (an L or upright) so you see
  running. Include a texture or incised line to show breaking over edges.
- Dip half the tile once, a third twice — glaze **thickness** matters as much as recipe.
- Mark each tile underneath with an iron oxide/underglaze code that matches your notebook.
- Fire in the same kiln, position and schedule as production; record cone packs.

## Line blend
To find how a variable behaves (e.g. 0–10% iron oxide, or the ratio between two glazes):
1. Mix glaze A and glaze B as wet slurries of known dry weight.
2. Combine by volume/weight in steps: 100/0, 90/10, 80/20 ... 0/100.
3. Tile each step. The tiles show where the transition happens; narrow the range
   and repeat at finer steps.
A **triaxial blend** extends this to three end-members on a triangle grid.

## Mixing
- Weigh dry ingredients to 0.1 g on small batches (100 g dry base, colourants as
  percent additions on top of 100).
- Add to water, slake, then sieve through 80-mesh (finer for some glazes).
- Wear a well-fitted respirator rated for fine dust when handling dry materials;
  several (silica, barium, manganese, some colourants) are hazardous.

## Fault diagnosis
| Fault | Looks like | Usual causes / first fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **Crazing** | Fine crack network in glaze | Glaze contracts more than body (high expansion). Reduce sodium/potassium, add silica or boron; fire to full maturity. Crazed functional ware leaks and harbours bacteria. |
| **Shivering** | Glaze flakes off rims and edges | Opposite: glaze under too much compression. Increase expansion (more sodium/potassium) or reduce silica. Can be dangerous (sharp flakes in food). |
| **Crawling** | Glaze pulls back into beads, bare patches | Dusty or greasy bisque, glaze applied too thick, high-shrinkage raw glaze (too much raw clay or zinc). Wipe bisque, calcine part of the clay. |
| **Pinholing / blistering** | Small holes or burst bubbles | Gases from body or glaze escaping late; underfired bisque, too-thick glaze, too-fast firing. Add a slow soak or controlled cooling at top temperature. |
| **Running** | Glaze pools at foot, sticks to shelf | Over-fluxed, over-fired or too thick. Add alumina/silica, keep glaze off the bottom ~1 cm, use cookies/waste slabs while testing. |
| **Dry/underfired** | Rough, sugary surface | Not enough flux for the cone, or kiln underfired — check cone packs first. |

## Gotchas
- One variable per test series; change two and you learn nothing.
- Kiln position changes results (hot and cool spots); always note shelf and height.
- Glaze-fit can only be confirmed with stress tests (freezer-to-boiling-water cycling,
  or the boiling-water / ice-water test on sample mugs); fit claims above are general
  chemistry, not guarantees for your body.
- Food safety of glazes containing copper, manganese, barium or lead cannot be judged
  by eye; leach testing by a lab is the confirmation.
