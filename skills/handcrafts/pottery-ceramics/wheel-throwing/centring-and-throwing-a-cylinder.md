# Centring and throwing a cylinder

**Goal:** a cylinder with an even wall (roughly 6–8 mm for a beginner, thinner with
practice), a floor about as thick as the wall, and no S-crack. Almost every thrown
form — bowl, mug, vase, jar — is a cylinder modified, so this is the thing to drill.

## 1. Prepare the clay
- **Wedge** 0.5–1 kg of clay (ram's-head or spiral wedging) for a few minutes to
  even out moisture and drive out air. Cut the lump with a wire to check for air
  pockets: a clean cross-section with no holes is what you want.
- Clay should be soft enough to dent easily with a thumb but not sticky. Stiff clay
  is exhausting to centre; slop-soft clay collapses.
- Pat it into a rounded cone and slap it hard onto the **centre** of a clean, *dry*
  wheel head (or bat), so it sticks.

## 2. Centre
- Wheel fast (for most wheels, well above half speed). Wet the clay, not a flood.
- Brace elbows on thighs or the splash pan; your arms should be locked into your body,
  not floating. The wheel does the work, your body is the jig.
- **Cone up:** squeeze inwards with both palms so the clay rises into a cone.
  **Cone down:** heel of one hand pushes down from the top while the other hand
  steadies the side. Two or three cones aligns the clay particles and evens it.
- Then hold steady pressure with the left hand on the side (for an anticlockwise
  wheel) and the right hand on top until the clay runs true under your hands with
  no wobble. Release pressure **slowly** — a sudden release knocks it off-centre.

## 3. Open
- Medium speed. Find the centre with your thumbs or fingers and press straight down,
  leaving a floor of about 6–10 mm. Check depth with a needle tool pushed in from
  the top; you'll learn to feel it.
- Pull the floor outward to the width you want, keeping the floor flat. Compress the
  floor with fingers or a rib: uncompressed floors are the usual source of
  **S-cracks** (an S-shaped crack in the base after drying or firing).

## 4. Pull the walls
- Slower speed (wheel slows as walls rise). Inside fingertip and outside knuckle or
  fingertip pinch the wall at the base, then rise together at a steady pace — about
  one turn of the wheel per finger-width of rise.
- Three or four pulls, each thinning and raising. Re-wet between pulls but **sponge
  out water pooling in the bottom** — standing water softens the floor and causes
  slumping and cracks.
- Keep the rim compressed with a fingertip or chamois after each pull; a thin, wavy
  rim tears.
- Keep the wall vertical or slightly in; a wall that flares out wants to collapse.

## 5. True and finish
- A metal or wooden **rib** run up the outside removes slurry and firms the wall.
- Trim an uneven rim with a needle tool held steady while the wheel turns, lift the
  ring off.
- Undercut the base with a wooden tool, then pull a taut **wire** through under the
  pot while the wheel turns slowly or is stopped. Lift with dry hands, or throw on a
  bat and lift the whole bat.

## Diagnosis
- **Off-centre (wobble you can feel):** you released too fast, or arms weren't braced.
- **Wall twisted/torn:** too much speed for the height, or too little water.
- **Thick base, thin top:** you're not starting the pull low enough — get into the
  corner at the floor.
- **Cut it in half** with a wire after throwing (practice pieces): the profile shows
  exactly where the wall is thick or thin. This is the fastest feedback there is.

## Gotchas
- Left-handed throwers often run the wheel clockwise and mirror hand positions.
- Typical wall-thickness figures are teaching rules of thumb, not standards; they
  vary with clay body and form.
- Clay dust (silica) is a lung hazard: wet-clean the studio; don't dry-sweep.

## Verify
- [ ] Clay runs true under a still hand at speed.
- [ ] Floor compressed, roughly the wall thickness, no water left standing in it.
- [ ] Sectioned practice cylinder shows even wall from base to rim.
- [ ] Rim compressed and level; pot cut off cleanly without distortion.
