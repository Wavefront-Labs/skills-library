# Arches, hanging installations and rigging mechanics

**Goal:** large floral structures that look effortless, stay fresh for the event, and
never fall on anyone. At this scale the florist is also doing light structural and
rigging work — know where your competence ends.

## 1. Site visit and approvals
- Visit the venue: ceiling height, access route and door widths, floor surfaces,
  existing **rated rigging points** or truss, power, water, lift access and build time
  allowed. Photograph and measure.
- Ask the venue for their rules: many forbid fixing to ceilings, open flames or
  loose petals; many require a **risk assessment and method statement (RAMS)** and
  public liability insurance.
- Anything overhead hangs only from points the venue or a qualified rigger confirms
  are rated for the load. Do not hang from light fittings, sprinkler pipes or decorative beams.

## 2. Estimate the weight
- Wet foliage and flowers are heavy. Weigh a sample: build 30 cm of the design on a
  bathroom scale and multiply up. Add frame, wire, water tubes and water.
- Rough figures for planning only: dense fresh-flower installations can run to many
  kilograms per metre. Use your own sample weight, not a rule of thumb (uncertain).
- Apply a safety margin; rigging hardware has a working load limit (WLL) marked —
  stay well inside it and use rated shackles/slings, not hardware-store hooks.

## 3. Mechanics
- **Arches / moongates**: a rented or welded steel frame, weighted base plates or
  sandbags (outdoors, wind is the main risk). Wrap sections with **chicken wire
  "sausages"** stuffed with damp moss or simply cable-tied to the frame, then build into
  them with foliage first, flowers last.
- **Hanging clouds**: a chicken-wire cage or mesh grid on a rigid frame, suspended from
  rated points via steel wire rope or rated chain; foliage and flowers inserted into the mesh.
- **Water**: stems in water tubes (vials) zip-tied in; or long-lived flowers that cope
  without water (chrysanthemum, gypsophila, dried/preserved stems). Fill tubes after
  inserting so they don't drip onto guests; check tube caps seal.
- Hide mechanics with foliage that tolerates being out of water (ruscus, eucalyptus,
  smilax, asparagus fern).

## 4. Build plan
- Pre-build modules in the studio where possible (sections of garland, cloud segments),
  then assemble on site. Number the modules and photograph the layout.
- Build heavy-to-light, bottom-up for arches, and **raise hanging frames before adding
  most of the flowers** only if lifting gear allows — otherwise build at a working height
  and lift with a rated hoist or truss motor operated by competent people.
- Work at height from proper access equipment (podium steps, scaffold tower, MEWP with
  trained operator), never from chairs or tables.
- Keep a clear exclusion zone under anything being lifted.

## 5. Strike
- Agree strike time; venues charge for overruns. Bring bins, sacks, trolleys and enough
  people. Compost green waste; remove all zip ties and wire.
- Strike is when people get hurt: tired crews, ladders and heavy wet material.

## Gotchas
- Outdoors, an arch is a sail: weight or anchor for wind, or don't build it.
- Petals and pollen on cream carpets and gowns cause venue damage claims.
- Fresh material wilts fastest in hot marquees and under lighting; choose sturdy varieties.
- If you aren't trained in rigging, contract a rigger. This skill doesn't make you one.

## Verify
- [ ] Every overhead point is confirmed as rated for your estimated load plus margin.
- [ ] Venue has your RAMS and insurance certificate; you have their written OK.
- [ ] Freestanding pieces don't move when pushed firmly at shoulder height.
- [ ] No drips: water tubes sealed and checked before guests arrive.
- [ ] Strike crew, tools and waste plan are booked for the agreed time.
