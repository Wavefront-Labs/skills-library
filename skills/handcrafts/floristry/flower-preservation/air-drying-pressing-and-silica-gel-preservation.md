# Air drying, pressing and silica-gel preservation

**Goal:** keep flowers for months or years with their shape or colour as intact as
possible, choosing the method that suits each flower and the finished piece.

## 1. Pick the method by flower and outcome
| Method | Keeps | Good for | Poor for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hang/air drying | 3D shape (some shrink) | statice, strawflower (helichrysum), lavender, gypsophila, grasses, hydrangea, roses in bud, nigella pods | fleshy flowers, lilies, tulips |
| Pressing | colour, flat form | pansies, violas, ferns, cosmos, larkspur, single-petalled flowers, leaves | thick-centred flowers (whole roses, dahlias) unless split |
| Silica gel | 3D shape **and** good colour | roses, peonies, ranunculus, dahlias, zinnias — wedding-bouquet keepsakes | very delicate petals that crush, large volumes (cost) |

Start with flowers at or just before peak and **dry to the touch** — surface water
causes browning and mould. Pick after the dew has gone.

## 2. Hang drying
1. Strip leaves (they shrivel messily), gather into small bunches of 5–10 stems.
2. Bind with a rubber band — stems shrink and a band keeps gripping; string loosens and
   bunches fall.
3. Hang **head down** in a dark, dry, airy place; warm is fine, damp is not. Darkness
   preserves colour.
4. Typical time 1–3 weeks; done when stems snap rather than bend.
- Hydrangea dries best "in water": leave late-season heads in a few cm of water until it
  evaporates and the heads papery-dry in place.

## 3. Pressing
1. Lay flowers face-down (or split thick ones in half vertically) between sheets of
   absorbent paper — blotting paper, newsprint without ink on the contact side, or
   the press's card sheets.
2. Press under even pressure: a screw-down press, or heavy books with paper buffers.
3. **Change the blotting paper after 2–3 days** for juicy flowers; this is what keeps
   colours bright and prevents mould.
4. Usually 1–3 weeks. Microwave presses work in minutes but scorch easily — short bursts.
5. Store finished pressings flat between paper in a box, away from humidity.

## 4. Silica gel
1. Use fine flower-drying silica gel (some brands have colour indicator crystals).
   Cut stems to ~2 cm.
2. Put a layer of gel in an airtight container, set the flowers face-up, and **gently
   spoon gel between and over every petal** until buried, keeping petals in shape.
3. Seal. Typical drying 2–7 days depending on flower size; check one early — over-drying
   makes petals brittle.
4. Pour gel off slowly, lift flowers out, brush grains away with a soft paintbrush.
5. Dry the gel out in a low oven to reuse it. Avoid inhaling the dust; it's an irritant.

## 5. Finishing and display
- **Framing pressed work**: arrange on acid-free card with tiny dots of PVA or archival
  glue, use a deep frame or spacer so glass doesn't crush, and **UV-filtering glazing**.
- Dried arrangements: a light hairspray or matte sealer reduces shedding but can darken
  some petals — test.
- Preserved (glycerine-treated, commercially "stabilised") flowers are a different
  industrial process; buy them in rather than attempting it at scale.

## Gotchas
- Direct sunlight fades almost every dried flower within months; tell customers.
- Humidity reverses drying: bathrooms and kitchens are bad homes for dried pieces.
- Reds often go dark/near-black when dried; whites may yellow. Set expectations.
- Keepsake bouquets should be started within a day or two of the event; browned petals don't recover.
- Times above are typical ranges and vary with humidity and flower thickness.

## Verify
- [ ] Each flower used a method from the right column of the table.
- [ ] Hang-dried stems snap cleanly; no soft or mouldy heads.
- [ ] Pressed flowers are flat, papery and free of brown spots.
- [ ] Silica-dried flowers have no grains left and are stored airtight or sealed.
- [ ] Finished pieces are behind UV glazing or out of direct sun.
