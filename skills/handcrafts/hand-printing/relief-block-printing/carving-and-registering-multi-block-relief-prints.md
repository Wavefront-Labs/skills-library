# Carving and registering multi-block relief prints

**Goal:** clean-edged blocks that print evenly by hand, and multi-colour prints where the
colours land exactly where intended — on paper or cloth.

## 1. Blocks and the mirror
- **Lino** (traditional hessian-backed is crisper; softer "easy-carve" vinyl is easier but
  crumbles on fine detail). Warm lino slightly to soften it.
- **Wood**: side-grain plywood (shina/cherry ply for mokuhanga) or solid hardwood for
  traditional textile blocks.
- The print is a **mirror** of the block. Transfer the design reversed: trace with soft
  pencil and rub face-down onto the block, or flip it digitally. Text especially.
- Darken the block surface with a thin ink wash so cuts show clearly.

## 2. Carving
- **V-gouge** for outlines and fine lines; small and large **U-gouges** to clear; a knife
  for crisp edges in wood. Keep them razor sharp (strop often).
- Cut **away from your body**, with the non-cutting hand **behind** the blade; use a bench
  hook so the block can't slip. Most injuries are slipping gouges into the steadying hand.
- Undercutting weakens lines; angle cuts so each printing edge has a sloped "shoulder".
- Clear wide areas deeper than narrow ones — shallow broad areas pick up ink and "chatter".
- Proof early with a rubbing (paper + soft graphite or crayon) or a test print.

## 3. Inking and printing by hand
1. Roll ink out on a glass slab with a **brayer** until it looks like fine velvet (hissing, not tacky clumps).
2. Roll onto the block in several directions, thin layers. Too much ink fills fine lines.
3. Lay paper, burnish with a **baren** or wooden spoon in small circles, firm pressure.
   Lift a corner to check before peeling off.
- Oil-based relief inks give rich, permanent prints; water-washable ones dry fast on the slab.
- Mokuhanga (Japanese) uses water-based pigment with rice paste brushed onto the block and
  printed on damp washi — a distinct technique.

## 4. Registration
- **Kento** (Japanese): an L-shaped corner notch and a straight side notch carved into each
  block at the same position; the paper is laid into them every time.
- **Pin registration**: tabs on the paper fit over pins (e.g. Ternes-Burton) fixed in a jig;
  the jig holds each block in a fixed corner.
- **Corner jig**: an L-shaped frame of board; block goes in the corner, paper aligned to marks.
- Make a **key block** first, print it onto acetate or onto the other blocks (offsetting
  wet ink) to transfer exact positions to each colour block.

## 5. Reduction printing
One block, cut away between colours: print the lightest colour for the whole edition (+ extras),
carve away what should stay that colour, print the next colour, and so on. You can't reprint
— decide edition size first and print 10–20% spares.

## 6. Fabric block printing
- Indian traditions (Bagru, Sanganer, ajrakh) use carved teak blocks, each colour its own
  block, with tiny pegs/pitch marks for alignment, on padded tables.
- Use **textile ink** or pigment with binder; print on pre-washed natural fabric pinned
  taut over a padded surface; press firmly with the heel of the hand or a mallet.
- **Heat-set** per the ink's instructions (often ironing on the reverse several minutes)
  before washing. Ajrakh's resist/mordant/dye processes are a separate natural-dye technique.

## Verify
- [ ] Text and asymmetrical elements read correctly in the print.
- [ ] Solid areas print evenly; fine lines aren't filled.
- [ ] Colours register within your tolerance across the edition.
- [ ] Reduction edition count and spares fixed before the first cut.
- [ ] Fabric prints heat-set and pass a test wash.
