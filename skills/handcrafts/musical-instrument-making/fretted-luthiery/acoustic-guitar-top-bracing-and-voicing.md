# Acoustic guitar top: bracing and voicing

**Goal:** a soundboard light and responsive enough to sound alive yet stiff enough to
resist string tension for decades without bellying. Voicing is judgement built over many
instruments; this is the method that lets you learn from each one.

## 1. Wood selection
- **Spruce** (Sitka: strong, robust; Engelmann/European/Adirondack: varying stiffness and
  weight) or **western red cedar** (lighter, warmer, softer — common on classicals).
- **Quartersawn** (growth rings near perpendicular to the face), with minimal **runout**
  (grain running out of the face; split a sliver or look for silking that follows the grain).
  Runout weakens the top and makes thicknessing unpredictable.
- Check **cross-grain stiffness** by flexing: quartersawn, high-stiffness tops can go thinner.

## 2. Joining and thicknessing
1. **Bookmatch** the two halves; joint the edges on a shooting board until they meet with
   no light visible; glue with a rope, wedge or caul jig on a flat board.
2. Thickness with a drum sander or plane and scraper. Typical finished steel-string tops
   land around 2.3–3.0 mm and classical tops around 1.8–2.6 mm — but **thickness by
   stiffness, not a number**: many makers deflection-test (a fixed weight on a top clamped as a
   beam) and target a stiffness value from their own records. Note numbers on every top.
3. Cut the **soundhole** and inlay the rosette before bracing.

## 3. Bracing patterns
- **X-bracing** (steel-string): two main braces crossing below the soundhole, tone bars
  below, a bridge plate (maple or rosewood) under the bridge area, upper transverse brace
  above the soundhole. Handles high string tension.
- **Fan bracing** (classical, Torres pattern and variants): several thin fan struts radiating
  below the soundhole, plus harmonic bars. Lower tension, lighter top.
- Brace stock: quartersawn spruce, split for straight grain, oriented with grain vertical
  to the top (on edge).

## 4. Gluing the braces
- Steel-string tops are usually given a slight **dome**: braces are sanded on a radius dish
  (e.g. 25–28 ft radius for the lower bout; varies by maker) and glued in the dish using a
  **go-bar deck** (flexible rods pressing each brace down).
- Hide glue (hot) or aliphatic resin (Titebond). Hide glue sets hard and is reversible.
- Clean squeeze-out before it sets; it adds mass and looks sloppy inside.

## 5. Carving and voicing
- **Shape** braces: taper ends, **scallop** (thin) sections to reduce stiffness where wanted;
  a parabolic profile is common for X-braces. Remove a little at a time.
- **Tap tuning**: hold the top at a node point, tap near the bridge area and listen. Over
  successive carving, the tap tone drops and becomes more open and sustained. Some makers
  use Chladni patterns (glitter on a top driven by a speaker) or a spectrum app to record
  mode frequencies. Write it down every time.
- Leave final adjustments conservative; removing more after the box is closed is hard.
  An under-braced top can belly behind the bridge or crack; over-braced sounds tight.

## Gotchas
- Humidity: brace and close boxes at moderate humidity (~45–50% RH is common shop
  practice). Bracing in dry conditions then moving to humid can dome excessively; the
  reverse cracks tops.
- Runout in braces makes them weak — always split, not saw, brace stock.
- The bridge plate size and grain direction matter for durability; don't skimp.
- Figures above are typical ranges from common practice; your own records outrank them.
