# Flue pipe voicing and tonal finishing

**Goal:** every pipe in a rank speaks promptly with the intended tone and loudness, and the
ranks together build a coherent chorus in the actual building. Voicing is done first on a
voicing machine in the workshop, then finished in the room.

## 1. Anatomy of a flue pipe
- **Foot** with a **toe hole** at the bottom (admits wind).
- **Languid**: the plate inside the mouth level; the narrow slit between the languid and the
  lower lip is the **flue**. Wind sheet exits the flue across the **mouth**.
- **Upper lip** (the edge the wind sheet strikes) and **lower lip**. The **cut-up** is the
  mouth's height (upper lip to lower lip), usually expressed as a fraction of mouth width.
- **Body** (resonator) length sets pitch; diameter (**scale**) sets character — narrow
  scales are stringy, wide scales flutey; principals in between.
- Tuning: open pipes by **slides**, cone tuning (coning top in/out), or ears/beards on some stops.

## 2. The voicing variables (and what they do)
- **Wind pressure** (in mm or inches water gauge, set by the reservoir weights/springs):
  the global starting point. Measure with a manometer.
- **Toe-hole size**: controls how much wind reaches the pipe → loudness. Opening = louder.
  The primary loudness control in many styles ("open-toe" voicing does it at the flue instead).
- **Flue width**: wider flue = more wind sheet, louder, fuller.
- **Cut-up**: higher cut-up = slower, fluty, darker; lower cut-up = brighter, quicker,
  more harmonic, but can overblow. Raise in tiny increments — you can't lower it.
- **Languid height** relative to the lower lip, and lip positions (upper lip in/out): steer
  the wind sheet onto the upper lip; affects speech promptness and stability.
- **Nicking**: small notches on the languid edge (and/or lower lip). Heavy nicking reduces
  the attack "chiff" and stabilises tone; little or no nicking gives the articulate chiff
  of historic styles. A stylistic choice, not a correction for bad work.

## 3. Workshop voicing
1. Set the voicing machine to the organ's wind pressure.
2. Voice a **reference pipe** per octave to the intended tone and loudness.
3. Voice the remaining pipes to blend smoothly between references: listen for even
   loudness and colour progression, prompt speech without chiff/spit beyond the style's taste.
4. Rough-tune each pipe; mark settings.

## 4. Tonal finishing in the room
The building's acoustics change everything; finishing is where the organ is made.
- Regulate each rank's loudness and colour note by note from the console with a helper at
  the keys, adjusting toes and flues at the pipe.
- Balance ranks against each other: the principal chorus (8', 4', 2', mixtures) should build
  evenly; flutes and strings blend with it.
- Watch for **wind stability**: notes that waver when many pipes sound may reveal reservoir
  or windway problems, not voicing faults.
- Final tuning to the chosen **temperament** (equal or a historical unequal temperament),
  at a stable temperature, as the room will be in use.

## 5. Reeds (brief)
Reed pipes (trumpet, oboe) are voiced by shaping the **tongue's curve** and adjusting the
**tuning wire** against the shallot; the resonator length shapes tone. A separate specialism.

## Gotchas
- Metal pipe lips and languids are soft (tin-lead): handle pipes by the body, never the mouth.
- Cut-up only goes up — over-cut pipes have to be replaced or re-lipped.
- Temperature changes pitch markedly; tune at service temperature, and reeds/flues drift
  apart with temperature.
- Practices differ hugely between historic and modern schools; numbers are omitted on purpose.

## Verify
- [ ] Wind pressure measured and matches the design.
- [ ] Each rank speaks promptly with an even loudness curve across the compass.
- [ ] Chorus builds smoothly with no individual pipe standing out.
- [ ] Tuned to the agreed temperament at service temperature.
