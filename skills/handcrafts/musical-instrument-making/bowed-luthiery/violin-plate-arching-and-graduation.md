# Violin plate arching and graduation

**Goal:** a belly (top) and back with a stable, musical arch and thicknesses tuned to the
wood — the core of a violin's sound. Methods follow the Cremonese tradition using an
inside mould; patterns and numbers below are orientation, not a model's specification.

## 1. Wood and outline
- **Belly**: quartersawn spruce, bookmatched, glued, centre joint tight. **Back**: quartersawn
  (often flamed) maple, one- or two-piece.
- Outline: trace the **ribs** (built on the inside mould) onto the plates and add the
  overhang (often around 2.5–3 mm on violin). Saw and file to the line; the edge is left
  thick (~4–4.5 mm) for now.

## 2. Outside arching
1. Carve the outside with gouges, then **finger (thumb) planes**, guided by **arching
   templates**: longitudinal and several transverse templates cut from the model.
2. Typical violin arch heights roughly 15–16 mm (belly) and 14.5–15.5 mm (back), but the
   **shape** matters more than the height: full, gently sloping arches vs high narrow ones
   change the sound and stability. Follow one model consistently until experienced.
3. Cut the **purfling channel** (marking with a purfling cutter, cleaning with a pick) and
   inlay the three-strip purfling with hot hide glue.
4. Carve the **channel**: the shallow scoop just inside the edge, blending into the arch;
   then scrape the whole surface smooth, checked under raking light.

## 3. Inside graduation
1. Hollow the inside with gouges, then planes, working toward target thicknesses.
2. Mark a grid and measure with **graduation calipers** (dial gauge on a C-frame). Pencil
   the readings on the wood; remove wood where too thick.
3. Common patterns: spruce belly roughly even, around 2.5–3 mm; maple back thicker in the
   centre (often ~4–4.5 mm) thinning toward the flanks (~2.5 mm). Adjust for the wood's
   **density and stiffness** — a stiff, light piece can go thinner.
4. Smooth with scrapers. The inside surface is never seen but bad tool work shows in tap tones.

## 4. Tap tones and modes
Many makers check free-plate modes by holding a plate at node points and tapping, or with
Chladni patterns (tea leaves/glitter on a plate driven by a speaker). **Mode 5** (ring mode)
frequency and its relation to mode 2 are tracked against previous plates. Use these as
records to learn from, not targets that override judgement.

## 5. F-holes and bass bar
- Mark f-holes from the model's template, drill the eyes, cut with a fine saw and knife;
  the wings get fluted. Symmetry and clean edges are judged by every player who looks.
- **Bass bar**: spruce, fitted **under the bass-side foot of the bridge**, slightly angled to
  the centre line, its gluing surface fitted to the inside arch exactly (chalk-fit until
  contact is complete), glued with hide glue under clamps. Many makers fit it with
  slight **spring** (gap at the ends before clamping). Then shape it, tapering to the ends.

## Gotchas
- Graduate *after* the arch is final — arching changes after graduation throw off thicknesses.
- Too-thin edges and corners crack later; keep the edge rounding substantial.
- Fit everything (purfling, bass bar) with **hot hide glue** — reversibility is essential
  for future repairs, and the trade expects it.
- Numbers vary per model, wood and maker; follow your own records.

## Verify
- [ ] Arch matches the templates with no flats or hollows under raking light.
- [ ] Graduation map recorded on the plate and in your notes.
- [ ] Purfling flush, gap-free, with clean mitred corners.
- [ ] Bass bar chalk-fit fully along its length before gluing.
- [ ] Tap tones/modes recorded for both plates.
