# Making and voicing a wooden flute or whistle

**Goal:** a simple-system wooden flute or fipple whistle that plays in tune across two
octaves with an even, clear tone. Bore, holes and voicing interact — the method is
calculate, then adjust by ear with a tuner.

## 1. Wood and blanks
- Dense, stable, close-grained hardwoods: African blackwood (grenadilla), boxwood, cocobolo,
  mopane, also maple/cherry for simple whistles. Season blanks thoroughly; rough-bore and rest
  them weeks before final boring to let stresses settle.
- Turn between centres; keep the blank's axis true to the bore.

## 2. Bore
- **Drilling**: a long **gun drill** or a long auger on a lathe, drilling from both ends to
  meet is common for long bodies. Straightness is critical.
- **Reaming**: flute bores are usually **conical** (tapering toward the foot) — use a
  tapered reamer made to your design. Whistles often have a cylindrical bore.
- Finish the bore smooth and seal with oil (e.g. almond or linseed-based bore oil) to resist moisture.
- Bore diameter vs length sets the scale; copy proven dimensions from published designs
  before inventing.

## 3. Tone-hole placement
1. Start from a **calculator** (e.g. Flutomat-style or acoustic modelling software) that
   accounts for bore diameter, wall thickness and hole size. Simple "fraction of length"
   rules are inaccurate because of end corrections.
2. Drill holes **small and slightly low** (closer to the mouthpiece = sharper; you can
   always raise pitch by enlarging or moving a hole up by undercutting, but lowering is hard).
3. Tune each note from the bottom up with a tuner, enlarging holes to raise pitch.

## 4. Undercutting and tuning
- **Undercutting**: enlarging the hole on the bore side, under the wall, with a small rasp or
  burr. Raises pitch, improves response and octave tuning without changing the finger feel.
- Octave relationships: check each note's second octave; undercutting toward the top or
  bottom of the hole shifts octaves differently. Record every change.
- To lower a note slightly that's gone sharp, partially fill with wax or a sleeve — but
  avoid where possible.

## 5. Voicing
- **Transverse flute embouchure**: an oval hole, typically with steep, sharp walls undercut
  toward the player and the far side; its size and undercut shape set tone and response.
  Distance from the cork/stopper to the embouchure centre tunes the upper octave.
- **Fipple (whistle/recorder)**: the **windway** (flat, narrowing slightly toward the exit),
  the **block** (fipple), and the **labium** (sharp edge). The air jet must hit the labium
  edge precisely; small changes in windway height, ramp angle and labium sharpness change
  the tone dramatically. Make adjustable test heads.
- Finish the windway smooth; a rough windway gives a breathy, unstable tone.

## 6. Finishing
- Oil inside and out (several thin applications), or for some woods a hard finish outside only.
- Fit rings/ferrules on tenons to prevent cracking; tenons wrapped with cork or thread.
- Play-in gradually; new wooden instruments are vulnerable to cracking from rapid moisture
  changes — short sessions, swab out, and warm before playing in cold weather.

## Gotchas
- Drill pilot holes slightly undersize and angled exactly perpendicular to the bore.
- Wall thickness changes tuning (thicker = flatter): measure it.
- Calculator results are starting points; physical instruments deviate (uncertain margins).

## Verify
- [ ] Bore straight and smooth; dimensions recorded.
- [ ] Each note of the first and second octave within a few cents of target on a tuner.
- [ ] Clear tone and easy response with no breathiness across the range.
- [ ] Tenons protected; wood oiled; player told how to play in.
