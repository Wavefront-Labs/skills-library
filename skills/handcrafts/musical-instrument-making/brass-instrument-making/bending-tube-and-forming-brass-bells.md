# Bending tube and forming brass bells

**Goal:** tube bends with no kinks or ovality and bells with a smooth, true flare —
because bore discontinuities change intonation and response. This is the metalworking
core of brasswind making; valve fitting and acoustic design are separate topics.

## 1. Brass and annealing
- **Yellow brass** (~70/30 copper/zinc) is bright and common; **gold/rose brass** (more copper)
  is softer and darker-sounding to many players; nickel-silver for slides and trim.
- Brass work-hardens as it's bent and hammered. **Anneal** by heating evenly with a torch to
  a dull red glow (seen in dim light), then cool — brass can be quenched or air-cooled.
  Anneal before major forming and again whenever it stiffens. Overheating causes grain
  growth (orange-peel surface) and weakens it.

## 2. Bending tube
- Bending empty tube kinks and flattens it. Fill it:
  - **Pitch** (traditional): melted bending pitch poured in, cooled solid; after bending,
    melt it out gently. Fumes — ventilate.
  - **Lead**: low-melting alloy poured in (historic); health hazard — avoid where possible.
  - **Water/soap frozen (ice)**: freeze the filled tube, bend quickly while frozen.
  - Commercial low-melt bismuth alloys (melt in hot water) are a cleaner option.
- Bend gradually around a form or by hand over a block, keeping the cross-section round.
- **Bending balls**/drawing: after bending, steel balls of graded size are pushed or
  hydraulically forced through to restore the round bore and remove small dents.
- Check bends for wrinkles on the inside radius — sign of insufficient fill or too fast a bend.

## 3. Seamed bell (hand method)
1. Cut a flat **bell blank** (a curved flare shape plus a separate gusset for the widest
   flare, depending on design) from sheet brass, often ~0.4–0.6 mm.
2. Cut **teeth/serrations** along the seam edge, interlock them, and **braze** with silver
   solder and flux; file and hammer the seam flat so it's invisible after spinning.
3. Anneal; then hammer the cone over a **steel mandrel** (bell mandrel) of the target bore
   profile, turning steadily, annealing frequently, until it conforms.
4. Burnish/draw the bell on the mandrel (in a lathe) to fit exactly, smoothing the profile.
5. **Flare**: the rim is spun/hammered outward; a **wire rim** (bead) is rolled in to stiffen
   the edge and soldered.

## 4. Bell spinning
Industrial and many small makers spin bells on a lathe from a cone or blank over a form,
using a lubricated spinning tool. Faster and repeatable; hand-hammered bells are prized
for character. Either way, wall thickness affects tone colour.

## 5. Assembly
- **Soft-solder** (tin-based) ferrules, braces and slide tubes; **silver solder** (brazing)
  for structural or high-stress joints like the bell seam.
- Slides: inner and outer tubes must be perfectly straight and concentric; they're lapped
  with fine compound to a free, airtight fit.
- Leadpipe taper is critical to response; it's usually drawn on a mandrel.
- Clean flux thoroughly — residues corrode brass. Pickle, rinse, polish.
- Finish: lacquer, silver-plate, or raw brass.

## Safety
Torch work: fire-safe bench, eye protection, ventilation for flux and pitch fumes; never
heat a sealed tube (steam/pressure). Avoid lead fills; if used, follow lead-handling rules.

## Gotchas
- An unannealed bell cracks under the hammer — anneal more often than you think.
- Pitch left in a bend can scorch and stick; melt out slowly and clean with solvent.
- Seam solder that's not fully penetrated opens on spinning.
- Thicknesses and alloys are typical; specific designs vary.

## Verify
- [ ] Bends are round, unwrinkled and free of dents (gauge or ball check).
- [ ] Bell conforms to the mandrel along its length; seam invisible.
- [ ] Rim wire fully soldered, no gaps.
- [ ] Slides move freely and are airtight (pop test).
- [ ] All flux removed before finishing.
