# Forging scrolls and collaring joints

**Goal:** scrolls with tapered, well-formed ends and smooth, open spirals that match
each other, held together with tight collars, laid out to a full-size drawing.

## 1. Draw it full size
Draw the panel or bracket at full size on a steel layout table (soapstone/chalk) or
on paper. Measure the **length of each scroll** along its centreline with a piece of
wire or string laid on the drawing — that's the bar length for that scroll (add a
little for the end and trim).

## 2. Forge the scroll end
The end sets the whole scroll's quality. Common ends:
- **Taper/"pigtail":** draw to a fine taper, then curl the very end tightly over the
  anvil edge or horn.
- **Fishtail:** taper, then flatten the end wide and thin on the anvil face, giving a
  flared tip.
- **Penny end:** upset the end slightly then flatten into a round disc, rolled in.
- Start the curl on the very tip **before** the main spiral: a sharp, tight start
  makes the scroll look intentional. Tap the end over the anvil edge, then roll it.

## 3. Form the spiral
- **By eye:** heat a section, curl on the anvil face or horn by hammering the bar
  edge over the curl. Work in short heats, from the inside outward; only the hot
  part bends, so heat control places the curve.
- **Over a scroll jig/iron:** a forged steel spiral fixed in the vice or the hardy
  hole. Hook the end in, then wrap the heated bar round with a scrolling wrench or
  by hand. Repeatable for matched sets.
- Keep the scroll **flat**: tap it flat on the anvil face after each heat; a
  dished scroll won't sit in the panel.
- Check against the drawing every heat. Adjust with **scrolling tongs** to open or
  close a turn.

## 4. Matching sets
For several identical scrolls: forge one master, measure its length and shape,
and use a jig or a template made from it. Lay them on top of each other: they
should coincide.

## 5. Collars
- A **collar** is a short strip wrapped round the joined bars, making a traditional
  mechanical joint.
- Measure: wrap a strip of thin wire round the bundle at the joint to get the length,
  then add about one-and-a-half times the stock thickness (a common rule — test on a scrap).
- Pre-bend the collar into a U or a rectangle with an open side over a mandrel or
  in a collar jig.
- Clamp the parts together in position on the layout table. Heat the collar to a bright
  orange-yellow, put it over the joint, and hammer the open ends round, closing it
  tight. It shrinks as it cools, gripping the joint. Work quickly; a cold collar won't
  close.
- **Rivets** (hot-set) are the other traditional joint; modern work often uses hidden
  welds, but collars and rivets are the honest and traditional choice.

## 6. Assembly and finish
Assemble the frame, check it against the drawing, and wire-brush. Finish with
paint, wax or oil-based blacking for indoors; outdoors needs proper rust
protection (galvanising or an appropriate paint system — galvanising needs vent
holes in closed sections).

## Gotchas
- Too-quick tapers make weak, bendy ends; too-thick ends look clumsy.
- Heating the whole bar: it bends everywhere — localise the heat.
- Collars on cold work don't grip.

## Verify
- [ ] Full-size drawing and bar lengths measured from it.
- [ ] Scroll ends formed before the main spiral; tight start.
- [ ] Scrolls flat, matching the drawing and each other.
- [ ] Collars hot-wrapped tight; no movement in the joint.
- [ ] Finish appropriate to indoor/outdoor use.
