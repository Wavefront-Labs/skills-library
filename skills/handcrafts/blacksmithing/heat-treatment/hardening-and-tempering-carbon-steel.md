# Hardening and tempering carbon steel

**Goal:** a tool or blade hard enough to hold an edge, tough enough not to chip or
snap — consistently, not by luck. This is forge-level practice for simple carbon
steels; alloy and stainless steels need controlled furnaces and their datasheet
schedules.

## 1. Know the steel
- Only steels with enough carbon (roughly 0.5% and up) harden usefully. **Mild steel
  won't harden** significantly.
- Get the datasheet. It tells you the hardening temperature, quench medium and
  tempering chart. Simple examples: **1084** (oil), **1095** (fast oil), **W1** (water/
  brine), **O1** (oil), **5160** (oil; spring steel).
- Unknown scrap: test a piece first — harden, snap it, look at the grain.

## 2. Normalise / anneal first
- **Normalise** after forging: heat to non-magnetic, cool in still air; repeat 2–3
  times. Refines grain and relieves stress.
- **Anneal** (soften for filing/drilling): heat to non-magnetic and cool **very**
  slowly — in vermiculite, wood ash, or a cooling forge. Simple carbon steels can
  be annealed this way; some alloys need controlled furnace cooling.

## 3. Hardening heat
- Heat **evenly** to just above the critical temperature. Practical test: a
  **magnet** stops sticking at roughly the critical point (the Curie point is
  near it for these steels), then go a little hotter and **soak** briefly so the
  whole section is even. For 1084, a common figure is ~815 °C (~1500 °F); check
  your datasheet.
- **Overheating** coarsens grain and makes the steel brittle; underheating means it
  won't fully harden. Judge colour in shade, or use a thermocouple/kiln.
- Thin edges heat faster — keep them from overheating by moving the piece in the fire.

## 4. Quench
- **Oil steels**: in oil warmed to ~50 °C (~120 °F) — commercial fast quench oil is
  best; canola/vegetable oil is a common hobby substitute (slower; may not fully
  harden some steels). Plunge **edge first** and move the piece **up and down** (not
  side to side — that warps blades).
- **Water/brine steels**: much more severe; risk of cracking. Only for steels that
  need it.
- Have a metal lid ready to smother an oil flash fire. Quench outdoors or
  ventilated; oil smokes.

## 5. Test
A sharp file should **skate** off the hardened edge without biting. If it bites,
it didn't harden: re-heat, check temperature, re-quench (repeated hardening
increases the risk of warp/cracks).

## 6. Temper immediately
Fully hardened steel is glass-brittle and can crack just sitting there. Temper
**right away**:
- **Oven tempering** (most consistent): typically 175–230 °C (~350–450 °F) for
  blades, 1–2 hours, **twice**, cooling to room temperature between cycles. Check
  the oven with a separate thermometer.
- **Colour tempering** (traditional, for chisels, punches, springs): polish a
  patch, heat the back/thick part, and watch oxide colours run toward the edge —
  pale straw (~220 °C) → dark straw → bronze → purple → blue (~300 °C). Quench
  when the edge reaches the target colour: **straw** for edge tools and knives,
  **bronze–purple** for cold chisels and punches, **blue** for springs. These
  temperatures are approximate and the colours are surface oxide, not a measure
  of the core.

## Gotchas
- Warping: unequal grinding/stress before hardening, or side-to-side quenching.
- Cracks: sharp internal corners, water quenching oil steels, thin edges overheated.
- Decarburisation of the surface in an oxidising fire: grind past the soft skin.

## Verify
- [ ] Steel identified with a datasheet; normalised before hardening.
- [ ] Heated evenly just past non-magnetic and soaked; not overheated.
- [ ] Correct quench medium; edge-first, up-and-down motion; lid ready.
- [ ] File skates on the hardened area.
- [ ] Tempered immediately to the use; double temper for blades.
