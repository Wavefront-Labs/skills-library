# Fire management, drawing out and upsetting

**Goal:** heat steel evenly to forging temperature without burning it, and move it
where you want with the hammer — longer and thinner (drawing out) or shorter and
fatter (upsetting).

## 1. The fire
**Coal/coke forge**
- Build a mound of **coke** (coal already burned to coke at the edges of the fire)
  around the tuyere; the hot heart is coke, fresh coal goes round the outside to coke.
- Keep the fire **deep**: the steel should sit in the middle of the fire, not on top of
  the air blast. Steel too close to the tuyere sits in excess oxygen (an
  **oxidising** zone) — it scales heavily and can burn.
- **Clinker** (fused ash) builds up over the tuyere and chokes the air. Remove it with
  a poker when the fire goes dull and patchy.
**Gas forge**
- Simpler, cleaner; adjust gas pressure and air for a slightly **reducing**
  (fuel-rich) flame to cut scale. Hot spots exist near the burner.
**Either:** ventilate. Forges produce **carbon monoxide**; never use one in a closed
space. Gas: check fittings for leaks with soapy water.

## 2. Reading heat (mild steel)
- Judge colour in **shade**, not sunlight. Approximate: dull red → cherry → orange →
  yellow → bright yellow/white (welding range, sparkling = burning).
- Mild steel forges best at **orange to yellow**. Stop working when it drops to dull
  red — hitting cold steel cracks it or work-hardens it and bruises your arm.
- Heat **through** the bar, not just the surface: a thicker bar needs more time.
- High-carbon and alloy steels have narrower windows — see
  `handcrafts.blacksmithing.heat-treatment.hardening-and-tempering-carbon-steel`.

## 3. Hammer and anvil
- Hammer weight about 1–1.5 kg (2–3 lb) for most beginners. Grip loosely, let the
  hammer's weight do the work; elbow near your side, wrist relaxed. Anvil face
  roughly at your **knuckle height** with arms hanging.
- Hit the steel **on the anvil**, never the bare anvil face (it bounces back hard).
- Hearing protection and safety glasses; scale flies.

## 4. Drawing out
- To lengthen/thin: hit with the hammer face across the bar while it lies on the anvil,
  turning it 90° every few blows to keep it square. For speed, work over the **horn**
  or the anvil's edge, which acts as a fuller, then smooth on the face.
- **Taper to a point:** hold the bar at an angle to the anvil face, hit, rotate 90°,
  hit, keep it square in section. Then make it octagonal, then round, only at the end.
- A **diamond** section (squashed square) means you hit harder on one diagonal —
  correct by hitting the long corners.

## 5. Upsetting
- To thicken: heat **only the area to be thickened** (quench the rest if needed), stand
  the bar on the anvil, and strike its end. The heated portion bulges.
- Keep it straight: correct any bend immediately, or the upset folds into a
  **cold shut** (a lap that looks fused but isn't).

## 6. Bending, punching, drifting
- Bend over the horn or in the vice at a good heat; hot steel bends where it's
  hottest — use that.
- **Punch** a hole hot: drive a punch from one side most of the way, flip, and drive
  through from the other side over the pritchel/hardy hole to shear out the slug.
  Cool the punch often. **Drift** to enlarge to final shape.

## Verify
- [ ] Fire deep and clean; clinker removed; ventilated.
- [ ] Working only at orange-yellow heat; stopping at dull red.
- [ ] Bar kept square while drawing; no diamond sections.
- [ ] Upset straight, no folds or cold shuts.
- [ ] Hearing and eye protection on.
