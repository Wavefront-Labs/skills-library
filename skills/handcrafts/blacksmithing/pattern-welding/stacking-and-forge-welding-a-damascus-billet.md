# Stacking and forge-welding a Damascus billet

**Goal:** a solid, fully welded billet of alternating steel layers, with no
delaminations or inclusions, that etches to a visible pattern. This is modern
**pattern-welded** steel; historical "wootz" crucible Damascus is a different material.

## 1. Choose steels
- Two steels with **similar hardening behaviour** and **contrasting etch**: the
  common pair is **1084** (etches dark) and **15N20** (nickel-bearing, stays bright).
  Mismatched steels can crack or warp in heat treatment.
- Start with clean, flat bar of the same width; 5–9 layers to start is typical.

## 2. Prepare the stack
- **Grind every surface bright** — remove all mill scale and rust. Scale between
  layers is the most common cause of failed welds.
- Stack alternating, **weld the corners or a handle on** (a bar welded to one end),
  and tack or MIG-weld the sides so layers can't shift.

## 3. Heat and flux
- Heat in a forge capable of **welding heat** — bright yellow, around 1,250 °C
  (~2,300 °F) is often quoted for these steels; the bar looks wet/shimmering.
- Most smiths **flux** with anhydrous borax: sprinkle it on at around a red-orange
  heat so it melts and seeps into the seams, excluding air. Too early and it doesn't
  melt; heavy flux eats forge refractory. "Dry" (fluxless) welding is possible in a
  controlled reducing atmosphere or with the stack sealed — a more advanced method.
- Soak until the whole billet is at an even welding heat, through the thickness.

## 4. Set the weld
- Take it straight to the anvil, press or power hammer. First blows are **light and
  quick** to set the weld and squeeze out flux — heavy blows on unset layers push
  them apart.
- Work from the **centre outwards** (or from one end along) so flux and air are
  expelled, not trapped.
- Re-heat to welding heat and hit firmer to consolidate. Wire-brush scale between heats.

## 5. Draw out and restack
- Draw the billet out at forging heat (below welding heat), grind the surface clean,
  cut into pieces, grind the cut faces clean, and **restack** (cut-and-stack) to
  multiply the layer count: e.g. 7 layers → cut in 4 → 28 layers. Repeat. Fold-over is
  another method but traps scale more easily.
- Layer count governs the pattern's fineness; a few hundred layers is common for
  blades.

## 6. Manipulate the pattern
- **Twist:** draw to a square bar and twist hot.
- **Ladder / raindrop:** grind or press grooves/dimples into the surface, then forge flat.
- Pattern is set by what's done to the layers before the final shape.

## 7. Check for defects
- Grind a test area and **etch**: dark/light layers should be continuous. Cracks or a
  dark line along a layer = a **cold shut/delamination**; it will open up in use or in
  heat treatment.
- Small defects at the edge can be ground off; inside the blade, the billet is scrap.

## 8. Etching
After heat treatment and a fine hand-sanded finish (higher grit shows a crisper
pattern), etch in **ferric chloride** (diluted with water, e.g. 1:3–1:4 is common),
in short dips, neutralise (e.g. baking soda), clean, and repeat to depth.
Ferric chloride is corrosive and stains — gloves, eye protection, ventilation.

## Safety
Welding heat sends sparks and molten flux off the billet: face shield, leather
apron, no synthetic clothing, clear floor. Borax splashes stick and burn.

## Verify
- [ ] Matched steels; all surfaces ground bright; stack tacked.
- [ ] Billet at even welding heat through; fluxed at the right time.
- [ ] Welds set with light blows from the centre outward.
- [ ] Cut faces ground clean before every restack.
- [ ] Test etch shows continuous layers; no delaminations.
