# Hot sand casting glass

**Goal:** a solid glass casting with a crisp sand-textured face, no cracks, and a
fully annealed interior. Thick glass is unforgiving: most failures come from annealing,
not pouring.

## Mould material
- Fine silica sand bonded with **bentonite** clay and a little water (green sand style);
  many studios add **carbon** (graphite/seacoal) for a cleaner surface. Typical
  proportions are around 5–10% bentonite and a few percent water — mix by feel until a
  squeezed handful holds a sharp print and breaks cleanly. (Exact recipe varies by studio.)
- Sand should be just damp: too wet and steam blows bubbles into the glass and causes
  violent spitting; too dry and the impression collapses.
- Silica sand dust is a lung hazard: mix wet, wear a fitted respirator when handling dry.

## Making the impression
- Pack sand firmly in a steel box or wooden frame on a steel table.
- Press a pattern (wood, found objects, carved shapes) into the sand, remove cleanly.
  Or carve directly into packed sand. Undercuts tear out — keep draft angles.
- Optional: dust the impression with coloured glass **frit or powder** (compatible with
  the furnace glass) or place inclusions (metal foil, wire) — they fuse into the surface.
  Embedded materials must be compatible or they will crack the glass.

## Pouring
- Glass is ladled from the furnace with a preheated steel ladle, or gathered heavily
  on a pipe/punty and dripped/sheared into the mould.
- Pour steadily into one spot and let it flow out to fill; avoid folding which traps air.
- For thicker castings, pour quickly so the whole mass is roughly even in temperature.
- Once it skins over and holds shape, the casting is lifted (with heat-protective gloves
  and tools) into the annealer as soon as it can move without distorting — often within
  a minute or two. Brush off loose sand.

## Annealing (the critical part)
- Cast blocks need **far longer** annealing than blown ware. Rough scaling: time and
  slowness increase with the **square** of thickness. A 25 mm casting may need hours at
  the annealing soak and a slow cool over many hours; 100 mm thicknesses run to days.
- Use your glass manufacturer's or established studio anneal charts for the thickness
  (and treat a casting with one thick side as the thickest dimension). Examples published
  by fusing/casting glass makers are a good reference.
- Uneven thickness = longer anneal still, because thin parts cool faster and set up strain.
- After annealing, check under crossed polarisers for residual stress (bright colour bands
  = strain).

## Hot sculpting (sibling technique)
Solid hot sculpting on the punty — pulling and shaping a solid gather with jacks, tweezers
and shears into figures — follows the same annealing rule: solid = thick = long anneal.

## Finishing
- Cold-work faces as needed (grinding and polishing); the sand texture is often left as the
  feature face.

## Safety
- Molten glass ~1100 °C+; spitting from damp sand or ladles. Full face shield, natural
  fibre clothes, leather boots, aluminised gloves/sleeves where appropriate.
- Clear communication, choreographed moves; floor clear of trip hazards.

## Gotchas
- Cracks appearing hours/days later: under-annealed.
- Surface pocked: wet sand, or sand too loose.
- Thickness numbers above are general scaling rules, not a specific schedule; always use
  a published chart for your glass.

## Verify
- [ ] Sand bonded, just damp, holding a sharp print.
- [ ] Inclusions and frit compatible with the furnace glass.
- [ ] Casting in the annealer promptly; schedule chosen for its thickest section.
- [ ] Polariscope check shows no significant strain.
