# Cleaving, shaping and tenoning oak spokes

**Goal:** a set of identical, strong spokes whose fibres run the full length,
with a tight foot tenon for the nave and a round tongue for the felloe.

## 1. Cleave, don't saw
- Spokes take compression and bending shock with every turn of the wheel; riven
  stock follows the fibres and has no run-out (see
  `handcrafts.woodworking-joinery.green-woodworking.riving-and-shaving-at-the-shave-horse`
  for the riving technique).
- **Oak** is the British traditional spoke timber; **hickory** is the classic in
  North America and for lighter carriage wheels; ash is weaker in this role.
- Cleave from straight, knot-free logs into blanks a little over finished size.

## 2. Season
Air-dry the blanks thoroughly, traditionally for a long time (years for oak in old
practice) stacked with air flow. A spoke that shrinks after driving loosens in the
nave. Rough-shaping before seasoning speeds drying.

## 3. Shape
- Work on a shave horse or with the spoke held in a **spoke dog**/cramp.
- Shape with a **drawknife**, then **spokeshave**, to a pattern: spokes are
  commonly oval or flattened in section, wider front-to-back (in the direction of
  load from the wheel's dish) and tapering from the foot toward the felloe end.
  Exact profiles vary by tradition and wheel type.
- Use a template or pattern so every spoke in the set is the same; check sections
  with calipers.

## 4. The foot (nave) tenon
- A rectangular tenon (often slightly tapered) cut to fit the nave mortice.
  Shoulders should be square to the spoke and **bear evenly on the nave** when
  driven.
- Fit each spoke to **its own** mortice and number them: mortices vary slightly.
- The spoke's set (the angle of the shoulder relative to the spoke) controls dish
  at that point; it is checked with a spoke-set gauge or by sighting on a
  straightedge after driving (see
  `handcrafts.wheelwrighting.wheel-assembly.driving-spokes-and-setting-dish`).

## 5. The felloe tongue
- After the spokes are driven into the nave, their outer ends are cut to length to
  the wheel's radius, and a **round tongue** (tenon) is cut — traditionally with a
  hollow auger/"spoke trimmer" or by hand — to go into a bored hole in the felloe.
- The shoulder of the tongue must be at the right radius so the felloes sit
  correctly; a story stick or trammel from the nave centre marks it.

## Gotchas
- Grain run-out through the tenon: the tenon snaps when driven.
- Spokes shaped too thin at the shoulder flex and loosen.
- Cutting tongues before driving the spokes: they won't line up with the felloe
  holes because each spoke's true position is only known when driven.

## Verify
- [ ] Riven stock, grain continuous end to end; seasoned.
- [ ] Shaped to pattern; sections consistent across the set.
- [ ] Foot tenons fitted to numbered mortices; shoulders bear evenly.
- [ ] Tongues marked to radius and cut after driving.
