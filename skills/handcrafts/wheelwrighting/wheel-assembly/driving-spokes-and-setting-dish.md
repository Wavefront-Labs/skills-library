# Driving spokes and setting dish

**Goal:** hub, spokes and felloes brought together into a wheel with consistent
**dish** that runs true, ready for the tyre.

## Why dish
A traditional wheel is a shallow cone, not a flat disc: the spokes lean outward
from the nave. Combined with an axle that tilts the wheel ("pitch"), the lower
spoke stands near vertical under load, and side loads from a swaying cart push the
spokes **into** the cone rather than snapping them. A dished wheel also resists the
side thrust of a horse's gait. The amount of dish depends on the wheel's size and use;
it is set by the wheelwright's pattern and isn't universal.

## 1. Set up
- The nave is held horizontal on a **wheel stool** or in a wheel pit, mortices
  up, the face side of the wheel uppermost (or downward, depending on tradition —
  check what your pattern assumes).
- Spokes numbered to their mortices (see
  `handcrafts.wheelwrighting.spoke-making.cleaving-shaping-and-tenoning-oak-spokes`).

## 2. Driving
- Drive each spoke with a heavy **sledge** (sledgehammer), struck on the end, in
  steady blows until the shoulder bears on the nave. The spoke should drive tight,
  with a ringing note.
- Drive **opposite** spokes in sequence rather than going round in order, to keep
  stresses balanced.
- Check the **set** of each spoke as it goes home: a **spoke-set gauge** (an arm
  pivoting on the nave centre, or a straightedge from the axle line) shows whether
  each spoke's end reaches the same point. A spoke that leans too far out or in can
  sometimes be corrected by paring a shoulder face and re-driving.
- A spoke that won't go home or splits: stop and fix; never leave a spoke short.

## 3. Trim to radius
- Mark every spoke at the wheel's radius from the nave centre (story stick or
  trammel), allowing for felloe depth, and cut the shoulders and round tongues
  (see `handcrafts.wheelwrighting.felloe-making.sawing-boring-and-jointing-felloes`).
- Spokes need to be pulled together in pairs to enter one felloe's two holes —
  a spoke dog (lever clamp) is used to draw them together.

## 4. Fit the felloes and true
- Drive felloes on evenly. Check with the wheel on the stool:
  - **Face:** all felloes at the same height relative to the nave (the dish is
    consistent) — sight across or use the gauge on every spoke.
  - **Round:** spin the wheel on a spindle and watch against a pointer; the rim
    should not wobble side to side or go up and down.
- Correct by tapping felloes and paring, before tyring. A tyre will lock in errors.

## Gotchas
- Driving all spokes in one direction round the wheel pulls the nave out of line.
- Consistent set matters more than exact angle: a wheel with uneven dish wobbles and
  loads some spokes more than others.
- Dish increases slightly when the hot tyre shrinks on — wheelwrights allow for
  that in the set. How much is experience; not quantified here.

## Verify
- [ ] Spokes driven home on opposite pairs, shoulders bearing.
- [ ] Every spoke checked with the set gauge; dish even.
- [ ] Spokes trimmed to radius from the nave centre.
- [ ] Felloes fitted, joints left open for the tyre.
- [ ] Wheel spun: no side wobble, no hop.
