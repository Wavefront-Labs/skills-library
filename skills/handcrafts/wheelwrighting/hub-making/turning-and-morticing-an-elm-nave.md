# Turning and morticing an elm nave

**Goal:** a sound hub (nave) that holds every spoke at the correct angle for the
wheel's dish and resists splitting under years of shock. This is a summary of
traditional British-style practice; carriage and American traditions differ in
detail (and later hubs used metal and hickory).

## 1. Timber
- **Elm** is the traditional nave wood because its interlocked grain resists
  splitting when a dozen mortices are cut through it. (Dutch elm disease has made
  large sound elm scarce; other dense, interlocked or tough timbers are sometimes
  substituted — oak, or for lighter wheels hickory/ash. Expect debate here.)
- Cut from the butt, bark left on or ends sealed, and **seasoned for years**; old
  practice was rough-turning or boring through the centre to speed drying and
  reduce checking. A wet nave shrinks and loosens every spoke.

## 2. Turning
- Mount between centres on a large lathe; turn the profile to the pattern — a
  barrel shape thickest at the spoke line, with shoulders for the **nave bands**
  (iron rings shrunk on later to resist splitting).
- Mark the **spoke line** (the plane of the mortices) — not necessarily at the
  centre of the nave's length: it's placed to suit the dish and the axle.

## 3. Laying out the mortices
- Divide the circumference at the spoke line into the number of spokes (often
  10–16 depending on wheel size). Dividers or a paper strip.
- Mortices are commonly **staggered** — alternately slightly forward and back of
  the spoke line — so the spokes don't all line up in one plane of weakness
  across the grain, and so mortices don't run into each other.
- **Angle for dish:** the mortices are cut leaning slightly outward (toward the
  face of the wheel), so the spokes form a shallow cone. The dish angle is set by
  the wheelwright's pattern for the wheel; it's checked with a spoke-set gauge.
  The exact angle is wheel-specific and not given here.

## 4. Cutting the mortices
- Bore out most of the waste with an auger, then chop with mortice chisels,
  traditionally with a **mortice slightly narrower at the inner end** so the
  spoke tenon drives home tight.
- Hold the nave in a cradle/stand; check each side wall against the angle gauge
  as you go.
- Keep the mortice **faces** (the walls the spoke bears against front and back)
  clean and true; that is where the load goes.

## 5. Boring for the axle box
- Bore the centre to take the **axle box** (cast iron bushing) — traditionally
  with a large auger then a **boxing engine** (a tapered cutter that sizes the
  bore). The bore is tapered to match the box.
- Many wheelwrights bore after the spokes are driven and the wheel is tyred, so
  the box is centred on the finished wheel. Practice varies; check your tradition.
- Fit nave bands hot so they shrink tight before or after spoking.

## Gotchas
- Mortices cut at slightly different angles = a wheel that's not true, which
  shows when the tyre goes on.
- Short-grain between mortices near the ends of the nave can break out.
- Unseasoned stock is the commonest failure.

## Verify
- [ ] Elm (or justified substitute) seasoned; no shakes or checks.
- [ ] Turned to pattern; spoke line marked.
- [ ] Mortices evenly spaced, staggered, angled for dish, checked with gauge.
- [ ] Mortice faces clean and true; slight taper to the inner end.
- [ ] Axle box bore centred and tapered to the box.
