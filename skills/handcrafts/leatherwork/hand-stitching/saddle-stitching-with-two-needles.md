# Saddle stitching with two needles

**Goal:** a hand-sewn seam that is stronger than machine lockstitch (one broken stitch
does not unravel the line), with even slanted stitches on both faces. This is the core
seam for wallets, belts, cases and tack.

## 1. Tools and materials
- **Pricking irons / stitching chisels** (diamond or French pattern) at your chosen
  pitch: 2.7–3 mm for small goods, 3.38–4 mm for belts and bags, 5–6 mm for heavy
  strapwork. Matching 1-prong and 2-prong for curves.
- **Wing divider** or adjustable **stitch groover** to mark the line 3–4 mm from the
  edge (further on heavy work, ~5–6 mm).
- **Harness needles** (blunt), size 002–004 for thin thread; two per seam.
- **Thread:** waxed polyester (braided) or waxed linen. Typical diameters: 0.45–0.55 mm
  for 2.7–3 mm pitch, 0.65–0.8 mm for 3.85–4 mm, 1.0 mm+ for heavy pitch. Too fat for
  the hole and it bunches; too thin and the line looks starved.
- **Stitching pony or clam** to hold the work so both hands are free.
- Contact cement or water-based leather glue; mallet (poly or rawhide), poundo board or
  thick rubber mat to punch on.

## 2. Prepare the seam
1. Cut both layers, **skive** edges if needed so the combined thickness is even.
2. Rough up the glue zone (flesh side and any finished grain under the seam) and glue
   both faces, let it tack (contact cement: until dry to the knuckle, ~5–10 min), press
   together, then hammer the seam flat.
3. Trim the edges flush, then mark the stitch line with the divider/groover. A groove
   sinks the thread a little so it wears less.
4. Walk the irons along the line: put the first prong of each new set **into the last
   hole you made**, so spacing stays even. Use the 2-prong on curves. Punch through
   both layers square to the surface; check the back for wandering holes.

## 3. Thread length and needles
- Thread length ≈ **3.5–4 × seam length** plus ~30 cm for handling (heavier leather,
  more). Too long and it frays from being dragged through the holes; better to join
  than to fight 3 m of thread.
- Thread a needle on each end; pierce the thread 2–3 times about 5 cm from the end and
  pull back over the eye so it locks.

## 4. The stitch
1. Pass one needle through the first hole and centre the thread so both sides are equal.
2. For every hole, **always in the same order**: needle from the **back** (far side)
   goes through the next hole first; then the needle from the **front** goes through
   the same hole. Before the second needle goes in, pull the first thread to the side
   (always the same side, usually toward you / up) so the second needle passes
   **above or below it consistently** and does not pierce it.
3. Pull both threads with the **same tension** each time, and tug them in the same
   direction. Consistency of order and tension is the whole secret of the slant.
4. At the far end, **backstitch 2–3 holes** with both needles (they go back through holes
   already filled — expect it to be tight; an awl or pliers help).
5. Trim threads flush. Polyester: carefully melt the tip with a lighter and press the
   nub into the hole. Linen: trim, add a dab of glue.
6. Tap the line with a mallet or roll a stitch wheel/overstitcher over it to settle the
   thread into the groove.

## 5. Edge finish (after stitching)
Bevel the edges with an edge beveler (size 1–2 for thin, 3 for thick), sand 220→400
grit, wet lightly or apply gum tragacanth/a burnishing compound, and burnish with a
wooden slicker until glassy. Some makers burnish before stitching on edges they
cannot reach later.

## Faults and fixes
- **Uneven slant / zigzag:** the order of needles or the side you hold the first thread
  changed mid-seam. Pick one and never vary it.
- **Split thread:** the second needle pierced the first thread. Pull the first thread
  firmly aside before passing.
- **Wandering holes on the back:** irons not square, or layers slid. Glue first;
  punch on a hard flat surface.
- **Gaps at corners:** irons were not stepped to place a hole exactly at the corner;
  plan the corner hole first and work away from it.
- **Thread fluffing:** thread too long, or not waxed enough; re-wax or shorten.

## Safety
Chisels and round knives are razor sharp; punch away from fingers. Lighter-sealing
polyester: keep the flame away from finishes that are solvent-based or still wet.

## Verify
- [ ] Stitch line parallel to the edge with even spacing; holes straight on the back.
- [ ] Every stitch slants the same way on the front, and the back is neat.
- [ ] Ends backstitched 2–3 holes and sealed; no loose tails.
- [ ] Pulling the seam hard shows no gaping; layers are glued with no voids.
- [ ] Edges bevelled, sanded and burnished smooth.
