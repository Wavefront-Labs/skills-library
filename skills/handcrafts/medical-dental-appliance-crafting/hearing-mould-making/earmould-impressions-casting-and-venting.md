# Earmould impressions, casting and venting

**Goal:** a custom earmould (for a hearing aid, hearing protection or in-ear monitors)
that seals or vents as intended, stays comfortable all day, and doesn't feed back. Taking
ear impressions is a clinical procedure performed by an audiologist/hearing care
professional after otoscopy; the lab works from their impression or scan and order.

## 1. The impression (clinical step, summarised)
1. **Otoscopy** first: no impression if there is wax occlusion, perforation, infection,
   recent surgery or other contraindication.
2. Place an **otoblock (oto-dam)** — foam or cotton with a thread — gently past the second
   bend, sized to the canal, so impression material can't reach the eardrum.
3. Inject impression silicone with a syringe/gun from the block outward, filling the canal and
   concha without air pockets; the patient keeps the jaw still (some protocols use an open
   jaw with a bite block for in-ear monitors).
4. Cure per the material time; remove slowly with a rotating motion; re-examine the ear.
5. Check the impression: smooth, complete past the second bend, no voids or folds, the
   otoblock seated at the tip.
Increasingly, **3D ear scanning** replaces impressions.

## 2. Lab: preparing the impression or scan
- Mark the **canal length** (shorter for comfort, longer past the second bend for high-gain
  aids and protection), the style (full shell, half shell, skeleton, canal), and the sound
  bore and vent paths.
- **Trim** the impression to the style; buff and, if casting, dip in wax for a slightly
  larger, smoother result (wax thickness changes fit).
- **Digital**: scan the impression, model the mould in software (trim, vents, sound bore,
  receiver cavity), and **3D print** in biocompatible resin; post-cure per the resin's IFU.

## 3. Casting (traditional)
1. Invest the trimmed impression in a flask with hydrocolloid or silicone investment.
2. Remove the impression, leaving a negative.
3. Fill with **hard acrylic** (UV- or heat-cure) or **soft silicone/vinyl** material.
4. Cure, demould, drill sound bore and vent, grind and polish.
- Hard acrylic: durable, easy to modify; soft silicone: better seal and comfort for high-gain
  or children (growing ears need frequent remakes), shrinks and discolours sooner.

## 4. Venting: occlusion vs feedback
- A **vent** (parallel channel) relieves the "talking in a barrel" **occlusion effect** and
  lets low frequencies escape — good for mild-moderate low-frequency loss.
- Larger vents increase the risk of **acoustic feedback** (whistling) at high gain. Severe
  losses need small or no vents and a deeper, tighter seal.
- Vent sizes are usually ordered in mm (e.g. ~0.8 mm pressure vent to ~2–3 mm+ open);
  the audiologist specifies, often guided by the hearing-aid fitting software.
- Hearing protection: a sealed mould or one with an acoustic filter for musicians' plugs.

## 5. Tubing and finishing
- BTE moulds: fit the right tube (diameter, wall thickness), with any horn or step bore
  specified for high-frequency boost; glue securely.
- Smooth all edges; polish/lacquer; engrave or colour code (red = right, blue = left).
- Clean and disinfect before dispatch.

## Gotchas
- Impressions with voids at the tip produce moulds that pinch or leak — ask for a retake.
- Over-trimming the helix/concha lock makes the mould work loose.
- Large vents + high gain = feedback complaints.
- Material and vent specifics vary by manufacturer and fitting software; follow the order.

## Verify
- [ ] Impression complete past the second bend with no voids.
- [ ] Canal length, style and vent match the order.
- [ ] Mould smooth, no sharp edges; labelled left/right.
- [ ] Sound bore and vent clear (blow/air test).
- [ ] Tubing secure; device cleaned and disinfected before dispatch.
