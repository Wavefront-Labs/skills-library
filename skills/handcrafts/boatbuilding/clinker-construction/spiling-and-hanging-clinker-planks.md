# Spiling and hanging clinker planks

**Goal:** a lapstrake hull where each plank overlaps the one below on a land of
consistent width, with a bevel that changes smoothly along its length, and laps
fastened tight with copper rivets. Applies to solid timber; glued plywood
lapstrake follows the same geometry with epoxy instead of rivets.

## 1. Line off the strakes
- With the moulds/frames set up on the strongback, bend **battens** along the hull
  to mark each plank's upper edge (the strake lines). Lines should look fair from
  every angle — sight from the bow, stern and side. Planks are widest amidships and
  narrow at the ends, but should **not** taper to a point.
- Mark plank widths at each mould with a pair of dividers stepped up from the
  keel and adjusted to the sheer.

## 2. Spile the plank shape
- A plank lying on a curved hull is not straight when flattened out. **Spiling**
  transfers its shape:
  1. Tack a thin, wide **spiling batten** (a strip of plywood) along the hull where
     the plank will go, sitting flat, without edge-bending it.
  2. With dividers set to a fixed distance, mark arcs from the edge of the plank
     already hung (the lower plank's land line) onto the batten at each mould and
     between. Label each mark.
  3. Remove the batten, lay it on the plank stock, and step off the same divider
     distances back to the stock. Join the points with a fair batten — that's the
     lower edge. The upper edge is set off from the widths at each mould.
- Cut and dress the plank, then **offer it up**: check it lies fair and fits before
  any bevelling.

## 3. The land and rolling bevel
- The upper edge of the lower plank is bevelled so the next plank sits flat on it.
  The bevel **changes** along the length — it twists ("rolls") as the hull shape
  changes. Cut it with a rebate or bevel plane, checking with a short straightedge
  laid across two moulds: it should sit on the bevel and touch the mould face.
- Land width: often in the region of 3/4 in to 1 in (~19–25 mm) on small craft;
  follow the plans.

## 4. Gains at the ends
At stem and transom, the laps would show steps. **Gains** are tapering rebates cut
into the ends of the laps so adjacent planks become flush (carvel-like) where they
land on the stem or transom, allowing a neat, tight fastening.

## 5. Fastening
- Clamp the plank in place along its land. Drill for **copper nails** through both
  planks, roughly evenly spaced along the land (spacing per plans, often a few inches).
- **Riveting:** drive the nail, hold a **dolly** (heavy iron) on the head, push a
  copper **rove** (washer) over the point with a rove punch, cut the nail off a
  little above the rove, and **peen** it over into a rivet head with a ball-pein
  hammer while the dolly holds the head. Two people make this quicker.
- **Clench nails** (point turned over and driven back) are a traditional alternative.
- Fasten into stem, transom and frames as the plans require.

## 6. Timber
Larch, mahogany, oak, cedar and pine are traditional planking woods; quartersawn,
knot-free, straight-grained. Hard bends at the ends may need steaming the plank.

## Verify
- [ ] Strake lines fair from every angle; no planks tapering to points.
- [ ] Every plank spiled from the hull, not measured, and offered up before bevelling.
- [ ] Rolling bevel checked with a straightedge across moulds along the whole length.
- [ ] Gains cut so ends lie flush at stem and transom.
- [ ] Rivets tight with roves seated; no daylight along the lands.
