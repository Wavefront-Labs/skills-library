# Fitting thwarts, coamings and scribed joinery

**Goal:** joinery that meets a curved, sloping hull tightly everywhere — on a boat
almost nothing is square, level or plumb, and every edge needs its own bevel.

## 1. Reference lines, not the hull
- Establish your own references: the boat **levelled to her designed waterline** on
  the cradle (spirit level across the sheer and along a known line), and a
  **centreline** string. Measure and mark from these; the hull surface itself is
  never a reliable straight line.

## 2. Templates and scribing
- **Cardboard or thin plywood templates** first. Cut close, trim with a knife until
  it fits, then trace onto timber.
- **Joggle stick (spiling stick):** a straight stick with a distinctive notched end.
  Lay a template blank (oversize) in place; hold the stick so its point touches the
  hull at a point and trace the stick's notched outline on the template; repeat
  round the shape. On the timber, line the stick up with each tracing and mark where
  its point lands. Join the points. Accurate for complex shapes (bulkheads, floor
  boards, shelf ends).
- **Compass scribe:** for a nearly-fitting part, hold it in place, set a compass to
  the largest gap, and run it along the hull, scribing the hull's shape onto the part.

## 3. Bevels
Use a sliding **bevel gauge** at intervals along the edge, held square to the edge
(not to the bench), to read how the hull surface slopes against the part. Mark each
reading on the part and plane a bevel that changes smoothly between them. Write the
bevels down; they're easy to mirror wrongly from side to side.

## 4. Thwarts and knees
- **Risers** (longitudinal strips fastened inside the frames) carry the thwarts. Check
  they're at the same height port and starboard from the reference.
- Cut the thwart a bit long, offer it up, scribe the ends to the hull/riser with
  bevels, and fit. Fasten through to the riser.
- **Knees** (grown crooks of oak, or laminated) brace thwart to hull side; fit with
  templates and bevels, through-fastened with bronze or copper.

## 5. Coamings
- Coamings bend in plan (and often twist). Use thin, clear stock steamed or
  laminated to the shape, cut to a pattern taken off the deck opening.
- The bottom edge needs a bevel to sit on a cambered deck: mark it with the compass
  scribe.

## 6. Fasteners and bedding
- Use **marine-compatible fastenings** — bronze, copper, or appropriate stainless
  above the waterline — and avoid mixing metals that corrode each other in sea water.
- **Bed** joinery against the hull with a bedding compound where water could
  sit, and leave drainage paths; water trapped behind joinery rots both.
- **Seal end grain** (epoxy or the finish system) on every cut before final fitting.

## 7. Brightwork
Teak, mahogany and similar are traditional for varnished trim. Plan a finish
schedule and keep varnish up; sun degrades bare varnish fast.

## Gotchas
- Fitting the second side by copying the first: hulls are rarely truly
  symmetrical — scribe each side independently.
- Undersize part from too many trial trims: start oversize, remove little.

## Verify
- [ ] Boat levelled, centreline set; measurements from references only.
- [ ] Templates made and fitted before cutting timber.
- [ ] Bevels read at intervals and transitioned smoothly.
- [ ] Thwarts at matching heights; knees fitted and fastened.
- [ ] End grain sealed, joinery bedded, drainage left.
