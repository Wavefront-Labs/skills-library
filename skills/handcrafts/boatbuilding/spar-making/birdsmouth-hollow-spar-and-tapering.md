# Birdsmouth hollow spar and tapering

**Goal:** a light, stiff, round hollow mast or boom from eight staves joined by
a V-notch (birdsmouth), tapered to the designer's diameters and finished round.
The same eight-siding and rounding method applies to solid spars and oar looms.

## 1. Timber
**Sitka spruce** is the classic (light, stiff, straight); Douglas fir is heavier
and stronger; other clear softwoods for small spars. Clear, straight, vertical
grain, seasoned; scarf to length (8:1 to 12:1 scarfs commonly quoted) and stagger
the scarfs between staves.

## 2. Stave geometry
- For an eight-stave spar, each stave's width ≈ the spar's outside diameter
  × 0.414 at that point (the side of an octagon; tan 22.5° × diameter). Wall
  thickness from the plans — often around 1/5 of diameter, adjusted for loads.
  These are the usual published ratios; check your designer's or calculator's
  figures, since builders round them differently.
- Cut the **birdsmouth**: a 90° V-notch along one edge of each stave, depth about
  half the stave thickness, on a table saw with the blade at 45° (two passes) or a
  router bit made for it. The other edge of the next stave sits into it.
- **Taper:** plot the diameters at stations along the spar and taper each stave's
  width to suit, cutting the taper on the edge without the notch (marked from a
  batten through the points), then re-run the birdsmouth or cut the notch first and
  taper the other edge, depending on method.

## 3. Glue-up
- Dry-fit all eight staves around a **straight reference** (a taut string or on a
  straight bench line).
- Fit **solid blocking** inside where fittings will go (mast head, partners, step,
  spreaders, gooseneck) — a hollow spar crushes under a through-bolt otherwise.
- Glue with epoxy (thickened slightly), assemble and pull together with **hose
  clamps**, rope or tape every 150–300 mm. Check the spar is straight and not twisted
  along the reference line before it cures. Clean squeeze-out inside where you can reach.

## 4. Eight-siding and rounding (works for solid spars too)
- Glued up, the spar is octagonal. For a **solid** spar, start from a square tapered
  blank: mark it to eight sides with a **sparmaker's gauge** (two pins in a block
  that, when the gauge is held across the face, mark the lines for the octagon's
  corners — about 29% in from each edge), then plane off the corners.
- Then sixteen sides (plane each corner again), then round with a spokeshave, a
  long round-bottom plane, and finally sanding strips used like a shoe-shine strap.
- Check roundness by eye and with a cardboard template of the diameter at each
  station.

## 5. Finishing
Several coats of spar varnish (UV protective) or paint; seal the ends and every hole
drilled for fittings with epoxy so water doesn't enter the end grain.

## Gotchas
- A twisted glue-up can't be fixed — check before cure.
- No blocking at a fitting = crushed spar.
- Tapers taken off one face only produce a banana spar; take them evenly from all
  sides.

## Verify
- [ ] Stave widths and taper derived from the designer's diameters.
- [ ] Birdsmouths consistent, staves dry-fit tightly round.
- [ ] Blocking placed at every fitting location.
- [ ] Glued straight and untwisted against a reference.
- [ ] Rounded to template at each station; holes and ends sealed.
