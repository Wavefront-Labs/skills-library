# Caulking and paying carvel seams

**Goal:** carvel planks edge to edge, with seams filled by driven caulking that the
wood swells against to make the hull watertight, then finished flush with seam
compound. Too little caulking leaks; too much splits planks or forces them off the frames.

## 1. The seam
- Carvel planks are planed so that adjacent edges touch on the **inside** of the
  hull and open slightly toward the outside — the **outgauge** or caulking bevel.
  A common description is a V opening to around 1/16 in (~1.5 mm) for a small boat's
  thin planking, to more on heavier planking. Plank thickness decides it; follow
  the builder's practice. Seams tight on the inside are what the caulking is driven
  against.
- Clean seams of old compound and caulking (reefing iron) if re-caulking.
- **Prime** the seam (traditionally red-lead or a modern seam primer per the
  compound's instructions) so the compound bonds. Let it dry.

## 2. Material
- **Cotton** for lighter planking (dinghies, launches, yachts): wicks spun loosely
  from cotton caulking.
- **Oakum** (tarred hemp fibre) for heavier planking and ships, often over or
  instead of cotton.
- Amount: one or more strands depending on the seam; experience is the guide.

## 3. Driving the caulking
- **Tuck it in:** with a making iron or the edge of a caulking iron, tuck the strand
  into the seam in small loops or a continuous strand so it lies fully along the
  seam.
- **Drive it:** with the caulking iron and a **caulking mallet** (a long-headed
  wooden mallet with steel bands; its ring tells you how tight the caulking is),
  drive the strand down evenly. A dull sound is loose; a rising, ringing sound
  means the caulking has firmed against the inside of the seam.
- **Set it:** finish with a **crease/setting iron** to consolidate it, leaving it a
  little below the surface to make room for compound.
- Work evenly along the seam; don't caulk one spot hard and leave another loose.

## 4. Paying
- Fill the remainder with **seam compound** (stopping) — flexible compounds for
  below the waterline, with different products for topsides; follow the maker's
  guidance. Push it in with a putty knife, strike off flush.
- Traditional paying with hot pitch is used on decks and some heavier work.

## 5. Over-caulking — the main danger
- Driving too much caulking, or driving hard into a seam without enough outgauge,
  forces planks apart edgewise. When the hull swells in the water, the planks
  can't expand — they **split**, **pull fastenings**, or push away from the frames.
- On an older boat, a seam that's too wide to caulk well may need a **spline**
  (glued wedge of wood) or plank replacement rather than more cotton.

## 6. Launching
A new or dried-out carvel boat leaks until the planks **take up** (swell). Launch and
let her soak; some builders wet the hull for days first, or use a pump while she
takes up. Don't panic-caulk harder on the first day's leaks.

## Verify
- [ ] Seams tight inside, open on the outside to an appropriate outgauge.
- [ ] Seams cleaned and primed before caulking.
- [ ] Caulking tucked full-length, driven evenly to a firm ring, set below the surface.
- [ ] Compound suited to above/below waterline, struck off flush.
- [ ] No over-caulked seams; boat allowed to take up before judging leaks.
