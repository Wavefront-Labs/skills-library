# Building and skinning a skin-on-frame kayak

**Goal:** a light, flexible kayak whose wooden frame is joined with pegs and
lashings and covered in a tight, sealed fabric skin — modern practice derived from
Arctic (Greenland, Aleut) kayaks, which used seal skin over driftwood frames. The
same frame-then-skin principle applies to currachs and coracles.

## 1. Size to the paddler
Traditional kayaks were sized from the paddler's body: e.g. beam from hip width
plus a couple of fists, length a multiple of arm span, depth from hand and fist
measures. Modern builders and courses publish their own ratios — **follow one
system's numbers consistently**; they differ between sources and aren't given here as
authoritative.

## 2. The gunwales and deck beams
- Two **gunwales** (often clear softwood, e.g. western red cedar or spruce) form the
  outline. They're the backbone.
- **Deck beams** are fitted between them, mortised or tenoned into the gunwales and
  pegged (wooden dowels), spreading the gunwales into shape. Their lengths set the
  beam at each point; spring the gunwales into a fair curve, checked from above.
- The **stems** at bow and stern join the gunwale ends.

## 3. Ribs
- Mortises are cut along the gunwales' underside at regular spacing (often around
  100 mm, depending on design).
- **Ribs** of white oak (or ash), sawn or riven thin and straight-grained, are
  **steam-bent** (see `handcrafts.woodworking-joinery.chairmaking.steam-bending-chair-parts`
  for steaming principles) and pushed into the mortises while hot, shaped by hand
  to the hull curve. Work quickly, and set each rib's depth to a fair line — a
  string or batten along the bottom helps.

## 4. Keelson and chines
- The **keelson** runs along the bottom centre, lashed to each rib; **chines**
  (stringers) along the sides. They must lie fair.
- **Lash** with artificial sinew (waxed polyester), with a consistent lashing
  pattern at each crossing. Lashings let the frame flex rather than crack.

## 5. Skinning
- Fabric: **nylon** (e.g. ballistic nylon, which can shrink tight with heat) or
  **polyester**. Lay it over the inverted or upright frame, centred.
- Pull tight and **sew** it along the deck centreline with a strong thread and an
  even stitch (a common pattern is a running/overhand stitch; follow your course or
  plan). Close the stems neatly — the fabric must be under even tension so no
  wrinkles remain.
- Nylon is often heat-shrunk or wetted and dried to tighten it.
- Fit the **cockpit coaming**, sewn or lashed into the skin.

## 6. Seal
- Coat with a waterproof sealer — two-part polyurethane is common, sometimes
  pigmented. Several coats; the first soaks in. Follow the coating's safety data
  (isocyanate in some two-part urethanes needs proper respiratory protection,
  not just a dust mask).

## Gotchas
- An unfair gunwale curve shows through the skin everywhere.
- Rib pushed in cold: it cracks at the bend.
- Tight skin pulls the frame out of shape if one side is tensioned more — work
  symmetrically.

## Verify
- [ ] Dimensions from one consistent sizing system for this paddler.
- [ ] Gunwales sprung fair; beams pegged.
- [ ] Ribs bent hot, set to a fair bottom line.
- [ ] Keelson and chines lashed fair at every crossing.
- [ ] Skin sewn evenly, tight without wrinkles; sealed per coating instructions with correct PPE.
