# Shibori resists with natural dyes

**Goal:** control where dye can't reach, by compressing, binding or blocking cloth,
so a pattern appears. The tightness of the resist is the whole craft. Japanese
names used here for convenience; resist traditions exist worldwide. Draft.

## 1. Cloth and dye choice
- Fine, tightly woven, scoured cloth (cotton lawn, silk habotai, linen) shows crisp
  patterns; thick cloth needs tighter resists.
- **Indigo** is the classic partner (short dips, no heat, so resists hold). Heated
  mordant dyes (madder, cochineal) work too, but heat and long times let dye creep
  under resists, and wooden clamps/threads may shift. Mordant the cloth before tying.

## 2. Itajime (fold and clamp)
- Accordion-fold the cloth lengthwise, then fold into triangles or squares.
- Clamp between shaped blocks (wood, acrylic) with C-clamps or bound strings. The
  blocks resist; folds and edges take dye. Tighter clamp = crisper shape.
- Wet thoroughly before dyeing so dye wicks evenly into open areas.

## 3. Arashi (pole wrapping)
- Wrap cloth around a pole (PVC pipe), on the bias for diagonal stripes, bind with
  string spiralling down, then **push the cloth along the pole** to compress it into
  tight pleats. Dip. Makes rain/storm-like diagonal lines.

## 4. Binding (kumo, kanoko)
- **Kumo** ("spider"): pinch up points, pleat around them, and bind tightly from base
  to tip and back with thread — yields spider-web circles.
- **Kanoko:** many small bound points in rows ("fawn spots"). Labour-intensive; the
  traditional pattern needs very tight, even binding.

## 5. Stitched (nui) shibori
- **Mokume:** parallel lines of running stitch across the cloth (~5 mm apart), each
  pulled tight and knotted; gives wood-grain texture.
- **Ori-nui:** fold, stitch close to the fold, pull up — crisp lines.
- Use strong thread (polyester or waxed) — cotton thread breaks when pulled tight.
  Pull up **all** threads very firmly; loose gathers blur.

## 6. Wax and paste resists
- **Batik:** hot wax (paraffin/beeswax blends; beeswax more flexible, paraffin cracks
  for the "crackle" look) applied by brush or **tjanting**. Dye cold (indigo works);
  remove wax by ironing between papers and boiling out/scraping.
- **Paste resist (katazome/tsutsugaki style):** rice-bran paste through a stencil or
  cone; dried, then dyed (indigo) and soaked off. Paste softens in hot baths.

## 7. Undoing
Rinse while tied, then unbind/unstitch carefully (cut threads with small scissors,
not through the cloth), rinse again, and acid-rinse after indigo.

## Gotchas
- Too-loose resists are the No. 1 failure — patterns look smudged.
- Dry folded cloth resists unevenly; pre-wet.
- Pulling stitched threads with fibre under strain can tear fine silk.

## Verify
- [ ] Cloth scoured (and mordanted if heat dyes used) before tying.
- [ ] Resists tight; stitched threads pulled fully and knotted.
- [ ] Cloth pre-wetted before dyeing.
- [ ] Dye method suited to resist (short cold dips for wax, paste, delicate ties).
