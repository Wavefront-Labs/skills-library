# Formulating and making cold-process soap

**Goal:** a mild, hard, long-lasting bar from oils and sodium hydroxide, with no free
lye. The lye calculation is non-negotiable; everything else is craft. Draft:
widely published practice. Always run your own recipe through a lye calculator.

## 1. Safety first
- **Sodium hydroxide** (lye, NaOH) causes severe chemical burns and blindness. Goggles
  (not glasses), gloves, long sleeves, ventilation. Keep vinegar for surfaces, but for
  skin and eyes: **flush with lots of running water** (eyes 15+ minutes and seek care).
- **Always add lye to water**, never water to lye ("snow falls on the lake"). It heats
  sharply and fumes for a minute — don't lean over it.
- Stainless steel, heat-safe PP plastic or silicone only. Never aluminium (reacts).
- Dedicated tools; no kids or pets in the room.

## 2. Formulate
- Oils give properties: **coconut** (hardness, big bubbles, cleansing — drying
  above ~30%), **olive** (mild, slow to trace, soft at first), **shea/cocoa butter**
  (hardness, creaminess), **castor** (lather boost, ~5%; more is sticky), **palm** or
  its alternatives (hardness). A classic beginner recipe is in the region of olive
  ~40-50%, coconut ~25-30%, a butter ~15-20%, castor ~5%.
- Run the oils by weight through a **lye calculator** (SoapCalc-type or the vendor's).
  Set **superfat** ~5% (oil left unsaponified for mildness). The calculator gives
  NaOH and water amounts. Weigh in grams, not volume.
- **Lye concentration** ~30-35% (or water as "x times lye") is common; less water
  = faster trace and quicker unmoulding, more risk of overheating.

## 3. Make
1. Mix lye solution; let cool to roughly 30-45 °C (cooler gives more working time).
2. Melt hard oils, combine with liquid oils, aim for a similar temperature.
3. Pour lye into oils; **stick blend** in short bursts, stirring between.
4. **Trace:** soap batter thickens so drizzles leave a trace on the surface. Light trace
   for swirls; medium for layers.
5. Add fragrance/essential oils (within IFRA/safety limits for wash-off products),
   colours, then pour into the mould.

## 4. Gel or not
Warm batter can heat itself into **gel phase** (translucent, vaseline-like); gelled
soap is brighter and unmoulds sooner. To force gel: insulate with towels. To prevent:
refrigerate. Partial gel shows as a darker centre ring (cosmetic only).

## 5. Unmould, cut, cure
- Unmould after 24-48 h (high-olive soaps take longer), cut into bars.
- **Cure** on racks with airflow **4-6 weeks** (Castile-type high-olive: longer, many
  wait months). Water evaporates; bars harden and get milder.

## Troubleshooting
- **Seizing** (sudden solid lump): fast-accelerating fragrance (many florals and
  spices). Work fast, plop into the mould; test new fragrances in small batches.
- **Ricing:** small lumps from fragrance reacting; stick blend smooths sometimes.
- **Soda ash** (white powder on top): harmless; steam or plane it off, or spritz
  alcohol after pouring to reduce it.
- **False trace:** hard oils set because they cooled, not because they saponified —
  temperatures too low.
- **Lye pockets / zap:** clear liquid pockets or a "zap" on the tongue tip (old test)
  mean incomplete mixing or a measurement error — discard; pH strips (bars typically
  ~9-10) are the safer check.

## Verify
- [ ] Recipe run through a lye calculator with ~5% superfat.
- [ ] All weighed in grams; goggles and gloves on.
- [ ] Lye added to water; batter to trace by stick blender.
- [ ] Bars cured at least 4 weeks; no clear pockets or zap; pH in normal range.
