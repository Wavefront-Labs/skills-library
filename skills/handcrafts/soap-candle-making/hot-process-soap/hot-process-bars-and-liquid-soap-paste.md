# Hot-process bars and liquid soap paste

**Goal:** finish saponification with heat, so soap is usable sooner (bars) or can
become a clear liquid soap (potassium soap). Same lye safety as cold process
(`handcrafts.soap-candle-making.cold-process-soap.formulating-and-making-cold-process-soap`).
Draft: common methods; verify every recipe in a lye calculator, choosing the right
lye type.

## 1. Hot-process bars (NaOH)
1. Formulate and calculate as for cold process (NaOH). Many add the superfat oil
   **after** the cook, so it remains unsaponified by design.
2. Melt oils in a slow cooker on low. Add lye solution, stick blend to trace.
3. Cover and cook on low. Batter goes through stages: thickens, **"champagne"
   bubbling** and rising edges, then to a translucent, smooth **vaseline** /
   mashed-potato look. Stir occasionally; watch for rising "volcanoes".
4. When the whole mass is translucent and the pH strip reads in normal soap range
   (or a zap test is negative), cooking is done — often 45-90 minutes.
5. Cool slightly, stir in superfat oil, fragrance (lower flash-point fragrances
   evaporate at cook temps — add as late as possible), and colour. A splash of
   yogurt or sugar solution helps fluidity some say.
6. Glop firmly into the mould, bang to remove air pockets. Unmould next day.
   Bars are safe to use sooner but still **cure** 1-2+ weeks for hardness.
Look: rustic, textured top. Swirls are hard.

## 2. Liquid soap paste (KOH)
- Use **potassium hydroxide (KOH)**, which makes soluble soap. KOH has a different
  SAP value from NaOH — the calculator **must** be set to KOH, and note KOH purity
  (often ~90%).
- Oils: coconut and olive are typical; castor and sunflower add clarity/lather.
  Superfat is usually kept low (0-3%) — free oil clouds liquid soap.
- Cook as above until it becomes a thick, translucent paste ("taffy"). It can take
  hours. **Clarity test:** dissolve a spoonful in hot water; clear or slightly cloudy
  = done; milky with oil = keep cooking or lye short.

## 3. Dilute
- Weigh paste; add hot water (often 1:1 to 1:3 paste:water by weight), cover and let
  dissolve overnight. Adjust for viscosity: liquid soap doesn't thicken like
  detergents; a little salt solution can thicken some recipes, others need none.

## 4. Sequester and neutralise
- **Neutralising** with acids (citric, boric) used to be common; many modern makers
  just calculate accurately and skip it. Over-neutralising causes cloudiness and
  separation.
- **Sequestering** agents (e.g. sodium citrate, or citric acid which forms citrate
  in the soap) help with hard-water scum and clarity. Add as a solution.
- Fragrance added after dilution; some cloud the soap.

## 5. Preservation
Diluted liquid soap is water-rich: add a preservative suited to high-pH
products (not all are), or make small batches and use quickly. I'm not certain
which preservatives are reliable at soap pH — check supplier guidance.

## Gotchas
- NaOH-calculated KOH soap (or vice versa) is a dangerous error.
- Overcooking hot-process bars dries them; batter becomes crumbly.
- Volcano: soap rising out of the cooker. Stir down, lower heat.
