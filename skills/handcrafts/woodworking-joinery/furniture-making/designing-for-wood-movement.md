# Designing for wood movement

**Goal:** a solid-wood table, chest or cabinet that does not split, crack, gap or
jam across a year of humid summers and dry, heated winters.

## 1. The rule underneath everything
Wood swells and shrinks with moisture **across the grain** (width and thickness),
almost not at all **along** it. Any design that fixes a wide board rigidly across
its width to something that runs along the grain will eventually split or bow.

## 2. Estimate how much it will move
- **Tangential** movement (flatsawn width) is roughly **twice radial**
  (quartersawn width) for most species.
- A rough workshop figure: a 500 mm wide flatsawn oak or ash panel can change by
  several millimetres between a damp summer and a centrally heated winter.
  Exact figures come from species shrinkage tables (e.g. the USDA Wood Handbook)
  and the expected swing in equilibrium moisture content — treat the "several mm"
  here as an order of magnitude, not a design value.
- Build at the moisture content the piece will live at. A cheap pin meter tells you
  if stock is far off; let timber acclimatise in the workshop (weeks, stickered)
  before final dimensioning.

## 3. Choose and orient stock
- **Quartersawn** for parts where stability matters (drawer sides, door stiles,
  panels in tight frames): less movement, less cupping.
- In glued-up tops, alternating growth-ring orientation is debated; more important
  is matching colour/grain and using stock at the same moisture content.
- Flatsawn boards cup **away from the heart** as they dry; face the heart side
  where a slight cup will be held flat by the fixings.

## 4. Construction details that allow movement
- **Tabletops:** fix to the aprons with **buttons** (wooden blocks in an apron
  groove), steel **Z-clips** or **figure-8 fasteners** — never glued or screwed
  rigidly along the cross-grain apron. Leave gaps in the button groove on the long
  aprons so the top can slide. Screw through slotted holes if screwing at all.
- **Fix the centre:** pin or glue the top at the middle of the end aprons so
  movement goes equally both ways and the overhang stays even.
- **Frame-and-panel:** the panel floats in a groove, **unglued**; size it so it is
  not bottomed-out when swollen and doesn't show an unfinished edge when shrunk.
  Pre-finish panels so a dry-season shrink doesn't reveal bare wood. Space-balls
  or a dot of glue at centre top and bottom keeps it from rattling.
- **Breadboard ends:** glue only the centre portion of the tenon (or the middle
  peg), and **elongate the peg holes** in the tenon towards the outer edges.
- **Cross-grain battens and cleats** under a seat or chest lid: screw through
  slots, or use a sliding dovetail glued only at one end/the centre.
- **Drawers:** fit wider in winter, tighter in summer; a drawer fitted snug in
  dry January will jam in August.
- **Mouldings across a case side:** glue only the front few cm, then nail/pin or
  slot-screw the rest.

## 5. Finishing doesn't stop movement
Finish slows moisture exchange; it doesn't prevent it. **Finish both faces** of
tops and panels equally or the slower-drying side will cup the board.

## Gotchas
- Plywood and MDF don't move like solid wood, so mixing them with solid wood in
  a rigid cross-grain joint is the same problem in reverse.
- A tight frame-and-panel made in a humid workshop can split its frame in a dry
  house; plan for the house, not the shop.

## Verify
- [ ] Every cross-grain junction on the drawing has a named movement allowance.
- [ ] Stock at the expected service moisture content before final milling.
- [ ] Top fixed at the centre; fasteners elsewhere free to slide.
- [ ] Panels floating, pre-finished, sized for both extremes.
- [ ] Both faces of every wide component finished alike.
