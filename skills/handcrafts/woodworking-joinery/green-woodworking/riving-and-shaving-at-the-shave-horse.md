# Riving and shaving at the shave horse

**Goal:** turn a green log into straight, strong blanks (chair legs, rungs,
spokes, pegs) whose fibres run unbroken end to end, then shave them to size fast.

## Why rive at all
A split follows the fibres; a saw cuts across them wherever they wander. Riven
stock has **no run-out**, so it bends and takes load far better at small sections.
This is why chair rungs and wheel spokes were traditionally cleft.

## 1. Pick the log
- Straight, **knot-free**, ideally fresh-felled: oak, ash, sweet chestnut, hickory,
  and many other ring-porous species split readily. Twisted (spiral) grain shows
  in the bark — avoid it, the split will spiral too.
- Work it within weeks of felling, or keep the log ends sealed and shaded.

## 2. Halving, quartering (wedges and maul)
- Read the end grain. Start a split **through the pith** with a wedge or axe on the
  end, along a check if one exists.
- Drive a second wedge into the opening down the side to follow it, leapfrogging
  wedges. A wooden **glut** helps in the wider opening without damaging steel tools.
- Halve, then quarter. Always split into roughly **equal halves** — an unequal split
  runs out towards the thinner side.

## 3. Smaller stock with the froe and brake
- **Froe** (an L-shaped splitting blade struck with a wooden club) for the
  sub-divisions: radial splits (along the rays, like orange segments) and
  tangential splits (along the growth rings).
- A **cleaving brake** (a forked or triangulated frame holding the stock at an
  angle) lets you lever the froe and use the stock itself as a lever.
- **Steering a split that runs off:** if the split heads out of the thinner side,
  put downward pressure on the **thicker** side (press it into the brake's fork) —
  the split moves towards the side under bending stress. Lever, look, adjust.
  This is the core skill, and it takes many blanks to get a feel for.

## 4. Shaving at the shave horse
- The shave horse is a bench with a foot-operated clamp: push with your feet to
  grip, release to turn the work. It holds instantly, so you can turn the blank
  after every few strokes.
- **Drawknife**, bevel up for straight work and deep cuts, bevel down for hollows
  and control. Pull with straight arms, using your back, and skew the blade to
  slice.
- Work **with the grain** from the middle toward each end; if the knife digs in
  and lifts fibres, turn the blank end for end.
- Shape square → octagonal → round. **Spokeshave** for final shaping.

## 5. Drying strategy
- Make the joint so drying works for you: a green, slightly oversize rung driven
  into a hole in a wetter leg that shrinks around it; or **dry the tenon** (e.g.
  near a stove) and leave the mortised part green, so the mortice shrinks onto
  the tenon. Exact timing depends on species and conditions.
- Stored blanks: stack loosely with air flow, out of sun, ends sealed.

## Gotchas
- Splitting from a knot end — split from the clean end.
- Steel wedges struck with a steel hammer can chip; use a maul or wooden beetle on
  wooden gluts, and wear eye protection when striking steel.
- A blank with a slight twist can still make a fine rung; a run-out across the
  thin dimension cannot.

## Verify
- [ ] Log straight, knot-free, green, ends sealed if stored.
- [ ] Every split roughly halves the piece.
- [ ] Run-out corrected by levering the thicker side in the brake.
- [ ] Blanks shaved with the grain; no fibres cut across along the length.
- [ ] Drying plan chosen for each joint (dry tenon, green mortice).
