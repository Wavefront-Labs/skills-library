# Surface prep and a wipe-on oil/varnish finish

**Goal:** a durable, even, low-sheen finish on new furniture that doesn't show
sanding scratches, glue spots or blotches, built without spray equipment. (For
shellac rubbed to a high gloss see
`repair-maintenance.furniture-restoration-upholstery.french-polishing.french-polishing-and-shellac-repair`.)

## 1. Surface prep: finish shows everything underneath
- Best surface: a **sharp smoothing plane** or **card scraper**, which slice fibres
  cleanly. Sand afterwards only lightly.
- Sanding: start at the coarsest grit that removes the defect (often 120 or 150 for
  machined surfaces), then go up **without skipping more than one grade**
  (120 → 150 → 180 → 220). Stop around 180–220 for oil finishes on most hardwoods;
  going much finer can reduce penetration (a common opinion; the effect is debated).
- Sand **with the grain**; a random-orbit sander leaves swirls if you rush the grits.
- **Find glue spots**: wipe the surface with mineral spirits/white spirit (or
  naphtha) — dried glue shows as pale patches that would reject the finish. Scrape
  them off.
- **Raise the grain** for water-based finishes (and often for any finish on
  surfaces that will be cleaned with water): dampen with a cloth, let dry, sand
  lightly at the final grit.
- Break sharp arrises with a light pass of sandpaper; finish won't stay on a knife edge.

## 2. Choose the finish
- **Pure oils** (boiled linseed, pure tung): very low protection, need many coats,
  long cure times; easily repaired.
- **Oil-varnish blends** ("Danish oil" and similar): easy, low build, modest
  protection.
- **Wiping varnish** (oil-based varnish or polyurethane thinned roughly 1:1 with
  mineral spirits): builds a real protective film in thin coats; the best wipe-on
  choice for table tops.
Product names don't reliably tell you what's in the can; read the label/safety
data sheet. Test on an **offcut of the same board**, sanded the same way.

## 3. Blotch-prone woods
Pine, cherry, birch and maple can blotch with stains and sometimes with oil. A
**washcoat** — a thin coat of dewaxed shellac (~1 lb cut) or a commercial
conditioner — before colour evens it. Test first.

## 4. Applying
- Dust off with a vacuum and a slightly damp cloth (or tack cloth for oil-based).
- **Flood on, wait, wipe off**: apply generously with a rag, let it soak ~5–15 min
  (check product), then wipe off **all** the excess across and then along the grain.
  Excess left on goes gummy.
- **Let each coat dry fully** (often overnight; longer in cold or humid conditions).
- Lightly denib between coats with fine abrasive (e.g. 320–400 grit or a grey
  abrasive pad), wipe clean.
- Coats: 3 is a starting point for oil-varnish blends; wiping varnish on a table top
  may want 4–6 thin coats.
- Do undersides and insides at least once: uneven finishing contributes to cupping
  (see `handcrafts.woodworking-joinery.furniture-making.designing-for-wood-movement`).

## 5. Curing and rubbing out
Dry to the touch is not cured: oil-based finishes keep hardening for weeks. Avoid
heavy use, rubber feet or plastic on the surface during that time. For a satin feel,
rub out the final coat with fine steel wool or a pad and wax.

## Safety: oily rags
Rags with drying oils (linseed, tung, oil-varnish blends) can **self-heat and ignite**.
Lay them flat outside to dry, hang them singly, or submerge in water in a sealed metal
can. Never leave a bundle in a bin. Ventilate; check the product's safety sheet.

## Verify
- [ ] Surface planed/scraped or sanded through grits, no scratches visible under raking light.
- [ ] Glue spots revealed with spirits and removed.
- [ ] Finish tested on an offcut of the same timber.
- [ ] Excess wiped off each coat; each coat fully dry before the next.
- [ ] All faces finished; oily rags dried flat or submerged.
