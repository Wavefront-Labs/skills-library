# Setting up a kanna (Japanese plane)

**Goal:** a new or neglected kanna that takes fine, full-width pull-stroke
shavings with the blade adjusted by hammer taps. A new kanna is **not** ready to
use from the box — setup is expected and part of the craft.

## Parts
- **Dai:** the wooden body (usually white or red oak).
- **Kanna-ba:** the blade, a hard steel edge laminated to a soft iron back, with a
  **hollow-ground back (ura)** leaving a narrow flat land at the edge.
- **Uragane:** the chipbreaker, held by a pin across the throat.

## 1. Fit the blade to the dai
- The blade is held by friction against grooves in the dai's cheeks. Too tight
  splits the dai; too loose and it slips.
- Insert the blade with light taps. Look at its fit: blacken or chalk the blade's
  sides, tap in, withdraw, and pare the shiny spots in the grooves lightly with a
  chisel. Aim for it to project slightly at the mouth with firm hand pressure and
  moderate taps. Go slowly — wood removed can't go back.

## 2. Flatten the back (ura-oshi) and ura-dashi
- The back's flat land at the edge must be flat and polished. Flatten on a flat
  coarse surface (a steel plate with carborundum grit, or a diamond plate), then
  progress through waterstones.
- When the flat land at the cutting edge disappears from sharpening, it's restored
  by **ura-dashi**: tapping the bevel side (the soft-iron area, never the hard steel
  edge) just behind the edge with a hammer on an anvil to push the edge slightly
  toward the back, then flattening again. Strike only the soft iron: a blow on the
  hard steel can crack it. Learn this on a cheap blade first.

## 3. Sharpen the bevel
Waterstones, coarse to fine (e.g. ~1000 → ~6000 → finer), holding a consistent
bevel angle — often around 25–30°, varying with blade and wood. Remove the burr on
the back on the finest stone only, flat.

## 4. Tune the dai sole (dai-naoshi)
The sole is **not** made flat. A common pattern for a finishing plane: contact at
the leading edge and just in front of the mouth, with the area between and behind
the mouth relieved by a very small amount (fractions of a mm). Do it with a
**scraper plane** (a dai-naoshi kanna) or a card scraper, checked with a straightedge
and light. Re-check with the blade in tension: the dai flexes when the blade is
seated, so tune with it inserted (retracted just below the sole).
Wood moves with humidity: expect to re-tune the sole periodically.

## 5. Set the chipbreaker
Seat the uragane so its edge sits a very small distance behind the cutting edge
(roughly 0.1–0.2 mm for fine finishing; further back for rough work). It must contact
the blade fully across, or shavings jam beneath it.

## 6. Adjusting by hammer
- **Advance:** tap the blade's head (top end).
- **Retract:** tap the back end (heel) of the dai, at the top surface behind the blade.
- **Lateral:** tap the side of the blade's head to skew.
Use a small hammer; light taps. Sight down the sole against a light to see the blade
projection.

## 7. Using it
Pull, don't push. Stand behind the work, hold the dai with one hand forward over
the blade, and pull with your body in one continuous stroke. Shavings should be
continuous and even across the width.

## Gotchas
- Leaving the blade tensioned in the dai long-term in dry conditions can split the dai;
  back it off when stored.
- Grinding the back on a coarse wheel can remove the hollow; work the back only on
  stones/plates.
- Specific angles and relief depths here are typical starting points; makers'
  recommendations vary.

## Verify
- [ ] Blade seats firmly with taps; dai not split; no slop.
- [ ] Back land flat and polished right to the edge.
- [ ] Sole relieved per pattern, checked with blade seated.
- [ ] Chipbreaker fully in contact, set close for finishing.
- [ ] Full-width, thin, continuous shavings on a pull stroke.
