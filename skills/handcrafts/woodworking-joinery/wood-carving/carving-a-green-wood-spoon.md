# Carving a green-wood spoon

**Goal:** a usable eating or cooking spoon, carved from freshly cut wood with
three tools, without cutting yourself or letting it crack as it dries.

## Tools and wood
- **Carving axe** (small, sharp, single or flat-bevel), **straight sloyd knife**
  (e.g. a Mora-type with a ~60–75 mm blade), **hook/spoon knife**.
- Wood: **fresh-cut**, fine-grained, diffuse-porous hardwood — birch, cherry,
  sycamore/maple, alder, lime. A branch 75–100 mm across. Green wood carves
  several times easier than dry. Store blanks in a plastic bag in a cool place, or
  frozen, if you can't carve straight away.
- **Sharpness matters more than anything**; a dull knife needs force, and force
  is what slips. Strop often.

## 1. Split and plan
- Split the branch through the pith with the axe and a baton. **Remove the pith**
  — it cracks.
- Spoon orientation: the bark side or the split face can become the bowl's top.
  Bark side up gives a gentle natural crank; many carvers choose a crooked branch
  so the grain follows the spoon's bend, which makes a stronger neck.
- Draw the top-view outline on the flat face.

## 2. Axe work
- Stand to the side of a chopping block, axe in your hand, blank held so **your
  hand is always above the axe's path** and the cut finishes into the block.
- Remove the bulk: side profile first (thickness, crank), then top outline. Use
  stop cuts at the neck so a split can't run into the bowl.
- Aim to get within a few mm of final shape; axe work is faster than knife work.

## 3. Knife grips (learn these, not "cutting toward yourself" randomly)
- **Forehand / power grip:** blade away from you, wrist locked, arm does the work.
- **Chest lever:** both hands at the chest, blade edge out, opening the chest and
  closing the shoulder blades — powerful and controlled.
- **Thumb push:** the off-hand thumb pushes the back of the blade, small fine cuts.
- **Pull stroke / paring toward the thumb:** with the thumb protected by the
  wood, and your thumb **below** the edge's path — only with a stop built in.
Always know where the blade goes if the wood suddenly gives.

## 4. Shape with the straight knife
Refine the handle, neck and the bowl's outside. Cut **downhill** with the grain;
if the surface tears, reverse direction. At the neck, cut from both sides toward
the narrowest point.

## 5. Hollow the bowl
- Hook knife, cutting **across the grain** (side to side) in the middle of the bowl,
  and with the grain toward the ends, always from the rim down into the bottom.
- Keep walls even — around 3–5 mm for an eating spoon — by feeling with fingers
  either side. Leave the rim slightly thick at first; it is where cracks start.

## 6. Dry slowly
- Dry a few days away from heat and sun — in a paper bag or among wood shavings
  helps. Fast drying cracks the bowl rim and the end grain.
- Do final finishing cuts when dry: the knife leaves a crisp, faceted surface on
  dry wood that sanding can't match (sanding is optional).

## 7. Finish
Soak in a **food-safe drying oil** (raw linseed sold for food use, walnut oil,
or tung) and wipe off; let it cure for days to weeks. Note: cured drying oils are
widely regarded as food-safe; check your specific product's labelling.
Oily rags can **self-heat and catch fire** — lay them flat outside to dry or
put them in water.

## Verify
- [ ] Pith removed; the grain runs through the neck, not across it.
- [ ] Every cut has a known blade path that misses your body.
- [ ] Bowl walls even, rim not paper-thin.
- [ ] Dried slowly without cracks; finished with a food-safe oil; rags disposed of safely.
