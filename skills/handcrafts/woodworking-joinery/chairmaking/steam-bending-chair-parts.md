# Steam-bending chair parts

**Goal:** a bow, crest rail or back post bent to the form's curve without
fracturing, and holding its shape after drying.

## 1. Stock is 90% of success
- Species that bend well: **white oak, red oak, ash, hickory, beech, elm**. Many
  softwoods and most exotics bend poorly.
- **Green or air-dried**, never kiln-dried. Kiln drying sets the lignin and the
  wood fractures on the outside of the bend. Green, riven stock is ideal.
- **Straight grain running the full length.** Riving (splitting) follows the fibres;
  sawn stock with grain running out of the face will fail where the grain exits.
  Any knot, check or grain run-out on the **outside (tension) face** is where it
  breaks.
- Dress to near-final size before steaming; you can't plane a bent part easily.

## 2. The steam box
- A box of plywood or pipe, a source of steam (a kettle element on a can, a
  wallpaper steamer, or a purpose-built generator), a rack inside so parts don't
  sit in condensate, and a small vent so steam flows.
- Rough rule: **about one hour per inch (25 mm) of thickness**, from the time the
  box is fully steaming. Green wood can need less. Over-steaming makes some species
  mushy and they crush on the inside of the bend.

## 3. Form and strap
- The **form** is the inside shape, slightly **overbent** to allow for springback
  (a few degrees to ~10% tighter — learn it per species and part by trial; this
  figure is a starting guess).
- A **compression strap**: a steel band (spring steel or mild steel ~1–1.5 mm)
  with end stops/blocks and levers. The strap takes the tension that would otherwise
  break the outside fibres, forcing the wood to compress on the inside instead.
  Tight bends need a strap; gentle curves (some ladder-back posts) can be done without.
- Have **clamps, wedges and pegs ready** before the part comes out.

## 4. The bend
- Work **fast**: you have perhaps a minute before the wood cools and stiffens.
  Gloves on, part straight from the box into the strap.
- Start the bend from the **centre** of the form and work out, keeping the strap
  tight to the wood, bending smoothly and continuously — stopping and restarting
  is where creases happen.
- Clamp or peg at each point as it touches the form.
- A crackle as you bend usually means the outside fibres are lifting; keep going
  and look later — a few lifted splinters can be glued; a fracture across the grain
  can't be saved.

## 5. Drying and setting
- Leave it on the form until it **holds its shape** — usually at least overnight,
  often a few days to a week in a warm, dry spot. Some makers transfer to a
  drying jig to free the form.
- Expect some springback once released; this is why you overbend.

## Gotchas
- Tight bends on the **flat face** of a riven bow are easier than on the edge.
- Bending across a knot or checked end: trim the ends long and cut defects away
  after bending.
- Sawn kiln-dried hardwood from the timber yard is the most common cause of failure.

## Verify
- [ ] Stock green or air-dried, straight-grained, defect-free on the tension face.
- [ ] Steamed roughly one hour per inch with a full head of steam.
- [ ] Strap and form used; part bent continuously from centre outward.
- [ ] Left to dry and set before release; springback within your allowance.
