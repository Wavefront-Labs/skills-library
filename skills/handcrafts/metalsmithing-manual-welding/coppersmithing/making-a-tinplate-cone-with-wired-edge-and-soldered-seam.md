# Making a tinplate cone with a wired edge and soldered seam

**Goal:** a tapered tinplate (or thin copper/brass) vessel — cup, funnel, measure —
laid out from geometry, with a rolled-wire rim for stiffness and a tight seam that
holds liquid.

## 1. Develop the pattern (radial-line method)
For a frustum with bottom diameter *d*, top diameter *D*, vertical height *h*:
1. Draw the side elevation full size. Extend the two sloping sides until they meet at
   the apex. Measure the slant lengths from apex to the top rim (*R*) and to the base (*r*).
2. On the sheet, swing arcs of radius *R* and *r* from one centre.
3. Arc length of the outer edge = π × *D*. The included angle of the sector is
   θ = 360° × *D* / (2*R*). Mark it with a protractor, or step the circumference off
   with dividers in 12–16 small chords (slightly short — add 1 mm).
4. Add allowances:
   - **Wired edge:** about 2.5 × wire diameter on the top arc (common workshop rule;
     check against a test piece — sheet thickness changes it).
   - **Lap seam:** 5–6 mm on one straight side for a soldered lap; for a **grooved
     (lock) seam**, 3 × seam width split across both sides (folds of 4–5 mm).
   - **Base:** if the bottom is to be "paned" or soldered in, leave a 4–5 mm flange
     allowance on the base disc rather than the side.
5. Clip the corners where the seam meets the wire allowance at 45° so the wire tucks
   in without bulk.

## 2. Cut and prepare
- Cut with straight and curved snips (or bench shears), keeping the cut line visible.
- Deburr edges with a fine file. Tinplate: avoid scratching the tin coating; it is
  the rust protection.

## 3. Form the cone
- Roll it over a **blowhorn** or **funnel stake**, or a pipe of suitable taper,
  working with a rawhide/wooden mallet. Keep the apex of the curve in line with the
  pattern's centre or the seam will spiral.
- For a grooved seam: fold opposite hooks (one up, one down) on a folding bar or in
  the vice with hardwood jaws, hook them together, and close with a **hand groover**
  whose groove is ~1.5 mm wider than the seam.

## 4. Wire the edge
1. Turn the wire allowance over a **hatchet stake** to about 90°, then bend the wire
   (galvanised or tinned iron, 2–3 mm for a cup) to the rim circumference and lay
   it in the fold.
2. Close the metal over it with the mallet, then finish tight by tapping with a
   hammer face held at an angle, or run it in a **wiring/burring machine**.
3. The wire should be fully enclosed, edge of the sheet tucked under, no gap.

## 5. Soft-solder the seam
- Clean bright with steel wool where tin coating is missing. Flux with **killed
  spirits** (zinc chloride) or a paste flux suited to the metal.
- Heat a **soldering copper** (bit 300–500 g for this scale) in a gas stove until a
  green flame tinge shows; file the faces bright and tin them (dip in flux, touch
  solder).
- Tack the seam every 50 mm, then draw the copper slowly along the seam, feeding
  solder ahead; let the heat soak so solder *sweats* through the joint, not sits on it.
- For copper/brass vessels holding hot liquid, rivet first (copper rivets 2–3 mm,
  25 mm pitch, set with a rivet set and snap) and solder over the rivets.
- Neutralise flux residue: wash in hot soapy water, rinse. Zinc chloride corrodes.

## Gotchas
- Cone won't close: θ calculated on mean rather than outside diameter, or seam
  allowance forgotten. Test with paper.
- Wavy wired edge: wire allowance too long; fold not at 90° before wiring.
- Dry joints: copper not hot enough, faces dirty, or work acting as a heat sink.
- Food or drinking vessels: use **lead-free** solder (tin-copper or tin-silver).

## Safety
Zinc chloride flux is corrosive — gloves and goggles. Solder fumes and flux smoke:
ventilate. Sheet edges cut deeply: gloves when handling blanks.

## Verify
- [ ] Paper or card mock-up closes with the planned seam allowance.
- [ ] Rim wire enclosed all round, no bulges at the seam.
- [ ] Seam solder visible on both faces (sweated through).
- [ ] Water test: no weeping after 10 minutes.
- [ ] Flux washed off; lead-free solder on anything for food or drink.
