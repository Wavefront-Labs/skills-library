# Silver soldering and brazing with a torch

**Goal:** a full-penetration, void-free joint between steel, copper, brass or
stainless parts using capillary silver solder, or a filleted bronze braze where gaps
are wide — first time, without overheating the work.

## 1. Pick the process
- **Silver solder (silver brazing),** ~600–800 °C: capillary joints, close-fitting
  parts, model engineering, fittings, tool tips. Strong and neat.
- **Bronze brazing / braze welding,** ~870–900 °C: brass-type rod builds a fillet
  across gaps and on cast iron repairs; needs oxy-fuel heat on anything sizeable.
- **Soft solder,** 180–250 °C: sealing, not strength (see coppersmithing seam work).

## 2. Joint design
- **Clearance:** 0.05–0.15 mm (2–6 thou) for silver solder. Too tight — alloy can't
  flow in; too loose — capillary fails and strength drops.
- **Lap length:** about 3× the thinner part's thickness for lap joints.
- Provide a way for flux and air to escape; blind holes trap gas.
- Fixture the parts so they cannot move (wire, clamps of stainless, firebrick).

## 3. Alloy and flux
- **General silver solder:** ~55% silver, cadmium-free (e.g. the type often sold as
  "55" or EN ISO 17672 Ag155 — confirm grade with the supplier). Melting range
  roughly 630–660 °C; flows freely.
- **Copper to copper:** a silver-copper-phosphorus alloy ("Sil-Fos" type) is
  self-fluxing on copper. **Never use phosphorus alloys on steel or nickel** — brittle joints.
- **Flux:** general-purpose silver-soldering paste for most work (active roughly
  550–800 °C); high-temperature or "stainless" flux for long heats and stainless;
  borax-based flux for bronze brazing.
- **Avoid cadmium-bearing solders** — the fume is highly toxic. Old stock may contain it.

## 4. Flame
- Air-propane torches handle small work (up to ~25 mm diameter steel joints) when
  the job is surrounded by firebrick to hold heat.
- **Oxy-acetylene/oxy-propane:** set a **neutral flame** — inner cone sharp and
  rounded, no feather. A slightly carburising (feathered) flame is kinder for
  brazing; oxidising (short, hissing, pointed cone) burns flux and copper.
- Use a soft, bushy flame for even heating rather than a tight hot spot.

## 5. The sequence
1. Clean both surfaces bright (emery, file, degrease). Flux and oxide hate each other.
2. Flux both faces and assemble. Place solder as a pre-formed ring or snippets
   at the joint mouth if you can.
3. Heat the **heavier part** first and broadly; flux goes clear and glassy (around
   600 °C) — a useful indicator.
4. Bring the joint to temperature, then touch the rod to the **work, not the flame**.
   It should flash in and run towards the hottest side. Draw it through by moving
   the heat.
5. One continuous fillet visible at the far side means the joint has filled.
6. Let it lose its colour, then quench in water or pickle to crack off flux (do not
   quench cast iron or parts that will crack or distort).

## Faults
- **Solder balls and sits on top:** the work is too cold — the flame melted the rod.
- **Flux burns black and stops flowing:** too long a heat or too hot; use
  higher-temperature flux, heat faster.
- **Solder runs everywhere:** surfaces cleaned too widely; limit with typists'
  correction fluid or graphite pencil as a stop-off.
- **Joint leaks/voids:** clearance too wide or trapped gas.
- **Earlier joint opens:** use a higher-melting alloy first, easier grades after.

## Safety
Oxy-fuel: check regulators and flashback arrestors, purge each line before lighting,
light fuel first, shut oxygen first (follow the equipment maker). Fluoride fluxes
give off irritant fume — ventilate. Goggles shade 4–5 for gas work. Keep a fire
extinguisher and a clear bench.

## Verify
- [ ] Gap measured or fit checked; parts fixtured.
- [ ] Alloy cadmium-free; no phosphorus alloy on steel.
- [ ] Flux went glassy before solder was applied; solder melted by the work.
- [ ] Fillet visible on the far side of the joint.
- [ ] Flux fully removed; joint inspected or test-loaded.
