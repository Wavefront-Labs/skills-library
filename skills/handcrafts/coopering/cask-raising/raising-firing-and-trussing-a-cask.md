# Raising, firing and trussing a cask

**Goal:** turn a set of jointed staves into the familiar bellied cask shape, with
every joint closed and no stave cracked, ready for heading. Traditional practice
summarised; details vary between cooperages and countries.

## 1. Raising up
- Set the **raising hoop** (a strong iron hoop sized to one end of the cask) on the
  floor or a block.
- Stand staves inside the hoop one at a time, edge against edge, leaning against
  the hoop. Mix wide and narrow staves around the ring.
- When the ring closes, the last stave should **just fit**. If there's a gap, add
  a narrow stave; if too tight, swap a wide one for a narrower one. Never force a
  stave in by hammering it edgewise — it splits or opens other joints.
- Drop a second (and third) hoop over the top to hold the staves; drive these
  down with a **hammer and driver** (a blunt steel wedge tool) a little at a time,
  working round the hoop.
- The result is a "rose" or "tulip" — staves held together at one end, splayed open
  at the other.

## 2. Firing (heat-bending)
- The staves must bend to close the open end. Heat and moisture plasticise the oak.
- **Firing:** stand the cask over a **cresset** (an open iron basket fire of wood
  shavings and offcuts). Wet the staves inside and out; the heat, kept moderate and
  even, steams and softens them. Keep them damp to avoid scorching too early (unless
  the cask is to be charred later, it's still not the moment).
- Some cooperages **steam** casks instead (a steam bell or chamber) before drawing in.
- Time varies with stave thickness and species — commonly tens of minutes. You learn
  by feel: staves that are ready flex under pressure.

## 3. Drawing in (trussing)
- Pull the open end closed with a **windlass** (a rope or wire loop around the open
  end, tightened by a winch) or by driving successively smaller **truss hoops**
  (heavy wooden or steel hoops) down over the open end.
- Draw in steadily and evenly: you are bending all staves at once.
- Once closed, drive working hoops down to hold the shape, and keep the cask over
  the fire to **set the bend** before it cools.

## 4. Watch for failures
- A crack across the grain during the draw usually means the stave had run-out, a
  defect, or wasn't hot/wet enough. Stop, release, replace the stave and redo the
  draw; a cracked stave can't be patched in wet work.
- Joints that open during the draw point to bevel errors from jointing
  (`handcrafts.coopering.stave-preparation.listing-and-jointing-cask-staves`).

## 5. Next steps
With the shape set: cut the **chime** (bevel on the ends), the **croze** (head groove),
and toast or char the interior if required
(`handcrafts.coopering.toasting-charring.toast-and-char-levels-and-leak-testing`), then
head up.

## Safety
Open fire, hot iron and a heavy object under stress. Heat-resistant gloves,
eye protection when driving hoops, and clear space around the cresset. The windlass
rope stores energy; stand clear of its line.

## Verify
- [ ] Ring closes in the raising hoop without forcing a stave.
- [ ] Staves heated and moist through before drawing in.
- [ ] Draw-in steady and even; working hoops driven to hold.
- [ ] Bend set by heat before cooling; no cracked staves, no open joints.
