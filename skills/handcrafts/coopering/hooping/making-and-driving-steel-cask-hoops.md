# Making and driving steel cask hoops

**Goal:** hoops that sit at the right positions, hold the staves tight, and don't
slip or cut into the wood. Usual hoop set on a traditional cask: **chime hoops**
at each end, **quarter hoops** next in, and **bilge hoops** near the belly
(names and number vary by cask type).

## 1. Material
- Mild steel hoop iron — flat strip, often galvanised for durability. Width and
  thickness scale with cask size; a small cask uses lighter strip. Exact sizes vary
  by maker; match an existing cask if repairing.
- Rivets: soft iron or steel, sized to the strip.

## 2. Measuring
- Measure the circumference **at the hoop's final position** with a flexible tape
  or a strip, with the existing (working) hoops driven tight.
- Allow for the overlap at the rivet joint, then **make the hoop slightly small**:
  it's driven on to tension. How much smaller is learned by trial on that cask
  size — too small and it won't drive home; too big and it's loose.

## 3. Flaring (the taper)
- A cask is a tapering shape at each hoop, so a flat hoop must be **flared**: one
  edge longer than the other so it sits flat on the cone of staves.
- The cooper hammers along one edge of the strip on a bick iron or anvil to
  stretch it, or uses a hoop-flaring machine. Too little flare: the hoop bites at
  one edge and sits proud at the other.

## 4. Riveting
- Overlap the ends, punch or drill holes (usually two rivets), and set the rivets
  from the inside so the outside is neat, peening them over on a bick.
- Make sure the overlap is oriented so that when driving the hoop down, the
  driver doesn't catch the end.

## 5. Driving
- Place the hoop, then drive with a **hammer and driver**, working round the hoop
  in small steps — never all at one point, which kinks the hoop or tilts it.
- Listen: the sound changes from a dull knock to a ringing note when the hoop is
  tight on the staves.
- Drive the chime hoops so they sit a consistent distance from the end; drive
  bilge hoops toward the belly.

## 6. Re-tightening and re-hooping
- Casks that have dried out often have loose hoops. First try **soaking** the cask
  (fill with water for a day or more — "swelling it up") before driving the hoops
  down.
- If a hoop still won't tighten, it's stretched or the staves have shrunk; cut the
  rivets and re-rivet shorter.
- **Before** driving hoops down on a dry cask, check heads and staves for cracks.

## Traditional wooden hoops
Split hazel, willow or chestnut, shaved thin, bent round and fastened with a
notched lock — used on many traditional dry casks and some tubs. Lighter but less
durable; driven the same way.

## Gotchas
- Hoop iron edges are sharp; gloves.
- Overdriving a chime hoop can split the stave ends or crush the chime.
- Specific sizes and the "how much smaller" allowance are deliberately not given —
  they depend on cask size and the strip; practise on a scrap hoop first.

## Verify
- [ ] Each hoop measured at its final position, made slightly undersize.
- [ ] Flared so it sits flat across its full width.
- [ ] Rivets set cleanly; overlap oriented for driving.
- [ ] Driven evenly all round to a ringing note; no kinks or tilt.
- [ ] Loose casks soaked before re-tightening; cracks checked first.
