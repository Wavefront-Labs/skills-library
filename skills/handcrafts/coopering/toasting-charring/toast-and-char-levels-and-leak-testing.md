# Toast and char levels, and leak testing

**Goal:** give the cask interior the heat treatment a winemaker or distiller has
specified, consistently, and deliver a cask that holds liquid without weeping.

## 1. Toasting vs charring
- **Toasting**: a long, moderate heating of the interior (often over a small fire
  or gas burner, with the cask open-ended) that browns the wood without burning.
  It degrades oak polymers into flavour compounds — vanilla, caramel, spice, smoke —
  and softens harsh tannins. **Wine** casks are typically toasted.
- **Charring**: a short, intense fire that sets the interior surface alight,
  creating a carbon layer. Char filters and the red layer beneath contributes
  colour and flavour. **American whiskey** (e.g. bourbon, which by US rules uses new
  charred oak containers) is the best-known use.
- Many spirit casks are toasted first then charred.

## 2. What levels mean (and why to be careful)
- Toast: **light / medium / medium-plus / heavy** — increasing time and
  temperature, increasing colour depth into the wood.
- Char: commonly **#1 to #4** (sometimes higher), by duration of flame — roughly
  from tens of seconds to around a minute; #4 is often called "alligator char" for
  the cracked, scaly surface.
- **These labels are not standardised between cooperages**: one's "medium" is
  another's "medium-plus". Agree the spec with the customer by sample stave or
  reference to that cooperage's own scale, and record times and temperatures.
  The durations above are indicative, not authoritative.

## 3. Controlling it
- Fire size and fuel (oak offcuts and shavings are traditional), distance from the
  wood, time, and keeping the cask turning.
- Water mist or a lid to put out a char at the right moment.
- Toast: monitor the interior colour and surface temperature (infrared
  thermometer); some cooperages use controlled gas or convection toasting for
  consistency.
- Timing starts from when the interior reaches temperature, not when the fire is lit.

## 4. Leak testing
- After heading up, **fill with water** (often warm) and let it stand; look for wet
  patches ("weeps") on staves, joints and heads.
- Many cooperages also **pressure test**: seal the bung, apply low air pressure, and
  brush soapy water or immerse to spot bubbles. Low pressure only — high pressure
  can push a head out.
- New casks often weep slightly at first and **swell tight** within hours; a weep
  that persists needs repair.

## 5. Stopping leaks
- **Joint leak:** drive hoops tighter, or open the joint slightly and insert a
  strip of **flag (reed/rush)**.
- **Wormhole or small hole:** drive a tapered wooden **spile** (peg) into it.
- **Head leak at the croze:** re-flag the croze, re-drive hoops.
- **Cracked stave:** replace the stave — no reliable patch for wet work.

## Safety
Charring involves a tall, intense fire inside a wooden vessel: fire extinguisher or
water to hand, eye and face protection, no flammable clothing. Let the cask cool
before handling.

## Verify
- [ ] Toast/char spec agreed against the cooperage's own reference scale.
- [ ] Time, temperature/flame and fuel recorded for repeatability.
- [ ] Cask filled and inspected after swelling; pressure test at low pressure.
- [ ] Every weep traced and repaired by tightening, flagging or spiling.
