# Croze cutting and heading up

**Goal:** flat, liquid-tight heads seated in grooves (crozes) at each end of a
raised cask. Traditional hand practice summarised; machine cooperages do this with
crozing machines and head-turning lathes.

## 1. Prepare the ends
- With the cask trussed and set, level the stave ends with an adze or saw so the
  end is flat and square to the cask's axis.
- **Chime:** cut a bevel on the inside of the stave ends (the chime) with a chiming
  adze or knife. This guides the head in and lets the croze sit a set distance in.
- **Howel:** a plane-like tool that levels and smooths a band inside the end,
  giving a consistent surface (and thickness) for the croze.

## 2. Cut the croze
- The **croze** is a tool that rides on the end of the staves and cuts a groove
  round the inside at a fixed distance from the end.
- Take the groove to a consistent depth all round; too shallow and the head
  pushes out under pressure, too deep and the stave is weakened at the end.

## 3. Find the head diameter
- Step round the inside of the croze with **dividers**: adjust until **six steps**
  go exactly round the circumference. The setting is then the radius
  (six chords of a circle's radius make the circumference of the inscribed hexagon).
  This accounts for the croze depth and gives the head's cutting radius.
- Add a small allowance for the head's edge bevel to sit into the groove.

## 4. Make the head
- Heads are usually several boards (middle piece, cants, and so on) of the same
  quality oak as the staves, rays oriented for tightness.
- Joint the edges straight and **dowel** them together (wooden or metal dowels, no
  glue traditionally); some traditions add a strip of **rush or flag (reed)** between
  joints as a seal.
- Scribe the circle with the divider setting from step 3 and saw it out.
- **Bevel the edge** on both faces, more on the outside, leaving a thin edge that
  fits snugly into the croze.

## 5. Heading up
- Loosen the hoops at that end slightly so the staves spring open a fraction.
- Insert the head at an angle, lever it level into the croze using a heading tool or
  a "flagging iron" — some coopers lay rush/flag in the croze first.
- Drive the hoops back down to close the staves onto the head.
- The second end: same procedure, inserted through the open end, then the cask
  must be checked for leaks (water test, see
  `handcrafts.coopering.toasting-charring.toast-and-char-levels-and-leak-testing`).

## Gotchas
- A head slightly too large prevents the staves closing — it shows as an open joint
  at the chime. Too small leaks at the croze.
- Split or loose dowels let the head pieces work, leaking along the joint.
- Measurements for croze depth and chime angle are not universal; they follow cask
  size and tradition.

## Verify
- [ ] Ends level; chime and howel even round the circumference.
- [ ] Croze depth consistent all round.
- [ ] Dividers set so six steps go exactly round the croze.
- [ ] Head pieces jointed and dowelled tight, edges bevelled to fit.
- [ ] Hoops driven to close staves onto both heads; cask tests tight.
