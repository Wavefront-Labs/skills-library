# Listing and jointing cask staves

**Goal:** a set of staves that, raised into a ring and bent, close into a
liquid-tight cask with no light showing through the joints. The cooper uses
almost no measurement here: **the joint angle and curve make the cask.** This is
a summary of traditional hand coopering; modern cooperages machine most of it.

## 1. Stock
- **Oak**, quartersawn or riven so the **medullary rays run across the stave's
  width** (parallel to the face). Rays are what stop liquid seeping through the
  wood itself; flatsawn oak leaks through the face.
- Liquid-tight ("wet") work: white oak (American *Quercus alba*) or European oak
  (*Q. robur*/*petraea*). Red oak has open vessels and leaks — it's for dry casks.
- **Seasoned**: traditionally air-dried outdoors for one to several years.
  Unseasoned staves shrink after the cask is made and open the joints.

## 2. Listing (shaping the outline)
- Staves are wider at the middle (the **bilge/belly**) and taper to each end.
  The cooper shapes the outline with a side axe or drawknife, by eye or with a
  pattern. Staves within one cask can vary in width — wide and narrow staves are
  mixed; their **angles** are what must match.

## 3. Backing and hollowing
- **Backing:** the outer face is shaped convex across its width with a backing knife
  (a curved drawknife) on a horse, so the outside is part of a circle.
- **Hollowing:** the inner face is made concave with a hollowing knife, especially
  at the ends where the stave will be thinnest after the croze and chime are cut.
  This also thins the stave where it must bend most.
- Thickness: consistent through the set — thick staves don't bend into line with
  thin ones.

## 4. Jointing (the critical step)
- Edges are planed on a **cooper's jointer**: a long, inverted plane fixed with
  the sole up at an angle, the stave pushed down over it.
- Each edge needs two things at once:
  1. **The bevel (shooting angle):** the edges must point at the cask's centre.
     The angle depends on the cask's diameter at that point — a smaller cask needs a
     steeper bevel. Coopers check it with a gauge/template or by experience.
  2. **The curve (the "shot" along the length):** edges are shaped so the stave is
     widest at the middle and the curve matches the cask's profile; the taper here
     is what lets the stave bend into the belly shape.
- Try two staves together: the joint should close along its length, with neither
  rocking nor showing light.

## Gotchas
- A bevel slightly too steep or too shallow, repeated over 25–35 staves, leaves the
  ring unable to close or overlapping.
- Wide staves bend less easily; narrow staves are used where the bend is tightest.
- Specific angles, widths and counts depend on cask size and tradition — the
  numbers aren't given here because they aren't universal; cooperage apprenticeships
  pass them on as patterns.

## Verify
- [ ] Oak of the correct species and ray orientation for the cask's use.
- [ ] Seasoned stock; consistent stave thickness across the set.
- [ ] Outline listed, back convex, inside hollowed at the ends.
- [ ] Every edge jointed to the centre-pointing bevel and length curve.
- [ ] Adjacent test pairs close without light.
