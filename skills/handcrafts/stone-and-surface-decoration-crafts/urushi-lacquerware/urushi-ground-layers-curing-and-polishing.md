# Urushi: ground layers, curing and polishing

**Goal:** a durable, deep lacquer surface on wood (or other cores) built from many thin,
fully cured coats of true urushi, finished to a gloss or a wiped finish. The craft takes
years to master; this is the working skeleton.

## 1. Safety first: urushiol
Urushi is the sap of *Toxicodendron vernicifluum* — the same allergen family as poison ivy.
Uncured urushi causes severe, delayed (1–3 day) **allergic contact dermatitis**; sensitivity
can increase with exposure.
- Nitrile gloves (double), long sleeves; never touch your face; clean tools and spills with
  vegetable oil then soap, not solvent on skin.
- Work in a dedicated area; keep it away from children and pets.
- **Fully cured** urushi is inert and food-safe; uncured or under-cured is not.

## 2. How urushi cures (the surprising part)
Urushi doesn't dry by evaporation; it **polymerises** via the enzyme laccase, which needs
**warmth and high humidity** — roughly 20–30 °C and 70–85% relative humidity is the typical
working range (approximate; follow your supplier). It cures in a **furo/muro**: a cabinet
kept humid with wet cloths or a humidifier. Too dry = it stays tacky for days; too humid/hot
= it cures too fast, wrinkles and darkens.
- Thin coats only: a thick coat skins over and wrinkles (chijimi).

## 3. Substrate and grounds (shitaji)
1. **Wood core**: well-seasoned, stable wood. Seal with thinned raw urushi (ki-urushi) wiped
   in — **kijigatame**.
2. **Reinforce** vulnerable edges and joints with hemp or linen cloth laid in urushi mixed
   with rice paste (**nunokise**).
3. **Ground layers**: urushi mixed with powdered fired clay (**jinoko** coarse, **tonoko**
   fine) and/or water-mixed tonoko (**sabi**). Apply with a spatula in thin layers, cure,
   and sand flat with whetstone or abrasive between layers.
4. **Kokuso** (urushi + wood flour + rice paste) is used to fill and build up, as in kintsugi.
The quality of the ground decides the flatness of the final gloss — most of the hours go here.

## 4. Middle and top coats
- Filter urushi through **washi** (twisting the paper) to remove dust and lumps.
- Brush thin coats with a human-hair urushi brush (**hake**), in a dust-free space.
- After each coat cures (often 1–3 days), **sand** with fine abrasive, charcoal (togidashi
  charcoal) or waterstone, wet, until the dust nibs and brush marks are gone. Repeat.
- **Roiro** (refined black urushi without oil) is used for high-polish finishes; it's applied,
  cured and then polished rather than left as brushed.

## 5. Finishing options
- **Fuki-urushi / suri-urushi** (wiped): raw urushi rubbed in and wiped off, cured, repeated
  many times. Shows the grain; practical for bowls and chopsticks.
- **Roiro-shiage** (mirror polish): sand top coat with fine charcoal, then polish with fine
  powders (e.g. tonoko or polishing compound) and oil on the palm, wipe on a thin coat of raw
  urushi (suri-urushi), cure, polish again — several cycles.
- **Maki-e**: drawing a design in urushi and sprinkling metal powders while tacky — a
  separate specialist technique built on this foundation.

## Gotchas
- Synthetic "lacquer" (cashew, nitrocellulose) is not urushi; techniques don't transfer.
- Dust is the enemy: wet the floor, dedicated clothing, filter urushi each time.
- Sanding through a coat to the ground means rebuilding that area.
- Exact curing ranges, ground recipes and coat counts vary by school and supplier — treat
  the numbers here as orientation.

## Verify
- [ ] Gloves, cleanup oil and a separate work area are in place before opening urushi.
- [ ] Each coat fully cured (no tack, no smell) before sanding or recoating.
- [ ] Furo temperature and humidity logged and in the supplier's range.
- [ ] Ground sanded flat with no hollows (check with raking light).
- [ ] Finished surface fully cured before handling ungloved or food use.
