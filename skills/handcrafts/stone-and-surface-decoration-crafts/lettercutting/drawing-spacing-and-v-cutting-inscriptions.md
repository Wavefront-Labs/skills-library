# Drawing, spacing and v-cutting inscriptions

**Goal:** a legible, evenly spaced, well-proportioned inscription cut with clean,
sharp v-section letters that catch light and shadow — or carry gilding or paint.

## 1. Material
- **Slate** (e.g. Welsh): fine-grained, takes very fine letters, cuts crisply, black or
  grey — the lettercutter's favourite for small lettering and indoor/outdoor plaques.
- **Limestone** (Portland, Hopton Wood): warmer, softer, needs slightly larger, bolder letters.
- **Sandstone**: coarser; bigger letters; can be crumbly at sharp arrises.
- **Granite**: v-cut largely by sandblast or pneumatic — not hand-chisel-friendly.

## 2. Design and setting out
1. Agree the wording in writing (spellings, dates, punctuation) with the client before
   anything else; for memorials, check cemetery/churchyard regulations (sizes, materials,
   permitted wording and finishes).
2. Draw the whole inscription **full size** on paper. Decide letter height from viewing
   distance (bigger for a headstone read from a path than a plaque at eye level).
3. Letterform: Roman capitals (in the Trajan tradition) are the classic model; draw them with
   consistent stroke weights and serifs, not traced from a font.
4. **Optical spacing**, not mechanical: equalise the *area* of space between letters.
   Two straight stems (H I) need the most space; round to round (O O) least; open letters
   (L, T, A, V) tucked in. Squint or turn the drawing upside down to see uneven patches.
5. Line spacing: often roughly letter height or slightly less; centre or range lines by eye.

## 3. Transfer
Fix the drawing to the stone, slip carbon or graphite-backed paper underneath, and trace
over every line with a hard pencil or stylus. Firm up lines on the stone with a fine pencil
or white chinagraph (slate) before cutting.

## 4. V-cutting
- Tools: **letter chisels** (flat, tungsten tipped for hard stone) in several widths, a light
  **dummy mallet** (iron, or nylon for soft stone).
1. Cut a **stop cut** or start at the centre line of a stroke, angling the chisel to about
   60° from the surface, so the two walls of the stroke meet at an exact central "v" bottom.
   The walls typically make roughly a 90° v-angle (~45° each side) — keep it consistent;
   that consistency is what makes letters read as one family.
2. Work from the centre line out to the drawn edges, both walls, keeping the v centred.
   Wider strokes are deeper automatically.
3. Serifs: cut into the serif last with a narrower chisel, flaring the v to a sharp end.
4. Curves: work with a narrow chisel in short overlapping cuts, rotating your body.
5. Keep the arris (edge) clean: final light paring cuts at the edge, never bruising the surface.
6. Clean pencil lines away with a soft eraser or water when done.

## 5. Finishing the letters
- **Gilding**: size the cut letters (oil size), wait for tack, apply gold leaf (23.5 ct for
  outdoor durability) with a gilder's tip, burnish gently, clean off excess with a soft brush.
  See handcrafts.stone-and-surface-decoration-crafts.gilding.water-and-oil-gilding-on-gesso-and-bole.
- **Paint**: signwriting enamel or proprietary letter paint, wiped flush from the surface.
- **Plain**: many slate and stone inscriptions are best left uncoloured, read by shadow.

## Gotchas
- Spelling errors cannot be undone — proofread three times and get written client approval of the drawing.
- Chisel too upright = a broken arris; too flat = shallow, weak letters.
- Rushing curves produces flats — the most visible amateur flaw.
- Angles here are typical working figures; adapt to stone and letter size.

## Verify
- [ ] Client signed off the full-size drawing with exact wording.
- [ ] Spacing looks even when viewed upside down from a distance.
- [ ] Every v bottom runs down the centre of the stroke; walls clean.
- [ ] Arrises sharp with no chips or bruises.
- [ ] Finish (gold, paint, plain) matches the agreed spec and regulations.
