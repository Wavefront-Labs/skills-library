# Roughing out and modelling stone with hand tools

**Goal:** take a block to a finished carving by removing stone in controlled stages
— coarse to fine — without breaking off what you need, and without silicosis.

## 1. Choose and read the stone
- **Soft** (beginner-friendly): soapstone/steatite, alabaster (gypsum). Carve with rasps,
  files and knives; little or no mallet work. Alabaster is soluble — keep it out of the rain.
- **Medium**: limestones (e.g. Portland, Bath, Hoptonwood), many sandstones. The classic
  mallet-and-chisel stones.
- **Hard**: marble, granite — slower, need tungsten-tipped tools and more skill; granite
  largely uses pneumatic/abrasive methods.
- Look for **vents**, cracks, shakes, inclusions and the **bedding plane** (sedimentary
  layers). Tap it: a ringing sound is sound stone; a dull thud warns of a flaw. Wet the
  surface to reveal veins. Sedimentary stone should usually be oriented so its natural
  bed is horizontal in the finished piece, especially outdoors (it weathers by delaminating).

## 2. Plan
- Make a **maquette** (clay or plasticine) at scale, and draw the main views on the block.
  Carve from the outside in, keeping the highest points longest. Draw lines back on after
  every session — they get carved away.
- Proportional dividers or pointing transfer measurements from maquette to stone.

## 3. Roughing out
- **Pitching tool** + lump/club hammer: knocks off big pieces from a square edge. Set the
  tool a little in from the edge, angled so the force breaks stone off outward.
- **Point** (punch): struck with a hammer/mallet at a steep angle, in parallel furrows,
  removing stone quickly. Work **toward** the mass of stone, never toward a thin projection
  or edge — stone breaks away in front of the tool.
- Keep the block well supported on a **banker** (sturdy bench), on sandbags or a rubber
  mat so it doesn't ring and crack.

## 4. Modelling
- **Claw (tooth) chisel**: lower angle, in criss-cross directions, to bring forms to near-final
  shape; the teeth leave a grid that shows the surface truly.
- **Flat and bullnose chisels**: refine planes and curves. Hold at a low angle; light, regular
  blows with a **dummy** (round soft-iron or nylon mallet) for chisels with mallet heads, a
  hammer for steel-headed ones.
- Use the **right tool for the stone**: tungsten-carbide tips for hard and abrasive stone;
  plain steel wears fast on sandstone. Keep edges sharp on a diamond plate or grinder.

## 5. Finishing
- **Rasps and rifflers** shape fine forms and undercuts.
- **Abrasives**: wet-and-dry papers or diamond pads progressively (e.g. 80→120→240→400 and up);
  wet sanding controls dust and shows the polish. Marble and alabaster take a high polish;
  limestones are usually left with a fine tooled or honed surface.
- Wax (microcrystalline) for indoor pieces if desired; nothing on outdoor limestone.

## Safety
- **Respirable crystalline silica** from sandstone, granite and many stones causes silicosis.
  Wet methods, local extraction, and **FFP3/P100 respirator** for any dust-making work;
  sweep never — vacuum with an H-class/HEPA unit.
- Eye protection always (flying chips); hearing protection with power tools; gloves for
  abrasives. Lift blocks with proper technique or equipment.

## Gotchas
- Undercuts and thin projections are where carvings break — leave them thick until last.
- Hitting toward an edge spalls off the edge: work in from edges toward the centre mass.
- Soapstone may contain asbestiform minerals in some deposits — buy from suppliers who
  certify asbestos-free stone.

## Verify
- [ ] Stone sound when tapped; bed orientation decided.
- [ ] Highest points still have stock until the final stages.
- [ ] Tool marks progress from point to claw to flat to abrasive with no coarse marks left.
- [ ] Dust controlled (wet or extracted) and respirator used for dusty work.
