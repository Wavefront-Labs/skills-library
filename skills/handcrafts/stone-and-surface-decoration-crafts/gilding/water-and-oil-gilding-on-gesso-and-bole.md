# Water and oil gilding on gesso and bole

**Goal:** a continuous gold surface — matte (oil) or mirror-burnished (water) — that
adheres well and suits where it'll live (indoors, outdoors, handled or not).

## 1. Choose the method
| | Oil gilding | Water gilding |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | any sealed surface (wood, metal, stone, glass, letters) | traditional gesso + bole on wood |
| Adhesive | oil size (linseed-based, or water-based acrylic size) | water + a little alcohol, reactivating bole's glue |
| Finish | soft matte sheen, cannot burnish | can be burnished to a mirror |
| Outdoors | yes (with high-carat gold) | no — water sensitive |
| Skill / time | moderate | high, slow |

## 2. Leaf
- **Genuine gold** in carats: 22–24 ct for outdoors (doesn't tarnish), 23–23.5 ct common.
  Lower carats (12–18 ct) are paler and may tarnish outdoors.
- **Loose leaf** (on a gilder's cushion, cut with a gilder's knife, picked up with a squirrel
  hair **gilder's tip**) for water gilding and fine work. **Transfer (patent) leaf** pressed on
  tissue — easier for oil gilding and in breezes.
- **Imitation leaf** (Dutch metal, schlag — brass) is cheaper and thicker, handled with
  fingers/tissue, but **tarnishes** — always seal it with shellac or lacquer.
- Leaf is extremely thin; any draft moves it. Close windows, switch off fans, don't breathe on it.

## 3. Oil gilding
1. Seal porous surfaces (shellac, primer) and make them smooth — gold shows every flaw.
2. Brush a thin, even coat of **oil size**. Sizes are labelled by **open time** (e.g. 1–3 hour
   "quick", 12 hour "slow"); slow sizes give a brighter, better result.
3. Wait until it reaches **tack**: drag a knuckle lightly — it should feel tacky, with a
   faint squeak, but not transfer size to your skin. Too wet = leaf drowns and looks dull.
4. Lay leaf, overlapping slightly; tamp down with a soft brush or cotton wool.
5. After the size has fully cured (a day or more), **skew** off loose overlaps with a soft brush.
   Fill holidays (gaps) by re-sizing small spots.

## 4. Water gilding (on wood)
1. **Gesso**: rabbit-skin glue (soaked, then warmed — never boiled) mixed with gilder's whiting.
   Apply many thin coats (often 8–12) warm, each before the last fully dries. Dry, then sand and
   recut to a flawless surface.
2. **Bole**: fine clay (red, yellow, grey, black) mixed with dilute glue; several thin coats
   over gesso, polished smooth. Red bole warms the gold and shows through wear.
3. Brush **gilder's liquor** (water + a little alcohol, sometimes a trace of glue) on a small
   area; lift the leaf on the tip and lay it on the wet bole — it snaps down.
4. When the leaf and bole are just dry enough (timing is learned by feel), **burnish** with
   an **agate burnisher** in gentle strokes to a mirror. Too early: it tears; too late: no shine.
5. Leave some areas matte (unburnished, or **matted** with size) for contrast.

## 5. Finishing
- Distressing and **toning** (rottenstone, thin washes, shellac tints) to age new gilding on frames.
- Genuine high-carat gold needs no sealing; sealing dulls it. Imitation leaf must be sealed.

## Gotchas
- Gold reveals the substrate's flaws — 90% of the work is preparation.
- Gesso cooked too hot loses strength; overly strong glue in gesso cracks.
- Oil gilding at the wrong tack is the most common failure — test on a scrap with the same size.
- Coat counts and timings vary with climate and recipe; follow your materials' guidance.

## Verify
- [ ] Method matches the location (water gilding indoors only).
- [ ] Surface smooth and sealed before sizing.
- [ ] Tack tested on a scrap before laying leaf.
- [ ] No holidays (bare spots) under raking light.
- [ ] Burnished areas are mirror-bright and unbroken; imitation leaf sealed.
