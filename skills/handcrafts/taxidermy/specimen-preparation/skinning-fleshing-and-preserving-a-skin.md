# Skinning, fleshing and preserving a skin

**Goal:** a clean, thin, degreased, preserved skin with no hair slip, ready to fit
to a form. Everything you do wrong here shows on the finished mount, and most
failures (slipping hair, grease burn-through, cracking) start in the first 48 hours.
General practice summarised; chemical products and times vary by supplier — follow
the label of whatever tan or pickle you use.

## 1. Field care decides everything
- **Cool fast.** Bacteria loosen hair follicles ("slippage") within hours in warmth.
  Get the carcass shaded and cooled; skin as soon as practical.
- **Don't cut the throat** or drag a cape-intended animal; blood and abrasion stain
  and damage hair. For a shoulder mount, cut the skin well behind the shoulders
  (generous — you can trim, never add).
- If you can't skin now: freeze whole or rough-skinned in a sealed bag with air
  pressed out. Freezer burn (dry, leathery patches) ruins thin areas like ears
  and lips.

## 2. Skinning cuts
- **Dorsal cut** (along the back of the neck) for shoulder mounts — seam hides in
  the mane. **Ventral cut** for life-size mammals that will be viewed from the side.
- Work the face last and slowly: cut ear butts close to the skull, cut eyelids
  right at the bone so lids stay intact, cut lips deep at the gum line, and free
  the nose cartilage leaving it attached to the skin.

## 3. Turning, splitting, fleshing
- **Turn ears** (separate skin from cartilage to the tip), **split lips** (open
  the double layer), and **split the nose** — preservative must reach every layer.
  Unsplit lips are the classic slip spot.
- **Flesh:** remove all meat and fat on a fleshing beam with a fleshing knife, or
  a rotary fleshing machine on larger skins. **Thin** thick hides (neck, shoulders)
  so they tan evenly and stretch onto the form; too thin and you'll cut hair roots
  (shows as bald patches from the hair side).

## 4. Salt and pickle (for tanned mounts)
- Rub fine non-iodised salt into every surface, roll flesh-to-flesh, drain on a
  slope overnight, shake out, re-salt; dry salting sets hair and pulls moisture.
- Rehydrate and put into an **acid pickle** (commercial "safety acid" type) at the
  manufacturer's pH and time. Check pH with strips; a rising pH means it's spent.
- **Degrease** fatty species (bears, raccoons, waterfowl) with a degreasing agent
  or solvent soak; residual grease bleeds through finished paint and rots stitches.
- Neutralise per the product, then **tan** (brush-on or soak tans for taxidermy).
  Brush-on tans are faster but less forgiving; soak tans give more even results
  on thick hides — my understanding, not tested across brands.

## 5. Dry preservative alternative
Small birds and some small mammals are often preserved with a borax-based dry
preservative rubbed into the flesh side instead of tanned. Quick and traditional,
but the skin is less durable and may be less pest-resistant; museums and some
pros prefer tanned skins for anything that will be handled.

## Gotchas
- Hair coming out with a light pull = slip. Stop and salt immediately; you may
  save it, but large slipped areas usually mean a new cape.
- Iodised salt can discolour; rock salt doesn't penetrate.
- Wear nitrile gloves and eye protection with acids and tans; work ventilated.
- **Legal status:** many species need permits, tags or proof of legal take, and
  protected birds are usually illegal to possess at all. Check local law before
  accepting any specimen.

## Verify
- [ ] Ears turned to the tips, lips and nose split, eyelids intact.
- [ ] No flesh or fat left; thick areas thinned evenly, no hair-root damage.
- [ ] Pickle pH monitored; skin degreased where needed and tanned per label.
- [ ] Gentle tug test anywhere on the skin pulls no hair.
- [ ] Specimen's legal paperwork recorded with the job.
