# Tying a Turk's head and covering a handle

**Goal:** a tight, even Turk's head band on a handle, rail, bracelet mandrel or
tiller — and a full grip covering underneath it — the bread-and-butter of sailors'
fancywork. Once this works you can grow it to more leads and bights.

## 1. Leads and bights
- **Leads (L):** how many strands you see side by side crossing the band.
- **Bights (B):** how many scallops sit around each edge.
- A single-strand Turk's head exists only when L and B share **no common factor**:
  3L4B, 3L5B, 3L7B, 4L5B, 5L4B work; 3L6B or 4L6B do not.
- More bights = a band that suits a fatter cylinder; more leads = a wider band.

## 2. Material and length
- Use cord that holds shape: tarred bank line or marline, waxed cotton, 550
  paracord (core removed for flatter knots) or leather lace.
- No reliable formula exists for length: tie the knot once (one pass) in cheap
  string on the same cylinder, unpick it, measure, multiply by the number of passes
  (usually 2 or 3), and add 30–40 cm for handling.

## 3. 3-lead Turk's head as a closed plait (on a cylinder)
1. Hold the standing end against the cylinder with your thumb. Take a **round turn**
   around it, the second turn crossing **over** the first on the front, making an X.
2. Take the working end round the back and come forward again; you now have three
   parts on the front — left, middle, right — like the start of a three-strand plait.
3. **Plait:** cross the left part over the middle, then the right part over the
   (new) middle, tucking the working end **over-under** so every crossing alternates.
   Rotate the cylinder away from you as you go so you always work on the front.
4. Each completed left-and-right crossing adds bights. Stop when the number of
   bights on each edge equals your target (5 for a 3L5B) and the working end lies
   alongside the standing end, running parallel to it — the knot is now closed.
5. Check: every crossing must alternate over, under, over, under. A
   two-overs-in-a-row means a missed tuck; back up and fix it now.

## 4. Doubling, tripling and tightening
1. **Follow the lead:** run the working end alongside the standing part, staying
   on the same side of it throughout and copying every over and under, until you
   are back at the start. Do a third pass for a bolder band.
2. Tighten in stages, starting at the standing end: with a fid or blunt
   marlinspike, work slack along the path, pass by pass, round and round. Two or
   three gentle rounds beat one hard one.
3. Trim both ends and hide them under a crossing; a dab of glue or (on synthetics)
   a careful heat seal locks them.

## 5. Covering a handle beneath it
1. Lay the handle bare and roughen glossy paint; a wrap of tape stops sliding.
2. **French whipping (half-hitch spiral):** make a continuous series of half
   hitches, each pulled snug against the last, all hitches going the same way so
   the knots spiral round the handle as a ridge. Keep tension even.
3. Alternatives: **coachwhipping** (an over-two-under-two flat sennit with 4–8
   cords) for a chequer pattern, or **cockscombing** for rails.
4. Finish each end of the covering with a Turk's head that hides the start and end.

## Faults
- **Band loose and floppy:** cylinder diameter too big for the L/B chosen, or
  tightened in one hard pull rather than gradually.
- **Strands cross, look messy:** working end changed sides during doubling.
- **Ends visible:** cut long and bury under a crossing on the inside pass.
- **French whipping twists into a barber pole unevenly:** inconsistent hitch direction.

## Verify
- [ ] L and B have no common factor; number of bights counted on both edges.
- [ ] Every crossing alternates over/under on the single pass.
- [ ] Doubled/tripled passes lie parallel and never cross each other.
- [ ] Tightened gradually; band sits snug and does not slide by hand.
- [ ] Ends buried and secured; covering fully hidden at each end.
