# Keeping an ancestral shrine and pouring libation

**Goal:** maintain the relationship between the living family and its ancestors —
the practice at the heart of many traditional religions across Africa, East Asia,
the Americas and Oceania — in the form your family or community keeps it.

Forms differ widely: West African libation (Akan, Yoruba, Ewe and many others),
ancestral tablets and seasonal rites in Chinese, Vietnamese and Korean traditions,
Día de Muertos ofrendas in Mexico, and countless family-specific practices.
Authority sits with family elders, lineage heads and ritual specialists. Where they
teach something different from this outline, follow them.

## 1. Who the ancestors are
Typically those of the lineage who lived well, died well and are remembered by name
— often those who had descendants. Many traditions distinguish recent dead (still
known personally) from the long-dead collective ancestors. Know your genealogy: write
out the names back as far as the family remembers, with relationships and death dates.

## 2. Setting a shrine
Common elements (choose per tradition):
- A dedicated, clean place — a shelf, table, room or outdoor spot; in many
  households it is kept at or above head height and never used for other purposes.
- Photographs or **ancestral tablets** bearing names (in Chinese practice, wooden
  tablets in the home or lineage hall).
- A water vessel, candle or lamp, incense, a place for food offerings.
- Objects belonging to the ancestors; for Día de Muertos ofrendas, marigolds, salt,
  papel picado and the favourite foods of the dead.
Many traditions require only the eldest or a designated person to tend certain shrines.

## 3. Daily and weekly tending
- Fresh water daily or weekly; replace offerings before they spoil.
- Light incense or a candle; greet the ancestors by name; tell them family news.
- Offer the first portion of special meals; in many households, place a serving or a
  little drink before family members eat.
- Remove old offerings respectfully — commonly placed outside on earth, at the
  base of a tree, or eaten by the family afterward, depending on custom.

## 4. Pouring libation (West African form, in outline)
Libation is a spoken prayer accompanied by pouring a liquid — water, palm wine,
schnapps or gin — onto the earth or at the shrine.
1. The officiant is usually an elder or a person designated by the family.
2. Hold the vessel in the right hand; the community stands quietly.
3. **Call** the Creator, the earth, the deities, and the ancestors by name, in order
   of seniority — and often the ancestors of the land where you stand.
4. Pour a little at each invocation.
5. **State the purpose** — thanks, a request for blessing, protection at a naming,
   wedding, funeral or meeting.
6. **Close** with the community's spoken response (affirmations such as "truly" or
   "so be it" in the local language; the exact words vary by community and were not
   verified for this draft — learn them from your elders).
7. The remainder may be shared or poured out fully.
Libation opens many public occasions in Ghana and across the diaspora.

## 5. Seasonal and memorial rites
- Annual death-day rites and offerings.
- Seasonal festivals: Qingming (tomb-sweeping, early April) and Chongyang in Chinese
  tradition; Chuseok and Seollal in Korea (*charye* table rites); Día de Muertos
  (1–2 November); Odwira and other yam/harvest festivals honouring royal and lineage
  ancestors in Ghana.
- Grave care: cleaning, repainting names, offerings, and recalling stories.

## 6. Passing it on
Teach children the names, stories and protocols. The relationship is maintained by
remembrance; a forgotten ancestor is, in many teachings, truly lost.

## Gotchas
- Mixing protocols from different traditions in one shrine without guidance.
- Using shrine space or objects casually (as décor, or moving them without asking).
- Visitors photographing family shrines without permission.
- Leaving perishables to rot; it's widely seen as neglect.

## Verify
- [ ] Family genealogy written and kept with the shrine.
- [ ] Shrine placement, contents and keeper agreed with family elders.
- [ ] Regular tending schedule followed; old offerings disposed of by custom.
- [ ] Libations name ancestors in correct order of seniority and state their purpose.
- [ ] Annual memorial and seasonal dates are calendared.
