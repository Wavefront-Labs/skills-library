# Making offerings and keeping an altar

**Goal:** understand and carry out the giving that sustains relationships with
spirits and deities in traditional religions — routine offerings, first-fruits rites
and altar care — and know how sacrifice is handled within the traditions that
practise it.

Offering is grounded in **reciprocity**: the spirits, deities and land give life and
protection; people return a share. What is offered, by whom, and how, is set by each
tradition, often determined by divination (e.g. Ifá or obi divination telling what
an orisha requires). The specialists and elders of your tradition direct this.

## 1. Kinds of offering
- **Daily or routine:** water, a little food or drink, a lit candle, tobacco, flowers.
- **Votive:** promised in return for help, given when the request is fulfilled.
- **Prescribed (by divination):** e.g. Yoruba *ẹbọ* (sacrifice/offering) or *adimú*
  (food offerings) specified by a diviner — items, quantity and place.
- **First-fruits:** the first of the harvest given before the community eats.
- **Sacrifice of animals:** in some traditions, for major occasions, performed by
  trained specialists.

## 2. Altar or shrine care
1. Keep the altar clean and dedicated; nothing unrelated placed on it.
2. Arrange items as your tradition directs — e.g. orisha vessels in their colours,
   ancestral items separate from deity shrines (many traditions keep them apart).
3. Offer at set times (weekly days sacred to particular deities are common).
4. Speak while offering: greet, name what you give, say why.
5. Remove offerings after the prescribed time (often a day, or when the candle has
   burned down) and dispose of them as taught: at a crossroads, in a river, the
   bush, buried, or eaten by the community.
6. Note offerings and outcomes in a notebook if your teacher asks.

## 3. First-fruits rites (examples in outline)
- **New Yam festivals** (e.g. Igbo *Iri ji*): after the yam harvest, the first yams are
  offered to the deities and ancestors, often by the eldest man or titled leader,
  before the community eats new yams; then feasting, masquerades, dance.
- **Zulu *umkhosi wokweshwama*:** the first-fruits ceremony held by the Zulu king;
  no one eats new crops until it is held.
- Household form: set aside the first of any harvest or the first portion of a
  special meal for the spirits.

## 4. The Andean despacho (pago a la tierra)
In Quechua and Aymara communities, a "payment to the earth" to Pachamama and the
mountain spirits (*apus*), usually by a ritual specialist (*paqo*, *yatiri*):
1. A paper is laid out; items are placed in a meaningful arrangement — coca leaves
   in sets of three (*k'intu*) held while praying, sweets, grains, seeds, shells,
   wool, flowers, often a dried llama foetus in some regions.
2. Participants breathe their prayers into the k'intu before adding them.
3. The bundle is folded, tied, and burned or buried; the place and direction
   matter. August, when the earth is said to be "open", is a key time.

## 5. Animal sacrifice (how it is handled)
In traditions that practise it (many African traditional religions, Lukumí, Vodou,
Candomblé, some other indigenous systems):
- Performed only by initiated/trained specialists, after divination specifies it.
- Done quickly with a sharp knife by a skilled person, with prayers; the animal is
  typically cooked and eaten by the community afterward, except where a rite
  requires otherwise.
- Legal: in the US, the Supreme Court (Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of
  Hialeah, 1993) protected religious animal sacrifice from targeted bans; general
  animal-welfare, public-health and slaughter laws still apply, and rules differ by
  country. Communities usually know the local framework.

## Gotchas
- Offering items a deity is known to dislike (traditions specify taboos per deity).
- Leaving perishables to rot on an indoor altar.
- Improvising major offerings without divination or elder direction.
- Outsiders photographing or touching offerings at shrines or crossroads.

## Verify
- [ ] What you offer, when and where is set by your tradition or by divination.
- [ ] Altar is clean, dedicated and arranged as instructed.
- [ ] Each offering is spoken: who, what, why.
- [ ] Old offerings disposed of by the prescribed method and timing.
- [ ] Any sacrifice is done by a trained specialist within applicable law.
