# Sacred-site protocol and seasonal ceremony

**Goal:** understand and uphold the obligations that bind a people to particular
places and times — caring for sacred sites, preparing and holding seasonal and
renewal ceremonies — and behave correctly as a guest on another people's ground.

In many indigenous religions the land is not a backdrop but a relative and a source
of law: Aboriginal Australian *Country*, Māori relationships to *whenua* and
ancestral mountains, Native American sacred mountains and springs, West African
sacred groves (e.g. the Osun-Osogbo grove, a UNESCO World Heritage site), Andean
*apus*. Specific custodians — clans, families, societies — hold rights and
responsibilities for specific places and ceremonies.

## 1. Custodianship (for community members)
- Know which places your family or group is responsible for, and what each
  requires: cleaning, burning off or tending vegetation, keeping water sources,
  restricting access, singing or speaking to the place.
- Keep the seasonal calendar: which ceremonies happen when, often set by natural
  signs (a star rising, a plant flowering, animal migration) rather than fixed dates.
  - Example: Matariki, the Māori New Year marked by the pre-dawn rising of the
    Pleiades (a New Zealand public holiday since 2022, its date set by tohunga
    and a Matariki advisory group).
- Pass knowledge on through the correct people — often by gender, age or
  initiation status; not everything is for everyone.

## 2. Preparing ceremonial grounds
- Elders/ceremonial leaders set the date and assign roles.
- Clear and prepare the ground: e.g. building or renewing an arbour, lodge or dance
  ground; gathering the specific wood, plants, foods and materials required.
- Purify participants: abstentions beforehand, sweat lodge or washing, smoke.
- Arrange camping, food and water for participants and guests over the days.

## 3. Shape of seasonal and renewal ceremonies
Commonly found elements (each tradition has its own form):
1. **Opening** — prayers, smoke, songs; acknowledging the land and spirits.
2. **Renewal acts** — dances, songs and ritual actions that renew the world, the
   people, the animals and plants (e.g. California's World Renewal ceremonies;
   Green Corn ceremonies among Southeastern nations; Sun Dance among Plains nations;
   Inti Raymi at the winter solstice in Cusco).
3. **Offerings and feasting** — first foods, gifts, giveaways.
4. **Teaching** — stories and law passed to the young.
5. **Closing** — prayers, clean-up, restoring the ground.
Some ceremonies are closed to outsiders entirely; some have public parts and closed parts.

## 4. As a visitor
- **Ask permission** through the right channel (a land council, tribal office,
  cultural centre, the family that holds the site). Australian events open with a
  *Welcome to Country* by Traditional Owners — or an *Acknowledgement of Country* when
  none is present.
- Follow signs: don't enter restricted areas, climb sacred formations, or remove
  stones, plants or objects. Uluru was closed to climbing in October 2019 at the
  request of its Aṉangu custodians.
- **No photographs or recording** of ceremonies or sites unless explicitly allowed;
  some sites forbid photography altogether.
- Dress modestly; follow gender-specific restrictions.
- Leave offerings only if invited to and as instructed.

## 5. Law and protection
Many countries protect sacred sites in law (e.g. heritage statutes in Australian
states, the US National Historic Preservation Act consultation process, New Zealand
heritage law); damage can be an offence. Development near a site generally requires
consultation with custodians.

## Gotchas
- Assuming a public park is free of restrictions — many contain sacred sites.
- Sharing location details of restricted sites online.
- Treating a ceremony as a tourist performance.
- Fixing ceremony dates to the Gregorian calendar when the tradition reads natural signs.

## Verify
- [ ] The custodians and the correct channel for permission are identified.
- [ ] Ceremonial calendar keyed to the tradition's own markers.
- [ ] Ground preparation, roles and purification are set by the elders.
- [ ] Visitors briefed on restrictions, photography and offerings.
- [ ] Applicable heritage law checked for any activity near the site.
