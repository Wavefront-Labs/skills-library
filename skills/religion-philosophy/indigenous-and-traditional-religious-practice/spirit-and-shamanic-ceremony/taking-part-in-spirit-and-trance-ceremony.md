# Taking part in spirit and trance ceremony

**Goal:** understand the shared shape of ceremonies in which spirits are engaged —
through the journey of a shaman, the possession of a medium, or a vision sought in
solitude — and take part as a community member or invited guest in the way the
tradition expects.

"Shamanism" is an outside umbrella term (from the Evenki *šaman*). Each tradition
has its own name, specialists and law: Korean *mudang* and the *gut* (kut) ritual;
Tuvan, Buryat and Mongolian shamans; Vodou *lwa*, Candomblé *orixás* and Lukumí
*orishas* who "mount" initiated devotees; the Hausa *bori* and the Horn of Africa and
Middle East *zar*; Lakota *hanbleceya* (crying for a vision). Ceremonies are led by
specialists who are chosen, called or initiated by their tradition and trained over
years. This skill describes participation, not leadership.

## 1. The specialist
- Becomes one by call (often illness, dreams or a crisis interpreted as spirit
  selection), by lineage, or by initiation — commonly all three.
- Is recognised by the community; trained by a senior specialist.
- Holds the ceremony's safety: knows how to open, manage and close contact with spirits.
Find ceremonies through relationships with the community, not online advertising.

## 2. Preparation (typical)
- **Purpose** brought to the specialist: healing, guidance, honouring a spirit or
  ancestor, a rite of the calendar.
- **Purification** beforehand: abstaining from alcohol, sex or particular foods for
  a set period, bathing, wearing prescribed (often white) clothes.
- **Offerings** the specialist asks for: food, drink, cloth, tobacco, money for the
  spirits and practitioner.
- Ask, in advance, what guests may and may not do; whether photography or recording
  is ever allowed (often never).

## 3. Phases of a ceremony
1. **Opening and purification** of space and people — smoke, water, song, prayer.
2. **Invocation** — drumming, rattling, song or chant calling the spirits; rhythm
   (often steady, rapid drumming) supports altered states.
3. **Contact** — the shaman journeys (sometimes narrating), or spirits arrive in a
   possessed medium; in Afro-diasporic rites, initiates may be mounted by their
   orisha/lwa during drumming and dance, then dressed and greeted by the community.
4. **Communication** — the spirit speaks, diagnoses, advises, blesses, or demands;
   assistants interpret.
5. **Healing or offering** acts as needed.
6. **Departure and closing** — spirits are thanked and sent off; the medium is
   brought back and cared for; the space is closed.
Ceremonies may last hours or all night (a Korean gut can run a full day).

## 4. As a participant or guest
- Follow the assistants' instructions about where to stand, when to sing, clap or
  dance, how to greet a manifested spirit (e.g. bowing, touching the floor).
- Don't touch a person in trance unless invited; assistants handle them.
- Don't interrupt, film, or approach the altar uninvited.
- Accept food or drink offered as part of the rite unless you have a health reason
  to decline — then explain quietly to an assistant beforehand.
- Leave only when the ceremony is closed or you are excused.

## 5. Vision seeking (in outline)
Where a tradition practises solitary vision seeking (e.g. Lakota hanbleceya), it is
undertaken under the guidance of a recognised spiritual leader, with purification
(sweat lodge), prayer offerings, a set place and period without food or water, and a
debrief with the leader. It is not a self-guided wilderness exercise; many Native
nations object to outsiders conducting or selling it.

## Safety and integrity
- Ceremonial use of psychoactive plants (e.g. ayahuasca in some Amazonian
  traditions, peyote in the Native American Church) carries medical risks and legal
  restrictions varying by country; risks are serious with some medications
  (notably antidepressants) and heart conditions. Don't take part outside a
  trusted, accountable setting.
- Commercial "shaman" retreats may not represent any living tradition.
- Respect closed ceremonies: some are for members, initiates or a nation only.

## Gotchas
- Treating someone in trance as performing.
- Arriving without the requested offerings or in the wrong clothing.
- Recording audio "just for memory".
- Assuming one tradition's rules (e.g. Siberian) apply to another (e.g. Candomblé).

## Verify
- [ ] Ceremony reached through a recognised specialist or community, not an ad.
- [ ] Purification, offerings and clothing requirements met.
- [ ] Guest protocol (no recording, no touching, when to leave) confirmed in advance.
- [ ] Any medical or legal risk (plant medicines) checked beforehand.
- [ ] You can describe the ceremony's phases in that tradition's own terms.
