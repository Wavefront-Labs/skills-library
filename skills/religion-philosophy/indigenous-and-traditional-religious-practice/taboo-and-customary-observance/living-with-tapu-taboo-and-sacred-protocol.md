# Living with tapu, taboo and sacred protocol

**Goal:** understand how sacred restriction works in traditional religions, observe
the protocols of your own community consistently, and behave correctly as a guest in
another's.

The English word "taboo" comes from Polynesian *tapu* (Māori, Tongan) / *kapu*
(Hawaiian). In these systems tapu is not just "forbidden": it is sacredness or
spiritual power that sets a person, place or thing apart and must be handled
correctly. Its complement **noa** is the ordinary, unrestricted state. Comparable
systems exist worldwide; the specifics belong to each people.

## 1. How the logic works (Māori example, in outline)
- People, places and objects can carry tapu: the head is highly tapu; chiefs, burial
  grounds, ceremonies and carving houses carry tapu.
- **Food is noa** and makes things noa; that's why food must be kept away from tapu
  things — don't sit on tables where food is served, don't put hats on tables, don't
  pass food over someone's head, don't touch someone's head casually.
- **Water and food lift tapu**: after visiting an urupā (cemetery) people wash their
  hands (water is often provided at the gate); after a pōwhiri (welcome) the shared
  meal makes manuhiri (guests) noa with the hosts.
- Breaches are understood to have spiritual consequences and are put right by
  specific rites, often led by a tohunga or kaumātua (elder).

## 2. Common kinds of customary observance
- **Food taboos:** for whole groups (by clan totem), particular life stages
  (pregnancy, initiation, mourning), or particular specialists. In Akan tradition
  each clan and many shrines have *akyiwadeɛ* (things to avoid).
- **Totemic restrictions:** not killing or eating one's totem animal or plant.
- **Avoidance relationships:** e.g. in many Aboriginal Australian societies, a man
  and his mother-in-law don't speak directly or face-to-face; communication goes
  through others or a special speech register.
- **Naming the dead:** many Aboriginal communities avoid naming the deceased and
  showing images for a period; a substitute name may be used. Other cultures (e.g.
  some Native American nations) have similar customs.
- **Purity and pollution rules:** around birth, death, menstruation, or sacred work —
  periods of separation and cleansing rites. Rules vary greatly and are discussed
  within communities; don't assume.
- **Sacred-place and season rules:** no fishing, hunting or harvesting in certain
  places or times (Polynesian *rāhui* placed on a fishery after a death or to let
  stocks recover is a well-known example).

## 3. Learning the rules (as a member)
- Learn from the right people — elders, parents, aunties and uncles; some knowledge
  is gender- or initiation-specific.
- Ask *why* when appropriate; the reasons connect rules to genealogy and story.
- Keep a personal list of rules that apply to you (clan, life stage) and when they
  start and end.
- When unsure, ask before acting; err toward restraint.

## 4. Guest protocol (Māori marae example)
1. Arrive with your group; wait at the gate for the karanga (call of welcome).
2. Women generally walk in front on entry; men take front seats for speeches
   (practices vary between iwi).
3. Whaikōrero (speeches) followed by waiata (song) from each side; a koha (gift,
   usually money in an envelope) is laid down.
4. **Hongi** (pressing noses) and handshakes with hosts.
5. Share food — you are now noa together.
6. Shoes off in the wharenui (meeting house); no food inside it; don't step over
   people sleeping on the floor.
Similar orderly welcomes govern visits in many indigenous communities; ask the host
what to expect.

## Gotchas
- Treating taboo as superstition to be explained away rather than law to be observed.
- Sitting on tables, pillows (headrests) or touching heads in Polynesian settings.
- Using the name or photo of a deceased person in an Aboriginal context.
- Generalising one people's rules to another.

## Verify
- [ ] You know which rules apply to you (clan, gender, life stage) and from whom you learned them.
- [ ] You know how breaches are put right in your tradition.
- [ ] Food kept separate from tapu people, places and objects.
- [ ] Guests briefed on welcome protocol before arriving.
- [ ] Naming and image restrictions for the deceased observed.
