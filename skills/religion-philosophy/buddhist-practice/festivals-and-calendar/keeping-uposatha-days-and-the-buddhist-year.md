# Keeping uposatha days and the Buddhist year — living by the moon

**Goal:** know which days of the month and year your community observes, what each
asks of a lay practitioner, and how to take part without missing the core rite.

## 1. Get the right calendar
1. Buddhist observance days follow the **lunar calendar**, so they move against the
   civil calendar every year. Theravada countries even differ among themselves (Thai,
   Burmese and Sri Lankan reckonings can fall a day apart).
2. Use **your own temple's printed or online calendar**, not a generic one. Tibetan and
   Chinese communities use their own lunisolar calendars; Japanese temples mostly use
   fixed Gregorian dates.

## 2. Uposatha (observance) days
- Fall on the **full moon, new moon and the two half-moon days** — about four times a
  month. Sri Lankans call the full-moon day *Poya*; Thais *Wan Phra*.
- Lay practice on the day:
  1. Visit the temple in the morning, wear white if that is the custom (Sri Lanka,
     Thai devotees).
  2. Request and take the **eight precepts** from a monk after the refuges, e.g. ending
     *Vikala-bhojana veramani sikkhapadam samadiyami* (refraining from food at the wrong
     time, i.e. after noon).
  3. Offer food before noon, listen to a Dhamma talk, meditate, keep the precepts until
     dawn next day. Many keep them only on full-moon days — that is fine.
- Monastics recite the patimokkha on the full- and new-moon days.

## 3. The main festivals
**Theravada**
- **Vesak / Visakha Puja** (full moon of Vesakha, usually May): birth, awakening and
  final passing of the Buddha. Precepts, dana, lanterns and free food stalls (*dansal*)
  in Sri Lanka, lantern and bathing-the-Buddha rites in Malaysia and Singapore.
- **Asalha Puja** (full moon around July): the first sermon; next day the **vassa**
  (rains retreat) begins.
- **Pavarana** (full moon around October): end of vassa, followed by the kathina season.
- **Magha Puja** (full moon around February/March): 1,250 disciples gathering; the
  Ovada Patimokkha is recited.
- In Thailand, on Visakha, Asalha and Magha evenings, *wian tian*: walk **three times
  clockwise** around the ordination hall or stupa holding a candle, three incense sticks
  and flowers, recollecting Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha in turn.

**Japanese Mahayana**
- **Hanamatsuri** (8 April): flower-decked shrine with a small standing infant Buddha;
  ladle sweet hydrangea tea (*amacha*) over the statue.
- **Obon** (13–15 July in Tokyo and some regions, mid-August in most): welcome the
  ancestors with a small fire (*mukaebi*), make offerings at the butsudan and graves,
  have a priest chant, join *bon odori* dances, then send them off (*okuribi*, floating
  lanterns — *toro nagashi*). Rooted in the Ullambana story of Maudgalyayana.

**Tibetan**
- **Saga Dawa** (fourth lunar month, peak on its full moon): the Buddha's birth,
  awakening and parinirvana; merit is held to be greatly multiplied. Circumambulate,
  release animals bought for slaughter, give alms, recite mantras.
- **Losar** (new year): household cleaning, offerings and prayer flags.

## Gotchas
- Don't schedule a dana meal on a festival without booking — the monastery may be
  overwhelmed or already sponsored.
- Wearing white is a lay devotional custom in some communities, not everywhere — look
  around first.
- On precept days avoid alcohol and, for eight-precept keepers, solid food after noon.
- Dates and customs vary by country and temple — ask the resident teacher.
