# Funeral chanting and the 49-day rites — helping the dead and the living

**Goal:** support a dying person and their family through a Buddhist death, funeral
and the memorial cycle that follows, doing the parts a lay family or friend is
expected to do and leaving the rest to the monastics or priest.

## 1. At the deathbed
1. Call the family's temple early so a monk, priest or lama can come.
2. Keep the room calm. Help the person recollect the Buddha, their generosity and
   good deeds; play or softly chant familiar verses — paritta in Theravada, the
   nembutsu *Namu Amida Butsu* in Pure Land, *Om mani padme hum* or the teacher's
   name in Tibetan practice.
3. **Tibetan tradition:** after breathing stops, many families leave the body
   undisturbed for some time (often up to three days if possible) while a lama may
   perform *phowa*; if touched, the crown of the head is touched first. Follow the lama.
4. Avoid loud lamenting at the bedside — it is believed to disturb the mind at death.

## 2. Theravada funeral (Thai, Sri Lankan, Burmese)
1. Monks are invited to chant at the home or temple, often each evening for several
   nights before cremation (Thai practice commonly 3 or 7 nights).
2. The community takes the refuges and five precepts.
3. **Pamsukula / matakavastra:** a white cloth (or robe) is laid on or across the
   coffin connected by a cord; the monks take it as "rag-robe" cloth while chanting:
   *Anicca vata sankhara, uppada-vaya-dhammino; uppajjitva nirujjhanti, tesam
   vupasamo sukho* — "Impermanent are all conditioned things, arising and passing
   away; having arisen they cease; their stilling is bliss."
4. **Water pouring** to transfer merit to the deceased as the monks chant the anumodana.
5. Memorial dana is offered on the **7th day**, and commonly at **3 months** and
   **1 year** (Sri Lanka), or **7, 50 and 100 days** (Thailand).

## 3. Japanese funeral (most sects)
1. **Tsuya** (wake) the evening before, **soshiki** (funeral) next day; the priest
   chants sutras and gives the deceased a **kaimyo** (Buddhist posthumous name;
   *homyo* in Jodo Shinshu).
2. Mourners bring **koden** (condolence money) in a black-and-white envelope, wear
   black, and carry a **juzu** (prayer beads) in the left hand.
3. **Shoko (incense offering):** bow to the family, then the altar; take a pinch of
   powdered incense with right thumb and two fingers, (in many sects) raise it to the
   forehead, drop it on the coals; once to three times according to the sect.
   **Jodo Shinshu** does not raise it to the forehead. Gassho, bow, step back.
4. Never pass food chopstick to chopstick or stand chopsticks in rice — this echoes the
   handling of bones after cremation (*kotsuage*), where relatives pass bone fragments
   with chopsticks into the urn.

## 4. The 49 days and memorial years
- Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Tibetan practice treat **49 days** as the period
  between death and rebirth (Tibetan: the bardo). Rites are held **every seventh day**,
  with the 49th (*shijukunichi* in Japanese) the most important — often the interment
  of ashes. Tibetan families have prayers said and may have the *Bardo Thodol* read.
- Japanese memorial years (*nenki hoyo*): 1st, 3rd (second anniversary), 7th, 13th,
  17th, 23rd/25th, 27th, 33rd, often ending at the 33rd or 50th. Obon gathers all ancestors yearly.
- Throughout, merit from dana, chanting and good deeds is dedicated to the deceased.

## Gotchas
- Sects differ on number of incense pinches, flowers and whether the deceased "becomes
  a buddha" — ask the officiant rather than assuming.
- In Theravada, offerings to the monks at a funeral follow normal etiquette: before noon
  for food, women offering via cloth, money to the lay steward.
- Red ink, bright clothes and festive wrapping are avoided around Japanese funerals.
