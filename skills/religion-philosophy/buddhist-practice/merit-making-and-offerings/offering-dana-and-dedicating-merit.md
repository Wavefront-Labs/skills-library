# Offering dana and dedicating merit — giving well, then sharing the good

**Goal:** organise or join an offering to the monastic community (a meal, robes or
other requisites) in a way the monastery can accept under its discipline, and close
it with a proper dedication of merit, for example to a deceased relative.

## 1. What to offer
The traditional gifts are the **four requisites**: robes, almsfood, lodging and
medicine. In practice:
- **Food** for the day's meal — cooked dishes, rice, fruit, sweets, drinks.
- Toiletries, candles, robes (in the right colour for that monastery — Thai forest
  ochre differs from Burmese maroon), books, medicines.
- **Money** is given to the monastery's lay committee or into the donation box, often
  in an envelope with a note of what it is for — not placed in a Theravada monk's hand.
- Avoid: alcohol, anything you killed or had killed for the monks, and in Mahayana
  monasteries (Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean) meat, garlic and onions — they keep a
  vegetarian, often five-pungent-free, kitchen. Ask what is already oversupplied.

## 2. A meal offering (sanghadana), step by step
1. **Book a date** with the monastery's steward; popular days fill months ahead.
2. **Arrive by about 10:00–10:30.** Theravada monastics eat once (or twice before
   noon); food reaching them after midday cannot be eaten that day.
3. Remove shoes, bow three times to the shrine, then to the senior monk.
4. Lay food out; the community may first take the **five or eight precepts** together.
5. **Formally offer** each dish: kneel or stand at a lower height, lift the dish with
   both hands so the monk can receive it (touching it, or the cloth). In Thai practice a
   woman places it on a cloth the monk spreads in front of him rather than into his hands.
6. The monks chant a blessing (*anumodana*) and eat; lay people eat afterwards,
   from what is left.
7. Wash up and leave the kitchen as you found it — that is part of the offering.

## 3. Dedicating merit (the water-pouring rite)
Common in Thai, Lao, Cambodian, Burmese and Sri Lankan practice:
1. During the anumodana, when the monks begin *Yatha varivaha pura...*, slowly pour
   water from a small jug into a bowl, hand-over-hand if several people share it.
2. Silently dedicate: *Idam me natinam hotu, sukhita hontu natayo* — "May this be
   for my relatives; may my relatives be happy" — naming the person if you wish.
3. When the chant finishes, stop pouring. Afterwards empty the water at the base of a
   tree or onto earth, not down a drain.
Mahayana and Tibetan practitioners dedicate merit in words instead, e.g. the
Tibetan "Through this merit may all beings..." (*Ge wa di yi...*) or a temple's
standard verse.

## 4. Kathina
- Held during the **one month after the rains retreat ends** (roughly October–November).
- The cloth is offered to the **Sangha as a whole**, which then gives it to one monk by
  formal act; a monastery can hold one kathina a year and needs enough monks who
  completed the vassa there (traditionally at least five).
- Usually sponsored by a family or group, with a procession, a meal and robe-sewing or
  presenting of a ready-made robe plus requisites. Book well in advance.

## Gotchas
- Dana is the intention as much as the gift; don't negotiate blessings or ask a monk
  to name a price.
- Never point feet at monks or the shrine; sit lower than the monks.
- Customs on women offering, vegetarian kitchens and money vary — ask the steward.

## Verify
- [ ] Date booked and food delivered well before noon.
- [ ] Gifts are allowable for this monastery (no alcohol; vegetarian if Mahayana).
- [ ] Each item formally offered with both hands, in the community's manner.
- [ ] Money went to the lay committee, not into a monk's hand.
- [ ] Merit dedicated and the water returned to earth.
