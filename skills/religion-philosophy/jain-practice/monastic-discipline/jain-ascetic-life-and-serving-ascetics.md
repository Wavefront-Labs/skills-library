# Jain ascetic life and serving ascetics

**Goal:** understand the conduct of Jain monks and nuns well enough to prepare for
diksha (if that is your path) or — far more commonly — to support mendicants
correctly as a layperson: offering alms, hosting, and receiving teaching.

> Draft. Rules differ sharply between orders. **Svetambara** mendicants wear white and
> carry bowls and a woollen brush (rajoharan/ogha); **Digambara** munis are naked, own
> only a peacock-feather whisk and a water gourd, and eat standing with food placed in
> cupped hands; aryikas (Digambara nuns) wear a white sari. Each gaccha/sangha has its own
> samachari (code of conduct). Follow the guidance of the acharya concerned.

## 1. Diksha (initiation)
- Preceded by years of study, often living alongside mendicants, family consent, and the
  acharya's acceptance.
- The ceremony: a public renunciation of household life; the candidate gives away
  possessions, receives the mendicant's kit and new name, and the first **kesh-lochan**
  (pulling out the hair by hand, repeated a few times a year).
- From then the **five great vows (mahavratas)** apply absolutely, for life.

## 2. The five great vows in practice
1. **Ahimsa:** no harm to any being — no use of fire, unfiltered water, vehicles or
   electricity; the brush clears insects before sitting; the muhapatti (Svetambara) guards
   breath and speech.
2. **Satya:** speak only truthful, kind and necessary words.
3. **Asteya:** take nothing not given — including a place to sit.
4. **Brahmacharya:** total celibacy; no being alone with the opposite sex.
5. **Aparigraha:** no money or property; only the permitted items.

## 3. Gochari (alms round)
Mendicants never cook, buy or order food; they collect small amounts from several
households, like a cow grazing (go-chari), so no house is burdened.
**Lay etiquette when they come to your door:**
- Invite them in with folded hands; do not pre-arrange "special" food for them — food
  prepared specifically for a mendicant is unacceptable to many orders. Offer from what the
  household has already cooked for itself.
- Offer only acceptable food: no root vegetables, nothing raw with living seeds, nothing
  cooked after dark, nothing touched by fire or unfiltered water *after* they arrived.
- Do not touch fire, water, green vegetables or switch on lights while giving.
- They may refuse or take very little. Do not insist.
- Digambara munis eat once a day, standing, from a family that has performed the formal
  welcome (*padgahan*) — families stand outside holding a kalash to invite them; follow
  the local sangha's instructions exactly.

## 4. Vihara and chaturmas
- Mendicants travel only on foot (barefoot), usually in groups, never by vehicle; laypeople
  often walk along, arrange the next **upashraya** (lodging hall), and carry no items for them
  unless permitted.
- During the four monsoon months (**chaturmas**, roughly July–November) they stay in one place
  to avoid harming the life that flourishes in the rains. This is the main teaching season for
  the host community: daily discourses (pravachan), group samayika and Paryushana.

## 5. Visiting a mendicant
- Do **vandana** (bowing, with the appropriate formula) on arrival and departure.
- Dress modestly; women do not touch male mendicants and vice versa.
- Do not offer money; donations go to the sangha or trust.
- Ask permission before photographing; turn phones off — mendicants do not use them.

## Gotchas
- Leaving a light on or cooking while a mendicant is in the house can make the visit unacceptable.
- "Preparing a nice meal for Maharaj-saheb" defeats the rule; cook for your household.
- Expect them to leave on foot at dawn even in heat or rain — help plan the route instead.

## Verify
- [ ] You know which order the mendicants belong to and its specific rules.
- [ ] Food offered was already cooked for the household, Jain-acceptable, given without
      touching fire, water or green produce.
- [ ] Vandana done on arrival and departure; no money handed to them.
