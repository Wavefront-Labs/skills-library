# Pendulum querying and rod dowsing

**Goal:** set up a consistent yes/no response, ask questions that can be answered
cleanly, use charts for multiple-choice answers, and keep your own expectations from
steering the result.

## 1. Equipment
- **Pendulum:** a symmetrical weight (crystal, brass, wood) of roughly 10–30 g on a
  chain or thread. A ring or key on a thread works.
- **L-rods:** two L-shaped metal rods (e.g. coat-hanger wire, long arm ~30–40 cm,
  short arm ~12 cm). Sleeves (a cut pen barrel) let them swing freely.
- **Y-rod:** a forked stick or plastic Y, held under tension — the traditional water
  dowser's tool.

## 2. Pendulum grip
- Hold the chain between thumb and forefinger, ~10–15 cm above the weight (adjust
  until it swings easily).
- Rest your elbow on a table; keep the wrist relaxed.
- Start with the pendulum still, or with a gentle neutral back-and-forth swing.

## 3. Calibrate (every session)
1. Ask aloud: "Show me yes." Wait. Note the movement (e.g. clockwise circle, or
   swing toward/away from you).
2. "Show me no." Note it.
3. Optionally "Show me *don't know / not to answer*".
4. Test with questions whose answers you know: "Is my name ___?" "Is it raining?"
Movements can differ between people and occasionally between sessions — hence
calibrating each time.

## 4. Asking questions
- One idea per question; answerable yes/no.
  Bad: "Should I take the job or stay?" Good: "Is taking the new job in my best
  interest at this time?"
- Avoid questions where you strongly want a particular answer — or have someone else
  hold the pendulum while you ask (or use a blind setup: the question written on
  folded cards you can't see).
- Many practitioners open by asking "Can I ask about this?" "Am I ready?" and
  close with thanks.

## 5. Pendulum charts
Draw a half-circle "fan" divided into wedges (e.g. numbers 0–10, days of the week,
letters, percentages, or options you write in). Hold the pendulum over the centre
point; ask; note which wedge it swings along. Keep wedges large enough that the
swing is unambiguous.

## 6. Map dowsing
Lay a map flat; move a pointer (pen tip) slowly along grid lines with one hand while
holding the pendulum with the other; note where the "yes" response occurs. Narrow
down by grid square.

## 7. L-rods in the field
1. Hold one rod loosely in each fist, short arm vertical, long arms parallel and
   pointing forward, slightly downward.
2. Walk slowly while holding the question or target in mind.
3. Common responses: rods **cross** = yes/target found; swing **apart** = no.
   Calibrate as with the pendulum.
4. Mark each response point and walk the area from a different direction to confirm.

## Keeping it honest with yourself
The motion comes through tiny unconscious hand movements — the **ideomotor effect**
— whatever further meaning practitioners give it. Either way, your expectations can
drive the answer, so: calibrate, ask neutrally, use blind setups for anything
important, and record results to compare against outcomes. Don't use dowsing in
place of a professional for health, structural, gas/utility or water-supply
decisions — use a utility locator before digging.

## Gotchas
- Gripping too tightly or holding the arm unsupported (fatigue produces swings).
- Compound questions.
- Re-asking until you get the answer you want.
- Breeze or a ceiling fan moving the pendulum.

## Verify
- [ ] Yes/no (and "don't know") calibrated at the start of this session.
- [ ] Calibration tested on known-answer questions.
- [ ] Each question is single, neutral and yes/no (or matched to a chart).
- [ ] Important questions done blind or by someone without a stake.
- [ ] Results logged; no safety-critical decision rests on a dowsing answer.
