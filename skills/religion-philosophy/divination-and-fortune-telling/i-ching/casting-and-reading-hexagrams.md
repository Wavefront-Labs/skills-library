# Casting and reading I Ching hexagrams

**Goal:** cast a hexagram correctly, identify any changing lines and the resulting
second hexagram, and read the relevant texts in the right order.

## 1. Background you need
- A **hexagram** is six stacked lines, each solid (yang, —) or broken (yin, - -).
  There are 64. Each is two **trigrams** (lower lines 1–3, upper lines 4–6), from
  eight: Qian ☰ Heaven, Kun ☷ Earth, Zhen ☳ Thunder, Kan ☵ Water, Gen ☶ Mountain,
  Xun ☴ Wind/Wood, Li ☲ Fire, Dui ☱ Lake.
- Lines are **numbered from the bottom** (1) to the top (6) and cast in that order.
- Each hexagram has a **Judgment** (the core text), an **Image** (commentary on the
  two trigrams), and six **line texts**.
- Translations differ substantially. Wilhelm/Baynes is the classic Western
  translation; others (e.g. Lynn, Huang, Karcher, Minford) differ in tone and
  scholarship. Use one consistently and note which.

## 2. Frame the question
Write it down. Classic practice favours questions about how to act or what attitude
to take ("What should I keep in mind as I take this role?"). Ask once; repeating the
question is traditionally discouraged (hexagram 4 itself warns about it).

## 3. Three-coin method (fast, common)
1. Take three identical coins. Assign heads = 3, tails = 2 (the usual convention;
   some reverse it — be consistent).
2. Toss all three; sum the values:
   - **6** = old yin: broken line, **changing** (write - -x-)
   - **7** = young yang: solid, stable
   - **8** = young yin: broken, stable
   - **9** = old yang: solid line, **changing** (write —o—)
3. Draw line 1 at the bottom. Repeat five more times, building upward.
Probabilities: 6 = 1/8, 7 = 3/8, 8 = 3/8, 9 = 1/8.

## 4. Yarrow-stalk method (traditional, slow)
Uses 50 stalks, one set aside, with the remaining 49 divided and counted off in
fours three times per line, giving 6/7/8/9 with different odds (old yin 1/16, old yang
3/16, young yang 5/16, young yin 7/16). The procedure takes ~15–30 minutes and is
part of its meditative value; learn it from a detailed source before relying on it,
since small counting mistakes change the result.

## 5. Identify the hexagrams
1. Read the lower trigram (lines 1–3) and upper trigram (4–6).
2. Look them up in your book's trigram key table to find the **primary hexagram**
   number (King Wen sequence 1–64).
3. If there are changing lines, flip each one (6 → yang, 9 → yin) to get the
   **relating** (or "resulting") hexagram.

## 6. Read in order
1. Primary hexagram: Judgment, then Image.
2. **Only the changing lines'** texts of the primary hexagram (not all six).
3. Relating hexagram: Judgment and Image (not its line texts) as where the situation
   is moving.
Conventions with many changing lines vary (some readers then emphasise the relating
hexagram, or follow Zhu Xi's rules). With hexagrams 1 or 2 and all six changing,
read the special "all lines" text.

## 7. Record and reflect
Note date, question, lines with values (e.g. 7-8-9-7-6-8), both hexagrams, and your
reading. Revisit the entry weeks later — the practice deepens through the journal.

## Gotchas
- Building the hexagram top-down — always bottom-up.
- Reading every line text instead of only the changing ones.
- Mixing translations mid-reading; titles and numbering of commentaries differ.
- Forgetting which coin side you assigned 3.

## Verify
- [ ] Question written before casting.
- [ ] Six values recorded in cast order, line 1 at the bottom.
- [ ] Primary hexagram identified by both trigrams; number checked against the key.
- [ ] Changing lines flipped correctly to give the relating hexagram.
- [ ] Only changing-line texts read; translation noted in the journal.
