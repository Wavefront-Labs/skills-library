# Leading a funeral — from the family meeting to the committal

**Goal:** a ceremony the family recognises as *true to the person*, delivered calmly
inside a fixed time slot, ending with a committal that lets people say goodbye.

## 1. Before the family meeting
- Get from the funeral director: date, venue, **slot length** (crematorium chapels
  often allow 20–45 minutes including entry and exit — confirm), burial or cremation,
  coffin present or not, music system, livestream, who the family contact is.
- Ask whether the family wants religious content (a prayer, a hymn) inside a civil
  ceremony. Many do; it's their ceremony, not yours.

## 2. Family meeting (60–120 minutes, ideally in person)
Listen more than you write. Useful prompts:
1. Full name, name they were known by, dates and places of birth and death.
2. Family: partner, children, grandchildren, siblings — exact spellings, who has died.
3. Childhood, work, places lived, passions, humour, phrases they always said.
4. "What would they hate us to say?" and "What would make them laugh?"
5. Difficult territory: estrangements, cause of death, a second family. Agree what is
   said, what is softened, what is left out.
6. Who else speaks? Agree a time limit for each (3–5 minutes) and get scripts in advance.
7. Music (entry, reflection, exit) and readings; photo slideshow length.
Read the draft back or send it for approval; correct facts are non-negotiable.

## 3. Running order (example, 40-minute slot)
| Time | Element |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Entry music; coffin and family enter |
| 3–5 | Welcome, purpose of the gathering, housekeeping |
| 5–7 | Reading or poem |
| 7–17 | Tribute / eulogy (celebrant), about 1,200–1,500 words |
| 17–22 | Family or friend tribute |
| 22–26 | Reflection music with slideshow, or a moment of silence |
| 26–29 | **Committal** |
| 29–32 | Thanks, wake details, closing words |
| 32–35 | Exit music; family leaves first |
Leave a 3–5 minute buffer — overrunning a crematorium slot delays the next family.

## 4. Writing the tribute
- Structure it as a life, not a CV: one or two stories per era beat a list of jobs.
- Use their voice: quotes, sayings, a recipe, a terrible joke.
- Name the grief honestly; don't promise an afterlife unless the family's beliefs do.
- Read aloud at roughly 130–150 words per minute to time it.

## 5. The committal
Template (secular):
```
[NAME], we now commit your body to be [cremated / laid to rest].
We give thanks for your life, which touched so many here.
Your story is part of ours, and we carry it with us.
Go gently / Rest in peace, [NAME].
```
- **Crematorium:** agree whether curtains close, the coffin lowers, or stays in view.
  Know where the button is or who operates it.
- **Graveside:** move people close, speak over the wind, check whether the family will
  lower the coffin or scatter earth or petals.
- **Memorial with no body** (body donated, already cremated, missing): adapt to
  "we commit [NAME] to our memory" and use a symbolic act — lighting a candle, laying
  a flower.

## Gotchas
- Never skip the phonetic spelling of names; mispronouncing the dead is unforgettable.
- Have a printed copy of every guest speaker's words in case they can't go on — offer
  in advance to read for them.
- Sudden or traumatic deaths (suicide, a child) need gentler language and close
  agreement with the family on wording; follow safe-messaging guidance on suicide.
- Livestream: mention it in the welcome; avoid identifying strangers on camera.
- Keep water, tissues and the funeral director's mobile number at the lectern.

## Verify
- [ ] Facts, names and spellings approved by the family in writing.
- [ ] Script timed aloud and fits the slot with a buffer.
- [ ] Every speaker knows their order and time limit; backup copies printed.
- [ ] Committal mechanics confirmed with the venue or funeral director.
- [ ] Music cues and slideshow tested in the venue or with the operator.
