# Conducting the shidian ceremony

**Goal:** understand and help stage the **shidian** (釋奠, "setting out offerings") —
the formal sacrifice to Confucius held at Confucius temples — and apply the same
principles of **li** (ritual propriety) to smaller community ceremonies. Main living
versions: Taiwan (Taipei and other Confucius temples, held on **28 September**,
Teachers' Day), Korea (**Seokjeon Jerye** at Sungkyunkwan, Seoul, in spring and
autumn), and Qufu in mainland China (revived, around 28 September).

## 1. The idea behind the form
In Confucian practice li is learned by **doing it correctly together**: precise
movement, dress and order express reverence and harmonise participants. So
rehearsal is not optional — the rite *is* the exactness.

## 2. Roles
| Role | Job |
|---|---|
| Chief offerant (zhuxian guan / 主獻官) | presents the first offering; traditionally the highest local official or a descendant of Confucius |
| Second and final offerants (亞獻 / 終獻) | present the second and third offerings |
| Masters of ceremonies (通贊, 引贊) | call each step aloud and guide offerants |
| Musicians | ritual music (yayue): bells, stone chimes, zithers, flutes, drums |
| Dancers (佾生) | **yi dance** with feathers and flutes in square formation; Taiwan uses **8×8 (bayi)**, the highest rank |
| Assistants | carry silk, wine, prayer text; manage offerings |
All wear ritual robes; offerants rehearse their routes and bows.

## 3. Sequence (Taiwan model; Korea and Qufu vary in names and detail)
1. **Drums and bells** begin before dawn (Taipei: around 6 a.m.); participants take
   positions.
2. **Opening the gates and welcoming the spirit (迎神)** — music, all bow.
3. **Presenting silk and the first offering (初獻禮)**: chief offerant washes hands,
   is led to the altar, offers silk and wine; the **prayer text (祝文)** is read.
4. **Second offering (亞獻禮)** and **final offering (終獻禮)**, each with wine,
   music and dance.
5. **Drinking the blessed wine and receiving the sacrificial meat (飲福受胙)** by the
   chief offerant.
6. **Removing the offerings (徹饌)**.
7. **Seeing off the spirit (送神)**.
8. **Burning the prayer and silk (望燎/望瘞)**, then closing the gates.
Each step is called aloud by the master of ceremonies; bows are now usually standing
bows (formerly kneeling with three kneelings and nine kowtows).

## 4. Offerings
Traditional **tailao**: ox, sheep and pig, plus grains, fruit, wine, silk and
vegetables in prescribed vessels. Many modern ceremonies use symbolic or
reduced animal offerings — practice differs and has changed in recent years; check
the host temple.

## 5. Staging a smaller community li
Same principles at school openings, coming-of-age rites, or a community honouring
of teachers:
1. Write a **run sheet** with every call, route and position.
2. Appoint one master of ceremonies with authority; nobody else speaks.
3. Rehearse at least once in full, in robes, at the venue.
4. Keep the core shape: **welcome → offering(s) with a read text → shared blessing
   → farewell**.
5. Simplicity done exactly is better than elaboration done loosely.

## Gotchas
- In Taiwan, attendees traditionally pluck "**wisdom hairs**" from the sacrificial ox
  after the rite; this caused crowd surges — stewarding was needed. (Status of this
  custom today is uncertain; ask the temple.)
- Spectators should stay silent and outside the ritual courtyard lines.
- Confucian rites are often presented as civic heritage rather than worship; respect
  whichever framing the hosting body uses.
