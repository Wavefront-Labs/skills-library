# Keeping an ancestral altar and Qingming

**Goal:** care for the family's ancestors in the Chinese tradition — a home altar
with tablets, regular offerings, and the annual **Qingming** grave visit — as an
expression of **xiao** (filial piety). Customs vary enormously by region (Hokkien,
Cantonese, Hakka, Teochew…) and family; when in doubt, do what the eldest relative
does.

## 1. Setting up the home altar
- **Position:** a high shelf or cabinet in the main room, facing the front door if
  possible, never facing a toilet or in a bedroom. In homes with a deity altar, the
  deities sit on the higher/centre position and the ancestors to the **right** of
  the deities from the viewer's perspective (left from the deity's) — regional
  variation.
- **Tablets (祖先牌位):** wooden tablets with the ancestor's name, generation and
  dates, or a single collective tablet for "the ancestors of the X lineage". The
  family's eldest son traditionally keeps them. Photographs are a common modern
  substitute.
- **Equipment:** an incense burner (filled with sand or ash), two candles or electric
  lamps, three small cups (tea/wine), a plate for fruit, and optionally divination
  blocks (jiaobei).

## 2. Regular offerings
| When | What |
|---|---|
| Daily or 1st and 15th of the lunar month | incense (1 or 3 sticks), fresh tea, light refreshed |
| Death anniversary (忌日) | the ancestor's favourite foods, full meal, family bows |
| Lunar New Year eve | full meal offered before the family eats; reunion dinner |
| Ghost Festival (7th month) | offerings to ancestors and wandering spirits |
| Winter Solstice / Chongyang | further offerings in many families |
Order of a home offering: clean the altar → light candles → set food and pour drinks
→ light incense → each person bows (or kneels) three times, eldest first →
announce news to the ancestors (births, exams, marriages) → wait until the incense
has burned partway → burn spirit money if your family does → clear food and eat it.

## 3. Qingming tomb-sweeping (around 4–5 April)
1. **Check the date** (solar term; falls on 4 or 5 April) and plan for crowds and
   traffic; many families go the weekend before.
2. Bring: hoe/brush, gloves, bin bags, paint for re-colouring inscriptions, incense,
   candles, flowers, food offerings (roast pork, chicken, fruit, cakes, wine — by
   family custom), spirit money, a lighter, water.
3. **Clean the grave:** clear weeds and debris, wash the headstone, repaint faded
   characters.
4. **Honour the earth god first** in many regions: small offering at the
   **Tudi/Houtu** marker beside the grave.
5. Set out food, light candles and incense; family members bow three times in
   order of seniority.
6. Lay **grave paper** (五色紙 / 墓紙) weighted with stones on the mound (Hokkien and
   Taiwanese custom) to show the grave is tended.
7. Burn spirit money **in a metal burner**; pour wine on the ground as a final
   libation.
8. Pack out all rubbish.
For columbaria (niches): follow the site's rules — many ban flame, allowing flowers
and electronic candles only.

## Gotchas
- **Fire risk:** Qingming hillside fires are common. Burn only in burners, never in
  dry grass, and douse ashes. Many cemeteries now ban open burning outright.
- Don't point at graves or take photos of other families' graves (taboo for many).
- Mourners in the first three years after a death may follow different timing
  (e.g. visit before Qingming) — follow family elders.
- Christian or Buddhist relatives may prefer flowers and prayer instead of incense;
  combined observance is common and fine.
- Uncertain: altar orientation and left/right placement are the most variable
  conventions here.

## Verify
- [ ] Altar positioned respectfully, tablets/photos clean and in order.
- [ ] Offerings kept on the 1st/15th and death anniversaries.
- [ ] Qingming kit packed; earth god honoured; burning done safely or replaced by flowers where banned.
- [ ] Family seniority followed for bows.
