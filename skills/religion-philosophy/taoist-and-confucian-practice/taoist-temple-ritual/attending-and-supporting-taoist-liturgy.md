# Attending and supporting Taoist liturgy

**Goal:** take part properly in Taoist temple ritual as a lay worshipper or community
organiser — from a daily visit with incense to sponsoring a petition or helping a
community stage a **jiao** (offering rite) — while leaving the liturgy itself to the
ordained priests (**daoshi**). Practice differs a lot between the monastic
**Quanzhen** order (mainland China monasteries) and the householder **Zhengyi**
priesthood (Taiwan, Fujian, Southeast Asia); this skill notes where.

## 1. Temple etiquette and offering incense
1. Enter by the **side door**; the central door is for the deities (and the
   priests in procession). Many temples: right-hand door in (dragon side), left out
   (tiger side) — follow local signage.
2. Don't step on the raised **threshold**.
3. Buy incense/candles at the counter (or use the free ones). Common practice: **3
   sticks** per altar, lit from the temple's oil lamp or lighter (not your mouth),
   flame extinguished by waving, never blowing.
4. At each altar in the order the temple indicates (usually the **Heaven censer**
   facing outward first, then the main hall deity, then side halls): hold the
   incense at forehead height with both hands, state your name, address and wish
   silently, bow three times, plant the sticks with the **left hand** (custom in
   many temples; left = yang/clean hand by this convention).
5. Offerings of fruit, flowers, tea or sweets are placed on the table; take them home
   after worship to share (receiving the deity's blessing). Avoid meat at Quanzhen
   temples.

## 2. The daily liturgy (zaowan gongke)
- **Morning and evening recitations** by the resident clergy: scriptures such as the
  *Qingjing jing* (Scripture of Clarity and Stillness) and praises to the deities,
  sung with wooden fish, bells and chimes, while the priests circumambulate and bow.
- Lay people may stand at the back, bow when the clergy bow, and keep silent. Ask
  before photographing — many temples forbid it during rites.

## 3. Requesting a petition or talisman
- A **petition/memorial (shuwen)** is a written document the priest reads and then
  burns to transmit it to the deities: for healing, protection, safe childbirth,
  exam success, or on behalf of the dead.
- Process: approach the temple office; give your **full name, date and hour of birth
  (in lunar calendar if known), and address**; pay the fixed fee or donation;
  attend the rite at the given time.
- **Talismans (fu)** are prepared and empowered by the priest. Follow the
  instructions exactly — some are carried, some posted over a door, some burnt and
  the ash dissolved in water. Don't open a folded talisman unless told to.

## 4. A community jiao (outline for organisers)
- A **jiao** is a multi-day communal offering to renew the community's bond with the
  gods (often every few years, or after rebuilding a temple). Lengths: 1, 3, 5 days
  or more.
- Organiser tasks: engage a Zhengyi priest troupe (head priest, assistants,
  musicians); set the auspicious dates by consultation (often with the deity via
  divination blocks); arrange **vegetarian fasting** for the community during the rite;
  build the ritual area (altar of the Three Pure Ones, banners); register donor
  households, whose names are read in the memorials.
- Core rite sequence (varies): purification of the altar, invitation of the deities,
  presentation of memorials, offerings, **Universal Salvation (pudu)** for the
  wandering dead, sending off the gods.

## Gotchas
- Don't take photos of the inner altar or talismans without permission.
- Women who are menstruating are asked by some temples not to enter some halls;
  others don't. Follow posted rules without argument.
- Burning paper money is restricted in many cities for air quality; use the
  temple's furnace only.
- Uncertain: incense count, hand and door conventions are local and vary.
