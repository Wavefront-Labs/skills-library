# Consulting moon blocks and lot poems

**Goal:** ask a question of a temple's deity in the way worshippers at Chinese
popular-religion, Taoist and Mazu/Guanyin/Guandi temples do: with **jiaobei**
(moon blocks) for yes/no answers, and **lot-drawing** (求籤, *kau chim* in
Cantonese) for a numbered poem that addresses the matter. Within the tradition this
is a conversation with the deity, so it begins with worship, not with the sticks.

## 1. Before asking
1. Offer incense at the main altar first (see
   religion-philosophy.taoist-and-confucian-practice.taoist-temple-ritual.attending-and-supporting-taoist-liturgy).
2. Stand or kneel before the deity whose blocks/lots you'll use (each altar's set
   belongs to that deity).
3. **Introduce yourself silently or in a low voice:** full name, date of birth,
   address — then **one specific question**. Vague or multiple questions give
   unreadable answers. Good: "Should I accept the job offer from company X this
   month?" Bad: "How will my life go?"

## 2. Casting jiaobei (moon blocks)
Two crescent-shaped wooden or bamboo blocks, each with a **flat** and a **rounded**
side. Hold them together at chest height, palms together, ask, then drop them
gently to the floor in front of the altar.
| Result | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| one flat up, one round up | **shengbei / 聖杯** (holy blocks) | yes / approval |
| both flat up | **xiaobei / 笑杯** (laughing blocks) | unclear, the deity laughs — rephrase or ask again |
| both round up | **yinbei / 陰杯** (dark/angry blocks) | no / disapproval |
Many worshippers require **three consecutive shengbei** for an important matter.
Pick the blocks up, return them neatly to the altar when done.

## 3. Drawing a lot (kau chim / qiuqian)
1. Take the **bamboo cylinder** of numbered sticks (commonly 60 or 100) from the altar.
2. Kneel, hold it tilted forward at about 45°, state your question, and **shake it
   rhythmically** until **one stick** falls out. If several fall, put them back and
   start again.
3. **Confirm the stick** with jiaobei: ask "Is stick no. N your answer?" — a shengbei
   confirms; if not, return the stick and redraw. Many temples ask for **three
   shengbei** in a row on the same stick.
4. Note the number; return the stick to the cylinder.
5. Collect the matching **lot poem slip** (籤詩) from the numbered drawer/cabinet or
   the counter. Each is a short classical poem with a rank (upper/middle/lower
   fortune) and topical notes (marriage, travel, illness, lawsuits, lost items).

## 4. Interpretation
- Poems are allusive and in classical Chinese. Most temples have **interpreters**
  (解籤) — volunteer or paid at a booth (Hong Kong: Wong Tai Sin's interpreters charge
  a fee). Tell them your question; they read the poem against it.
- Many temples publish poem books or apps with modern Chinese/English explanations.
- A "lower" lot is advice to be careful, not a curse; worshippers often ask the deity
  what to do to improve the situation, with further jiaobei.

## 5. Afterwards
- Thank the deity with a bow; a small donation to the temple box is customary.
- Many keep the poem slip; others leave it at the temple (some temples have a place
  to tie unlucky slips).
- If the answer was fulfilled, worshippers traditionally return to give thanks (還願).

## Gotchas
- Don't throw blocks hard or toss them in the air — drop them from low height.
- One question per cast. Changing the question midway muddles the answer.
- Don't take the sticks or slips from another altar's set.
- Uncertain: number of sticks, the three-shengbei rule and fees vary by temple.

## Verify
- [ ] Incense offered and self-introduction given before asking.
- [ ] Question specific and single.
- [ ] Jiaobei outcome read correctly (holy / laughing / dark).
- [ ] Lot stick confirmed by shengbei before collecting the poem.
- [ ] Interpretation sought from the temple's interpreter or guide.
