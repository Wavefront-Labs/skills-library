# Traditional capping, wedding and mourning rites

**Goal:** plan or take part in the three classic Chinese life-cycle rites as they are
practised today — revived coming-of-age ceremonies, the traditional wedding, and
funeral and mourning — understanding the Confucian ritual frame (Zhu Xi's *Family
Rituals*) and where Taoist (or Buddhist) clergy usually enter. Family and regional
custom always takes precedence.

## 1. Coming of age: guanli and jili
- **Guanli (冠禮)** for young men (classically at 20) and **jili (笄禮)** for young
  women (classically at 15, hair pinned up). Revived today by schools, Confucian
  societies and hanfu groups, often at **18** as a *chengrenli* (成人禮).
- Classic sequence (Zhu Xi model):
  1. Announce the rite to the ancestors at the family altar.
  2. A respected **sponsor (賓)** officiates; the youth wears child's dress.
  3. **Three cappings (三加)**: successive caps/hats (or three hairpins), each with a
     spoken charge about adult virtue; the youth bows after each.
  4. A cup of wine/tea offered to the youth.
  5. The sponsor confers a **courtesy name (字)**, used by peers from now on.
  6. The youth bows to parents and elders and reports to the ancestors.
- Planning: venue (ancestral hall, school hall, Confucius temple), sponsor
  briefed, three garments/caps prepared, name chosen in advance, a written
  charge for each capping.

## 2. Wedding: the six rites in modern form
Classical **six rites (六禮)**: proposal (納采), asking names/birth dates (問名),
divining compatibility (納吉), betrothal gifts (納徵), requesting the date (請期),
fetching the bride (親迎). Common modern equivalents:
1. **Date selection** from an almanac or a temple/fortune master using both
   birth dates.
2. **Betrothal gifts (過大禮)** taken by the groom's family 2–4 weeks before:
   cakes, fruit, red envelopes, symbolic items (amounts and items by dialect group).
3. **Hair-combing ceremony (上頭)** on the eve (Cantonese custom), by a "good
   fortune" elder, with four blessings.
4. **Fetching the bride**: door games by bridesmaids, then the procession.
5. **Bowing (拜堂)**: to Heaven and Earth, to the parents (or ancestors' altar),
   then to each other.
6. **Tea ceremony (敬茶)**: couple kneel/bow and serve tea to each set of parents and
   elders in order of seniority; elders give red envelopes or jewellery.
7. Banquet; return visit to the bride's family on day 3 (回門).

## 3. Funeral and mourning
1. **At death:** inform family; place the body with feet toward the door; cover deity
   images in the house; set up a temporary spirit tablet/photo with incense and lamp.
2. **Mourning dress (五服)**: five grades by kinship — coarsest unhemmed sackcloth for
   children of the deceased, finer cloth for more distant kin. Today usually
   simplified to white/black with hemp or coloured patches marking relationship.
3. **Wake (2–7 days):** family keeps vigil; visitors offer incense, bow, give white
   envelopes (帛金, odd sums by many customs). **Taoist priests** (or Buddhist monks)
   perform rites (功德) to guide the soul: scripture recitation, breaking the hell
   gates, crossing the bridge.
4. **Encoffining and procession** at an auspicious hour; burial or cremation.
5. **Seven-day cycles (做七)** up to **49 days**, then 100 days and the first
   anniversary; the permanent ancestral tablet is installed and the deceased joins
   the ancestors (see religion-philosophy.taoist-and-confucian-practice.ancestor-veneration.keeping-an-ancestral-altar-and-qingming).
6. Mourners avoid weddings and festivities for a period (from 49 days to the
   traditional three years for parents — far shorter today).

## Gotchas
- Red is festive and forbidden at funerals; white is mourning and avoided at weddings.
- Clock gifts, white flowers, umbrellas: avoid as wedding gifts (homophone taboos).
- Funeral homes in Singapore/Hong Kong/Taiwan have set packages and hours; book the
  priest troupe through them or the family's temple.
- Uncertain: capping details in modern revivals are reconstructions; follow the
  organising society's script.
