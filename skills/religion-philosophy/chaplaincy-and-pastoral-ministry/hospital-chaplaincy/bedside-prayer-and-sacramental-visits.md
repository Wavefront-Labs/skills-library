# Bedside prayer and sacramental visits — a hospital visit that helps and does no harm

**Goal:** offer prayer, blessing or a sacramental rite at the bedside in a way that is
clinically safe, freely chosen by the patient, true to *their* tradition, and recorded
so the care team knows it happened.

## 1. Before you enter the room
1. **Check the referral or list and the chart flags** you are permitted to see: religion
   recorded on admission, isolation status, nil-by-mouth (NPO), allergies, "do not
   disturb", end-of-life or pre-op status. Access rules vary by institution — check.
2. **Speak to the nurse in charge.** Ask: is now a good time, can they swallow
   (matters for Communion), is a procedure imminent, any family dynamics to know.
3. **Infection control.** Hand hygiene before and after every patient. Follow the door
   sign: gown, gloves, mask or respirator as required. Do not carry books, stoles or pyxes
   between isolation rooms without the cleaning your policy requires; many chaplains use
   a laminated or wipeable prayer card and single-use oil swabs.

## 2. At the bedside
1. **Introduce yourself:** name, "one of the chaplains", and that the visit is optional.
2. **Ask consent before any prayer or touch.** "Would you like a prayer, or would you
   rather just talk?" Accept "no" without pressing; offer to return.
3. **Brief spiritual assessment.** Ask what gives them strength, whether faith matters
   to them now, and which tradition or community they belong to. Do not trust the
   admission form alone; "none" often still welcomes a blessing.
4. **Offer what fits.** A short prayer structure that works across most Christian and
   many generic settings:
   - *Address* ("Gracious God…") → *name the person and the situation* → *ask* (healing,
     peace, skill for the surgeons, courage for the family) → *close* in their tradition's
     form ("…through Christ our Lord. Amen", or a plain "Amen").
   - Keep it under a minute; invite them to add names or intentions.
   - **Pre-surgery blessing:** brief, with hand on shoulder or head only if they agree.
5. **Communion to the sick** (if you are authorised): confirm with the nurse they may
   swallow; a small piece of host or a few drops is acceptable in many churches —
   check your denomination's practice.

## 3. When the person needs a rite you cannot give
- **Roman Catholic** patient asking for anointing, confession or Viaticum: only a priest
  can anoint or absolve. Call the on-call Catholic priest; meanwhile you can pray with
  them and, if they wish, help them make an act of contrition privately.
- **Orthodox** patients may ask for their own priest for confession, Holy Unction or
  Communion — refer to their parish.
- **Muslim** patient: ask whether they would like to face the qibla (direction of Mecca)
  for prayer, if the bed can be turned; whether they want the imam or family to read
  Qur'an; and help with washing before prayer (wudu or tayammum with clean dust/stone
  if water is not possible). Near death, family may wish to recite or prompt the
  shahada — make space rather than lead it yourself unless asked.
- **Jewish** patient: offer the rabbi or community; near death, the *vidui* (confession)
  and the *Shema* are traditionally said, often by the person or family.
- **Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist** and others: ask what they need (holy water, a mala, a
  recording of chanting, their own priest or granthi) and contact the faith adviser.
Keep a current on-call list per tradition, including out of hours.

## 4. Afterwards
1. Hand hygiene; dispose of single-use items in the room.
2. **Document** in the chart or chaplaincy system per policy: date, time, visit
   requested by whom, what was offered (e.g. "prayer offered and accepted", "anointed by
   Fr X"), referrals made, follow-up plan. Keep confidences out of the clinical record —
   note *that* support was given, not the content of confession or private talk.
3. Tell the nurse if something affects care (fear of surgery, a wish not to be
   resuscitated expressed to you — escalate per policy, do not act on it yourself).

## Gotchas
- Isolation rooms: oil stocks and books are fomites. Use swabs and cards.
- A sacrament already given (e.g. anointing yesterday) may still be requested again;
  rules on repetition vary — let the priest decide.
- "Do you want me to pray?" in front of family can feel coercive; ask the patient alone if possible.

## Verify
- [ ] Nurse consulted and isolation/NPO status known before entering.
- [ ] Hand hygiene done before and after; PPE matched the door sign.
- [ ] Patient explicitly agreed to prayer or touch.
- [ ] The rite offered matched their tradition, or a referral was placed.
- [ ] Visit recorded with no confidential content, and follow-up planned.
