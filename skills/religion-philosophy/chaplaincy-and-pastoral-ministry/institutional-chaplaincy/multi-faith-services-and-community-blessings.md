# Multi-faith services and community blessings — ritual for people who share a place, not a faith

**Goal:** give a campus, workplace or team rituals that mark its moments — openings,
beginnings of term, seasons, losses — so that people of every faith and none can take
part honestly, while those who want their own tradition's worship still get it.

## 1. Know the setting's rules first
- **Who invited you and on what terms?** A university's charter, an employer's
  equality policy, a club's code of conduct or public-sector rules may limit prayer at
  official events. In some jurisdictions state-run institutions face legal limits on
  sponsored prayer — check before agreeing.
- **What your faith body allows:** some churches restrict shared worship with other
  faiths, or who may preside; multi-faith events are usually framed as "gathering" or
  "reflection" rather than joint worship.

## 2. Three formats and when to use them
1. **Inclusive invocation or reflection** (official events: commencement, opening a
   building, season start). One voice, words most can stand behind: gratitude, hopes
   for the community, those absent. Avoid doctrinal claims; close with "Amen" or with
   "May it be so" — ask the host which they prefer.
2. **Serial multi-faith gathering** (memorials, interfaith weeks). Each tradition offers
   its own reading or prayer in its own voice, in turn. Others listen respectfully; no
   one is asked to pray words they do not hold.
3. **Faith-specific worship** (a weekly Eucharist, Friday prayers, Shabbat dinner,
   meditation group). Voluntary, advertised as what it is, led by someone authorised in
   that tradition. The chaplaincy's job is to make room and find the leader.

## 3. A building or workplace blessing (about 10 minutes)
1. **Agree the purpose with the host** — e.g. "the safety and flourishing of all who work
   here".
2. **Gathering words:** why we are here; welcome to all beliefs.
3. **Reading:** a short passage, or one from each represented tradition.
4. **Blessing:** move through key spaces (entrance, workspace, a kitchen) with a sentence
   at each. If you sprinkle water or use incense, ask first — alarms and allergies.
5. **Closing:** a silence, then a sending word.
For a team: blessing before a season, prayer for safety; never pray for victory over
the opponents in a way that excludes teammates of other faiths.

## 4. When someone dies
A death in a community — a student, colleague or player — is where chaplaincy matters
most. Within 24–48 hours offer: a quiet room, an open drop-in, a book of condolence. Plan
a short memorial with family consent: welcome, the person's name, a few words from
friends, readings from the person's tradition if known, silence, a symbolic act such as
lighting candles, and where to get support. Refer to the family's own minister for the
funeral; a Muslim family may already have arranged a same-day or next-day burial, a
Jewish family may be sitting shiva — attend if invited and follow their customs.

## 5. Provision for every faith
- Keep a prayer or quiet room with qibla direction marked, ablution access if possible,
  shoe storage, mats and a calendar of festivals (Ramadan, Diwali, Passover, Easter,
  Vaisakhi and others).
- Keep a contact list of local leaders of each tradition for rites you cannot give
  (confession, anointing, Jumu'ah khutbah, bar/bat mitzvah).

## Gotchas
- An "inclusive" prayer that is really one tradition's with the name taken out pleases
  nobody; serial formats are more honest.
- Do not use institutional email lists to promote your own denomination's worship
  beyond what the host permits.
- Staff and students talk to chaplains in confidence; know the limits and the
  safeguarding route.

## Verify
- [ ] Host's rules and your faith body's rules on shared worship both checked.
- [ ] Format chosen to fit the event (invocation, serial, faith-specific).
- [ ] No one was asked to say words outside their belief; all participation voluntary.
- [ ] Ritual elements (water, flame, incense) cleared with the host.
- [ ] Referral contacts exist for every tradition present.
