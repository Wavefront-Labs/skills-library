# Scene prayer and post-tragedy memorials — ministry when the worst has happened

**Goal:** offer brief, wanted prayer and presence to victims, families and responders at
an emergency without getting in the way, and later lead a memorial that lets a whole,
mixed community grieve together.

## 1. Getting there the right way
1. **Do not self-deploy.** Go only when called by the agency you serve (police, fire,
   EMS, a disaster relief organisation) and report to the incident command post or the
   designated staging point. Unknown clergy are turned away or become a burden.
2. **Carry credentials** and wear the issued identifier (vest, badge). Bring water,
   weather gear, a pocket prayer book and cards, a notebook. Nothing that needs a table.
3. **Get a briefing:** where you may go, who leads chaplaincy, where families are being
   gathered (often a family assistance centre or reception centre), hazards.

## 2. At the scene
1. **Presence before words.** Most people want water, a blanket, a phone charger and
   information first. Use psychological first aid principles: safety, calm, connection,
   practical help.
2. **Ask before praying** — "Would a prayer help right now?" Many will say no; respect
   it. Ask about their faith rather than assuming.
3. **A scene prayer is 20–40 seconds:** name God in words they will recognise (or keep it
   general), name the people affected, ask for strength, rescue, comfort, close. For
   responders, a short blessing at shift change works better than a sermon.
4. **For the dying or recently dead at a scene:** you may be permitted to say a short
   prayer near the body, only with the commander's or coroner's permission — it may be a
   crime scene. A Catholic may want a priest for anointing (given conditionally soon
   after apparent death in some practice — the priest decides). Muslim, Jewish and
   other families will have strong wishes about the body and burial timing; pass them
   to the coroner's liaison, do not promise.
5. **Never** speculate about cause, numbers or identities, speak to media without
   authorisation, or share images.

## 3. Supporting responders
- Debrief and defusing are clinical/peer-support processes; you support them, you do
  not run them unless trained. Your contribution is availability, prayer if wanted,
  and noticing who is not coping — refer per agency policy.
- Line-of-duty deaths: the agency's honours protocol leads; you provide the rites.

## 4. Post-tragedy memorial or vigil
1. **Form a small planning group:** organisers, a family representative, civic leaders,
   and faith leaders reflecting the affected community. Ask families before naming
   anyone; some want privacy.
2. **Multi-faith shape:** each tradition speaks in its own voice in turn rather than
   blending prayers into one text. A common order:
   - welcome and purpose (civic leader);
   - silence or bell for each person lost, or a reading of names (with family consent);
   - readings or prayers from each represented tradition, kept to 2–3 minutes each;
   - a short reflection;
   - a symbolic act anyone can share: lighting candles, laying flowers, a minute's
     silence;
   - information on where to find support; a blessing or closing words.
3. **Inclusive language** for shared parts; faith-specific wording stays with its
   speaker. Include space for those of no faith.
4. **Practicalities:** accessibility, security, a quiet room with counsellors, media
   pen away from families, trigger warnings for graphic content in tributes.
5. **Anniversaries** often matter more than the first service; diary them.

## Gotchas
- Overlong prayers in a crisis feel like preaching; keep them short.
- A single tradition leading a public memorial can alienate others — check balance.
- Your own trauma exposure accumulates; get supervision.

## Verify
- [ ] You were dispatched and checked in with incident command.
- [ ] Every prayer was offered and accepted, not imposed.
- [ ] No information shared outside authorised channels.
- [ ] Memorial plan agreed with families and represented traditions.
- [ ] Support contacts announced; you have arranged your own debrief.
