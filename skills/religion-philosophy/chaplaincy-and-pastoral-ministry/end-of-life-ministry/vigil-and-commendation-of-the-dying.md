# Vigil and commendation of the dying — praying a person to their death

**Goal:** be present at a death in a way that honours the dying person's own tradition,
gets the right minister there in time, gives family words and gestures they can join,
and leaves them supported afterwards.

## 1. When you are called
1. **Ask the nurse how near death is** (hours? minutes?) and whether the person can
   swallow or hear. Hearing is often thought to persist late; speak as if they can.
2. **Find out the tradition** from the person, family or record, and whether a specific
   minister has been asked for. If a rite you cannot give is needed, call for it *first*,
   then go in.
3. Hand hygiene and PPE as on any visit.

## 2. "Last rites" — who does what
- **Roman Catholic:** "last rites" is a cluster: confession, anointing of the sick and
  **Viaticum** (Communion as food for the journey), given by a priest, often with the
  **apostolic pardon**. If the person cannot swallow, Viaticum is omitted. A deacon or
  lay minister can bring Viaticum and lead the commendation but cannot anoint or absolve.
  If no priest can arrive, pray with them anyway; the church teaches God is not bound
  by the sacrament's absence.
- **Anglican/Episcopal and Lutheran:** a priest or pastor may anoint, give Communion and
  lead prayers at the time of death; many lay chaplains can lead the commendation.
  Varies by church — check.
- **Orthodox:** their own priest for confession, Holy Unction and Communion; there are
  specific prayers at the parting of the soul.
- **Jewish:** the dying person, if able, may say the *vidui* (confession); the *Shema*
  ("Hear, O Israel…") is traditionally the last words said, by them or by those
  present. Ask whether family want a rabbi or the burial society (chevra kadisha).
- **Muslim:** family may gently prompt the dying person to say the *shahada*, and may
  turn them to face the qibla. Recitation of Surah Ya-Sin is common. After death, the
  body is handled minimally and burial is ideally within a day — tell staff.
- **Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist:** family may chant, place holy water or tulsi leaf, recite
  Gurbani, or ask that the body be left undisturbed for a time. Ask; do not guess.

## 3. A commendation structure (Christian, adaptable)
1. **Gather:** invite family to stand or sit close; touch if they wish.
2. **Short reading:** a psalm (e.g. Psalm 23) or a few lines of scripture.
3. **Litany:** brief petitions with a repeated response ("Lord, have mercy"), naming the
   person.
4. **Commendation:** a prayer entrusting the person to God, e.g. the traditional "Go
   forth upon your journey from this world, O Christian soul…" (wording varies by book).
5. **Lord's Prayer** together, then silence. Let silence be long.
For long vigils, repeat short familiar prayers, sing a hymn they loved, or read slowly.

## 4. At and after the death
- Note the time for staff, but let the clinician confirm death.
- **Prayer after death:** a short prayer for the deceased and for those who mourn;
  sprinkling with water or making the sign of the cross if the tradition does so.
- Offer family time; ask whether they want to wash or dress the body if the tradition
  and policy allow. Muslim and Jewish communities may want their own people to do this.
- Give practical next steps (bereavement office, funeral director, faith community).

## 5. Document
Record who was present, rites given and by whom, referrals and follow-up offered.

## Gotchas
- Waiting to "do it properly" when death is minutes away loses the moment; pray now,
  bring the priest as well.
- Families may disagree about faith; serve the dying person's wishes where known.
- Do not promise a priest will arrive in time.

## Verify
- [ ] Tradition and wishes identified; needed minister called before you began.
- [ ] Only rites you are authorised to give were given.
- [ ] Family invited into prayer, not made spectators.
- [ ] Post-death needs (body handling, burial timing) passed to staff.
- [ ] Visit documented and bereavement follow-up offered.
