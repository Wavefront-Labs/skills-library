# Doing cross-tradition comparison

**Goal:** produce comparative philosophical work (an essay, seminar paper, lecture)
that treats each tradition on its own terms, finds real points of contact and
difference, and avoids the classic mistakes of comparative work.

## 1. Start from a question, not a pair of names
Weak: "Confucius and Aristotle". Better: "How do Confucius and Aristotle understand
the role of habituation in becoming virtuous?" A shared problem gives the comparison
a *tertium comparationis* — the third thing in respect of which two items are compared.
Good question types:
- Same problem, different answers (personal identity in Buddhism and Locke).
- Same concept-family, different shapes (justice: *dharma*, *yi*, *dikaiosynē*).
- Method vs method (Nyāya debate vs Socratic elenchus).

## 2. Build a concept dossier for each side
For each key term, a page with:
1. The term in its original language and script, with transliteration.
2. Three or more passages where it occurs, with context.
3. How major commentators within the tradition read it.
4. How it is translated, and what each translation brings in or drops.
5. Its neighbouring concepts in the same system (e.g. *ren* with *li* and *yi*).
Compare dossiers, not dictionary glosses.

## 3. Map similarity and difference explicitly
A simple table works:

| Dimension | Tradition A | Tradition B | Same / different / not addressed |
|---|---|---|---|

Dimensions: what the concept is for, what it presupposes (metaphysics, human
nature), how one acquires it, what practices embody it. "Not addressed" is
important — absence of a question in one tradition is a finding, not a deficiency.

## 4. Choose a comparative mode
- **Contrastive:** illuminate each by difference.
- **Convergence:** argue a shared insight from different routes.
- **Problem-solving ("fusion"):** use resources from several traditions to address a
  contemporary problem directly.
- **Genealogical:** trace historical contact where it existed (Greek–Indian contact
  after Alexander; Islamic transmission of Aristotle; Jesuits in China; Buddhism's spread).
State the mode in your introduction.

## 5. Working with translation
- Use original-language texts where possible, or at least two translations.
- Flag terms you leave untranslated and explain why.
- Note when a translation already imports another tradition's vocabulary (e.g. early
  translations rendering *tian* as "God" or *li* as "law").

## 6. Read in, not just across
Engage with scholars *within* each tradition, including living practitioners and
non-Western scholarship in the original languages where you can. Cite
traditions' own debates (e.g. Mencius vs Xunzi) rather than presenting a monolithic
"Chinese view".

## 7. Writing it up
Structure: question → each tradition's position in its own frame → comparison
table/discussion → what the comparison shows that neither alone shows → limits.

## Gotchas
- **False equivalence:** treating *dao* = Logos or *nirvāṇa* = heaven because they
  sound alike.
- **Asymmetry:** using Western philosophy as the yardstick and the other tradition as
  "data". Test it: would your framing still make sense if reversed?
- **Essentialising:** "the Indian mind", "the African worldview". Traditions are
  internally plural.
- **Cherry-picking** quotes without context.
- Treating living traditions as historical curiosities.

## Verify
- [ ] The essay states one question and a tertium comparationis.
- [ ] Each key term has a dossier with original language, passages and commentators.
- [ ] Similarities and differences (and "not addressed") are mapped explicitly.
- [ ] The framing passes the reversal test for asymmetry.
- [ ] Each tradition's internal diversity is acknowledged with at least one internal debate.
