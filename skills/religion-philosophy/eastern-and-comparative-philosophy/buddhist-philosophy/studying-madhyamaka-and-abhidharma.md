# Studying Madhyamaka, Abhidharma and Buddhist logic

**Goal:** work through the main Indian Buddhist philosophical schools in a sensible
order, using the analytic methods they themselves use (categorisation, reductio,
debate), so you can read root texts and commentaries with understanding.

Within the tradition, philosophical study is part of the path: hearing, reflecting
and meditating (*śruta, cintā, bhāvanā*). This skill focuses on the reflective,
analytical part.

## 1. Foundations
- **Three marks:** impermanence, unsatisfactoriness (*duḥkha*), not-self (*anātman*).
- **Dependent origination** (*pratītyasamutpāda*): things arise in dependence on
  causes and conditions; the twelve links as its classical formulation.
- **Not-self analysis:** the person as five aggregates (form, feeling, perception,
  formations, consciousness) — nothing over and above them is found.

## 2. Abhidharma (c. 3rd c. BCE onward)
Systematic analysis of experience into momentary ultimate constituents (*dharmas*).
- Theravāda Abhidhamma: e.g. *Abhidhammattha-saṅgaha* as a manual (Bhikkhu Bodhi's
  *Comprehensive Manual* is a standard English edition).
- Sarvāstivāda/Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika debates, summarised in Vasubandhu's
  *Abhidharmakośa*.
**Exercise:** take one minute of experience (e.g. hearing a sound and feeling
annoyed) and list the mental factors present using an Abhidharma list.

## 3. Madhyamaka
- **Nāgārjuna's** *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK, c. 2nd c. CE): all dharmas are empty
  (*śūnya*) of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*) because they are dependently arisen.
- **Two truths:** conventional truth (how things function) and ultimate truth
  (their emptiness). Emptiness is itself empty — not a hidden essence.
- **Method:** *prasaṅga* (reductio) — show that any thesis asserting intrinsic
  existence leads to absurdity; the **tetralemma** (*catuṣkoṭi*): is, is not, both,
  neither — all four rejected for intrinsically existing things.
- Later Tibetan doxography distinguishes Prāsaṅgika (Candrakīrti) and Svātantrika
  (Bhāviveka) approaches.
**Exercise:** take MMK ch. 1 (conditions) — reconstruct one verse as an argument
with premises and conclusion, then state the opponent's view it targets.

## 4. Yogācāra
Asaṅga and Vasubandhu: the three natures (imagined, dependent, perfected), store
consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*), and the analysis of experience as
"cognition-only" (*vijñaptimātra*). Read Vasubandhu's *Twenty Verses* and *Thirty
Verses*. Scholars debate whether it is idealism; note the interpretation your source takes.

## 5. Pramāṇa (logic and epistemology)
Dignāga and Dharmakīrti: only two valid means of knowledge — perception and
inference. Inference uses a three-part reason: the subject has the property; the
reason is present in similar cases; absent in dissimilar cases.
Standard example: "On the hill there is fire, because there is smoke, as in a kitchen."
This tradition underlies the **Tibetan monastic debate** (*rtsod pa*), where a
challenger tests a defender's claims with consequences.

## 6. How to study
1. Read a root text verse with **one commentary** from within the tradition
   (e.g. Candrakīrti on Nāgārjuna) and one modern study (e.g. Garfield's MMK
   translation and commentary; Siderits & Katsura).
2. Write each argument in numbered premises.
3. Discuss with a partner, debate-style: one asserts, the other draws consequences.
4. Return to contemplation: what changes when you view this experience as empty?

## Gotchas
- Reading "emptiness" as nothingness/nihilism — Madhyamaka explicitly rejects this.
- Confusing schools: Abhidharma dharmas exist ultimately; Madhyamaka denies it.
- Over-relying on a single translation of technical terms (*svabhāva*, *vijñapti*).
- Skipping Abhidharma — Madhyamaka is largely a response to it.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state the two truths and why emptiness ≠ non-existence.
- [ ] You have formalised at least three MMK verses as arguments.
- [ ] You can list the five aggregates and explain the not-self analysis.
- [ ] You can give the three conditions of a valid reason in Dharmakīrti.
- [ ] You can describe one Yogācāra concept and one interpretive debate about it.
