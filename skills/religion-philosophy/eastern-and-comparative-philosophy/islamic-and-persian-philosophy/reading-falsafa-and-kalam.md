# Reading falsafa and kalām

**Goal:** understand the main traditions of philosophical and rational-theological
thought in the Islamic world well enough to read primary texts in translation,
reconstruct their arguments, and see how they responded to each other.

## 1. The three streams
- **Kalām** — rational theology defending and articulating faith. Main schools:
  **Muʿtazila** (God's justice and unity; human free will; the Qurʾan created), **Ashʿarī**
  (God's will and power primary; occasionalism — God creates each moment; acquisition
  *kasb*), **Māturīdī** (closer to a middle position on human agency; widely followed
  among Ḥanafīs).
- **Falsafa** — philosophy in the Greek (Aristotelian/Neoplatonic) line: al-Kindī,
  al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna), Ibn Rushd (Averroes).
- **Ḥikma / mystical philosophy** — Suhrawardī's Illuminationism (*ishrāq*), Ibn ʿArabī's
  metaphysics (later summarised as *waḥdat al-wujūd*, unity of being), and Mullā
  Ṣadrā's synthesis (primacy of existence, substantial motion), still taught in Iranian
  seminaries.

## 2. Key arguments to reconstruct
- **Avicenna — essence and existence:** what a thing *is* (essence) does not include
  that it *is* (existence); so contingent things need a cause of existence.
- **Avicenna — the Necessary Existent (*wājib al-wujūd*):** everything is either
  necessary or contingent in itself; an infinite chain of contingents needs a
  necessary ground; that ground is one, simple, and is God. (Metaphysics of *al-Shifāʾ*,
  and *al-Ishārāt*.)
- **Avicenna — the Floating Man:** a person created in mid-air without sensations would
  still know they exist — so self-awareness doesn't depend on the body.
- **Al-Ghazālī — *Tahāfut al-falāsifa*** (Incoherence of the Philosophers): charges the
  philosophers with error on twenty points, three judged grave (eternity of the
  world, God's knowledge only of universals, denial of bodily resurrection); argues
  causal necessity isn't observed — only conjunction.
- **Ibn Rushd — *Tahāfut al-tahāfut*** (Incoherence of the Incoherence): replies point
  by point; *Faṣl al-maqāl* argues philosophy is obligatory for those capable of it and
  agrees with revelation properly interpreted.

## 3. A reading order
1. A survey (e.g. Peter Adamson's *Philosophy in the Islamic World* or his podcast
   series) for the map.
2. Al-Fārābī, *The Political Regime* or *Attainment of Happiness* (short).
3. Avicenna, excerpts on essence/existence and the soul.
4. Al-Ghazālī, *Deliverance from Error* (*al-Munqidh*), then *Tahāfut* discussions 1 and 17.
5. Ibn Rushd, *Decisive Treatise*.
6. Suhrawardī, *The Philosophy of Illumination*, introduction.
7. Mullā Ṣadrā selections on the primacy of existence.

## 4. Method
- For each text, write the argument in numbered premises and identify the
  opponent addressed.
- Track key terms in Arabic/Persian: *wujūd* (existence), *māhiyya* (essence), *ʿaql*
  (intellect), *nafs* (soul), *ʿilla* (cause). Translations vary.
- Note the Greek source being used (Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, Plotinus via the
  "Theology of Aristotle", Proclus via the *Liber de causis*).
- Read within the tradition's own frame: these authors argued as Muslims (and
  Christians and Jews in the same milieu) about faith and reason, not as outsiders.

## 5. Practice within the tradition
In seminary (*ḥawza*) and madrasa settings these texts are studied line by line with
a teacher, often memorising key definitions and debating in pairs (*mubāḥatha*).
Emulate this: one partner reads and explains, the other questions.

## Gotchas
- Calling all of it "Arabic philosophy" — much was written in Persian, and
  contributors included Christians and Jews.
- Assuming philosophy "ended" with Averroes; it flourished in Iran and India for
  centuries after.
- Equating kalām with falsafa.
- Overreading Ghazālī as anti-reason; he uses logic extensively and endorsed it.
