# Reading the Laozi and Zhuangzi in practice

**Goal:** read the two foundational Daoist texts carefully — with awareness of
translation and commentary — and take up the stance they describe (naturalness,
non-forcing action, freedom from fixed views) in daily life.

The division between "philosophical" (*daojia*) and "religious" (*daojiao*) Daoism is
partly a modern scholarly convention; many practitioners see continuity. This skill
covers the texts' philosophical practice; ritual and liturgical Daoism is a separate
area.

## 1. Core concepts
- **Dao 道** — the way; the unnameable source and pattern of things ("The dao that can
  be spoken is not the constant dao", ch. 1).
- **De 德** — virtue/power: the potency a thing has when it follows its nature.
- **Wu wei 無為** — non-action, effortless or non-forcing action; not passivity.
- **Ziran 自然** — "self-so", naturalness, spontaneity.
- **Pu 樸** — the uncarved block: simplicity before artificial shaping.
- **Rou ruo** — softness and weakness (water overcoming the hard, ch. 78).
- In the Zhuangzi: **perspectivism** (the relativity of "this" and "that"),
  **uselessness** as freedom, **transformation of things** (the butterfly dream).

## 2. Reading the Daodejing
- 81 short chapters; the received text follows Wang Bi's (3rd c.) edition. The
  Mawangdui silk manuscripts (1973) and Guodian bamboo slips (1993) show earlier
  variants and different chapter order — good translations note these.
- Read **two translations side by side** (one literal/scholarly, e.g. Lau or
  Ivanhoe; one interpretive) — the classical Chinese is terse and translations vary
  enormously. If you can, look up key characters.
- Read one chapter a day: aloud, slowly, twice. Then write one sentence: what
  it asks of you.

## 3. Reading the Zhuangzi
- **Inner Chapters (1–7)** are traditionally attributed to Zhuangzi himself; the
  Outer and Miscellaneous chapters come from later hands. Start with the Inner.
- It teaches through stories and humour: Cook Ding carving the ox (ch. 3) as skill in
  harmony with the dao; the butterfly dream (end of ch. 2); the useless tree (ch. 1, 4).
- Guo Xiang's commentary (and edition) shapes the text we read; note when a
  translation leans on it.
- Ask of each story: what fixed view is being loosened?

## 4. Practices drawn from the texts
- **Wu wei in work:** before acting, ask "Where am I forcing?" Look for the "grain" of
  the situation, as Cook Ding follows the natural joints. Do less, more precisely.
- **Simplicity (ch. 19, 80):** reduce wants for a week — one fewer purchase, one fewer
  commitment — and notice the effect.
- **Fasting of the heart-mind (xin zhai 心齋, Zhuangzi ch. 4):** listen not with ears
  but with the heart, then not with the heart but with *qi*; let the mind become
  empty and receptive. A short daily seated practice of quiet receptivity.
- **Sitting and forgetting (zuowang 坐忘, ch. 6):** letting go of rules, knowledge and
  self-image in stillness. Later Daoist traditions developed it into meditation methods.
- **Perspective exercise:** take a strong opinion and write the case for the opposite
  "that" — Zhuangzi's hinge of the dao.
- **Time in nature:** both texts draw on water, valleys, trees; walk and observe.

## Gotchas
- Equating wu wei with laziness or withdrawal; the texts describe effective action.
- Treating a single loose translation as "what Laozi said".
- Reading the Zhuangzi as solemn scripture and missing its irony.
- Assuming Laozi and Zhuangzi say the same thing — the Daodejing often addresses
  rulers; the Zhuangzi is more individual and sceptical of politics.
