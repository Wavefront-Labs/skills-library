# Navigating the darshanas and Nyāya debate

**Goal:** know what each classical Indian school holds and how it argues, read its
texts in the traditional sūtra–commentary format, and practise the formal inference
and debate method that all schools shared through Nyāya.

## 1. The map
"Āstika" schools accept the authority of the Veda; "nāstika" ones (Buddhist, Jain,
Cārvāka) do not. The six āstika darśanas are traditionally paired:
- **Nyāya** (logic, epistemology) + **Vaiśeṣika** (atomist ontology, categories).
- **Sāṃkhya** (dualism of *puruṣa*, consciousness, and *prakṛti*, matter with three
  guṇas) + **Yoga** (Patañjali's *Yoga Sūtra*: practice to isolate *puruṣa*).
- **Mīmāṃsā** (Pūrva Mīmāṃsā: Vedic ritual hermeneutics, dharma as injunction) +
  **Vedānta** (Uttara Mīmāṃsā: the Upaniṣads on Brahman).
Vedānta's major sub-schools: **Advaita** (Śaṅkara — Brahman alone is real, the self
is Brahman), **Viśiṣṭādvaita** (Rāmānuja — qualified non-dualism, souls and world as
Brahman's body), **Dvaita** (Madhva — real difference between God, souls and matter).
**Jain philosophy:** *anekāntavāda* (many-sidedness of reality) and *syādvāda*, the
sevenfold predication ("in some respect it is…").

## 2. How the texts work
Each school has a root **sūtra** text (terse aphorisms — *Nyāya Sūtra*, *Brahma
Sūtra*, *Sāṃkhya Kārikā*, *Yoga Sūtra*) read through **bhāṣya** (commentary) and
sub-commentaries. You almost never read a sūtra alone.
Reading method:
1. Sūtra in transliteration with word-by-word gloss.
2. The main bhāṣya (e.g. Śaṅkara on the *Brahma Sūtra*; Vātsyāyana on the *Nyāya
   Sūtra*; Vyāsa on the *Yoga Sūtra*).
3. Identify the **pūrvapakṣa** (opponent's prima facie view) and the
   **siddhānta** (established conclusion). Most passages state the opponent's case
   first, strongly, then refute it.

## 3. Pramāṇas (means of valid knowledge)
Schools differ in how many they accept:
- Cārvāka: perception only. Buddhist (Dignāga): perception, inference.
- Sāṃkhya, Yoga: + testimony (*śabda*). Nyāya: + comparison (*upamāna*) = four.
- Mīmāṃsā (Bhāṭṭa) and Advaita: + postulation (*arthāpatti*) and non-apprehension
  (*anupalabdhi*) = six.
Knowing a school's pramāṇa list predicts how it will argue.

## 4. The Nyāya five-membered inference
1. **Pratijñā** (thesis): The hill has fire.
2. **Hetu** (reason): Because it has smoke.
3. **Udāharaṇa** (example + rule): Wherever there's smoke there's fire, as in a kitchen.
4. **Upanaya** (application): This hill has smoke pervaded by fire.
5. **Nigamana** (conclusion): Therefore the hill has fire.
**Fallacies of the reason (hetvābhāsa):** the classic five — inconclusive (*savyabhicāra*),
contradictory (*viruddha*), counterbalanced (*satpratipakṣa*), unestablished
(*asiddha*), and contradicted/mistimed (*bādhita*). Exact names vary by text.

## 5. Debate
Nyāya distinguishes:
- **Vāda** — honest discussion aimed at truth between teacher and student or peers.
- **Jalpa** — debate to win, using legitimate and some tricky means.
- **Vitaṇḍā** — destructive debate, only refuting without a thesis.
**Practice drill:** in pairs, one states a thesis with a five-membered inference; the
other must name a specific hetvābhāsa if one applies or concede; swap. Then rewrite
an argument from a Vedānta text as a five-membered inference.

## Gotchas
- Treating "Hindu philosophy" as one view — the schools disagree sharply.
- Reading the *Yoga Sūtra* as a posture manual; it is a philosophy of mind and liberation.
- Ignoring the pūrvapakṣa — misattributing the opponent's view to the author is a
  common error.
- Diacritics matter for searching: Śaṅkara/Shankara, Nyāya/Nyaya.
