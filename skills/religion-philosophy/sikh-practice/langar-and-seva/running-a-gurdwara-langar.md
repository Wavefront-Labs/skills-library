# Running a gurdwara langar

**Goal:** prepare and serve langar — the free community meal of the gurdwara — as
seva (selfless service): everyone fed equally, seated together, without asking who
they are, with the kitchen run safely enough to do it every week.

## 1. What langar is in practice
- Established by Guru Nanak and institutionalised by Guru Amar Das; it expresses
  equality — all sit in **pangat** (rows) and eat the same food.
- Open to **anyone**, Sikh or not, any time it is running; no payment, no questions.
- Food is **vegetarian** (no meat, fish or eggs) so that no one is excluded; SRM
  forbids meat in the langar. Most langars also avoid alcohol in cooking (obviously)
  and many avoid onion/garlic only by local custom.
- Funded and supplied by the sangat's **dasvandh** (tenth of income) and donations
  of food and labour.

## 2. Menu and quantities (typical Sunday langar)
Classic: **dal**, a **sabzi**, **roti/parshada**, rice, and **kheer** or a sweet;
yoghurt/raita, salad or achar. Plan per 100 people (rule of thumb — adjust to your
sangat's appetite):
| Item | Raw quantity / 100 people |
|---|---|
| Dal (dry lentils) | 5–6 kg |
| Rice | 6–7 kg |
| Atta (flour) for roti | 10–12 kg (~250–300 roti) |
| Vegetables for sabzi | 12–15 kg |
| Milk for kheer | 15–20 L |
Note who is baking roti: a team of 4–6 on tawa/machine is the usual bottleneck.

## 3. Kitchen seva rules
1. **Head covered, hands washed**, shoes removed; aprons where provided.
2. Cook while reciting or listening to gurbani — many langars play the diwan kirtan.
3. **Do not taste while cooking** (food is first offered to the Guru); traditionally
   a portion is placed before the Guru Granth Sahib and **ardas** is done to bless
   the langar before it is served.
4. Follow your country's food-hygiene rules: separate raw/cooked handling, fridge
   at ≤5 °C, hot-hold at ≥63 °C (UK figures; check local), food-safety training for
   lead cooks, allergen information (wheat, milk, nuts, mustard are common).
5. Large pots: use two people for lifting; stirring paddles, not hands.

## 4. Serving in pangat
- Diners sit cross-legged in rows on mats (chairs/tables for those who need them).
- Sevadars walk the rows with buckets/trays, serve **into the diner's thali** without touching
  the plate or the diner, and ask "*hor?*" ("more?") — don't pile food on.
- Roti is received with **both hands, cupped**.
- **Water** is served too; sevadars refill continually.
- Diners don't waste food: take what you will eat. Leftover langar is taken home or
  distributed, not binned where avoidable.

## 5. Other gurdwara seva
- **Joda ghar** (shoe-keeping and cleaning) — seen as especially humbling seva.
- **Bartan** (washing up), cleaning the darbar hall, sweeping, parking.
- Volunteers sign up on a **rota**; families often sponsor a whole Sunday langar.

## Gotchas
- The roti line and washing-up are where rotas fail; staff them first.
- Newcomers often try to pay — gently explain it is free; point to the golak if
  they wish to donate.
- Seva is to be done without ego; avoid announcing or photographing your own seva.
- Uncertain: some gurdwaras now serve langar at tables throughout; whether that is
  acceptable is debated locally — follow your committee's decision.

## Verify
- [ ] Menu fully vegetarian and allergens labelled.
- [ ] Ardas done over the langar before serving.
- [ ] Quantities scaled to expected numbers, roti and washing-up rota staffed.
- [ ] Everyone seated and served equally, with no one turned away.
- [ ] Hot/cold holding temperatures checked.
