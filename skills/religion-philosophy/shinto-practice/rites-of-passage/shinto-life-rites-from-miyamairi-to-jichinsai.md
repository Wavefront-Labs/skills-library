# Shinto life rites — from miyamairi to jichinsai

**Goal:** arrange and take part in the Shinto rites that mark a life and a household —
pregnancy, a baby's first shrine visit, children's blessings, marriage, and the
blessing of land before building — booked correctly and at the right time.

> Draft. Timing customs differ by region (especially for miyamairi) and families
> often adjust dates to fit weekends and weather. Each rite is a **gokitō** booked at the
> shrine office; fees are indicative only. Most Japanese funerals are Buddhist; Shinto
> funerals (*shinsōsai*) exist but are held at home or a hall, not in the shrine, because
> death is a source of impurity.

## 1. Obi-iwai — safe childbirth (5th month of pregnancy)
On a **Day of the Dog** (*inu no hi*, every 12 days in the traditional calendar — dogs
are associated with easy births) in the 5th month, the expectant mother visits the shrine
for a prayer for safe delivery and receives a blessed **haraobi** (maternity sash) or brings her
own to be blessed. Check the shrine's inu-no-hi dates; they are busy days.

## 2. Hatsumiyamairi — first shrine visit
- **When:** commonly around the 31st day after birth for boys and 32nd–33rd for girls
  (regional variation; many simply go at about one month, when mother and baby are ready).
- **Who:** parents and often grandparents; traditionally the **paternal grandmother carries
  the baby**.
- **Dress:** baby in white underclothes with a colourful kimono (*iwaigi*) draped over the
  carrier; adults in formal wear or kimono.
- Book a gokitō; the baby is presented to the ujigami as a new parishioner. Fee often
  ¥5,000–10,000.
- At home around the 100th day: **okuizome**, the first-meal ceremony.

## 3. Shichi-go-san (around 15 November)
- Boys aged **3 and 5**, girls aged **3 and 7** (by traditional *kazoe* age or actual age —
  families choose). Many go on a weekend in October–November.
- Children wear kimono or formal clothes (boys' hakama at 5; girls' full kimono with obi at 7).
- Gokitō for health and growth; children receive **chitose-ame** ("thousand-year candy").
- Photo studios book out — reserve early.

## 4. Shinzen-shiki — the shrine wedding
Booked months ahead with the shrine (and often a wedding planner). Typical order (~20–30 min):
1. **Sanshin no gi** — procession (*sanshin*) into the hall led by priests and miko under a
   red parasol.
2. **Shubatsu** — purification of all present.
3. **Norito** — the priest reports the marriage to the kami.
4. **San-san-kudo (sankon no gi)** — the couple share sake from three cups of increasing
   size, three sips each.
5. **Seishi sōjō** — the groom reads the vows; the bride adds her name.
6. Ring exchange (a modern addition).
7. **Tamagushi hōten** — the couple offer sakaki branches.
8. **Shinzoku-katame no sakazuki** — both families drink sake together, joining the families.
Dress: bride in *shiromuku* (white kimono) or iro-uchikake; groom in black *montsuki haori hakama*.
Register the marriage at the municipal office separately — that is the legal act.

## 5. Jichinsai — groundbreaking ceremony
Before building a house or building:
- Book a priest from the local shrine; held on the site on an auspicious day (many consult
  the *rokuyō* calendar and avoid *butsumetsu*).
- The site is marked with four bamboo poles and a sacred rope (*shimenawa*); an altar with
  offerings is set up; a mound of sand is prepared.
- Rites: purification, norito, the owner and builder make symbolic first cuts in the sand with
  a sickle, hoe and spade (*kama, kuwa, suki*), tamagushi offering, sake shared.
- Costs include the priest's fee (often ¥20,000–50,000) plus offerings; the builder usually
  arranges the tent and set-up.

## Gotchas
- Booking miyamairi or shichi-go-san on peak weekends without a reservation.
- Treating the shrine wedding as legal registration.
- Holding a funeral at a shrine — not done.
