# Hatsumōde and joining a matsuri

**Goal:** keep the Shinto year — the New Year shrine visit and the seasonal festivals —
and take a real part in your local shrine's **matsuri**, including the mikoshi
procession, as a parishioner (ujiko) rather than a spectator.

> Draft. Each shrine has its own festival calendar, rites and customs; dates for many
> matsuri are fixed by the shrine or moved to the nearest weekend. The local
> neighbourhood association and shrine office are the sources of truth.

## 1. The shrine year (common national observances)
| When | Observance | What people do |
|------|-----------|----------------|
| 1–3 Jan (to ~15 Jan) | **Hatsumōde** | first shrine visit of the year |
| ~3 Feb | **Setsubun** | bean-throwing (*mamemaki*) to drive out misfortune: *"Oni wa soto, fuku wa uchi!"* |
| 17 Feb | **Kinensai** | prayers for the year's harvest |
| Spring/autumn (varies) | **Reitaisai** | the shrine's main annual festival — often its matsuri |
| 30 Jun / 31 Dec | **Ōharae** | Great Purification (see `religion-philosophy.shinto-practice.purification-rites.misogi-and-oharae-purification`) |
| 15 Nov (season) | **Shichi-go-san** | children's shrine visits (see the rites-of-passage skill) |
| 23 Nov | **Niinamesai** | thanksgiving for the new rice harvest |
Also: 1st and 15th of each month are customary shrine-visit days.

## 2. Hatsumōde, step by step
1. Pick your **ujigami** (local guardian shrine) or a major shrine; expect very large crowds at
   famous ones on 1 January — the 2nd/3rd or late evening are calmer.
2. Return last year's **ofuda**, **omamori** and **hamaya** (arrows) to the collection point
   (*kosatsu-osamejo*) to be burned.
3. Pray at the hall (see `religion-philosophy.shinto-practice.shrine-ritual.shrine-visit-and-formal-prayer`),
   giving thanks for last year and stating hopes for the new.
4. Buy new **ofuda** for the kamidana, omamori and perhaps a hamaya; draw an **omikuji**.
5. Many also get a New Year gokitō for family safety.

## 3. Taking part in a matsuri
A matsuri is a ritual in which the kami is honoured and, in many cases, carried through the
parish in a **mikoshi** (portable shrine) to bless it. The core rites inside the shrine are
done by priests; the community carries the festival.
**Getting involved**
- Join through the **chōnaikai** (neighbourhood association) or the shrine's festival group;
  newcomers and foreign residents are often welcome if they ask early (weeks ahead).
- Expect preparation work: setting up lanterns, cleaning, rehearsals, contributing to the
  festival fund (*hōnōkin*).
**Carrying a mikoshi**
- Dress: **happi** coat in the group's colours, headband (hachimaki), shorts or momohiki,
  **jika-tabi** split-toe shoes; tape or pads for the shoulder.
- Rules: the leader (with clappers or a whistle) controls starting, stopping and setting down;
  follow the call — "*wasshoi!*" or "*seiya!*" depending on region.
- Shoulder under the pole, match the rhythm of the people beside you, and rotate out when tired;
  never let go suddenly.
- Before departure, the priest transfers the kami into the mikoshi (*mitama-utsushi*) — the
  mikoshi is sacred: do not sit on it, climb it or touch the roof ornaments.
- Alcohol: drinking is common, but drunk bearers are a safety hazard; many groups now limit it.

## 4. Other roles
Pulling **dashi/yatai** (festival floats), playing festival music (*hayashi*: flutes, drums),
children's mikoshi, food stalls run by the association.

## Gotchas
- Joining a mikoshi on the day without the group's permission — teams are organised and insured.
- Not returning old ofuda and amulets.
- Wearing street shoes under a mikoshi — slipping is a crush risk.
