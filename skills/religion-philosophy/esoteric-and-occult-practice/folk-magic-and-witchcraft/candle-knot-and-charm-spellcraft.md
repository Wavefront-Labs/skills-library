# Candle, knot and charm spellcraft

**Goal:** perform the common forms of practical folk magic with a clear structure,
safe handling of fire and materials, and awareness of where each practice comes from.

Folk magic is regional and family-transmitted: British and Irish cunning-folk
practice, Appalachian and Ozark folk magic, Scandinavian *trolldom*, Italian
*stregheria*, Mexican *curanderismo*, and **hoodoo** (rootwork/conjure), an African
American tradition with deep roots in Black Southern history and Protestant
Christianity. Modern witchcraft draws widely on these. Learn a tradition from its
practitioners where you can; hoodoo in particular is best learned from Black
practitioners and with acknowledgement of its origins.

## 1. The common structure
1. **Intent:** write one specific, positive sentence. "I find a job I enjoy in my
   field by spring" rather than "money".
2. **Timing (optional):** waxing moon to draw in, waning to release or banish;
   planetary days (Friday/Venus for love, Thursday/Jupiter for prosperity).
3. **Preparation:** cleanse self and space (bathing, smoke, sweeping, salt — per your
   tradition).
4. **The working** (below).
5. **Closing:** thanks to whatever powers you work with; dispose of remains
   appropriately.
6. **Action in the world:** apply for the jobs. Nearly every tradition expects
   magic to accompany action.
7. **Record** in a book of practice.

## 2. Candle magic
1. Choose a colour (common associations: green money, red love/passion, white
   purification, black banishing — traditions vary).
2. **Dress** the candle: carve a name or symbol with a pin; rub with an oil
   (e.g. olive oil, or a condition oil). Direction convention: toward you to draw,
   away to send; some anoint from middle outward.
3. Optionally roll in finely ground dried herbs — *lightly*; heavy herb coating can
   flare.
4. Light it while stating the intent; let it burn down in one sitting (small chime or
   tealight candles, 1–2 hours) or across days, snuffing (not blowing) between.
5. Read the burn if your tradition does (clean burn = favourable; heavy smoke or
   tunnelling interpreted as obstacles).
**Fire safety:** on a heatproof plate in a fireproof dish, away from curtains and
shelves, never unattended, away from pets and children; snuff before sleeping or
leaving.

## 3. Knot magic
Take a cord (often 9 inches or 9 feet in British lore). Tie nine knots while reciting
the intent; a widely used rhyme begins "By knot of one, the spell's begun…". Keep the
cord to bind the working in, or untie knots one per day to release it. A "witch's
ladder" adds feathers or beads in each knot.

## 4. Charm bag (mojo bag, gris-gris, sachet)
1. A small cloth or flannel bag (red flannel in hoodoo; colours vary elsewhere).
2. An odd number of items (3, 5, 7 is a common rule): herbs, roots, stones, a
   petition paper with the intent written on it.
3. Close it with a prayer or words; in hoodoo the bag is "fed" regularly with a few
   drops of whiskey or condition oil and kept hidden on the person.

## 5. Witch bottle (protection)
A jar with nails or pins, salt, rosemary, and traditionally urine (or vinegar/wine in
modern practice); sealed and buried near the doorstep or kept by the door. Early
modern English examples have been found archaeologically.

## Ethics and boundaries
- Many traditions hold that working on someone without consent (love spells,
  binding) carries consequences; decide your ethic and state it.
- Don't sell spells as guaranteed outcomes; consumer law applies.
- Don't offer magic in place of medical, legal or mental-health care.

## Gotchas
- A vague intent gives a vague result — practitioners stress specificity.
- Herbs on candles catching fire.
- Using toxic plants (e.g. some traditional baneful herbs) without knowledge; keep them
  labelled and away from food, children and pets.
- Copying closed practices out of context.
