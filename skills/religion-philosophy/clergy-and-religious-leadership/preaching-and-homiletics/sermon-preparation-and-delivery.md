# Sermon preparation and delivery — a weekly workflow

**Goal:** move from an assigned or chosen text to a sermon (homily, khutbah, d'var
Torah) that says *one* thing clearly, fits the service's time slot, and serves the
congregation's worship rather than displacing it.

The method below is the common core taught in most homiletics courses. Each tradition
adds its own requirements (see "Tradition specifics"); follow your own ordinal,
rubrics or school of jurisprudence where they differ.

## 1. Fix the frame (Monday or earlier)
- **Text:** the lectionary reading (Revised Common Lectionary, Roman Missal), the
  weekly Torah portion (parashah), or a chosen Qur'anic passage/hadith for the khutbah.
- **Slot:** how many minutes the service actually allows. Typical ranges: Catholic
  Sunday homily 8–12 min; Protestant sermon 20–35 min; Friday khutbah often 15–30 min
  across two parts; a d'var Torah 5–15 min. Ask, don't assume.
- **Occasion:** the liturgical season, a feast, a local event, a funeral in the week.

## 2. Study the text before reading commentaries
1. Read it aloud three times, ideally in the original language if you have it.
2. Write down observations: repeated words, surprises, who speaks, what is asked.
3. Note the context: what comes before and after; genre (narrative, law, poetry, letter).
4. *Then* consult two or three trusted commentaries or tafsir/midrash collections from
   your tradition. Doing your own reading first stops you preaching the commentary.

## 3. Name the one claim ("big idea")
Write a single sentence, under ~20 words, that states what the text says and what it
asks of this congregation. If you can't, you are not ready to outline. Everything
that doesn't serve the sentence goes in a file for another week.

## 4. Build the structure
A dependable shape (adapt freely):
1. **Opening** (1–2 min): a concrete situation or question the hearers recognise.
2. **The text:** what it says, with the one or two exegetical points that matter.
3. **The turn:** what it means — the big idea stated plainly.
4. **Application:** one or two specific, doable responses (not ten).
5. **Close:** return to the opening image; end on the claim, not on a new idea.

Spoken prose rules: short sentences, repeated key phrase, signpost transitions
("So here's the question…"), one illustration per main move.

## 5. Write, then cut
- Draft a full manuscript or detailed notes. Rough guide: ~120–140 spoken words per
  minute, so a 12-minute homily ≈ 1,500 words.
- Cut 15–20%. Almost every first draft overruns.
- Check each illustration: true, appropriate for children present, not a story about a
  congregant without permission, not exposing anything heard in pastoral confidence.

## 6. Rehearse and deliver
- Rehearse aloud at least once with a timer, in the room if possible.
- Mark the manuscript: pauses, emphasis, where to look up.
- Microphone: test level before the service; stay a consistent distance.
- Eye contact across the whole room; slow down for the big-idea sentence.

## 7. Review (Monday)
Note actual length, what landed, what confused people. Ask one or two trusted
listeners for specific feedback ("What was the main point?"). If their answer
doesn't match your sentence, the structure needs work, not the delivery.

## Tradition specifics (check against your own authorities)
- **Catholic/Orthodox homily:** expected to arise from the day's readings and lead into
  the Eucharist; preached by the ordained in most settings.
- **Khutbah:** usually two parts with a brief sitting between, opening with praise of
  God and blessings on the Prophet; often in Arabic with or without vernacular
  portions, depending on the mosque and school. Keep it shorter than the prayer custom
  of the community allows.
- **D'var Torah / drasha:** typically tied to the week's parashah, often citing
  rabbinic sources and ending with a hopeful or practical lesson.

## Gotchas
- Preaching the commentary instead of the text.
- Three sermons in one. Pick one.
- The "applause line" that shames a group present in the pews.
- Running long eats the rest of the liturgy and volunteers' time.
- Topical events: name them pastorally; avoid partisan endorsement where law or your
  tradition forbids it (in the US, for example, tax-exempt bodies may not endorse candidates).

## Verify
- [ ] You can state the big idea in one sentence, and a listener can repeat it back.
- [ ] Rehearsed length is within the slot with a minute to spare.
- [ ] Every illustration is accurate, permissioned and age-appropriate.
- [ ] Tradition-specific requirements for the form (readings, opening formulae, who may preach) are met.
- [ ] A short review note exists for next week.
