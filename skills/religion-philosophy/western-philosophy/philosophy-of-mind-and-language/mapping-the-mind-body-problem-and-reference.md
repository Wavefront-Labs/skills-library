# Mapping the mind–body problem and reference

**Goal:** locate any position in philosophy of mind or language on a standard map,
run the key arguments premise by premise, and see how the two areas connect
(intentionality: how thoughts and words are *about* things).

## 1. The mind–body option map
| View | Claim | Main problem |
|---|---|---|
| Substance dualism (Descartes) | mind is a non-physical substance | interaction: how does it move matter? |
| Property dualism | one substance, irreducibly mental properties | causal exclusion / epiphenomenalism |
| Identity theory (Place, Smart) | mental state types = brain state types | multiple realisability (octopus pain) |
| Functionalism (Putnam, Lewis) | mental states = causal-functional roles | absent/inverted qualia; China brain |
| Eliminativism (Churchlands) | folk-psychological states don't exist | seems self-undermining to many |
| Russellian monism / panpsychism | physics describes structure; intrinsic natures are experiential | combination problem |
| Illusionism (Frankish) | phenomenal consciousness is an introspective illusion | explaining the illusion's appearance |
Locate a view by asking: (1) one kind of stuff or two? (2) are mental properties
identical to, realised by, or distinct from physical ones? (3) do they cause anything?

## 2. Running the key arguments
**Knowledge argument (Jackson, "Mary's room")**
1. Mary knows all physical facts about colour vision, inside a black-and-white room.
2. On leaving and seeing red, she learns something new.
3. So not all facts are physical.
Replies: ability hypothesis (she gains know-how, Lewis/Nemirow); acquaintance;
phenomenal concepts (new concept, old fact). Jackson himself later rejected the
argument.

**Zombie (conceivability) argument (Chalmers)**
1. A physical duplicate of us without consciousness is conceivable.
2. If conceivable, then metaphysically possible.
3. If possible, consciousness is not physical.
Most physicalists attack premise 2.

**Causal exclusion (Kim)**
1. Every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause (closure).
2. Mental causes are not identical to physical ones (non-reductive physicalism).
3. No systematic overdetermination.
4. So mental properties are causally idle — unless they are identical to physical
   ones. Pressure on property dualism and non-reductive views.

## 3. Theories of reference
**Frege's puzzle:** "Hesperus = Hesperus" is trivial; "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is
informative, though both names refer to Venus. Frege: names have **sense** (mode of
presentation) as well as **reference**.

**Descriptivism (Frege, Russell):** a name means a description ("Aristotle" = "the
teacher of Alexander…"); Russell's theory of definite descriptions handles "The
present King of France is bald" without a referent.

**Kripke's objections (Naming and Necessity):**
- **Modal:** Aristotle *might not* have taught Alexander, but couldn't have failed to
  be Aristotle — names are **rigid designators**.
- **Epistemic/Gödel–Schmidt:** if the man we call "Gödel" stole the proof from Schmidt,
  "Gödel" still refers to Gödel, not to the description's satisfier.
**Causal-historical theory:** reference fixed at an initial baptism and passed along a
chain of communication.

**Twin Earth (Putnam):** "water" refers to H₂O here and XYZ on Twin Earth, though
speakers' internal states are identical — "meanings ain't in the head" (semantic
externalism). This feeds back into mind: is thought content externally individuated?

## 4. Method
For any paper or claim: place it on the map, identify which premise of which
standard argument it accepts or rejects, and check its consequences in the other
area (does a theory of content support the theory of mind?).

## Gotchas
- "Physicalism" needs a definition (current physics? ideal physics? Hempel's dilemma).
- Conceivability claims depend on what's imagined in detail; ask what exactly is conceived.
- Don't conflate *consciousness* (phenomenal) with *intentionality* (aboutness).
