# Applying normative theories to a case

**Goal:** analyse a moral question systematically by running it through the main
normative theories, seeing where they agree and diverge, and stating which
considerations drive your own judgement — then say what kind of claim that judgement
is (metaethics). For clinical or organisational ethics consultation, see the
applied-ethics field instead.

## 1. Fix the case
Write it in 3–5 sentences: who acts, what options exist (list at least three, not
just do/don't), who is affected, what is known and uncertain. Example: *A doctor can
tell a patient a frightening diagnosis now, delay a week until family arrive, or
let the family decide.*

## 2. Consequentialism (Bentham, Mill, Singer)
1. For each option, list affected parties and likely outcomes, with rough
   probabilities.
2. Assess **well-being** effects (hedonic, preference-satisfaction or objective-list —
   say which).
3. Choose the option with the best **expected** total (or, for rule
   consequentialism, the option a generally-followed rule with the best results would
   require).
Watch for: distribution ignored (aggregation), demandingness, rights violations
that maximise the total.

## 3. Kantian deontology
Run the **Formula of Universal Law**:
1. State the maxim: "When [circumstance], I will [act] in order to [end]."
2. Universalise it: could everyone act on it?
3. **Contradiction in conception** (the maxim couldn't even exist universalised — the
   lying-promise case) → perfect duty violated. **Contradiction in will** (could exist,
   but you couldn't rationally will it — never helping) → imperfect duty.
Then the **Formula of Humanity**: am I treating anyone merely as a means — using
them in a way they could not in principle consent to? Respect for autonomy often
decides medical-information cases.
Watch for: maxim description sensitivity; conflicts of duty.

## 4. Virtue ethics (Aristotle, Anscombe, Hursthouse)
Ask: **what would a virtuous person — honest, compassionate, courageous, practically
wise (phronesis) — characteristically do here?** Identify which virtues are in play
and what the mean between excess and deficiency looks like (honesty vs brutal
frankness vs evasiveness). Consider what each option would make of the agent's character.

## 5. Contractualism (Scanlon)
Is the action permitted by principles **no one could reasonably reject**? Compare
the strongest individual complaint against each principle — no aggregation of many
small complaints against one large one.

## 6. Side constraints and special doctrines
- **Doctrine of double effect:** harm foreseen but not intended may be permitted if
  the act is good in itself, harm isn't the means, and there is proportionate reason.
- **Doing vs allowing; special obligations** to family, patients, citizens.

## 7. Synthesise
1. Table: option × theory → verdict + the decisive consideration.
2. Where theories agree, confidence is higher. Where they diverge, name the
   underlying disagreement (outcomes vs consent vs character).
3. Use **reflective equilibrium**: let a firm case judgement test a principle, and a
   principle correct a shaky judgement.

## 8. Metaethics: what kind of claim is your conclusion?
- **Realism:** moral facts exist independently of attitudes (naturalist or non-naturalist).
- **Expressivism:** moral claims express attitudes (Blackburn, Gibbard).
- **Error theory:** moral claims aim at facts but are all false (Mackie).
- **Constructivism:** moral truths are what rational procedures would produce.
State which you assume; it changes what "I'm right" means, not usually the
first-order verdict.

## Gotchas
- Caricatures: utilitarianism isn't "ends justify the means" simpliciter; Kant does
  not forbid using people as means, only *merely* as means.
- Listing theories without a conclusion is not analysis — commit and defend.

## Verify
- [ ] Case states at least three options.
- [ ] Each theory applied with its own test, not a slogan.
- [ ] Option × theory table built and divergences explained.
- [ ] Your conclusion and its metaethical status stated.
