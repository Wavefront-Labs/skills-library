# Doing a phenomenological description

**Goal:** practise phenomenology as a method — describing an experience as it is
lived and drawing out its essential structure — rather than only reading about
Husserl or Heidegger. The steps below follow Husserl's method with the
existential and hermeneutic corrections of Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty and Gadamer.

## 1. Choose a manageable phenomenon
Pick an ordinary experience you can return to: hearing a melody, using a tool,
waiting for someone late, being looked at, feeling tired. Phrase it as "the
experience of X", not "the cause of X".

## 2. The epoché (bracketing)
1. **Suspend the natural attitude:** set aside (not deny) your assumptions about what
   the experience "really is" — neuroscience, psychology, common-sense explanations.
2. Write down those assumptions in a list so you can see what you are bracketing.
3. Ask only: **how is this given to me?** — in what manner it appears, not whether
   or why it exists.
Merleau-Ponty's caution: a complete reduction is impossible; the point is to loosen
the grip of assumptions, not escape the world.

## 3. Describe (first person, present tense)
Write 1–2 pages, concretely. Prompts:
- **Intentionality:** what is the experience *of*? How is the object given — as
  perceived, remembered, imagined, anticipated? (Husserl: the act — *noesis* — and
  the object-as-meant — *noema*.)
- **Horizons:** what is co-given but not focused on? (The back of the cup, the room,
  what I expect to happen next.)
- **Time:** what just passed is retained, what is coming is anticipated (Husserl's
  retention/protention); how does the experience unfold?
- **Body:** how is my body involved — posture, effort, the tool becoming
  transparent in use (Heidegger's *ready-to-hand*)?
- **Others and world:** who else is implicitly there? What meanings does the
  situation already carry?
Avoid explanatory vocabulary ("my brain processes…"). Describe; don't theorise yet.

## 4. Eidetic variation
1. Vary the experience **in imagination**: change the object, the setting, the
   speed, the mood, the person.
2. After each variation ask: **is it still the same kind of experience?**
3. What cannot be varied without the experience ceasing to be *that kind* is its
   **essential structure** (eidos). E.g. waiting: an anticipated arrival + a felt
   duration + something not yet present. Write the invariant structure in 3–6
   sentences.

## 5. The hermeneutic turn
Heidegger and Gadamer: description is already interpretation.
- State your **fore-understanding** (what you expected to find) and check whether
  the description has confirmed or upset it.
- Move between parts and whole (**hermeneutic circle**): revise the description in
  light of the structure, and the structure in light of new details.
- For texts or others' accounts (interviews), the same circle applies: read the
  whole, interpret parts, revise.

## 6. Writing it up
1. Name the phenomenon and what was bracketed.
2. Give the description, then the variations, then the invariant structure.
3. Relate it to the literature (e.g. Sartre on the look, Heidegger on boredom,
   Merleau-Ponty on the body schema) — after, not instead of, your own description.

## Gotchas
- The commonest failure is introspective reporting ("I felt sad") without
  structure; push to *how* the sadness discloses the world.
- Bracketing is not scepticism; you don't doubt the world exists.
- "Phenomenology" in qualitative social research (e.g. IPA) uses related but
  different procedures; say which you are doing.
- Continental traditions dispute one another's methods (e.g. Derrida on presence);
  the procedure above is a Husserlian core, not a consensus.

## Verify
- [ ] Assumptions listed and bracketed before describing.
- [ ] Description first-person, concrete, free of causal explanation.
- [ ] At least five imaginative variations tried.
- [ ] Invariant structure stated in a few sentences.
- [ ] Fore-understanding acknowledged and revisited.
