# Practising Stoic daily exercises

**Goal:** live Stoicism the way the ancient school intended — as a daily training
(askēsis) of judgement and character — using exercises taken directly from Epictetus,
Seneca and Marcus Aurelius, not as a set of motivational quotes.

## 1. The core idea to train
- **Dichotomy of control** (Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1): some things are "up to us"
  — our judgements, intentions, desires and aversions — and everything else (body,
  reputation, property, other people's actions, outcomes) is not.
- **Only virtue is good** — wisdom, justice, courage, moderation. Health, wealth and
  reputation are "preferred indifferents": reasonable to pursue, not the source of
  a good life.
- Distress comes from **judgements** about events, not the events (*Ench.* 5).
These are claims you test in practice through the exercises below.

## 2. A daily routine
**Morning (5–10 min) — premeditation (praemeditatio)**
1. Read one short passage (e.g. a chapter of the *Enchiridion*, a section of
   *Meditations* book 2.1).
2. Rehearse the day: list what is likely to go wrong — delays, rude people,
   setbacks. Marcus's version (*Med.* 2.1): "today I will meet the meddling, the
   ungrateful, the arrogant…"
3. For each, decide in advance what is up to you (your response) and which virtue
   it calls for.
4. Optionally, **negative visualisation**: briefly picture losing something you
   value, to loosen attachment and renew gratitude — keep it brief; it is not
   rumination.

**Through the day — attention (prosochē)**
- When an impression strikes (anger, fear, craving), pause and say: "*You are only
  an impression, not the thing you appear to be*" (*Ench.* 1.5). Then ask: is this up
  to me?
- **Reserve clause**: plan with "*if nothing prevents me*" (Stoic *hupexairesis*) — "I
  will catch the train, fate permitting" — so plans are pursued without depending on
  their outcome.

**Evening (10 min) — review (Seneca, *On Anger* III.36)**
1. In writing, go through the day: What did I do badly? What did I do well? What
   did I leave undone?
2. Judge yourself as a fair teacher, not a prosecutor; note one adjustment for
   tomorrow.

## 3. Further exercises
| Exercise | Source | How |
|---|---|---|
| View from above | Marcus, *Med.* 9.30 | picture the scene from high above — the city, the earth — to rescale concerns |
| Physical definition | *Med.* 3.11 | describe an object or event in plain physical terms, stripped of value-words |
| Voluntary discomfort | Seneca, *Letter* 18 | occasionally live simply (plain food, cold) to show yourself you can |
| Role ethics | Epictetus, *Disc.* 2.10 | list your roles (child, parent, citizen, colleague) and what each requires today |
| Keep notes (hupomnēmata) | Marcus's own practice | copy and rewrite key principles in your own words |

## 4. Reading plan
1. Epictetus, *Enchiridion* (short; read in full, then reread a chapter daily).
2. Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* (books 2–4 first).
3. Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* 1–30.
4. Background: Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life*; A. A. Long, *Epictetus*.
Use a reputable modern translation (e.g. Robin Hard; Gregory Hays for Marcus).

## Gotchas
- Stoicism is not suppressing emotion; it trains judgement so destructive passions
  don't arise, while "good feelings" (eupatheiai — joy, caution, wishing well) remain.
- The dichotomy of control is not passivity: Stoics argue duty to others is fully
  up to you to pursue.
- Social-media "Stoicism" often strips out the ethics of justice and community;
  read the sources.
- Not a substitute for mental-health treatment; CBT draws on these ideas but is a
  clinical method.
