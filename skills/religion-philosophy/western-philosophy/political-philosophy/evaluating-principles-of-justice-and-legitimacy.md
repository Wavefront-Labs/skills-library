# Evaluating principles of justice and legitimacy

**Goal:** assess a political principle or concrete policy (a wealth tax, a speech
restriction, compulsory voting) with the standard tools of political philosophy,
separating three questions people run together: **is it just? does it respect
liberty? is the authority imposing it legitimate?**

## 1. Frame the proposal
State: who is coerced or benefited, what is redistributed or restricted, and by
which authority. Example: *a 2% annual tax on net wealth above a high threshold,
funding universal childcare.*

## 2. Distributive justice tests
**Rawls — the original position**
1. Imagine choosing the basic rules behind a **veil of ignorance**: you don't know
   your class, talents, sex, conception of the good.
2. Would you accept the rule? Rawls argues for (i) **equal basic liberties**, (ii)
   **fair equality of opportunity**, and (iii) the **difference principle** —
   inequalities allowed only if they benefit the least advantaged. Lexical order:
   (i) before (ii) before (iii).
3. Ask: does the policy improve the position of the worst-off representative person?

**Nozick — entitlement theory**
1. Is the current holding the result of **just acquisition** and **just transfer**
   (or rectification of past injustice)?
2. If yes, coercive redistribution is presumptively unjust. The **Wilt Chamberlain**
   argument: any patterned distribution is upset by free exchanges; maintaining it
   requires continuous interference.

**Other lenses**
- **Luck egalitarianism** (Dworkin, Cohen): neutralise brute luck, not choices.
- **Capabilities** (Sen, Nussbaum): does it expand what people are actually able to
  do and be?
- **Utilitarian** aggregate welfare, with the usual distribution worry.
- **Sufficientarianism:** does everyone have enough, whatever the inequality above it?

## 3. Liberty tests
- **Negative vs positive liberty** (Berlin): freedom from interference vs freedom
  as self-mastery/capacity. Say which one the policy affects and which way.
- **Harm principle** (Mill): coercion of adults justified only to prevent harm to
  others — not for their own good, not for offence alone. Check: whom does the
  restricted conduct harm? Is the harm direct?
- **Republican liberty** (Pettit): freedom as non-domination — does the policy
  reduce someone's exposure to arbitrary power, even without actual interference?

## 4. Legitimacy and political obligation
Why must anyone obey this authority?
| Account | Ground | Main objection |
|---|---|---|
| Consent (Locke) | people agreed (express or tacit) | few actually consent; tacit consent by residence is thin (Hume's press-ganged sailor) |
| Fair play (Hart, Rawls) | you benefit from a cooperative scheme | unrequested benefits (Nozick's PA-system case) |
| Natural duty (Rawls) | duty to support just institutions | why *this* state's institutions? |
| Democratic procedure | equal say in making the law | majorities can be unjust |
| Public reason (Rawls, *Political Liberalism*) | justified by reasons all reasonable citizens could accept | what counts as "reasonable" |
Ask whether the policy could be justified in public reasons, not only by one
comprehensive doctrine.

## 5. Synthesise
Table: lens × verdict × decisive reason. Then: which lens do you weight most, and
why? Address the strongest opposing lens directly.

## Gotchas
- Don't confuse an empirical claim (the tax will reduce investment) with a normative
  one; state empirical premises separately and mark their uncertainty.
- The difference principle governs the *basic structure*, not each individual policy
  — apply it to the policy's effect on that structure.
- "Liberty" is used by all sides; always say whose liberty, from what, to do what.

- [ ] Justice, liberty and legitimacy questions answered separately.
- [ ] Veil-of-ignorance and entitlement tests both run.
- [ ] Harm principle applied with an identified harmed party.
- [ ] Legitimacy account named and its main objection addressed.
