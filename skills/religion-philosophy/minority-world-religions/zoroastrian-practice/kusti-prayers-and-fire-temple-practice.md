# Kusti prayers and fire-temple practice

**Goal:** keep the core Zoroastrian devotional practice — the kusti ritual several
times a day — and take part properly in fire-temple worship and the community's
seasonal feasts.

> Draft. Practice differs between Parsi (India/Pakistan and diaspora), Irani and
> Iranian Zoroastrian communities, and between traditional and reformist
> associations — e.g. on admission of non-Zoroastrians to fire temples, on
> conversion, and on which calendar (Shahenshahi, Kadmi, Fasli) is used. Exact
> prayer wording should be learned from a *Khordeh Avesta* in your community's
> tradition or from a mobed.

## 1. Sudreh and kusti
- **Sudreh (sedreh):** a white muslin undershirt, worn always, with a small pocket at the
  front neck (the "pocket of good deeds").
- **Kusti:** a long cord of 72 white lambswool threads, woven by hand, wrapped three times
  around the waist over the sudreh and tied with two knots in front and two at the back.
  The three wraps recall *humata, hukhta, hvarshta* — good thoughts, good words, good deeds.

## 2. The padyab-kusti ritual
Done on waking, after using the toilet, before prayers and meals, and at each **gah**.
1. **Padyab:** wash face, hands to elbows and feet, reciting the short formula for it.
2. Stand facing a source of light (the sun by day, a lamp or fire by night) — never facing north
   in traditional practice.
3. Untie the kusti while reciting *Kem na Mazda*; hold it, keeping it off the floor.
4. Recite **Ahunavar** (*Yatha ahu vairyo*) and **Ashem Vohu** while reknotting:
   the front knots and then, passing the cord around, the back knots, in the prescribed
   sequence; crack the ends gently at the line rejecting evil.
5. Close with *Hormazd Khodae* and *Jasa me avanghe Mazda*.
A complete kusti takes 2–5 minutes. Learn the movements in person; written descriptions
of the knots are hard to follow.

## 3. The five gahs (watches of the day)
Havan (sunrise–noon), Rapithwin (noon–3 p.m.; replaced by a second Havan in winter months),
Uziran (3 p.m.–sunset), Aiwisruthrem (sunset–midnight), Ushahin (midnight–dawn). Each has its
own short gah prayer, said after the kusti.

## 4. Navjote (initiation)
Children are initiated, typically between 7 and 15 (Parsi custom: before puberty), by a
mobed: ritual bath (nahan), first putting on of the sudreh, the first kusti tied with the priest,
a recitation of the confession of faith (*Din no Kalmo / Fravarane*), blessings and a family feast.
Prepare the child by teaching the kusti prayers months in advance.

## 5. Fire temple
- Grades: **Atash Behram** (highest, consecrated over long rites), **Agiary/Adaran**, and
  the simpler **Dar-e-Mehr**. The fire is tended continually by priests in five daily
  **boi** ceremonies.
- Etiquette: do padyab-kusti on arrival; cover the head (cap or scarf); remove shoes; offer
  sandalwood sticks to the priest for the fire (never throw anything into it yourself);
  pray standing facing the fire; take a pinch of ash (rakhya) to the forehead; leave without
  turning your back.
- Many Parsi fire temples in India admit only Zoroastrians; respect posted rules.

## 6. Seasonal feasts
- **Gahambars:** six five-day seasonal festivals of thanksgiving with communal feasting;
  contributing food or money is a merit.
- **Jashan:** a thanksgiving ceremony performed by priests for occasions.
- **Nowruz** (spring equinox in Fasli/Iranian practice; Parsi "Pateti/Navroz" in late summer
  on the Shahenshahi calendar), **Khordad Sal** (Zarathushtra's birthday), and the
  **Muktad** days honouring the departed before the new year.

## Gotchas
- Kusti touching the floor or tied with the cord twisted — redo it.
- Using the wrong community calendar and arriving on the wrong day for a feast.
- Blowing out candles or lamps — fire is not extinguished by breath.

## Verify
- [ ] Sudreh worn; kusti retied at waking, after the toilet and before prayer.
- [ ] You can recite Ahunavar and Ashem Vohu while knotting front and back.
- [ ] Today's gah prayers said in their watches.
- [ ] Fire-temple etiquette followed: head covered, sandalwood given to the priest.
