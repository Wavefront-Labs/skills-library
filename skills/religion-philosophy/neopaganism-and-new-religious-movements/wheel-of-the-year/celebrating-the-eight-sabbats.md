# Celebrating the eight sabbats of the wheel of the year

**Goal:** keep the eight seasonal festivals shared across much of modern Paganism —
knowing when each falls where you live, what it marks, and a practical way to
celebrate it alone or with others.

> Draft. The eight-fold wheel as a single system is a 20th-century synthesis (Wicca and
> Druidry in Britain, 1950s), drawing on Irish/Gaelic fire festivals, astronomical
> solar points and folk custom. Names vary: Wiccans often say Litha and Mabon, Druids
> Alban Hefin and Alban Elfed, Heathens keep a different set of holy tides. Themes below
> are common, not universal.

## 1. When they fall
| Sabbat | Northern hemisphere | Southern hemisphere | Marks |
|--------|---------------------|---------------------|-------|
| **Samhain** | 31 Oct – 1 Nov | 30 Apr – 1 May | end of harvest, the dead, new year in many traditions |
| **Yule** | winter solstice ~21 Dec | ~21 Jun | rebirth of the sun |
| **Imbolc** | 1–2 Feb | 1 Aug | first stirrings of spring; Brigid |
| **Ostara** | spring equinox ~20 Mar | ~22 Sep | balance; fertility, new growth |
| **Beltane** | 30 Apr – 1 May | 31 Oct – 1 Nov | summer's start; fire, fertility, union |
| **Litha** | summer solstice ~21 Jun | ~21 Dec | sun at its height |
| **Lughnasadh / Lammas** | 1 Aug | 1–2 Feb | first harvest, grain, Lugh |
| **Mabon** | autumn equinox ~22 Sep | ~20 Mar | second harvest, thanks, balance |
Solstice and equinox dates shift by a day year to year — look up the exact astronomical
time. Some celebrate cross-quarter days by the astronomical midpoint (e.g. Samhain ~6–7 Nov)
rather than the calendar date. Southern-hemisphere Pagans mostly follow their own seasons.

## 2. Typical practice for each
- **Samhain:** ancestor altar with photos and their favourite foods; a **dumb supper** (a meal
  in silence with a place laid for the dead); divination; carved turnips/pumpkins.
- **Yule:** stay up for the solstice sunrise; Yule log (burn a piece, keep a fragment for next
  year); evergreens and lights.
- **Imbolc:** candles in every window; Brigid's crosses woven from rushes; a bed (*Brideog*)
  made for Brigid; spring cleaning; well visits.
- **Ostara:** decorating and hiding eggs; planting seeds; balance meditations.
- **Beltane:** maypole dance (ribbons woven over and under, an even number of dancers);
  jumping a small bonfire or walking between two fires; flower crowns; handfastings.
- **Litha:** sunrise vigil; gathering herbs (St John's wort); bonfires; staying out on the
  shortest night.
- **Lughnasadh:** baking bread from new grain; making a corn dolly; games and contests.
- **Mabon:** harvest meal; thanks-giving; gathering apples, seeds and nuts; giving to food banks.

## 3. Planning a seasonal ritual
1. Pick a theme from the season around you (what is actually happening in your local
   landscape counts more than imported symbolism).
2. Choose a frame: Wiccan circle (see
   `religion-philosophy.neopaganism-and-new-religious-movements.wiccan-ritual.casting-a-circle-and-holding-an-esbat`),
   Druid ceremony (see
   `religion-philosophy.neopaganism-and-new-religious-movements.druidry-practice.leading-a-druid-grove-ceremony`)
   or a simple personal rite: light a candle, speak an intention, make an offering, share food.
3. Include one **seasonal action** (planting, weaving, baking, fire) — it anchors the meaning.
4. For open celebrations: agree a venue, publicise with clear start time, accessibility
   info and what to bring; keep it 45–90 minutes plus food.

## Gotchas
- Fire: check burn bans; use a raised fire pit; jump only small, controlled flames.
- Maypole ribbons tangle with odd numbers of dancers or no one calling the pattern.
- Using Northern dates in the Southern hemisphere (Yule in midsummer) without intending it.
- Stone circles and heritage sites: crowds at solstices — check access rules (e.g.
  managed solstice openings) and leave no candles or offerings on stones.
