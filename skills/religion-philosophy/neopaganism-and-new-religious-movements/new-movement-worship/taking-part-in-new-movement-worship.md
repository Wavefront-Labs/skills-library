# Taking part in worship in a new religious movement

**Goal:** join the worship of a newer or syncretic religious movement — as a member
learning the liturgy, or as a guest — correctly and respectfully, and recognise the
signs of a healthy community.

> Draft. "New religious movement" covers hundreds of very different faiths founded from
> the 19th century onward, often blending earlier traditions. This page gives a general
> method plus three brief examples described from published accounts; each movement's
> own clergy and printed service books are the authority. Practices change; confirm
> details locally.

## 1. A general method for any movement's worship
1. **Get the service order** in advance: most movements publish a service book, liturgy
   sheet or newcomer guide. Ask a member or the clergy which parts guests may join.
2. **Ask about etiquette:** dress (colours, head covering), shoes, seating by gender or
   rank, bowing or gestures, whether guests take part in sacraments or offerings.
3. **Learn the calendar:** founding days, the founder's anniversaries and seasonal services
   are usually the main observances.
4. **Practise the participatory parts:** responses, songs, hand movements — members are
   usually glad to teach them before or after.
5. **Find your role:** members typically serve in rotation (ushers, musicians, readers);
   learn by shadowing.

## 2. Worked examples
**Cao Dai (Caodaism, founded in Vietnam, 1926)**
- Four daily ceremonies at 6 a.m., noon, 6 p.m. and midnight; many lay members attend at
  least one, or pray at a home altar with the Divine Eye symbol.
- In the temple: adherents wear white áo dài robes (dignitaries in yellow, blue or red by
  branch), remove shoes, and sit in ordered rows, men and women on separate sides; prayers
  are chanted with music; offerings of flowers, fruit, tea/water and incense.
- Visitors usually watch from the gallery; follow the guide's instructions.

**Tenrikyo (founded in Japan, 1838)**
- The **service (otsutome)** combines sung verses (*Mikagura-uta*), musical instruments and
  hand movements (the *teodori*). Local churches hold a monthly service; the Church
  Headquarters in Tenri holds the main services.
- Members learn the hand movements and instruments step by step through classes; guests
  may sit and observe.
- Practice emphasises daily service, *hinokishin* (voluntary service to others), and the
  ideal of the "joyous life".

**Unitarian Universalist** (not strictly an NRM; a liberal, non-creedal congregation)
- Services open with **lighting the chalice**, include readings from varied sources, a sermon,
  music and **joys and concerns** (members share news, sometimes by lighting candles).
- Participation in rites is open to all; there is no creed to affirm.

## 3. Healthy-community checks
Most new movements are ordinary congregations. Use the same checks you would for any
group before committing time or money:
- Are you free to ask questions, read critics, take a break or leave without penalty?
- Is money asked for transparently, with accounts available?
- Are contacts with family and friends outside the group encouraged?
- Are leaders accountable to someone (a board, a constitution)?
- Are new members given time to decide, not pressured toward vows or large donations?
Answers of "no" are warning signs of a high-demand group; talk to people outside it.

## Gotchas
- Assuming one movement's customs (shoes off, gender seating) apply to another — ask.
- Taking photographs during services without permission.
- Joining a sacrament or vow reserved for initiated members.

## Verify
- [ ] Service order and guest/member etiquette obtained in advance.
- [ ] Dress and conduct match the community's guidance.
- [ ] You know which parts you may join and which to observe.
- [ ] Healthy-community checks answered before any formal commitment.
