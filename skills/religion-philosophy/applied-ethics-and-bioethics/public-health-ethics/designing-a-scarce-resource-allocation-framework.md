# Designing a scarce-resource allocation framework — before the shortage hits

**Goal:** produce a written, publicly justifiable framework that says who gets a scarce
resource (ventilators, ICU beds, antivirals, vaccine doses, organs in emergencies)
when there is not enough for everyone, and a process that applies it consistently
without leaving the choice to exhausted bedside clinicians.

Write it in advance, in calm conditions, with public input. Not legal advice:
anti-discrimination and disability law constrain criteria, and crisis standards of
care activation is usually a government or health-system declaration
(jurisdiction-dependent — check local law and health-department guidance).

## 1. Define the scarcity and trigger
- Resource, expected shortfall, duration, and whether it is **divisible** (doses) or
  **indivisible** (a ventilator).
- **Activation trigger:** who declares crisis standards, on what indicators, and how
  it is reviewed and stood down. Allocation rules must not run when there is enough.
- Exhaust conventional and contingency measures first (transfer, substitution,
  conservation, cancelling elective use).

## 2. Choose and rank the values explicitly
Commonly used values (Emanuel et al. 2020; Persad et al. 2009):
| Value | Operationalised as |
|---|---|
| **Maximise benefits** | Lives saved; sometimes life-years — short-term survival likelihood (e.g. SOFA-type scores) |
| **Treat people equally** | Lottery or first-come among those with similar prognosis |
| **Instrumental value** | Priority for those who keep the response running (health workers) |
| **Reciprocity** | Priority for those who took on risk (trial volunteers, frontline staff) |
| **Priority to the worst off** | Sickest first, or those most socially disadvantaged |
Most frameworks maximise benefits first, use instrumental value/reciprocity for some
resources (vaccines more than ventilators), and use a lottery as the final tiebreaker.
**First-come, first-served** is generally rejected for indivisible resources because it
rewards proximity and access.

## 3. Build the tiers
1. **Eligibility/exclusion** — keep exclusions minimal and clinical; avoid categorical
   exclusions by diagnosis or disability.
2. **Priority score** — e.g. short-term survival score bands.
3. **Tiebreakers** in a stated order (e.g. instrumental role → lottery).
4. **Reassessment** — scheduled reassessment of patients on the resource (commonly
   around 48 and 120 hours in published ventilator protocols; protocol-specific),
   with criteria for withdrawal and reallocation.
5. **Palliative care** guaranteed for everyone not allocated the resource.

## 4. Test for bias and fairness
- Run the scoring on historical or simulated cases split by race, disability, age, language.
- Remove or adjust inputs that measure chronic disability or structural disadvantage
  rather than short-term prognosis (e.g. functional-status scores; some creatinine-based
  measures have been criticised for racial bias).
- Age: life-cycle or life-years criteria are contested and may be unlawful in some
  jurisdictions — decide openly, with legal review.
- Check against **accountability for reasonableness**: publicity, relevance, appeal/revision, enforcement.

## 5. Process design
- **Triage officer/team** separate from the treating team, applying the rules; the
  treating clinician advocates for the patient.
- **Appeals:** fast, narrow (was the rule applied correctly?), not re-litigating values.
- Documentation of each decision; daily data review for disparities.
- Communication scripts for patients and families; staff moral-distress support.

## 6. For restrictive measures (quarantine, mandates)
Use the Childress/Kass checks: **effectiveness**, **proportionality**, **necessity**,
**least restrictive means**, **public justification** — plus **reciprocity** (support
people asked to isolate: income, food, care).

## Gotchas
- A framework written during the crisis by those running it loses public trust.
- "Maximise benefits" via long-term life expectancy quietly penalises disabled and poorer groups.
- Withdrawing a resource from one patient for another is ethically and legally the
  hardest step; make the rule and its legal status explicit.
- Vaccine allocation also needs geographic and access equity, not just tiers.

## Verify
- [ ] Activation and stand-down triggers defined with an accountable authority.
- [ ] Values ranked and each mapped to a concrete criterion.
- [ ] Tiers, tiebreakers and reassessment intervals written.
- [ ] Bias testing done on subgroups; disability-related inputs reviewed.
- [ ] Triage team separate from treating team; appeal route and palliative care guaranteed.
- [ ] Legal review and public consultation recorded.
