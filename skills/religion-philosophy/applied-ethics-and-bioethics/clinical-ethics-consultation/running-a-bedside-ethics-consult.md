# Running a bedside ethics consult — from the request to the chart note

**Goal:** turn a vague "we need ethics" call into a clear ethics question, a fair
process that hears every stakeholder, an ethically supportable range of options,
and a chart note the team can act on. The consultant facilitates; the consultant
does not decide for the patient, surrogate or attending.

Not legal or medical advice: surrogate hierarchies, advance-directive law and
unilateral-withdrawal rules vary by jurisdiction and institution — check local law
and policy before advising on any of them.

## 1. Take the request (10 minutes)
Ask the requester:
- **Who is asking, and what is the question in one sentence?** ("Can we stop dialysis
  when the daughter refuses?", not "family is difficult.")
- **Why now?** Is there a deadline (procedure tomorrow, imminent arrest, discharge)?
- **Who else is involved** — attending, nurse, patient, surrogate(s), chaplain, palliative care?
- **Does the attending know** ethics was called? (Many policies allow anyone to call;
  the attending should still be told.)
Triage: is it actually an ethics question, or a communication, legal, risk or
pain-management problem? Redirect if so, but still offer support.

## 2. Gather facts with the four-box (Jonsen) method
Read the chart, then talk to people. Fill all four boxes before analysing.

| Box | Key questions |
|---|---|
| **Medical indications** | Diagnosis, prognosis, treatment options, likelihood of each goal (survival, function, comfort)? |
| **Patient preferences** | Does the patient have capacity? What do they want? Advance directive, POLST/MOLST, prior statements? Who is the legal surrogate? |
| **Quality of life** | Expected life with and without treatment, *by the patient's own standard*? Whose judgement is driving the QoL claim? |
| **Contextual features** | Family dynamics, religion/culture, cost, conflicts of interest, legal constraints, institutional policy, team moral distress? |

## 3. Capacity and surrogate check
Capacity is decision-specific and clinician-assessed. The four commonly used criteria
(Appelbaum): the patient can **communicate a choice**, **understand** the relevant
information, **appreciate** how it applies to them, and **reason** about options.
Higher-risk decisions warrant more scrutiny. If capacity is lacking:
1. Find a legally valid advance directive or appointed agent.
2. Otherwise apply the local default surrogate hierarchy (commonly spouse/partner,
   adult children, parents, siblings — jurisdiction-dependent, check local law).
3. Coach the surrogate on the standard: **substituted judgment** (what would the patient
   choose?) first; **best interests** only when the patient's wishes are unknowable.

## 4. Analyse
- Name the conflict in principle terms (autonomy vs beneficence, non-maleficence,
  justice) — this clarifies, it does not settle.
- Identify **ethically supportable options** (usually more than one) and any that are
  clearly impermissible.
- Look for the hidden cause: an unasked question, a prognosis nobody said plainly,
  mistrust, grief, a disagreement inside the team.

## 5. Facilitate the meeting
Agenda: introductions and purpose → medical update in plain language → "tell us about
your father as a person" → his values and prior wishes → options and recommendation →
agreed plan, time-limited trial if useful (e.g. "reassess in 72 hours against these
criteria") → who follows up. Let the attending give the prognosis; the consultant
keeps the process fair and summarises.

## 6. Write the consult note
1. Requester and ethics question.
2. Sources (chart, people interviewed, documents).
3. Four-box summary of facts.
4. Analysis: values in tension, relevant norms/policy.
5. **Recommendations** — ethically permissible options, clearly worded as advisory.
6. Follow-up plan and contact.
Avoid speculation about motives and avoid legal conclusions.

## Gotchas
- A capacity "consult" is often a disagreement in disguise: disagreeing with the team
  is not incapacity.
- "Futility" is contested; prefer "potentially inappropriate treatment" and follow your
  institution's dispute-resolution policy (process-based approaches, e.g. the 2015
  multisociety ATS statement, rather than unilateral declarations).
- Do not become the family's adversary or the team's enforcer — neutrality is the asset.
- Moral distress in nurses is real data; ask them separately.
- Consult outcomes and process vary widely between institutions (unconfirmed: no single
  binding standard; ASBH competencies are the common reference in the US).

## Verify
- [ ] The ethics question is written in one sentence.
- [ ] All four boxes are filled from chart *and* interviews.
- [ ] Capacity and the correct legal surrogate are documented.
- [ ] At least one ethically supportable option is stated, with reasons.
- [ ] Note is advisory, factual, and names the follow-up.
- [ ] Local law/policy was checked for surrogate and withdrawal questions.
