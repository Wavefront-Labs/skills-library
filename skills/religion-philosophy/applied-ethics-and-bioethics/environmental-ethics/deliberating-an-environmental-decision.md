# Deliberating an environmental decision — making the value choices visible

**Goal:** for a concrete decision (a land-use permit, a corporate sustainability
choice, a culling programme, a climate policy), lay out who and what counts, what is
at stake for each, and which value judgements drive the answer — so decision-makers
choose knowingly and affected people can see why.

Environmental decisions usually hide ethics inside technical inputs (discount rate,
baseline, boundary of "affected"). The job is to surface those. Not legal advice —
environmental impact assessment law, permitting and consultation duties are
jurisdiction-dependent.

## 1. Frame the decision
- Decision owner, options actually on the table (including "do nothing" and "do less"),
  deadline, reversibility.
- **Boundary:** geographic, temporal (10 years? 100?), and which impacts are in scope
  (upstream supply chain? downstream emissions?). Write down what is excluded and why.

## 2. Decide who and what has moral standing
| View | What counts directly | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Anthropocentric | Present and future humans | Nature valued for services, health, culture |
| Sentientist | All sentient animals | Animal suffering weighed in its own right |
| Biocentric | All living organisms | Harm to any life needs justification |
| Ecocentric (e.g. Leopold's land ethic) | Species, ecosystems, ecological wholes | Integrity and stability of systems weighed |
Stakeholders rarely share one view. Record which views are represented and run the
analysis under more than one; where the recommendation flips, that is the crux.

## 3. Justice checks
- **Distributional:** who bears costs, who gets benefits? Overlay with income,
  race/ethnicity, existing pollution burden.
- **Procedural:** were affected people informed early and able to influence the
  outcome? (Aarhus-style access to information, participation, justice.)
- **Recognition:** are cultural and Indigenous relationships to land treated as
  values, not obstacles? Where Indigenous rights are engaged, FPIC (UNDRIP) may apply.
- **Intergenerational:** what is left for people not yet born — irreversible losses,
  long-lived pollutants, climate.

## 4. Uncertainty and precaution
Rio Principle 15: lack of full scientific certainty should not postpone cost-effective
measures against **serious or irreversible** damage. In practice, state:
1. the threat and its plausibility,
2. how severe/irreversible,
3. what a proportionate precautionary measure would cost,
4. who carries the burden of proof.
Strong versions (ban until proven safe) and weak versions (consider precaution) give
very different answers — say which you are using.

## 5. Discounting and valuation
Cost-benefit analyses discount future harms. A pure time-preference rate near zero
(Stern Review's ~0.1%) versus market-based rates of several percent can change the
result by orders of magnitude for century-scale harms. Treat the rate as an ethical
parameter: run sensitivity at two or three rates and report all. Flag goods that
resist monetisation (species extinction, sacred sites).

## 6. Animal-impact decisions
Assess welfare with the **Five Domains** (nutrition, environment, health, behavioural
interactions, mental state). For research or management programmes apply the 3Rs
(replace, reduce, refine), require evidence the intervention will work, and prefer
humane methods; weigh wild-animal welfare alongside population goals.

## 7. Options table and recommendation
For each option: impacts by stakeholder (including non-human and future), justice
flags, reversibility, uncertainty, cost, mitigation per the hierarchy **avoid →
minimise → restore → offset** (offsets last, not first). State the recommendation and
the value judgements it depends on.

## Gotchas
- Offsets are often used to license avoidable harm; check that avoidance was genuinely tried.
- "Consultation" after the decision is fixed fails procedural justice.
- Aggregate net benefit can hide concentrated harm to one community.
- Greenwashing risk: corporate claims must be verifiable and scoped honestly.

## Verify
- [ ] Decision boundary (space, time, scope) written down with exclusions.
- [ ] Moral-standing views identified; analysis tested under at least two.
- [ ] Distributional, procedural, recognition and intergenerational checks recorded.
- [ ] Precaution version and discount rates stated, with sensitivity runs.
- [ ] Mitigation hierarchy applied in order; recommendation names its value premises.
