# Border biosecurity inspection and seizure

**Goal:** stop pests and diseases entering on goods, packaging, vehicles and travellers'
luggage, and handle intercepted material so it neither escapes nor loses its value as a
diagnostic sample. One missed consignment can cost an agricultural sector billions (an
outbreak of foot-and-mouth or African swine fever, or an established fruit fly).

## 1. Think in pathways
Risk arrives by pathway, not just commodity:
- **Passengers:** fresh fruit, meat and dairy products, seeds, soil on footwear and camping gear.
- **Mail and express parcels:** seeds, live plants and insects ordered online.
- **Cargo:** the goods themselves, and also **wood packaging**, container floors and
  external surfaces (hitchhiker pests such as brown marmorated stink bug, khapra beetle in
  container residues).
- **Vehicles and machinery:** soil and plant debris on used equipment.
Profile by origin country pest status, commodity, season (e.g. stink bug season measures in
Australia and New Zealand), and importer history.

## 2. Documents first
- **Phytosanitary certificate** (plants and plant products, issued by the exporting NPPO under
  the IPPC) — check it matches the consignment, is original/valid e-Phyto, and carries any
  required additional declarations.
- **Veterinary health certificates** for animal products, consistent with import conditions.
- **Import permits** where required.
- **ISPM 15** wood packaging: look for the IPPC mark with country code, producer number and
  treatment code (HT heat treatment, MB methyl bromide where still permitted, or others).
  Unmarked or insect-damaged wood is non-compliant regardless of paperwork.

## 3. Inspection
- Passengers: declaration card review, targeted questioning, X-ray, and detector dogs (beagles
  are common at airports); "declare or dispose" bins before the inspection point.
- Cargo: inspect a statistically or risk-based sample of units; open boxes from different
  positions in the container; check packaging, pallets and container internals with a torch;
  look for live insects, frass, boreholes, snails, seeds, soil.
- Use sample size tables where your agency specifies them (e.g. sampling to detect a given
  infestation level with stated confidence); record the sample.

## 4. Interceptions
- **Collect specimens** for identification: live insects into vials (ethanol for most, but
  follow lab instructions), plant material sealed in double bags, labelled with consignment
  reference, location in the consignment, date and inspector.
- Notify the diagnostic lab and, for high-priority pests, the emergency response team at once.
- Contain the consignment (seal, hold in place) until identification.

## 5. Decisions on non-compliant goods
Under typical regimes the options are: **treat** (fumigation, heat, cold), **re-export**,
or **destroy** (incineration, deep burial or approved methods). For travellers: seizure and
destruction, warnings, infringement notices or prosecution for deliberate non-declaration
(e.g. Australia and New Zealand issue on-the-spot penalties). Record the legal basis and
inform the owner of review rights.

## Gotchas
- Accepting a valid-looking certificate without checking the goods match it.
- Destroying an intercepted pest without keeping a specimen for ID — the pathway can't be
  managed without knowing what it was.
- Releasing the container before the lab identification returns.
- Missing hitchhikers on the outside of containers and vehicles.

## Verify
- [ ] Certificates matched to physical consignment.
- [ ] Wood packaging mark checked.
- [ ] Specimens labelled and sent; consignment held pending result.
- [ ] Decision (treat/re-export/destroy) recorded with legal basis.

*Uncertainty:* treatment schedules and penalties are country-specific; quarantine import
conditions databases (e.g. Australia's BICON, US APHIS) are the authority.
