# Detection-dog training aids, records and screening deployments

**Goal:** keep a border detection dog (drugs, currency, tobacco, firearms, biosecurity, or
explosives) reliable in a busy environment, and be able to prove that reliability. The
dog's nose is excellent; most errors come from the training aids, the handler or fatigue.

## 1. Alert types for screening
- **Passive alert** (sit or freeze at the source) is used for people and baggage in passenger
  screening — non-intrusive and safe around the public and fragile items.
- **Active alert** (scratching, barking) is sometimes used for cargo, vehicles and mail
  where contact doesn't matter.
- Train and document a single, clearly defined **final response**; record field alerts as that
  response or as "interest/change of behaviour" — never conflate them.

## 2. Training aids: the hidden failure point
- Store each odour type in its own sealed container, in separate locations, handled with fresh
  gloves. Cross-contamination teaches the dog the wrong odour (e.g. a dog that alerts to the
  plastic bag or the glove smell rather than the substance).
- Vary packaging, quantities and the "distractor" odours (food, plastics, gloves, clean
  containers) so the dog learns the target, not its container.
- Log every aid: issue, use, storage, quantity, disposal — controlled drugs and explosive aids
  are subject to strict legal accountability.
- Never place aids on members of the public or real passenger baggage without informed,
  authorised procedures; use staff and controlled set-ups.

## 3. Screening patterns
- **Passengers in queues:** work along the line with the wind/air flow in mind; move at a
  pace the dog can process; let the dog check bags at floor level.
- **Baggage hall/belts:** work from the side, against the belt direction where the layout
  allows, so the dog meets each bag fresh.
- **Cargo and mail:** systematic patterns (perimeter then interior, low to high), consistent
  direction so nothing is skipped; use sampling of air from containers where approved systems exist.
- Use the dog's natural behaviour change to direct closer examination by officers; the alert
  is grounds for examination, not proof of an offence.

## 4. Blind testing and handler cueing
- Handlers unintentionally cue dogs (body orientation, pausing, leash tension). Include
  regular **blind** (handler doesn't know) and **double-blind** (neither handler nor observer
  knows) exercises; SWGDOG guidance recommends them for proficiency assessment.
- Measure and record: correct alerts, misses, false alerts, and time to locate.
- Field "unconfirmed alerts" can be residual odour (e.g. currency dogs alerting to recently
  handled drugs or contaminated notes) — record them; trends indicate either contamination or
  a training issue.

## 5. Fatigue and welfare
- Detection performance drops with continuous work; many programmes limit active searching to
  short periods (often around 20–30 minutes) followed by rest — check your programme's standard.
- Heat, noise, crowds and slippery floors stress dogs; rotate, hydrate, and give rest away from
  the screening area.
- Reward every correct find in the field (use a pre-placed aid if needed to keep motivation),
  but never reward a wrong alert.

## Gotchas
- One shared aid box for several odours.
- Always hiding aids in the same places; the dog learns the location.
- Treating an alert as conclusive evidence in reports.
- Working the dog to the end of a shift without breaks.

## Verify
- [ ] Aid log complete with storage segregation.
- [ ] Blind/double-blind exercises recorded this period.
- [ ] Field alerts recorded with outcome, including unconfirmed ones.
- [ ] Work/rest schedule followed.

*Uncertainty:* work/rest durations and certification standards vary by agency and odour discipline.
