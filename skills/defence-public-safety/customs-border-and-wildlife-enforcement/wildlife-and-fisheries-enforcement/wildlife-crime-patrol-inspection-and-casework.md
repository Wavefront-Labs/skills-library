# Wildlife crime patrol, inspection and casework

**Goal:** enforce hunting, fishing and protected-species law in the field and build cases
that hold up — often alone, far from backup, and with evidence (an animal) that degrades
fast. Wildlife officers are typically fully sworn law-enforcement officers with additional
regulatory inspection powers.

## 1. Patrol and contacts
- Patrol where and when violations happen: season openings, night hours for spotlighting,
  closed areas, spawning runs. Data-driven tools such as SMART record patrol tracks and
  observations so effort can be targeted and reported.
- Most contacts are with lawful hunters and anglers who are **armed** or carrying knives;
  treat every contact with ordinary officer-safety discipline and courtesy. Tell dispatch
  where you are — remote areas often lack radio coverage; carry a satellite communicator.
- Compliance check: licence and tags/permits, species, sex/size, bag and possession limits,
  method (legal gear, firearm/ammunition restrictions, baits), place and time.
- Many statutes give inspection powers over game, licences and gear without individual
  suspicion; searches of persons, vehicles and homes still follow general constitutional rules.
  Know which power you're using.

## 2. Fisheries boarding and inspection
- Boarding is usually under a specific statutory or treaty power (e.g. national fisheries
  acts; port state measures and high-seas boarding schemes for IUU fishing).
- Plan: vessel identity and history, crew size, sea state, safe boarding method and boat crew.
  Personal flotation and cold-water safety are non-negotiable.
- Inspect: licences and logbooks, VMS/AIS records vs actual positions, catch by species
  and weight against declarations, gear (mesh size, escape devices), holds and hidden compartments.
- Record discrepancies with photographs and measurements in the presence of the master.

## 3. Processing a poaching scene
- Treat it as a crime scene: secure, photograph, map. Carcass position, wound locations,
  blood trails, vehicle tracks, spent cartridges, spotlight positions.
- Recover projectiles where possible (X-ray or careful dissection) for ballistic comparison.
- Time-of-death indicators: body temperature, rigor, eye changes, insect activity — use with
  caution; species and weather vary a lot.
- Take samples for DNA (tissue in ethanol or dried on cards) — wildlife DNA labs can match a
  carcass to meat in a freezer or a trophy.
- Evidence animals are perishable: photograph, sample, then follow policy for freezing,
  donation or disposal, recording continuity throughout.

## 4. Trafficking casework
- Species identification is the first evidential hurdle: use reference guides, agency experts
  or a wildlife forensic lab; record who identified it and how.
- CITES: check appendix listing and permits (export/import, re-export certificates); permit
  fraud and laundering wild-caught specimens as "captive-bred" are common.
- US: the **Lacey Act** makes it a federal offence to trade wildlife taken in violation of any
  state, tribal, foreign or federal law — widening jurisdiction. Similar provisions exist elsewhere.
- Follow the money and communications (online marketplaces, courier records) as in any
  organised-crime case.

## Gotchas
- Weighing or counting catch without a witness or calibrated scale.
- Freezing whole carcasses without sampling first; later DNA can be compromised.
- Misidentified species collapsing the charge (look-alike species, juveniles).
- Solo night patrols without check-in times.

## Verify
- [ ] Each contact records licence/tag details and the inspection power used.
- [ ] Scene photographed and mapped before carcass moved; samples taken.
- [ ] Species identification documented with method and identifier.
- [ ] Check-in plan in place for remote patrols.

*Uncertainty:* powers vary greatly between states, provinces and countries; the forensic notes
are general and should be checked with a wildlife forensics lab.
