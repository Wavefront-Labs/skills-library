# Risk targeting cargo and controlled deliveries

**Goal:** examine the few shipments most likely to be non-compliant out of millions that
aren't, and when contraband is found, decide whether seizing it now or following it to the
organisers serves the case better. This is the risk-management discipline set out in WCO
standards, not field concealment technique.

## 1. The risk-management cycle
1. **Establish context:** what threats matter (drugs, weapons, counterfeit goods, revenue,
   wildlife, sanctions) and their harm.
2. **Identify and analyse risk** from intelligence, seizure history, trade data.
3. **Treat**: decide controls — documentary check, non-intrusive scan, physical examination,
   or release. Most consignments should go through with no intervention; facilitation is part
   of customs' mandate.
4. **Monitor and review**: measure what the controls find and adjust.

## 2. Advance data
- Pre-arrival cargo information is mandated in most regimes (EU ICS2 Entry Summary
  Declaration; US 24-hour rule and Importer Security Filing "10+2"; WCO SAFE).
- Targeting systems (e.g. US CBP Automated Targeting System, national equivalents) score
  shipments against rules and models; officers review the high scores.
- Data quality is itself a risk signal: vague goods descriptions, missing consignee details
  and late or amended filings warrant attention.

## 3. Building and maintaining profiles
- Profiles combine indicators (route, commodity, parties, history, documentary anomalies)
  derived from past seizures and intelligence. Keep them specific enough to be selective.
- **Trusted traders** (Authorised Economic Operator programmes, C-TPAT) get lower risk weighting
  in exchange for audited security — freeing examination capacity for unknowns.
- Include a **random selection** component so you measure true compliance and detect threats
  your profiles don't model.
- Review profiles on a schedule; retire those with persistently nil results, document why.

## 4. The feedback loop — where most systems fail
Every examination result (positive *and* negative) must be recorded back against the profile
that selected it, with detail of what was found and where. Without results feedback, hit
rates can't be measured and targeting never improves. Typical metrics: hit rate per profile,
detection value, examination time, release delay to compliant traders.

## 5. Interdiction vs controlled delivery
When contraband is detected, the choice is:
- **Seize at the border** — certain removal of the goods; usually only low-level couriers or
  front companies are arrested.
- **Controlled delivery** — allow the consignment (often with the contraband substituted or
  partly removed) to continue under surveillance to identify and arrest the receivers and
  organisers. Recognised in the 1988 UN Convention (Art. 11) and the UN Convention against
  Transnational Organized Crime.
Decision factors: legal authority in every country the goods will cross; capacity to
maintain surveillance throughout; risk of losing the goods (substitute where possible);
prosecution strategy; public safety (never release items such as firearms into the community).
Authorisation sits at senior level and with prosecutors; international deliveries need prior
agreement through mutual legal assistance or customs cooperation channels.

## Gotchas
- Profiles so broad they select a large share of legitimate trade, swamping examiners.
- Recording only positive results.
- Seizing reflexively when the investigative opportunity is greater, or the reverse — running
  a controlled delivery without the resources to keep control.
- Ignoring compliance facilitation; delays have real economic cost and erode trader cooperation.

## Verify
- [ ] Every examination result logged against its selecting profile.
- [ ] Random-selection component in place and measured.
- [ ] Profiles reviewed on schedule with documented decisions.
- [ ] Controlled-delivery decisions recorded with authorisations and risk assessment.

*Uncertainty:* system names refer to EU and US regimes; national systems vary. Hit-rate
benchmarks are agency-specific and not stated here.
