# Passenger and baggage examination and seizure processing

**Goal:** examine travellers and their goods efficiently and courteously, escalate only as
far as the law and the indicators justify, and process any seizure so it survives challenge.
The detail below follows US (CBP) and UK (Border Force/CEMA 1979) frameworks; each
customs administration has its own manual.

## 1. The legal frame
- At the border, customs has a broad power to examine goods and baggage without a warrant
  or individual suspicion (US "border search exception", *US v. Ramsey*, *US v.
  Montoya de Hernandez*; UK CEMA 1979 s.78 and related powers).
- **More intrusive** searches need more: in the US, strip searches generally need reasonable
  suspicion and body-cavity/medical searches need a higher standard and medical staff; in
  the UK, CEMA s.164 personal searches need reasonable grounds to suspect, and the traveller
  can ask for a superior officer or magistrate review.
- **Electronic devices:** US CBP Directive 3340-049A separates basic (manual) from advanced
  (forensic) searches, the latter needing reasonable suspicion or national-security concern;
  case law varies by circuit. Check current policy before any device search.

## 2. Selecting and examining
- Selection from risk targeting (advance passenger information, PNR analysis), behaviour and
  documentary indicators, or random selection. Record the reason — "random" is legitimate but
  must actually be random.
- Explain to the traveller why they've been stopped and what will happen. Ask the standard
  questions: is this your bag, did you pack it yourself, are you carrying anything for anyone else,
  anything to declare (goods over allowance, currency above threshold, prohibited items)?
- Examine in front of the traveller where possible. Work systematically (outer bag, linings,
  each item); note anything structurally unusual (weight, false bottoms, glued seams) and refer
  to detector/X-ray rather than damaging goods unnecessarily.
- Respect religious and cultural items and gender-appropriate searching.

## 3. Classify the goods
- **Prohibited** (e.g. controlled drugs, indecent images, certain weapons) — seize and refer for
  criminal investigation.
- **Restricted** (firearms with licence, CITES species, plants/food/animal products, medicines) —
  check licences/permits; seize if missing. Biosecurity items may go to the agriculture authority.
- **Dutiable over allowance** — assess duty; seizure/penalty for deliberate non-declaration.
- **Undeclared currency** above the threshold (EU/UK €/£10,000; US $10,000 reporting) —
  failure to declare can lead to seizure and penalty, separately from any laundering enquiry.

## 4. Processing a seizure
- Issue a **seizure notice/receipt** at the time listing every item, quantity and the legal basis;
  the traveller signs or refusal is noted. Explain the right to challenge (UK: notice of claim
  within one month leads to condemnation proceedings; US: petition for relief or referral to
  court in forfeiture proceedings).
- Package and seal per evidence-handling practice; photograph; weigh drugs and count currency
  with a witness officer.
- Write the notebook/report: selection reason, questions and answers verbatim, search actions,
  find location, items, any admissions (and whether caution/rights were given before questioning
  moved from routine customs questions to criminal suspicion).

## Gotchas
- Continuing routine questions after suspicion of an offence crystallises, without a caution —
  answers may be excluded.
- Counting cash alone, with no witness or count record.
- Damaging lawful goods without justification — compensation claims and complaints.
- Treating the device search policy as static; it changes and differs by country.

## Verify
- [ ] Selection reason recorded.
- [ ] Each escalation of search level matched to its legal threshold.
- [ ] Seizure notice issued, signed, with challenge rights explained.
- [ ] Items sealed, photographed, weighed/counted with witness.

*Uncertainty:* legal thresholds summarised for US and UK only; EU, Canadian and Australian
customs powers differ in detail.
