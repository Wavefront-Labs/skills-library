# Field status checks, detention and removal safeguards

**Goal:** enforce immigration law in the field accurately (the right person, the right
status), lawfully (the right authority for each step), and humanely (vulnerabilities found
and acted on). Errors here are severe: citizens and lawful residents have been wrongly
detained and removed. Summarised mainly from US (ICE/ERO) and UK (Home Office Immigration
Enforcement) practice; policies change frequently, so check the current versions.

## 1. Verify before acting
- Identity: documents, biometrics (fingerprint checks against immigration and criminal
  systems), and the person's own account. Name matches alone are unreliable — common names
  and transliteration variants produce false hits.
- **Status:** check every system, not just the one that flagged the person. Pending applications,
  appeals, stays of removal, derivative citizenship (e.g. through a parent's naturalisation)
  and protected statuses (TPS, DACA in the US; pending asylum claims) commonly change the answer.
- A person's claim to be a citizen must be investigated before any detention continues;
  record the claim and the checks done.

## 2. Know what your paperwork authorises
- **US:** an administrative warrant (e.g. Form I-200 or I-205) is signed by an immigration
  officer, not a judge. It authorises arrest but does **not** authorise entry into a home
  without consent; that needs a judicial warrant or valid consent. Consent must be voluntary.
  Many US state and local laws limit cooperation with detainers.
- **UK:** immigration officers' powers of entry, search and arrest come from the Immigration
  Act 1971 Sch. 2 and later acts; entry to premises generally needs a warrant or specific statutory power.
- Locations policy: agencies have had policies restricting enforcement at or near schools,
  hospitals, places of worship and courthouses; these have been changed several times.
  Check which version is in force on the day.

## 3. Detention decision
- Detention is a decision, not an automatic result of arrest. Consider: removal prospects
  within a reasonable time, flight risk, risk to the public, and **vulnerability**.
- **Screen for vulnerability**: pregnancy, serious illness or disability, mental health,
  victims of trafficking or torture, children, primary carers of children. UK "Adults at Risk"
  policy and US detention standards require these to weigh against detention.
- Consider alternatives to detention (reporting, electronic monitoring, case management).
- Family units: follow specific policies on family detention and avoid unnecessary separation;
  arrange care for children if a carer is detained.

## 4. In custody
- Inform the person of the reason for detention, in a language they understand.
- Access to legal advice and the right to contact their consulate (Vienna Convention on
  Consular Relations Art. 36) — offer and record.
- Medical screening on arrival at a facility; medicines continued.
- Property inventory and receipt.

## 5. Removal
- Confirm there is no legal barrier on the day (late applications, injunctions, judicial reviews).
- Escorted removals: only approved restraint techniques, by trained escorts, with medical
  fitness-to-fly checks; restraint during removal has caused deaths (e.g. a 2010 death during
  a UK deportation flight led to guidance changes).
- Document the removal, handover and any force used.

## Gotchas
- Acting on a single database flag.
- Treating an administrative warrant as permission to enter a home.
- Missing a citizenship claim because it was voiced informally.
- Policy versions (locations, detainers) out of date in team briefings.

## Verify
- [ ] Status confirmed across all relevant systems, including pending matters.
- [ ] Legal authority for each step (stop, entry, arrest, detention) identified.
- [ ] Vulnerability screening recorded and weighed.
- [ ] Legal and consular access offered and recorded.

*Uncertainty:* policies cited are subject to frequent change; this summary is not legal advice
on any current policy version.
