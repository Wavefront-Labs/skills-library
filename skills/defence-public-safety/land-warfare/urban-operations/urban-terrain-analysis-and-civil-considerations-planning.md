# Urban terrain analysis and civil-considerations planning

**Goal:** understand why urban operations are treated as a distinct specialism, how
planners analyse a city, and how civilian protection and law shape every decision.
Unclassified, doctrine- and history-level summary as taught at staff colleges; it
deliberately omits close-combat drills and building-entry techniques.

## 1. Why cities are different
- **Three-dimensional terrain:** airspace, rooftops, building interiors, street level and
  **subterranean** (sewers, metros, basements, tunnels). Fields of view and fire shrink
  to a street or a room.
- **Density of people:** civilians are almost always present; their safety, movement
  and needs are a central planning factor, not background.
- **Infrastructure:** water, power, sanitation, hospitals — damage has cascading
  humanitarian effects (ICRC research stresses this).
- **Resource intensity:** urban fighting historically consumes troops, ammunition, time
  and casualties at far higher rates than open terrain. *(Specific ratios quoted in
  literature vary widely; treat them as rough.)*
- **Information environment:** every event is filmed; perception matters strategically.

## 2. The planning framework
US doctrine frames urban operations as **understand – shape – engage – consolidate –
transition**; other doctrines have equivalents. The planning emphasis is that the best
urban battle is often the one avoided (isolate or bypass), and that the end state is a
functioning city handed to civil authorities.

## 3. Analysing the urban environment
Treat the city as a **system of systems**:
- **Terrain:** urban patterns (core, high-rise, dense residential, industrial,
  outskirts), street grids, building construction (which governs protection and
  movement), key structures and the subsurface.
- **Society:** use **ASCOPE** for civil considerations — **A**reas, **S**tructures,
  **C**apabilities, **O**rganisations, **P**eople, **E**vents (markets, holidays,
  elections). Map protected sites (hospitals, schools, places of worship, cultural
  property) early.
- **Infrastructure:** what keeps the population alive and who runs it.

## 4. Law and civilian protection
The law of armed conflict applies with special force: **distinction**, **proportionality**
and **precautions in attack**, plus the debate over **explosive weapons in populated
areas** (the 2022 Political Declaration endorsed by many states). Planning implications:
no-fire areas around protected sites (see
defence-public-safety.land-warfare.field-artillery.fire-support-coordination-and-planning),
humanitarian corridors and evacuation planning, and coordination with humanitarian
organisations. *(Which states endorsed the declaration is not listed here — check.)*

## 5. Historical cases staff colleges use
- **Stalingrad (1942–43):** attrition in ruins; defender's advantage of rubble.
- **Hué (1968):** restrictions on heavy fire, then their lifting; cultural destruction.
- **Grozny (1994–95):** armour entering a city without infantry or preparation.
- **Fallujah (2004)** and **Mosul (2016–17):** isolation, civilian evacuation dilemmas,
  months of fighting for a city.
- **Marawi (2017)** and **Mariupol (2022):** sieges with very heavy civilian cost.
Common lessons: isolation matters, armour needs infantry in cities, sustainment and
casualty evacuation are decisive, and civilian harm shapes the strategic outcome.

## Gotchas
- Acronyms differ: MOUT (older US), FIBUA/OBUA (UK), "urban operations" (current).
- A map is not the city: building interiors, informal settlements and subterranean
  networks are often unmapped.
- Treating civilians as "terrain" rather than people misreads both law and strategy.
