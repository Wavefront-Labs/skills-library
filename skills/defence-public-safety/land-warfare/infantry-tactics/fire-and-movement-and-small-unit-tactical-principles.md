# Fire and movement and small-unit tactical principles

**Goal:** understand *why* infantry sections and platoons are organised and employed the
way they are, so you can read doctrine, analyse a historical engagement or follow a
training discussion with the right concepts. This is an unclassified, doctrine-level
summary of what is taught at infantry schools and staff colleges, not a set of drills for
a specific force; national doctrine differs in detail and terminology.

## 1. The organising idea: one element fires, another moves
Every infantry tactic since the late 19th century rests on **fire and movement**: a
**base of fire** suppresses the opponent (keeps them from observing and firing
effectively) while another element moves. Movement without suppression is exposure;
suppression without movement achieves nothing decisive. The rest is detail on top of
that pair.
- **Fireteam / section / squad** structures (typically 4-man teams in a 8–12-person
  section) exist so the unit can split into a firing and a moving element at every level.
- **Mutual support:** positions and moving elements are placed so each can help the
  others. A position nobody can support is isolated, however strong it looks.
- The key historical lesson (WWI infiltration tactics, Griffith 1994) is that small,
  decentralised groups using ground and local fire support beat linear waves.

## 2. Reading terrain: OAKOC
Doctrine analyses ground with a mnemonic (US **OAKOC**; UK and others use variants):
**O**bservation and fields of fire, **A**venues of approach, **K**ey terrain,
**O**bstacles, **C**over and concealment.
- **Cover** protects from fire; **concealment** only hides. Confusing them is the classic
  student mistake.
- **Key terrain** is ground whose control gives a marked advantage; it is not always the
  highest point.
- Combine with **METT-TC** (mission, enemy, terrain/weather, troops, time, civil
  considerations) for the overall estimate — see
  defence-public-safety.military-planning-and-command.operational-planning.military-decision-making-process-and-orders-production
  for the staff-level version.

## 3. Defensive principles
Doctrine lists characteristics of a good defence, commonly:
- **All-round defence** — no unobserved approach.
- **Depth** — successive positions so a single penetration does not unhinge the whole.
- **Mutual support** and interlocking arcs of fire between positions.
- **Concealment and deception**, **reserves** for counter-attack, and **offensive
  action** (the defence is a temporary posture, not an end).
- Positions are improved continuously: priorities of work start with security and
  observation before comfort.

## 4. Patrolling (conceptual)
Patrols are classed by purpose — **reconnaissance** (gather information, avoid contact),
**combat** (fighting patrols with a task) and **security/presence**. Planning concepts
include a clear task, routes chosen for cover, rally points, actions if contact occurs,
and a post-patrol debrief that feeds the intelligence picture. See
defence-public-safety.land-warfare.ground-reconnaissance.reconnaissance-fundamentals-and-reporting.

## 5. Leadership process
Small-unit leaders use a compressed planning routine (US **troop-leading procedures**,
UK **battle procedure**): receive the task, issue a warning order, make a tentative plan,
start movement, reconnoitre, complete the plan, issue the order, supervise. The
one-third/two-thirds time rule applies at every level.

## Gotchas
- **Doctrine is national and dated.** Section size, mnemonics and drill names vary by
  army and edition; always state which publication and year you are citing. *(Figures
  above are typical, not universal.)*
- **"Fire superiority" is a condition, not a volume of fire.** Doctrine defines it by its
  effect on the enemy, which is why historical analysis of ammunition expenditure alone
  misleads.
- Popular media portrays assaults as frontal rushes; doctrine treats frontal attack as
  the option of last resort and prefers flanking the base of fire.
- Modern conflicts (drones, persistent surveillance) are actively changing small-unit
  doctrine; treat pre-2020 publications as a baseline, not the current state. *(Exact
  current changes are not confirmed here.)*
