# Combined-arms integration of armoured forces

**Goal:** explain how armoured formations are employed and why modern doctrine insists
tanks never fight alone, with the historical evidence for that rule. Unclassified,
doctrine-level summary of what staff colleges teach; specific force structures differ by
nation and era.

## 1. What armour brings
Armour combines **protection, mobility and firepower** in one platform (see
defence-public-safety.land-warfare.armoured-fighting-vehicles.afv-classes-crew-roles-and-the-protection-mobility-firepower-trade-off).
Doctrinally that yields:
- **Shock action** — rapid, concentrated violence that breaks an opponent's cohesion
  rather than destroying it piece by piece.
- **Exploitation and pursuit** — speed to move through a gap before the defender can
  react, attacking command, logistics and reserves in depth.
- **Counter-attack** in the defence — the mobile reserve that restores a position.

## 2. Why armour needs the other arms
Each arm covers the others' weaknesses:
| Arm | Gives armour | Armour gives it |
|---|---|---|
| Infantry | close protection in woods, towns, defiles; clearing dismounted threats | protected firepower, mobility |
| Artillery / mortars | suppression of anti-armour weapons, obscuration | rapid exploitation of the effect |
| Engineers | breaching obstacles, gap crossing | protection while working |
| Air defence / aviation | protection from air and drones; deep reconnaissance | |
| Logistics | fuel and ammunition — armour is the hungriest arm | |

**Task organisation** turns this into units: tank and mechanised infantry companies are
**cross-attached** into mixed **company teams / combat teams** and **battlegroups / task
forces**, weighted tank-heavy for open terrain and mobile tasks, infantry-heavy for close
or urban terrain.

## 3. Historical evidence (the lessons doctrine was built on)
- **Cambrai, 1917:** massed tanks broke in, but lacked reserves and exploitation means;
  gains were lost. Lesson: break-in is not breakthrough.
- **Interwar theory:** Fuller and Liddell Hart (UK), Tukhachevsky's **deep battle**
  (USSR) and German combined-arms practice. The 1940 German success came less from tank
  numbers or quality than from radio-equipped, all-arms panzer divisions and
  decentralised command (**Auftragstaktik**). *(The term "Blitzkrieg" as a formal
  doctrine is contested by historians.)*
- **Kursk, 1943:** armour attacking prepared anti-tank defences in depth suffered
  heavily — the cost of attacking without the means to suppress or bypass.
- **Sinai, October 1973:** Israeli tank-pure counter-attacks against Egyptian infantry
  armed with anti-tank guided missiles failed; reintegrating infantry and artillery
  restored success. The most cited single case for combined arms.
- **1991 Gulf War (e.g. 73 Easting):** night sights, training and integrated fires
  produced lopsided results — a lesson about training and sensors as much as armour.
- **Since 2022 (Ukraine):** drones, mines and ubiquitous anti-armour weapons have
  renewed debate on armour's survivability. *(Doctrinal conclusions still emerging;
  treat any claim of "the tank is obsolete" — made after 1973 too — with caution.)*

## 4. Planning considerations (conceptual)
- **Terrain:** going (can vehicles cross it?), bridges' load classes, choke points.
- **Sustainment:** fuel, recovery and repair set the real tempo; an armoured advance
  usually halts on logistics, not combat.
- **Tempo vs. security:** exploitation trades flank security for speed; doctrine accepts
  that risk deliberately and says so in the commander's intent.

## Gotchas
- Don't judge armies by tank counts; integration, training and logistics dominate
  outcomes in every case above.
- Terminology varies: UK **battlegroup/combat team** ≈ US **task force/company team**;
  "armour" and "armor" are the same thing.
- Popular histories overstate single-weapon "revolutions"; doctrine and staff colleges
  teach these as combined-arms and command stories.
