# Analysing pre-modern battles: formations, siegecraft and cavalry

**Goal:** study land warfare before mechanisation the way military historians and staff
colleges do — understanding how the main formation types worked, what cavalry and
sieges contributed, and how far the sources can be trusted.

## 1. The questions to ask of any battle
1. **Sources:** who wrote it, when, for whom, how far from the event?
2. **Numbers:** are they plausible given population, logistics and the ground?
   (Delbrück's **Sachkritik** — test claims against physical possibility.)
3. **Ground:** walk it or study maps; ancient sites are often uncertain.
4. **Formations and arms** on each side, and how they combined.
5. **Command and morale:** most pre-modern battles ended when one side's cohesion broke,
   not when it was destroyed — the killing usually followed the rout.
6. **Logistics and campaign context:** why fight here, now?

## 2. Core formation families
- **Hoplite phalanx** (Greek, c. 7th–4th c. BC): dense shield-wall of armoured citizen
  spearmen; strong frontally, vulnerable on flanks and rough ground.
- **Macedonian phalanx** (Philip II, Alexander): long pikes (**sarissa**) as an anvil
  for the **Companion cavalry** hammer — a true combined-arms system (Gaugamela,
  331 BC).
- **Roman legion:** **manipular** (checkerboard lines, flexibility, replacement of tired
  lines) evolving into the **cohort** legion; strengths in drill, entrenchment,
  engineering and sustained campaigning. At **Cannae** (216 BC) Hannibal's double
  envelopment defeated a larger Roman army — still taught as the archetype of
  envelopment.
- **Medieval:** mixed arms of mounted men-at-arms, infantry and missile troops;
  English longbowmen with dismounted men-at-arms at **Crécy** (1346) and **Agincourt**
  (1415) — note that ground, mud and archer positions matter as much as the bow.
- **Pike and shot / tercio** (16th–17th c.): pikes protect slow-firing arquebusiers and
  musketeers, then linear tactics as firearms improved.

## 3. Cavalry roles
Scouting and screening, **shock** against shaken or flank-exposed infantry, pursuit,
and **horse archers** (Parthians, Mongols) wearing down armies by mobility. Cavalry
rarely broke steady, formed infantry frontally — a consistent lesson across eras.

## 4. Siegecraft
Most pre-modern war was sieges, not field battles. Concepts:
- **Blockade and starvation** vs **assault**; the attacker's **lines of circumvallation
  and contravallation** (Caesar at Alesia, 52 BC) protect against relief and sorties.
- **Gunpowder** made high medieval walls vulnerable, producing the low, angled
  **trace italienne / star fort**; Vauban systematised siege approaches in the 17th c.
- Disease, supply and time usually decided sieges more than technology.
See defence-public-safety.land-warfare.urban-operations.urban-terrain-analysis-and-civil-considerations-planning
for the modern legacy.

## 5. Logistics
Armies were limited by what men and animals could carry and forage; Engels' study of
Alexander shows campaigns shaped by water and grain. Use this to sanity-check numbers.

## Gotchas
- Ancient numbers are frequently inflated; modern estimates for many battles are a
  fraction of the sources' figures. *(Ranges vary between historians; state whose.)*
- The "military revolution" thesis (Roberts, Parker) is debated — present it as an
  argument, not settled fact.
- Avoid determinism: no formation was "invincible"; each lost when used on the wrong
  ground or against the right counter.
- Re-enactment and games are useful intuition pumps, not evidence.
