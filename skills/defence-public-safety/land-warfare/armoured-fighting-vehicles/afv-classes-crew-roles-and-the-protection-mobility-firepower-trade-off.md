# AFV classes, crew roles and the protection–mobility–firepower trade-off

**Goal:** be able to classify an armoured vehicle, explain the design trade-offs that
shape it, describe what each crew member does, and compare platforms without falling
for spec-sheet myths. Unclassified, public reference level; it does not cover weapon
employment procedures or vulnerabilities of specific vehicles.

## 1. Classes
| Class | Role | Typical traits |
|---|---|---|
| **Main battle tank (MBT)** | direct-fire shock and anti-armour | heaviest protection, large gun, tracked, 3–4 crew |
| **Infantry fighting vehicle (IFV)** | carry *and fight with* an infantry section | cannon/missiles, protected dismounts, tracked or wheeled |
| **Armoured personnel carrier (APC)** | protected transport | lighter weapons; drops infantry then withdraws |
| **Reconnaissance vehicle** | scouting, screening | speed, sensors, lower signature |
| **Self-propelled artillery / mortar** | indirect fire | armoured but not built for front-line combat |
| **Support** | engineer, recovery, bridging, command, ambulance | often on a common chassis |
Definitions vary; arms-control texts (CFE Treaty) and IISS *Military Balance* give
formal ones useful when counting.

## 2. The trade-off ("iron triangle")
**Protection, mobility and firepower** compete for the same weight, volume and money:
- More armour → more weight → bigger engine, lower bridge/road access
  (military load class — see
  defence-public-safety.land-warfare.combat-engineering.mobility-countermobility-survivability-and-gap-crossing-doctrine),
  more fuel.
- **Power-to-weight ratio** (hp/tonne) and **ground pressure** matter more for real
  mobility than top speed.
- **Wheeled vs tracked:** wheels give road speed, range and lower cost; tracks give
  cross-country mobility and carry heavier protection.
- Modern additions shift the triangle: **sensors and situational awareness**, **active
  protection systems** and **signature reduction** now compete for the same budget.
History repeatedly shows designs optimised for one corner (e.g. very heavy WWII
designs, or very light tanks) struggle in practice.

## 3. Crew roles (generic MBT)
- **Commander** — fights the vehicle: navigation, observation, target priorities,
  communications with the platoon. Classic doctrine teaches commanders to keep head-up
  observation where safe; modern sensors reduce but do not remove that need.
- **Gunner** — operates the main armament and sights under the commander's direction.
- **Loader** — loads the main gun (absent where an **autoloader** is fitted, e.g. many
  Soviet-lineage designs and the Leclerc), also manages radios and local security.
- **Driver** — moves the vehicle, chooses ground for cover, handles maintenance checks.
Crews work as a team through **crew drills** practised to habit; maintenance
(daily checks, track tension, fluids) is a shared crew duty and dominates real
availability. In IFVs the **dismount section** in the back adds an infantry leader with
their own task.

## 4. Comparing platforms soundly
- Published protection figures are estimates or marketing; real values are classified.
- **Training, crew quality, logistics and integration** decide outcomes more than specs
  (see defence-public-safety.land-warfare.armoured-warfare.combined-arms-integration-of-armoured-forces).
- Compare like with like: variant, year and upgrade package matter (an early and a late
  Leopard 2 or T-72 are very different vehicles).
- Losses in recent wars reflect how vehicles were employed as much as their design.
  *(Loss data from open-source trackers is useful but incomplete and should be cited
  with its limitations.)*

## Gotchas
- "Tank" in popular use covers any tracked AFV; doctrine is specific.
- Weight figures are often quoted without stating combat-loaded vs empty.
- Autoloader vs loader is a trade (crew size, rate, maintenance, volume) — not a simple
  better/worse.
