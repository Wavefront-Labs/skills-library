# Camouflage, concealment and land navigation

**Goal:** the basic field skills taught to every recruit — not being seen, knowing where
you are, and staying effective in the field. Doctrine-level summary; the navigation
half applies equally to hillwalkers and orienteers.

## 1. Why things are seen
Training uses a mnemonic of the giveaways; the UK version is the **S's and M**:
- **Shape** — regular outlines (helmet, rifle, vehicle) stand out in nature; break them
  up.
- **Shine** — glass, metal, sweat, plastic, screens.
- **Shadow** — your shadow, and the shadow you cast on a position; stay in shade.
- **Silhouette** — never on a skyline or against a plain background.
- **Spacing** — nature is irregular; evenly spaced objects look man-made.
- **Movement** — the biggest giveaway of all; the eye catches motion first.
Modern additions: **sound, smell, light** at night, and **thermal and electronic
signatures** — thermal imagers and drones see heat and emitters that paint and
foliage do not hide. *(Specific counter-thermal measures are equipment-dependent and
not covered here.)*

**Concealment principles:** use natural background, blend not cover-up, match the
season and vegetation, refresh cut foliage, and remember **discipline** (light, noise,
track, litter) is camouflage too. Cover ≠ concealment: cover stops fire, concealment
only hides (see
defence-public-safety.land-warfare.infantry-tactics.fire-and-movement-and-small-unit-tactical-principles).

## 2. Judging distance (briefly)
Taught methods: unit-of-measure (count known lengths, e.g. 100 m), appearance (what
detail is visible at what range) and bracketing. Things look **closer** in bright light,
across water or dead ground, and when large; **further** when looking up/down slope, in
poor light or when small.

## 3. Land navigation
**Map:** read the margin first — scale, contour interval, grid system, legend and the
**grid-magnetic angle** with its date and annual change.
- **Grid references:** "along the corridor, then up the stairs" — eastings before
  northings. A 6-figure ref is to 100 m, 8-figure to 10 m. Military maps commonly use
  **MGRS** (based on UTM).
- **Compass:** take a bearing from map, convert grid ↔ magnetic using the current
  declination (**"Grid to Mag, Add"** where magnetic is west of grid — the sign depends
  on location and date; always check the map's note).
- **Distance:** pacing — know your **pace count** per 100 m on flat ground, then adjust
  for slope, load, darkness and vegetation (it rises in all).
- **Techniques:** **terrain association** (match the ground to the contours),
  **handrails** (follow a linear feature), **catching features** (something that stops
  you overshooting), **aiming off** (deliberately aim to one side of a point on a linear
  feature so you know which way to turn), and **dead reckoning** (bearing + distance)
  when there are no features.
- **GPS** is a tool, not a replacement: batteries fail and signals can be jammed or
  spoofed; keep map and compass skills current.

## 4. Field living
Hygiene (feet, hands, teeth), water discipline, sleep management in rotation, and
recognising **cold injury** (hypothermia, non-freezing cold injury/trench foot,
frostbite) and **heat illness** early. Kit is kept packed so you can move at short
notice.

## Gotchas
- The commonest navigation error is **parallel error** (following the wrong but similar
  feature); confirm with two independent features.
- Declination changes year to year — an old map's note can be degrees out.
- Moving fast through cover is more visible than moving slowly across open ground in
  the right light.

## Verify
- [ ] You can list the visibility giveaways and fix each on a given position.
- [ ] You can give a 6- and 8-figure grid reference and convert grid ↔ magnetic bearing
      using a map's declination note.
- [ ] You know your pace count per 100 m and how it changes with terrain.
- [ ] You can plan a leg using a handrail, catching feature and aiming off.
