# Mobility, counter-mobility, survivability and gap-crossing doctrine

**Goal:** understand what combat engineers do for a ground force and how their tasks are
planned alongside manoeuvre. Unclassified, conceptual doctrine as taught at staff and
engineer schools. **Scope limit:** this skill deliberately covers roles, planning
principles and history only — no demolition, explosive, device or mine construction or
employment detail. Explosive ordnance *disposal* is its own field
(defence-public-safety.explosives-disposal).

## 1. The engineer functions
Doctrine groups combat engineering into lines of support:
- **Mobility** — keep own forces moving: breach obstacles, clear routes, cross gaps,
  maintain roads and airstrips.
- **Counter-mobility** — shape where the enemy can move by planned obstacles, always
  tied to a fire plan.
- **Survivability** — protect the force: field fortifications, vehicle fighting
  positions, protective works, camouflage support.
- **General engineering** — construction, water, power, infrastructure repair.

## 2. Breaching (conceptual)
An obstacle is a defended problem, not just a physical one. Doctrine's **breaching
fundamentals** are summarised as **SOSRA**:
1. **Suppress** the enemy covering the obstacle (fires —
   see defence-public-safety.land-warfare.field-artillery.fire-support-coordination-and-planning).
2. **Obscure** the breach site from observation.
3. **Secure** the far and near sides so the breach force can work.
4. **Reduce** — create and mark lanes through the obstacle.
5. **Assault** — pass forces through to seize the objective.
Breach forces are organised into **support, breach and assault** elements, and doctrine
stresses **mass** (more breach assets than needed, because some will be lost) and
**rehearsal**. Breaches can be *in-stride* (on the move), *deliberate* (planned
operation) or *covert*. The classic case study is the 1991 Gulf War breach of prepared
defences, where rehearsal and mass made a feared operation fast.

## 3. Counter-mobility: obstacle intent
Obstacles are planned by **effect**, integrated with direct and indirect fire:
- **Disrupt** (break up formations and tempo), **Turn** (divert into an engagement
  area), **Fix** (hold in place), **Block** (stop passage).
- Principle: *an obstacle not covered by observation and fire only delays, and briefly.*
- Legal frame: the use of landmines is governed by treaty (e.g. the Ottawa Convention
  for its parties, Amended Protocol II CCW for others); doctrine requires recording and
  marking. *(Treaty status by nation changes — check current status.)*

## 4. Gap crossing
Rivers and ravines are among the hardest operations:
- **Hasty** vs **deliberate** crossings; deliberate crossings need control lines,
  crossing-area commanders, staged movement and air defence over the bridges.
- Bridges and vehicles are matched by **military load classification (MLC)** — a
  vehicle's MLC must not exceed the bridge's.
- Bridging families: assault (vehicle-launched) bridges, floating/ribbon bridges and
  ferries, and longer-span support bridges.
- History: Rhine 1945; Egyptian Suez Canal crossing 1973 (high-pressure water used
  against sand ramparts — a notable engineer innovation); Ukrainian/Russian river
  crossings since 2022 show the cost when crossing sites are observed.

## 5. Planning integration
Engineers appear in every planning step: terrain and route analysis, obstacle overlays,
engineer annex to the order, and **sustainment of engineer assets**, which are always
scarcer than demand.

## Gotchas
- Engineers are a combined-arms enabler; doctrine frames breaching as a manoeuvre
  operation commanded by the manoeuvre commander, not an engineer side task.
- Terminology: "sappers" (UK/Commonwealth), "pioneers" (some armies) — check usage.
- Route-clearance doctrine overlaps with EOD, which is a separate specialism and field.
