# Deploying air-scent, trailing and disaster dogs

**Goal:** use a search dog team where it adds the most, brief it properly, and
report its coverage honestly so the search manager can rely on it. Reference for
handlers, flankers and search planners; certification standards differ by country
and organisation (e.g. NASAR or state standards in the US, IRO internationally, FEMA
for US&R disaster dogs) — use yours.

## 1. Disciplines
- **Air-scent (area) dogs** search an area for *any* human scent, ranging off-lead and
  working the scent cone back to its source. Good for large wilderness segments; don't
  need a scent article.
- **Trailing dogs** follow a *specific* person's scent path from a known start, on a
  long line, using a scent article. Good for direction of travel and from the IPP.
  (Tracking dogs, by contrast, follow the footstep track closely.)
- **Disaster (live-find) dogs** search collapsed structures for live people and bark
  at the source.
- **Human remains detection (HRD/cadaver) dogs** for recovery — a separate
  discipline; never assume a live-find dog will indicate on remains or vice versa.

## 2. Scent behaviour (enough to deploy well)
- Humans shed skin cells and vapours continuously; these move with air.
- Scent spreads downwind in a **cone**; air-scent dogs search across the wind and
  turn into the cone.
- **Thermals:** in the morning, air flows upslope as the ground warms; in the evening,
  scent sinks downslope and pools in drainages. Scent also pools against barriers and
  in depressions — dogs may alert far from the source.
- Heat, dry air and strong sun degrade scent; rain may refresh or wash it depending
  on conditions.

## 3. Deployment
- **Brief:** subject information, time missing, the assigned segment, hazards, other
  teams nearby (their scent is a distraction), and radio plan.
- **Air-scent:** enter the segment from downwind where possible and work across the
  wind in passes; the handler navigates while the dog ranges. A **flanker** handles
  navigation, radio and clue recording so the handler can watch the dog.
- **Trailing:** start at the IPP with a clean **scent article** collected correctly
  (item worn by the subject, handled minimally with gloves, sealed in a clean bag);
  record the time since the subject passed — older trails are harder.
- **Disaster:** work one structure section at a time, with machinery and other dogs
  cleared; confirm alerts with a second dog where possible.

## 4. Reading and confirming the alert
Most dogs are trained to a specific **indication**: bark at source, or a **refind**
(return to handler, indicate, lead back). Handlers must distinguish trained alerts from
interest (change of behaviour). Mark alert locations and wind direction at the time;
report them even if no subject is found — pooling scent may still point to the area.

## 5. Reporting
After each task, report the area covered (GPS track), wind conditions, alerts and
interest, and the team's **POD** estimate with its basis. Overstated PODs mislead the
search plan.

## Gotchas
- Dogs tire, overheat and get injured — plan rest, water and foot checks.
- Contaminated start points (many searchers, family) confuse trailing dogs.
- A dog not alerting is evidence about coverage, not proof of absence.

## Verify
- [ ] Discipline matched to task (area, trail, structure, remains).
- [ ] Scent article collected cleanly, if trailing.
- [ ] Deployment planned around wind and time of day.
- [ ] Alerts and interest mapped with wind direction.
- [ ] Honest POD reported with track log.
