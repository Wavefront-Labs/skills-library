# Swiftwater hazards and the rescue progression

**Goal:** rescue people from moving water without adding rescuers to the list of
victims. Moving water is deceptively powerful: force on an object rises with the square
of velocity, so doubling speed roughly quadruples the push. Reference for trained
responders; qualification (a recognised swiftwater rescue technician course) and team SOPs are required before in-water work.

## 1. Read the water
- **Strainers:** trees, fences, debris, vehicles — water passes through, people don't.
  Swim aggressively away from them (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- **Low-head dams/weirs:** uniform drop across the river creates a **recirculating hydraulic** that holds objects (and rescuers) at the base (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked). Never enter the boil line
  area; rescue from shore or with specialist techniques only.
- **Hydraulics/holes** behind rocks; **eddies** (calm, often upstream-flowing water
  behind obstructions) — rescuers use eddies as safe spots.
- **Downstream V** (points downstream) = the clear channel; **upstream V** = an
  obstruction.
- **Foot entrapment:** never try to stand in fast water above knee depth; feet wedge in rocks and the current pushes the person under (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- Floods add contaminated water, hidden debris, manhole covers lifted off, electricity,
  and undercut banks.

## 2. Rescue progression (lowest risk first)
**Talk → Reach → Throw → Row → Go** (sometimes adds Helo):
1. **Talk:** coach a conscious person to self-rescue (on their back, feet up and
   downstream, angle to shore).
2. **Reach:** pole, branch, extended paddle from a stable position on shore.
3. **Throw:** throw bag (rope bag) — aim *at or just past* the person; they hold the
   rope (not wrap it), on their back; rescuer belays dynamically and pendulums them
   into an eddy.
4. **Row:** boat-based rescue (inflatable, powered craft) by trained crew.
5. **Go:** in-water contact rescue by a qualified swimmer, tethered or untethered as
   protocols dictate. Highest risk; last resort.

## 3. Scene setup
- **Upstream spotter** with a whistle to warn of incoming debris.
- **Downstream safety:** at least one, ideally two, throw-bag-armed rescuers
  downstream to catch anyone (victim or rescuer) washed through.
- Communications: whistle signals and hand signals agreed beforehand (use your team's agreed signals).
- No responder at the water's edge without a PFD.

## 4. PPE
- **PFD** designed for whitewater rescue (with a quick-release chest harness only if
  you are trained to use it — a tethered rescuer who can't release can be pulled under).
- Helmet, thermal protection (drysuit or wetsuit), footwear, knife accessible, whistle.
- **Never tie a rope around anyone's waist** or wrap it around a hand in moving water.
- Structural turnout gear is dangerous in water.

## Gotchas
- Vehicles in floodwater: 12 in (30 cm) of rushing water can carry away most cars (NWS); rescuers must treat vehicle rescues as hazardous.
- Ropes stretched across the current (tensioned lines) can trap swimmers — only for
  specific techniques with release capability.
- Cold water shock and hypothermia affect both victims and rescuers.

## Verify
- [ ] Hazards identified and briefed (strainers, dams, hydraulics).
- [ ] Upstream spotter and downstream safety in place before any attempt.
- [ ] Rescue chosen from the lowest-risk rung that can work.
- [ ] Everyone near the water wearing a PFD and helmet; no waist ties.
