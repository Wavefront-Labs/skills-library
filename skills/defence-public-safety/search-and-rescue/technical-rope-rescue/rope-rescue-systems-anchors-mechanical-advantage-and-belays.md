# Rope rescue systems: anchors, mechanical advantage and belays

**Goal:** raise or lower a rescuer and casualty safely on rope, with no single point of
failure. Reference for trained rope rescue team members; qualification (NFPA 1006 sets personnel qualifications in the US; NFPA 2500 sets organisational capabilities and now carries the former NFPA 1983 life safety rope and equipment requirements; national equivalents elsewhere), team equipment and supervised
practice are mandatory before operating these systems.

## 1. Principles
- **Two-rope system:** a **main line** and an independent **belay** (or two tensioned
  lines sharing the load, TTRS, if your team's protocol allows). Either rope alone must
  hold the load if the other fails.
- **No single point of failure** anywhere: anchors, connectors, edge, people.
- **Safety factor:** teams design to a static system safety factor set by their standard (minimum breaking strength ÷ expected load) and plan a rescue load for one rescuer plus casualty plus litter. Take the exact factor and load from the current standard and your team's protocol; do not use a figure from memory.
- **Whistle test** (or "four-point check"): before load is applied, every component is
  checked by a second person — anchors, connectors closed and locked, ropes routed,
  people attached.

## 2. Anchors
- **Bombproof** natural anchors (large living trees, rock) or structural members,
  assessed by the rigger and checked by another.
- **Multi-point** anchors: load-sharing (static) or load-distributing (adjusting),
  with an interior angle kept small because force on each
  leg rises sharply as the angle widens.
- Edge protection under every rope at every edge; abrasion over an edge cuts a loaded
  rope quickly.

## 3. Lowering
A friction device (rack, descent control device, or Munter hitch for lighter loads)
on the main line, with a belay device on the belay line tended by a separate person.
The main-line operator controls speed; the belay is kept with minimal slack.

## 4. Raising and mechanical advantage
- **Simple 3:1 ("Z-rig"):** an anchor pulley, a traveling pulley attached with a rope
  grab (e.g. prusik or mechanical rope grab), and a **progress capture device (PCD)** so
  the load doesn't slip back when hauling stops.
- **Compound** systems (e.g. 3:1 on 3:1 = 9:1) for heavy loads or few haulers; **5:1**
  complex systems also common.
- Theoretical advantage is reduced by pulley friction; actual advantage is lower.
  More MA means more rope to pull and slower progress — choose to match haulers.
- Commands are standardised and spoken by one person (e.g. "haul", "stop", "set") —
  agree before you start.

## 5. Litter work
- A basket litter (Stokes-type) with the casualty packaged and secured, helmet and eye
  protection on them, and a vertical or horizontal bridle as the terrain demands.
- **Litter attendant(s)** on adjustable tethers guide it over edges and obstacles;
  in low-angle terrain several attendants carry weight while the system provides
  backup.

## Gotchas
- The edge transition is where most problems happen — plan who does what before the
  load goes over.
- Mixing devices and techniques from different team systems mid-operation.
- Cold, wet or frozen rope behaves differently in friction devices.

## Verify
- [ ] Main and belay independent; no single point of failure.
- [ ] Anchors assessed and angle kept small; edge protection in place.
- [ ] Whistle test done by a second person before every load.
- [ ] MA system includes progress capture; commands agreed.
- [ ] Casualty packaged and attendant tethered before going over the edge.
