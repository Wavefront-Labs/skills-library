# Avalanche companion and team rescue

**Goal:** find and dig out a buried person fast enough for them to survive. Companions
on the scene are the victim's best chance; organised teams arrive later and mostly
handle longer searches and evacuation. Get hands-on training (avalanche courses,
regular transceiver practice) — this is a reference, not a substitute.

## 1. Why speed decides everything
Survival-curve studies (Brugger and Falk, from Swiss and Canadian data) put survival at about 93% for people dug out within 15 minutes of complete burial, falling to about 25% beyond 45 minutes; a later Austrian and Swiss study found an inflection near 35 minutes. Asphyxia is the main early cause of death; later deaths come from hypothermia or trauma. Waiting for help before searching is usually fatal. Percentages vary by study and region.

## 2. Before you search
- **Scene safety:** is there hang-fire (unreleased snow above)? Post a lookout if one
  is spare. A second burial helps no one.
- **Last-seen point:** note where the person was caught and where they disappeared;
  the debris downslope of that line is the primary search area.
- Switch every transceiver in the group to **search** (one forgotten in transmit mode
  confuses the search). Call for help if it doesn't delay the search (or assign someone).
- Do a quick visual/audible check for surface clues (glove, ski) — mark them.

## 3. Transceiver search (457 kHz)
1. **Signal search:** move through the debris at search-strip widths per your device's range until you pick up a signal.
2. **Coarse search:** follow the direction indicator and decreasing distance readings
   along the flux line — move quickly while the distance is large.
3. **Fine search:** when the distance reading is small, slow down, transceiver close to the snow surface, and
   **bracket** in a cross pattern to find the lowest reading.
4. **Pinpoint with a probe:** probe perpendicular to the slope surface in a spiral outward from the lowest reading. Leave the probe in on a strike.
Multiple burials: use your device's marking/flagging function, and know micro-strip
or three-circle methods for close burials.

## 4. Strategic shovelling
Digging usually takes longer than searching. Start downhill of the probe and dig in from the side rather than straight down. With several
diggers, use a **V-shaped conveyor**: one at the probe, others behind, rotating every
few minutes. Clear the airway first once you reach the head; protect from cold;
check breathing and treat. Note any air pocket — it matters for medical decisions.

## 5. Team rescue (outline)
Mountain rescue teams add **avalanche dogs**, **RECCO** reflector detection, organised **probe
lines**, medical personnel and helicopter evacuation. ICAR/IKAR recommendations
guide medical management of avalanche victims.

## Gotchas
- Probes used at angles miss victims; probe perpendicular to the surface.
- Transceiver interference from phones and electronics: keep them away from the searching device by the distance your manufacturer states.

## Verify
- [ ] Group checks transceivers in transmit mode at the trailhead.
- [ ] Everyone carries transceiver, shovel and probe — and has practised.
- [ ] You can complete a single-burial search-and-probe drill to a target time.
- [ ] Shovelling started downslope with a conveyor when enough people are present.
