# Collapse-structure triage, marking and void search

**Goal:** find and reach people trapped in collapsed buildings while managing the very
real risk of secondary collapse. Reference for USAR responders and students; the
**INSARAG Guidelines** (international) and FEMA US&R / national doctrine set the
detailed standards, and actual operations require team training and a structural
specialist.

## 1. Collapse patterns and survivable voids
Survivable spaces form in recognised patterns (FEMA US&R):
- **Pancake:** floors stack; small voids near columns, furniture and appliances.
- **Lean-to:** one exterior wall fails, leaving the floor supported at one end only; triangular voids form beneath it.
- **V-shape:** an interior supporting wall or column fails; voids form beneath the two suspended pieces.
- **Cantilever:** a variant of pancake in which a floor projects unsupported from the debris pile — very unstable.
Concrete frames, unreinforced masonry and light timber collapse differently;
structural engineers on the team assess which voids are worth the risk.

## 2. Assessment, search, rescue (ASR) levels
INSARAG organises work in levels (ASR 1 wide-area assessment → ASR 2 sector assessment, called worksite triage assessment in Vol. II Manual B → ASR 3 rapid search and rescue → ASR 4 full search and rescue → ASR 5 total coverage search and recovery). Levels are defined as sequential but teams may be assigned them in any order; check the current Guidelines. Triage is about **savable lives per hour of
effort**: a building with known live victims and accessible voids is prioritised over a
heavier collapse with no signs of life.

## 3. Marking systems
Markings tell every later team what's been done and what's dangerous:
- **Worksite/structure marking** (INSARAG: a 1 m square marked G or N (go or no-go) with the team name and start and end times; FEMA US&R uses its own structure-marking system).
- **Victim marking:** location and status of known/potential victims (INSARAG uses L for live and D for dead, crossed out when removed).
- **Hazard** markings for gas, structural instability, hazmat.
Learn the system your team and the coordinating authority use; mixing systems at one
incident causes confusion.

## 4. Void search
- **Call-out and listen:** periods of silence (all machinery off), call out, tap and
  listen for responses.
- **Canines:** live-find dogs indicate scent sources; confirm with a second dog where
  possible because scent pools and drifts through rubble.
- **Technical search:** acoustic/seismic listening devices, search cameras on
  telescoping poles, thermal imagers; some teams carry radar-based life detectors.
- Mark and report each confirmed or suspected victim location.

## 5. Reaching victims
- **Shoring and cribbing** stabilise the route before entry: box cribbing under loads,
  raker and vertical shores — the structural specialist decides type and placement.
- **Breaching and breaking** reinforced concrete is slow; teams prefer to tunnel via
  existing voids and to lift with air bags or cribbing incrementally ("lift an inch, crib an inch").
- **Medical:** involve the medical team early for trapped casualties and follow your medical director's protocol.

## 6. Safety
Safety officer, structural monitoring (crack gauges, survey instruments) with a clear
**evacuation signal** understood by every team (agree it at the briefing), utilities isolated, dust and asbestos protection, rotation for fatigue,
and accountability of everyone in the hot zone.

## Gotchas
- Spontaneous rescuers (neighbours) often save most early survivors; organise them
  safely rather than chasing them off.
- Aftershocks after earthquakes — re-assess after each significant one.
- Removing a "small" piece of debris can be removing a load path.

- [ ] Every victim indication confirmed (second dog or device) and logged.
