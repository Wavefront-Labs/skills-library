# Handwriting comparison and alteration examination

**Goal:** answer the specific question asked about a disputed document — who wrote it,
whether the signature is genuine, whether it was altered, added to or produced on a
particular device — with a graded opinion that reflects the quality of the material.

## 1. Define the question and protect the original
- Write the question precisely: "Did the account holder write the signature on the
  cheque?" is examinable; "Is the document real?" is not.
- **Originals, not copies.** Photocopies and scans lose pressure, pen-lift, line quality
  and indentation evidence. Opinions on copies are limited and must say so.
- Handle with clean hands or gloves, no folding, no paper clips or sticky notes; don't
  write on top of the item (you create indentations). Examine for indented writing
  **before** any chemical treatment for fingermarks — ninhydrin and other reagents can
  destroy the ESDA result. Agree the sequence with the fingerprint lab.

## 2. Specimens (the usual weak point)
- **Collected (course-of-business) specimens:** writing made in normal life, ideally
  contemporaneous with the questioned item (within a couple of years), same style
  (signature vs text, block capitals vs cursive), similar writing conditions.
- **Request specimens:** dictated (not copied) text containing the questioned words,
  repeated several times on separate sheets, removed as each is completed, with similar
  pen and paper; seated and, if relevant, standing. Request writing may be disguised —
  collected writing guards against that.
- Adequacy: enough to show the writer's **range of natural variation**. Many examiners
  look for 10–20+ signatures; insufficient specimens is the most common reason for an
  inconclusive.

## 3. Handwriting comparison method
1. **Questioned item first:** is it naturally written (fluent, varied pressure, tapered
   starts and ends) or does it show signs of simulation (tremor, blunt starts/ends, pen
   lifts in odd places, patching, slow drawn line quality)?
2. **Known writing:** establish the writer's habits and range of variation.
3. **Compare** features: letter construction and stroke order, proportions and height
   relationships, slant, spacing, baseline habits, connecting strokes, pen pressure
   pattern, embellishments. Class features (taught copybook style) carry little weight;
   individual habits carry the weight.
4. **Evaluate** similarities and differences: a single significant, repeated difference
   outside the writer's range weighs heavily against common authorship; similarities in
   pictorial form alone are what a simulator copies.
5. Consider **alternative explanations**: disguise, illness, age, injury, intoxication,
   writing surface, or auto-forgery (the writer later denying their own signature).

## 4. Alterations and additions
- **VSC (video spectral comparator):** compare inks under different wavelengths;
  infrared absorption and luminescence differentiate inks that look identical; reveals
  obliterated or erased entries.
- **ESDA (electrostatic detection):** reveals indented writing from sheets written on
  above — powerful for diaries, notebooks, threatening letters.
- Oblique lighting for indentations and erasures; microscopy for line crossings
  (sequence of strokes is hard and often inconclusive).
- Printing processes: inkjet vs toner, page substitution, misaligned text, machine
  identification codes on some colour laser output (check current knowledge — presence
  and decoding vary by manufacturer).

## 5. Reporting
Use a graded scale, e.g. the SWGDOC/ASTM nine-point scale (identification, highly
probable, probable, indications, no conclusion, and the negative equivalents) or a
likelihood-ratio-style verbal scale as used in parts of Europe. State the limitations
(copies, limited specimens). Keep comparison charts for the court.

## Gotchas
- Signatures are the hardest: short, stylised and easy to simulate — expect lower-
  strength opinions than for extended text.
- A skilled tracing can be pictorially near-perfect; look for guide lines and indentations.
- Different signatures by the same person for different purposes (formal vs. scrawl)
  are common — match the specimen type to the questioned one.

## Verify
- [ ] Question written precisely; original examined (or limitation stated).
- [ ] Indentation/ESDA work done before destructive tests.
- [ ] Specimens comparable in time, style and conditions, enough to show variation.
- [ ] Simulation and disguise considered explicitly.
- [ ] Opinion given on a stated scale with limitations.
