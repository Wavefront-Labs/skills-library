# Medico-legal autopsy and cause and manner of death

**Goal:** establish, document and explain why and how a person died, in a way that
supports the investigation, can be independently reviewed, and states uncertainty
plainly. This is specialist-doctor work; this skill summarises the professional
standard for investigators, students and those who instruct or rely on pathologists.

## 1. Before the knife
- **Authority:** the coroner, medical examiner or procurator fiscal (jurisdiction-
  dependent) orders the examination and controls the body. Get the instruction and any
  consent/religious considerations in writing.
- **History and scene:** police briefing, scene photographs or attendance, medical
  records, whether there was resuscitation (CPR causes rib fractures and bruising that
  must not be read as assault), and who has handled the body.
- **Identification:** visual, fingerprints, DNA, dental, medical implants with serial
  numbers — record which was used.
- **Imaging:** full-body radiographs for gunshot, sharp-object fragments and infant
  deaths; **post-mortem CT (PMCT)** where available is strong for fractures and gas.

## 2. External examination
- Clothing examined and removed by the pathologist with the scene examiner present;
  trace evidence recovered first (tapings, nail scrapings/clippings, swabs).
- Document post-mortem changes: **rigor**, **livor (hypostasis)** — position and
  fixedness, which may show the body was moved — temperature and decomposition.
- **Each injury:** type, site (measured from anatomical landmarks, e.g. heel and midline),
  size, shape, orientation, colour, and associated features, photographed with a scale.
  Use precise terminology: an *incised wound* is longer than deep; a *stab wound* deeper
  than long; a *laceration* (tearing from blunt force) has bridging tissue and abraded
  margins — calling a laceration a "cut" misleads a court.

## 3. Internal examination and samples
- Systematic evisceration and examination of all organs; layer-by-layer neck dissection
  in a bloodless field (after removing the brain and chest organs) when compression is
  possible, to avoid artefactual bleeding.
- Samples: **femoral blood**, vitreous humour, urine, stomach contents, liver for
  toxicology (see the forensic-toxicology skill
  `defence-public-safety.forensic-science.forensic-toxicology.screening-confirmation-and-interpreting-results`);
  histology from major organs and injuries (wound age estimation is broad at best);
  DNA reference; microbiology if indicated.
- Retained organs and tissue: lawful authority and family notification as required.

## 4. Opinion
- **Cause of death**, in the conventional format: 1a (immediate disease/injury), due to
  1b, due to 1c; part 2 for contributing conditions. Keep it to a disease or injury, not a
  mechanism (avoid "cardiac arrest" alone).
- **Mechanism** (e.g. haemorrhage, cerebral hypoxia) explained in the report text.
- **Manner** (homicide, suicide, accident, natural, undetermined) — determined by the
  ME in US systems; in England & Wales the coroner/inquest reaches the conclusion and the
  pathologist doesn't certify manner. Know your system.
- **Time since death** estimates are wide. Body-cooling methods (e.g. Henssge nomogram)
  give ranges with stated confidence; rigor and livor are crude. Never give a single time.
- Interpret injuries cautiously: number, distribution, defence-type injuries, likely
  implement class, degree of force (graded qualitatively; many pathologists avoid force
  scales). Consider alternative explanations raised by the defence.

## 5. Reporting and court
- Report separates **findings** from **interpretation**; lists all materials relied on.
- Second post-mortems by a defence pathologist are common in homicide — keep photographs,
  notes and retained samples organised for them.
- Stay within expertise (e.g. defer to neuropathology, paediatric pathology, odontology).

## Gotchas
- Resuscitation and transport artefacts; post-mortem animal predation; decomposition
  mimicking bruising.
- Hypostasis in the neck/face can resemble congestion of asphyxia.
- Confirmation bias from the police narrative — record findings before reading strong
  case theories into them.

## Verify
- [ ] Legal authority, identification method and history documented.
- [ ] Every injury described with site, measurements, terminology and scaled photographs.
- [ ] Trace and toxicology samples taken and continuity recorded.
- [ ] Cause of death in 1a/1b/1c format; manner handled per jurisdiction.
- [ ] Time-of-death and force opinions given as ranges with limitations.
