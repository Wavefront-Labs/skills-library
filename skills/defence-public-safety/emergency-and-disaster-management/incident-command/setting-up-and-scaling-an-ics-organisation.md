# Setting up and scaling an ICS organisation

**Goal:** bring order to an incident from the first arriving unit to a multi-agency
operation: one clear command, objectives everyone works to, and an organisation that
grows and shrinks with the incident. Terms below follow US NIMS/ICS; equivalents are
noted (UK JESIP, Australia AIIMS, Canada ICS Canada).

## 1. First on scene: establish command
1. **Size-up:** what happened, what's at risk (life, property, environment), what's
   needed, hazards to responders.
2. **Assume and announce command** with a name and location: "Main Street Command,
   Engine 1 assuming command, command post at north lot." Every incident has an
   incident commander (IC) from the start — even if it's one person.
3. Record on an **ICS 201** (incident briefing): situation map, objectives, actions
   taken, current organisation, resources assigned/requested.
4. Set **objectives** that are specific and measurable ("evacuate the 3 blocks east of
   the leak by 14:00"), in priority order: life safety → incident stabilisation →
   property/environment.

## 2. Build only what you need
- The IC holds every function not delegated. Delegate when workload or span demands.
- **Command staff:** Public Information Officer, Safety Officer, Liaison Officer.
- **General staff (sections):** Operations, Planning, Logistics, Finance/Administration
  (plus Intelligence/Investigations where relevant).
- **Span of control:** 3–7 subordinates per supervisor, **5 optimal**. Past 7, add a
  layer: divisions (geographic), groups (functional), branches (when divisions/groups
  exceed span). Resources combine into **strike teams** (same kind) or **task forces**
  (mixed).
- **Unity of command:** each person reports to one supervisor. Common terminology — no
  agency codes on shared radio channels.

## 3. Unified command (multi-agency or multi-jurisdiction)
- Agencies with legal responsibility or significant resources share command at **one
  command post**, set **one set of objectives**, and produce **one IAP**. Each keeps
  authority over its own resources; Operations is still led by **one** Operations
  Section Chief.
- UK equivalent: co-located commanders working the **JESIP principles** (co-locate,
  communicate, coordinate, jointly understand risk, shared situational awareness) using
  the Joint Decision Model; strategic/tactical/operational levels.
- Agree early: who speaks to media, the operational period, and the radio plan.

## 4. The planning cycle ("Planning P")
For incidents beyond one operational period:
1. IC/UC develop or update **objectives** (ICS 202).
2. **Command and general staff meeting** — priorities, constraints.
3. **Tactics meeting** — Operations and Planning turn objectives into work assignments
   (ICS 215) and resource needs; Safety reviews hazards (ICS 215A).
4. **Planning meeting** — approve the plan; Logistics confirms it can support it.
5. **IAP** prepared and approved by the IC (objectives, organisation, assignments ICS 204,
   comms plan ICS 205, medical plan ICS 206, maps).
6. **Operational period briefing**, then execute and assess; repeat.
Operational periods are set by the incident.

## 5. Transfer of command and demobilisation
- Transfer **face to face** where possible, with a briefing using the 201, and announce
  the change on the radio. The outgoing IC usually stays in a role.
- Plan demobilisation early (Planning Section's demob unit): release surplus resources,
  check-out, rest requirements, accountability.

## Gotchas
- Freelancing — resources self-deploying without checking in — breaks accountability.
  Enforce **check-in** (ICS 211) and staging.
- Command post too close to the hazard, or moving without telling anyone.
- Filling every box on the org chart for a small incident adds overhead with no benefit.
- Unified command fails when agencies set separate objectives in practice — write them down together.
- Keep unit logs (ICS 214); they're needed for cost recovery and inquiries.

## Verify
- [ ] Command was named, located and announced; ICS 201 started.
- [ ] Objectives are written, prioritised and measurable.
- [ ] No supervisor exceeds ~7 direct reports; each person has one supervisor.
- [ ] Multi-agency incidents have one set of objectives and one IAP.
- [ ] Check-in and demob tracking cover every resource.
