# Hazard risk assessment, plans and exercises

**Goal:** a jurisdiction or organisation that knows its realistic emergencies, has a
plan people have actually read, and has tested that plan before needing it. The cycle is
**assess → plan → train → exercise → improve**, repeated (reviewed on a set schedule and after every real event).

## 1. Hazard and risk assessment
1. **Identify hazards** in all classes: natural (flood, storm, heat, earthquake,
   wildfire), technological (industrial accident, utility failure, cyber outage),
   human-caused (terrorism, civil disorder), biological (pandemic, animal disease).
   Sources: historical event records, national risk registers, insurers, utilities.
2. **Write a scenario** for each priority hazard — a **reasonable worst case** (UK term)
   or a THIRA "threat/hazard context": time, location, scale. Plans built on vague
   hazards produce vague plans.
3. **Score likelihood and impact** (e.g. 1–5 each across health, economic, social,
   environmental dimensions) and plot on a matrix. Record the rationale for each score;
   the scores will be argued over and that's useful.
4. **Translate into capability targets:** for the scenario, what must you be able to do
   ("shelter 2,000 people within 12 hours")? Compare with current capability → **gaps**.
5. Publish a community risk register (where required by law) in plain language.

## 2. The emergency operations plan (EOP)
Structure commonly follows FEMA CPG 101 (Version 3.1, May 2025):
- **Base plan:** purpose, scope, situation and assumptions, concept of operations
  (how the jurisdiction moves from routine to emergency), organisation and
  responsibilities, direction/control/coordination, information collection, comms,
  administration/finance/logistics, plan maintenance, authorities.
- **Functional annexes:** things you do in most emergencies — warning, evacuation,
  mass care and sheltering, public information, resource management, damage assessment,
  continuity of government.
- **Hazard-specific annexes:** only what differs for that hazard (e.g. flood trigger
  levels, chemical release protective-action distances).
- Rules: write for the people who'll use it (checklists and trigger points beat prose);
  name **roles, not individuals**; include activation triggers and who can activate;
  reflect access and functional needs populations; version-control and distribute.
- Planning is a **team activity** — agencies that wrote a plan together know each other
  when it's used.

## 3. Exercises (HSEEP-style)
| Type | Format | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Seminar / workshop | Discussion, briefing | Orienting to a new plan |
| **Tabletop (TTX)** | Facilitated scenario discussion | Testing decisions, roles, gaps |
| Game | Simulated, rules-based, often teams | Decision consequences |
| Drill | Single function, hands-on | e.g. call-out cascade, EOC activation |
| Functional | Simulated field, real EOC/command | Coordination under time pressure |
| Full-scale | Real resources in the field | Integrated multi-agency capability |
Progress up the ladder — don't run a full-scale exercise on an untested plan.

**Design steps:** set 3–5 **objectives** tied to capabilities (SMART), pick the type,
build the scenario and injects (master scenario events list), recruit evaluators with
evaluation guides keyed to the objectives, brief players on scope and safety ("no-play"
areas, a code word to stop for a real emergency).

**Evaluation:** hot debrief immediately; structured **after-action report (AAR)** with
strengths and areas for improvement against each objective; an **improvement plan** with
owners and due dates. Track completion — the improvement plan is where most programmes fail.

## Gotchas
- Plans designed around agency boundaries instead of community needs (vulnerable
  residents, care homes, non-native speakers) fail on contact with reality.
- Assumptions ("power will be restored within 24 hours") must be explicit and tested.
- An exercise designed to succeed teaches nothing; build in stress.
- Lessons "identified" are not lessons "learned" until the plan or training changes.

## Verify
- [ ] Each priority hazard has a written scenario with scored, justified likelihood/impact.
- [ ] Capability gaps are listed with owners.
- [ ] EOP has base plan, functional and hazard annexes, triggers, roles and a review date.
- [ ] Exercises have objectives, evaluators and an AAR.
- [ ] Improvement-plan actions are tracked to closure.
