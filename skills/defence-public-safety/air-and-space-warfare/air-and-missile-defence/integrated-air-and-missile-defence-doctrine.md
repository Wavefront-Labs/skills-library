# Integrated air and missile defence doctrine

**Goal:** understand how a nation or force defends its airspace, as taught in
unclassified doctrine and staff-college history: the organising ideas, the decision chain
from detection to engagement, and the recurring failure modes. Specific system
performance, coverage, procedures and any discussion of defeating a real defence are
out of scope by design.

## 1. The building blocks
- **Active defence:** detect, identify and engage threats (fighters, surface-to-air missiles,
  guns, electronic means).
- **Passive defence:** reduce the effect of attacks that get through — warning, dispersal,
  hardening, camouflage, deception, rapid repair and recovery.
- **Attack operations** (offensive counter-air) that destroy launchers and aircraft before
  launch are doctrinally part of the same problem; pure defence is rarely sufficient.
- **Integration** is the point: sensors, shooters and command linked so that the whole is
  greater than separate batteries.

## 2. The founding example: the Dowding system (1940)
Radar (Chain Home), the Observer Corps, filter rooms, sector operations centres and
fighter squadrons were linked by telephone so a single tactical picture drove the release
of scarce fighters. Lessons still taught: **centralised picture, decentralised execution**;
economy of force (don't launch everything at every raid); resilience of the network
mattered more than any single radar.

## 3. The detect-to-engage chain (concept)
1. **Surveillance / early warning** — find tracks.
2. **Identification** — friend, foe, neutral, unknown; procedural (routes, corridors,
   timing) and positive (electronic IFF, visual) means combined.
3. **Decision** — under delegated authority and **weapon control status** (in broad terms:
   free / tight / hold, defining how restrictive the rules on engaging are).
4. **Engagement** and **assessment** (did it work, re-engage?).
Doctrine usually places one **area air defence commander** in charge of the picture and
the rules, working with the airspace control authority so friendly aircraft are not engaged.

## 4. Layering and the missile problem
- **Layered defence / defence in depth:** long-, medium- and short-range layers so a
  threat missed by one meets another; point defence for critical assets.
- **Ballistic missiles** are discussed in three phases — boost, midcourse, terminal — each
  with different opportunities and challenges. Cruise missiles and drones fly low and
  exploit terrain; counter-UAS is now a distinct sub-specialism.
- **Cost-exchange and magazine depth:** interceptors often cost far more than the cheapest
  threats; a defender can be exhausted by volume (**saturation**). Public analysis of recent
  conflicts (Ukraine since 2022, Middle East 2023–24) focuses heavily on this. Treat
  published interception rates cautiously — they are contested and often unverified.

## 5. Identification failures (the hardest lessons)
- **Iran Air 655 (1988)**, **MH17 (2014)** and **Ukraine International 752 (2020)**: civilian
  airliners destroyed by air defences. Common threads in official reports: stressed crews,
  ambiguous or misread data, airspace left open to civil traffic near active defences.
- **Patriot fratricides (2003)** against coalition aircraft drove changes in identification
  and operator authority.
These cases are why doctrine emphasises combat identification, airspace coordination and
clear delegation of authority.

## Gotchas
- Equating "number of launchers" with defence; integration and sensors matter more.
- Ignoring passive defence and recovery — airfield repair often decides air campaigns.
- Over-trusting claimed intercept percentages from any party.
- Forgetting civil aviation in planning; closure or deconfliction of airspace is a
  defence task.
- Uncertainty note: terms (e.g. "IAMD", weapon control status labels) vary by nation and
  edition; check the current national doctrine.
