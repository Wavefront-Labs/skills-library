# Air combat doctrine and the history of fighter tactics

**Goal:** understand air-to-air combat the way a staff college teaches it — why control
of the air matters, the enduring principles fighter communities rediscover in every war,
and how to read a historical air campaign critically. This is unclassified doctrine and
history, not a current tactics manual; modern sensor, weapon and procedure specifics are
deliberately out of scope.

## 1. Why air combat exists: control of the air
- Doctrine (NATO AJP-3.3, USAF counterair publications) frames air combat as a means to
  **control of the air**, graded roughly as *favourable situation → air superiority → air
  supremacy*. Fighters are a tool for that, not an end.
- **Offensive counter-air (OCA)** attacks the enemy air force at source and in the air
  (sweeps, escort, attacks on airfields); **defensive counter-air (DCA)** protects friendly
  forces and territory (combat air patrol, intercept). Most historical air-superiority
  campaigns were won as much on the ground (airfields, fuel, pilot training pipelines)
  as in dogfights — e.g. the attrition of Luftwaffe fighter pilots in 1944.

## 2. Enduring principles (the "rules" that keep coming back)
- **Boelcke's dicta (1916):** seek the advantage before attacking (height, sun, surprise);
  press home once committed; fire only at close range; keep sight of the opponent; attack
  from behind; turn to meet an attack; over enemy lines, remember the line of retreat; fight
  in groups. Paraphrased here — they are a useful first lens on any era.
- **Mutual support:** the pair is the basic fighting unit; the leader attacks, the wingman
  protects. The **vic** (three-ship V) gave way to the looser German **Rotte/Schwarm**
  ("finger-four"), copied by the RAF and USAAF after 1940 because it let every pilot
  search and manoeuvre.
- **Tactics answer an aircraft disadvantage:** the **Thach weave** (1942) let slower-turning
  US Navy fighters counter more agile opponents by crossing pairs so an attacker on one
  became a target for the other.
- **Surprise dominates:** across WWI–Korea, analyses repeatedly found most losses were to
  attackers the victim never saw. Situational awareness, not aerobatic skill, is the
  headline lesson.

## 3. Theory: energy and decision cycles
- **Energy–manoeuvrability (E-M) theory** (Boyd and Christie, 1960s) quantified fighter
  performance as specific excess power across speed and altitude; it reshaped how aircraft
  are compared and designed. Conceptually: manoeuvring trades energy, and the side that
  manages energy better keeps options open.
- **The OODA loop** (observe–orient–decide–act) generalised this into tempo — getting
  inside an opponent's decision cycle. It is widely cited; be aware critics argue it is
  often oversimplified into "act faster".

## 4. The missile-era debate and training reform
- 1950s–60s thinking assumed missiles would end the dogfight; some fighters were built
  without guns. Over North Vietnam, missiles underperformed against early expectations,
  identification rules often forced close-range engagements, and exchange ratios
  disappointed.
- The US Navy's **Ault Report** (1968) led to the Fighter Weapons School ("Top Gun"); the
  USAF created **Red Flag** (1975) after studies suggested survival odds rose sharply after
  the first ~10 combat missions — so give pilots those missions in training. Exact figures
  quoted vary by source; treat specific percentages cautiously.
- **BVR vs WVR:** later conflicts (1991 Gulf War, 1982 Bekaa Valley) are often cited for
  long-range engagements and integrated command and control, but analysts disagree on how
  far they generalise to a peer opponent. Present it as a live debate, not a settled answer.

## 5. Reading an air-combat case study
Ask: what was the **control-of-the-air objective**? What were relative **training and
pilot pipelines**? What role did **ground control/early warning** play (e.g. the Battle of
Britain's Dowding system)? What were the **rules of engagement**? Were loss claims
cross-checked against the other side's records? (Overclaiming is endemic — wartime claims
often exceed verified losses by 2–3×.)

## Gotchas
- Treating kill counts and claims as fact; always compare with the opponent's loss records.
- Over-crediting aircraft performance and under-crediting training, numbers and C2.
- Reading a single conflict's lessons (one theatre, one opponent) as universal.
- Confusing doctrine-level principles with current tactics — the latter are classified and
  change with technology; this skill does not and should not cover them.
