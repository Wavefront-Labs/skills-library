# Close air support doctrine and air–ground integration

**Goal:** understand close air support (CAS) as joint doctrine defines and teaches it —
why it needs unusually tight integration, who controls it, how the risk of hitting friendly
troops is managed, and what a century of practice has taught. This is unclassified
doctrine; it deliberately omits procedural detail (brief formats line by line, release
parameters, specific weapon or sensor data) that belongs to qualified training.

## 1. What CAS is (and is not)
- Doctrine definition (JP 3-09.3, paraphrased): air action against hostile targets **in
  close proximity to friendly forces**, requiring **detailed integration** of each mission
  with the fire and movement of those forces.
- "Close" is defined by the need for integration, not a fixed distance.
- Contrast with **air interdiction**, which acts before the enemy reaches friendly forces and
  needs no such detailed integration (see
  `defence-public-safety.air-and-space-warfare.strike-operations.strategic-bombing-theory-targeting-doctrine-and-interdiction`).

## 2. The people
| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| Ground commander | Owns the fight and the decision to use CAS; accepts the risk |
| **JTAC** (joint terminal attack controller) | Qualified service member on the ground who directs CAS aircraft and grants clearance |
| **FAC-A** (airborne forward air controller) | Aircrew performing the controller role from the air |
| **TACP** (tactical air control party) | Air liaison team embedded with ground units; advises on air power |
| Air support operations centre / DASC | Allocates and routes CAS aircraft for the corps/division area |
| Fire support coordinator | Integrates air with artillery, mortars, naval fire |

## 3. Types of control (concept)
Joint doctrine distinguishes three types, essentially by **how much the controller must
see**: from the controller visually acquiring both aircraft and target for each attack,
through to cases where the controller grants clearance for multiple attacks without
observing each one. Choice depends on risk to friendly forces, the controller's ability to
observe, and the situation. Details and criteria are in JP 3-09.3; learn them there, not
from summaries.

## 4. Coordination and safety
- **Fire support coordination measures** — e.g. the fire support coordination line (FSCL),
  kill boxes, restricted fire areas — organise who may engage where without further
  coordination.
- **Airspace deconfliction** separates aircraft from artillery trajectories and other
  aircraft; doctrine treats it as a planning task, not an afterthought.
- **Positive identification** of targets and friendly positions is the core of fratricide
  prevention. A standardised CAS **brief** (often called the "nine-line") conveys target and
  friendly information in a fixed order so nothing is omitted; read-backs confirm it.
- **Risk acceptance** for attacks very close to friendly troops sits with the ground
  commander, not the aircrew.

## 5. History in five lessons
1. **WWI and interwar:** trench strafing showed promise but heavy losses; US Marines in
   Nicaragua and the Caribbean developed early air–ground coordination.
2. **WWII:** the German Stuka–Panzer combination (1939–40) and the Allied "cab rank" of
   on-call fighter-bombers with ground controllers (Italy, Normandy) showed integration,
   not aircraft type, made CAS work.
3. **Korea and Vietnam:** "Mosquito" and later Vietnam FACs in light aircraft proved the value
   of an airborne controller who knows the ground.
4. **Inter-service friction:** recurrent arguments (e.g. USAF vs Army over the A-10 and armed
   helicopters) about who owns and resources CAS.
5. **Afghanistan/Iraq:** widespread use of precision weapons with dispersed ground teams
   raised both effectiveness and the stakes of controller error; several fratricide and
   civilian-casualty incidents traced to coordinate or communication mistakes.

## Gotchas
- Treating CAS as "bombing near the front": the defining feature is integration and control.
- Assuming precision removes fratricide risk — most incidents are information errors, not
  weapon errors.
- Under-planning airspace and communications; CAS fails at the seams between services.
- Uncertainty note: exact role names and control criteria differ by nation and by doctrine
  edition; check the current national publication before relying on detail.
