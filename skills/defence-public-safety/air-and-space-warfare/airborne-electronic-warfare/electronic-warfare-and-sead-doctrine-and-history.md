# Electronic warfare and SEAD — doctrine and history

**Goal:** understand fighting in the electromagnetic spectrum from the air as unclassified
doctrine and history present it: the vocabulary, how EW fits into air operations, what
suppression of enemy air defences (SEAD) is trying to achieve, and what past campaigns
teach. This deliberately stays conceptual — no emitter parameters, system vulnerabilities,
jamming techniques or current procedures, which are classified and nation-specific.

## 1. The doctrinal vocabulary
| Division | Purpose | Plain-language example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electronic attack (EA)** | Use energy to degrade, neutralise or destroy enemy capability | Jamming, deception, decoys, anti-radiation attack |
| **Electronic protection (EP)** | Protect friendly use of the spectrum from either side's EW | Emission discipline, resilient comms, frequency planning |
| **Electronic warfare support (ES)** | Search for, intercept and locate emitters for immediate use | Threat warning, building the electronic order of battle |
Newer doctrine (e.g. US JP 3-85) folds EW into **electromagnetic spectrum operations
(EMSO)** alongside spectrum management, because friendly forces' own use of the spectrum
(radars, datalinks, GPS, comms) is congested and self-interfering.

## 2. Spectrum management and emission control
- Every emitter is both a capability and a signature. **Emission control (EMCON)** policies
  balance being seen against being able to see and talk.
- Friendly jamming can blind friendly systems; deconfliction ("fratricide in the spectrum")
  is a planning task, like airspace control.

## 3. SEAD and DEAD, conceptually
- **SEAD:** activity that neutralises, destroys or temporarily degrades enemy surface-based
  air defences so friendly air operations can proceed. Effects can be **destructive** or
  **disruptive** (forcing radars off or crews into caution).
- **DEAD** (destruction) is a common informal term for the destructive end.
- SEAD is usually part of a broader package (see
  `defence-public-safety.air-and-space-warfare.strike-operations.strategic-bombing-theory-targeting-doctrine-and-interdiction`)
  and is judged by whether friendly aircraft can operate at acceptable risk, not by systems
  destroyed.

## 4. History in six episodes
1. **Battle of the Beams (1940–41):** British scientists identified and countered German
   radio navigation beams — a founding story of ES feeding EA.
2. **"Window" (chaff) over Hamburg, July 1943:** strips of foil saturated German radar;
   both sides had delayed its use for fear of the other copying it — a classic EW dilemma.
3. **Vietnam, 1965 onwards:** Soviet-supplied SAMs drove the creation of dedicated
   **Wild Weasel** units and anti-radiation missiles; losses fell as the counter-SAM cycle
   matured, then rose again as the defence adapted.
4. **Bekaa Valley, 1982:** Israel's integration of decoys, ES, EA and strike against Syrian
   SAM sites is a standard staff-college case for combined-arms SEAD.
5. **Desert Storm opening night, 1991:** a large, coordinated opening package rapidly degraded
   Iraqi air defences; analysts caution the opponent was poorly integrated.
6. **Kosovo, 1999:** Serbian mobile SAMs largely survived by limiting emissions and moving;
   they shot down an F-117 and forced NATO to fly higher and longer — a lesson that
   suppression may be achieved without destruction, and that the reverse also holds.
Ukraine since 2022 is widely discussed as a contested-airspace case in which neither side
achieved durable suppression; much public detail is unverified.

## Gotchas
- Treating EW as a niche for specialists rather than an integral part of every air plan.
- Measuring SEAD by kills rather than by friendly freedom of action.
- Assuming technical superiority persists — every EW advance has drawn a counter, often
  within months.
- Forgetting friendly spectrum dependence (GPS, datalinks) as a vulnerability.
- Uncertainty note: national doctrines differ on terms (e.g. whether "EMSO" or "EW" is the
  umbrella); check the current edition.
