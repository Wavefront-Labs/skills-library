# Airlift, airdrop and air refuelling — planning principles

**Goal:** reason about military air mobility the way a logistics or air-operations staff
officer does: what moves by air and why, what limits how much you can move, how airdrop
and air refuelling fit, and what history shows. Numbers below are planning illustrations
from public sources, not a substitute for current national planning factors.

## 1. The mission set
- **Strategic (intertheatre) airlift:** between theatres — large jets, long range, prepared
  airfields.
- **Tactical (intratheatre) airlift:** within a theatre — shorter, rougher strips, airdrop,
  often in a threat environment.
- **Air refuelling:** extends range, endurance and payload of other aircraft; a force
  multiplier for every other air mission.
- **Aeromedical evacuation:** moving patients with in-flight medical crews; time-sensitive
  and often the most politically visible air mobility task.
- **Humanitarian and evacuation (NEO) airlift:** same skills, different rules and partners.

## 2. The arithmetic that decides plans
- **Throughput is usually limited by airfields, not aircraft.** The key constraint is
  **MOG (maximum on ground)** — how many aircraft an airfield can park, load/unload, fuel
  and service at once.
- Rough planning logic: daily tonnage ≈ (ground time slots per day × MOG) × average
  payload per sortie. If ground time is 3 h and MOG is 4, that is about 32 sorties/day per
  24 h — before weather, crew duty limits and maintenance. Adding aircraft beyond the MOG
  limit just creates queues.
- **Allowable cabin load (ACL)** falls with distance and runway/temperature limits — longer
  legs carry less. Air refuelling can trade tanker effort for payload.
- **Load planning:** standard pallets (US/NATO commonly the 463L, about 88 × 108 in),
  aircraft centre-of-gravity limits, and **dangerous goods** rules. Volume often fills before
  weight ("cubing out").
- **Airlift is scarce and expensive;** sealift moves most tonnage. Use air for speed,
  not bulk.

## 3. Airdrop (concept)
- Types: **personnel** (parachute assault), **container delivery** (bundles), **heavy drop**
  (vehicles, platforms), and **precision guided airdrop** (steerable parachutes, e.g.
  JPADS) which drops from higher altitude with better accuracy.
- Needs a surveyed **drop zone**, accurate wind information, recovery teams on the ground
  and acceptance that some loads are lost or damaged. Precision systems reduce, not remove,
  that loss.
- Airdrop sustains forces that can't be reached by road or runway — at a large premium.

## 4. Air refuelling (concept)
- Two main methods: **flying boom** (higher flow rate, used by many US Air Force aircraft)
  and **probe-and-drogue** (used by navies, many allied air forces and helicopters).
  Compatibility between tanker and receiver must be checked — it is a common
  coalition planning trap.
- Tankers are high-value, slow and must sit somewhere safe yet close enough; "tanker
  geometry" drives the whole air plan in long-range operations.

## 5. History in five cases
1. **Stalingrad (1942–43):** the Luftwaffe promised daily supply far beyond its capacity;
   weather, airfield loss and attrition made the pledge impossible — the classic warning
   against airlift promises made without arithmetic.
2. **Berlin Airlift (1948–49):** about 2.3 million tons in roughly 277,000 flights; Tunner's
   rigid scheduling, standard aircraft and airfield flow, not aircraft numbers, made it work.
3. **Dien Bien Phu (1954):** a garrison sustained only by air fell once artillery closed the
   airstrip — air lines of communication are only as strong as their airfields.
4. **Operation Nickel Grass (1973):** US resupply of Israel showed strategic airlift's
   political speed and its dependence on staging and overflight rights.
5. **Kabul (2021):** around 120,000+ people evacuated in about two weeks through one
   contested airfield — MOG, security and processing, not aircraft, set the pace.

## Gotchas
- Planning by aircraft count instead of airfield throughput (MOG).
- Forgetting crew duty limits, maintenance and weather margins.
- Ignoring diplomatic clearances and overflight rights for strategic legs.
- Assuming tanker/receiver compatibility in coalitions.
- Uncertainty note: the pallet size and historical totals are approximate public figures;
  national factors and current aircraft data should be taken from current publications.
