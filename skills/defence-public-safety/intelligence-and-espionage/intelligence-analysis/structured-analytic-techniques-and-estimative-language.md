# Structured analytic techniques and estimative language

**Goal:** turn incomplete, contradictory reporting into a judgement a decision-maker
can act on — explicit about what you think, how likely it is, how sure you are, and
what would change your mind. Usable for national-security, police, corporate and
humanitarian analysis alike.

## 1. Frame the question
Rewrite the customer's request as a precise analytic question with a time horizon:
not "what about Country X's army?" but "Will X launch a ground offensive across the
border in the next 90 days?" A badly framed question is the commonest root failure.

## 2. Key assumptions check (do it first)
List every assumption the current view depends on ("X's leader is rational in our
sense", "the source has access", "nothing has changed since last year"). For each:
why do we believe it, what would make it wrong, has it been wrong before? Iraq WMD
(2002) and many surprise attacks trace to unexamined assumptions, not missing data.

## 3. Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH)
1. List all reasonable hypotheses, including deception and "something else".
2. List evidence and arguments (including absence of expected evidence).
3. Build a matrix: for each item, is it Consistent, Inconsistent or Not applicable to
   each hypothesis?
4. Focus on **diagnostic** evidence — items that discriminate between hypotheses. Evidence
   consistent with everything tells you nothing.
5. Favour the hypothesis with the **least inconsistent** evidence, not the most consistent.
6. Test sensitivity: which few items, if wrong (fabricated, misread), would flip the result?
Gotcha: ACH done alone mainly documents your bias; run it with colleagues who disagree.

## 4. Other techniques worth having
- **Indicators and warning:** pre-agree observable signposts for each scenario and monitor
  them, so you notice change rather than rationalise it.
- **Devil's advocacy / Team A–Team B / red teaming:** formal challenge to the line.
- **Premortem:** assume the assessment proved wrong a year on; explain why.
- **Scenarios:** for genuinely uncertain futures, two key drivers on axes yield four
  plausible futures to plan against.

## 5. Bias traps
Confirmation bias, mirror-imaging (assuming the adversary thinks like you), anchoring
on the first estimate, layering (judgements built on earlier unverified judgements),
groupthink, and **vividness** (one dramatic report outweighing dull base rates).
Heuer's central point: knowing about biases does not remove them; structure does.

## 6. Estimative language
Sherman Kent found in the 1950s that readers interpreted "serious possibility" as anything
from ~20% to ~80%. Use a fixed yardstick and publish it with the product. The UK PHIA
yardstick (approximate): remote <5%, highly unlikely 10–20%, unlikely 25–35%, realistic
possibility 40–50%, likely/probably 55–75%, highly likely 80–90%, almost certain >95%.
ICD 203 uses a similar seven-band scale. Gaps between bands are deliberate.
- **Likelihood ≠ confidence.** "We assess with *low confidence* that an attack is *likely*"
  is coherent: likely is the judgement; low confidence reflects thin or uncorroborated sourcing.
- Avoid "could", "may", "possibly" — they commit to nothing and cannot be wrong.

## 7. Writing and briefing
**BLUF**: bottom line up front in one or two sentences. Then key judgements (each with
likelihood and confidence), the basis (source types, not sensitive identities), key
assumptions, alternatives considered, intelligence gaps, and indicators to watch.
Separate fact from judgement visibly. In oral briefings, lead with the judgement and
expect "how do you know?" — have the source basis ready.

## Verify
- [ ] The analytic question is specific and time-bound.
- [ ] Key assumptions are written down and each was challenged.
- [ ] At least three hypotheses (incl. deception) were tested against diagnostic evidence.
- [ ] Every judgement carries a yardstick term and a separate confidence level.
- [ ] The product names gaps and indicators that would change the assessment.
