# Authorising and governing a surveillance operation

**Goal:** understand covert surveillance by police and security services as a governed
discipline — the legal tests, accountability structure, records and oversight that make
it lawful and its product usable. This is the part owned by authorising officers,
supervisors, analysts, reviewers and prosecutors. Field techniques for following or
observing people are taught only on accredited courses to vetted officers and are
deliberately excluded. For private-investigator surveillance under civil law see
`defence-public-safety.investigative-locating-work.investigative-surveillance.lawful-case-surveillance-planning-logs-and-admissible-video`.

## 1. Why governance is the core of the specialism
Covert surveillance intrudes on private life (ECHR Article 8; US Fourth Amendment).
Unauthorised surveillance makes evidence excludable, exposes officers and the agency to
liability, and has produced public scandals. Courts and commissioners scrutinise the
paperwork as closely as the product.

## 2. Authorisation categories (UK model, widely studied)
- **Directed surveillance:** covert, for a specific investigation, likely to obtain private
  information, not in residential premises or private vehicles. Authorised internally by a
  senior officer.
- **Intrusive surveillance:** inside residential premises or private vehicles (or equivalent
  devices). Needs senior authorisation plus prior approval by a Judicial Commissioner.
- **Property interference** (e.g. placing a device) is a separate authorisation under the
  Police Act 1997 / Intelligence Services Act 1994.
- Online monitoring of a person can amount to directed surveillance even when the
  material is public — see the OSINT skill
  `defence-public-safety.intelligence-and-espionage.open-source-intelligence.osint-collection-plan-source-evaluation-and-reporting`.
- **US contrast:** no single statute; Fourth Amendment case law governs. *Jones* (2012)
  held attaching a GPS tracker to a vehicle is a search; *Carpenter* (2018) required a
  warrant for historical cell-site location data. Physical observation in public is
  generally not a search, but technology-enhanced and prolonged monitoring increasingly is.
  Uncertainty: US doctrine here is still developing state by state.

## 3. The authorisation tests
The application must show: the **legitimate purpose** (e.g. preventing serious crime,
national security); **necessity** — why less intrusive methods will not achieve it;
**proportionality** — the intrusion balanced against the seriousness of the matter and the
expected value; and **collateral intrusion** on non-subjects (family, colleagues,
bystanders) with steps to minimise it. Confidential material (legal privilege, journalistic
sources, medical, religious) triggers higher authorisation levels. Authorisations are
time-limited, reviewed at set intervals, and **cancelled** as soon as no longer needed —
lapsed or never-cancelled authorisations are a recurring commissioner finding.

## 4. Accountability structure
- **Authorising officer** — independent of the investigation where practicable; owns the
  necessity/proportionality decision.
- **Operation/team leader** — runs the deployment within the authorisation's terms and
  stops if those terms are exceeded.
- **Loggist** — maintains the contemporaneous surveillance log.
- **Operators** — collect within the authorised scope only.
- **Analyst/investigating officer** — tasks against the investigation's requirements and
  assesses product.
Gotcha: authority creep. Watching a new person, location or method not in the
authorisation requires a fresh or amended authorisation before, not after.

## 5. Records and evidence
A contemporaneous, sequential, unaltered log (time, observation, who observed), with
corrections struck through and initialled, never erased. Imagery with continuity and
metadata. Under disclosure rules (England and Wales CPIA 1996; US Brady/Giglio) the
defence may be entitled to material, and sensitive methods may need public-interest
immunity applications — plan for that at the start, not at trial.

## 6. Risk and welfare
Written risk assessment for officers, public and subject; contingency plans; welfare
support for staff on long deployments. Ethics: surveillance of protest, journalists or
political activity carries special sensitivity and political risk.

## 7. Oversight
UK: Investigatory Powers Commissioner inspections and the Investigatory Powers Tribunal for
complaints. Elsewhere: judicial warrants, inspectors-general, parliamentary committees.
Keep records so an inspector can reconstruct every decision.

## Verify
- [ ] The surveillance type is correctly categorised and authorised at the right level.
- [ ] Necessity, proportionality and collateral intrusion are each argued specifically.
- [ ] Activity stayed within the authorised subjects, places and methods.
- [ ] Reviews happened on schedule and the authorisation was cancelled when done.
- [ ] Logs are contemporaneous and unaltered; disclosure handling was planned.
