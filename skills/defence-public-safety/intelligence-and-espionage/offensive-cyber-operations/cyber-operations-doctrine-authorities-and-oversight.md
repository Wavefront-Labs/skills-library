# Cyber operations: doctrine, authorities and oversight

**Goal:** explain offensive cyber operations as a mission area — how states and law
enforcement organise, authorise, constrain and evaluate them — for policy staff, lawyers,
planners, students and defenders who need to understand the adversary's frame. This is a
policy and doctrine overview. It deliberately contains no intrusion technique, tooling or
targeting method. Securing systems is in `computing-software.cybersecurity-defensive.*`.

## 1. Terms and mission types
- **Cyberspace operations** (US JP 3-12) split into defensive (DCO), offensive (OCO) and
  network operations (DODIN ops). UK usage: "offensive cyber" by the National Cyber Force.
- **Cyber espionage** (access to collect) differs legally and politically from **cyber
  effects** (disrupt, deny, degrade, destroy). Same access, different authority and risk.
- Effects range from temporary denial and disruption of an adversary's command systems
  or propaganda distribution, through to physical damage (Stuxnet is the cited case).
- **Law-enforcement disruption** — court-authorised botnet and ransomware-infrastructure
  takedowns, typically multinational — is now a large share of publicly acknowledged activity.

## 2. Strategic concepts
- **Persistent engagement / defend forward** (US, from 2018): operate continuously against
  adversary capability rather than wait for attacks.
- **Responsible cyber power** (UK, 2023): emphasises legality, precision, accountability
  and "cognitive effect" (shaping adversary behaviour) over destruction.
- Cyber effects are typically slow to prepare, uncertain in outcome, and often single-use
  once revealed. They complement, rather than replace, kinetic and diplomatic tools.

## 3. Authorities (where most practitioners actually spend their time)
- **US:** military operations under Title 10; intelligence activity under Title 50
  (with covert-action findings and congressional notification); NSPM-13 (2018, details
  classified) delegated some approval authority; CYBERCOM and NSA share a "dual-hat"
  leader, a long-running debate.
- **UK:** NCF operates under the Intelligence Services Act 1994 (GCHQ/SIS) and Ministry
  of Defence authority, with ministerial approval and Investigatory Powers Commissioner
  oversight; police "equipment interference" falls under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016.
- Law-enforcement operations rest on judicial warrants (e.g. US Rule 41 amendments, 2016).
Uncertainty: much of the delegation detail is classified; public descriptions are partial.

## 4. International law and norms
- States broadly accept that the UN Charter and international humanitarian law apply
  (UN GGE 2015/2021). The **Tallinn Manual 2.0** (expert, non-binding) is the main reference
  for how: sovereignty, non-intervention, the threshold of "use of force" and "armed attack",
  and in armed conflict the principles of **distinction, proportionality, precaution**.
- Contested points: whether sovereignty is a standalone rule, when espionage becomes a
  violation, and due diligence obligations. Different states have published differing positions.
- Voluntary peacetime norms include not attacking critical infrastructure serving the public
  and not targeting emergency-response teams.

## 5. Risks planners must weigh
- **Collateral and cascading effects:** NotPetya (2017) spread far beyond its apparent
  target, causing global commercial losses; Stuxnet escaped its intended environment.
- **Proliferation:** released tools can be captured and repurposed (the 2017 leak of
  agency tools, later used in WannaCry).
- **Attribution and signalling:** effects may be misread, attributed to the wrong actor, or
  provoke escalation; public attribution is a political act with its own evidentiary
  standard.
- **Equities:** using an access for effects may destroy intelligence collection from it;
  vulnerability retention vs disclosure (US Vulnerabilities Equities Process) is a related trade-off.

## 6. Oversight and accountability
Legal review of each operation, ministerial or presidential approval tiers, legislative
committee notification, inspectors-general and commissioners, and post-operation
assessment of effects versus intent. Evaluate claims of success sceptically: measuring
cyber effects is hard and public accounts are often curated.
