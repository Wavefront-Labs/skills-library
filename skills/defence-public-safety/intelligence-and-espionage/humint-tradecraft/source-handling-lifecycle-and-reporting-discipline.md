# HUMINT: the source lifecycle and reporting discipline

**Goal:** understand human-source intelligence the way intelligence-studies courses and
unclassified doctrine teach it: the stages a source relationship goes through, the
failure modes that have burned services historically, and the reporting discipline that
makes a human's account usable by an analyst. Operational tradecraft (meeting methods,
covert communications, cover) is taught only inside vetted services and is out of scope.

## 1. What HUMINT is (and is not)
HUMINT is information obtained from people: debriefs of travellers, refugees and defectors,
liaison exchanges with partner services, military interrogation of detainees under the law
of armed conflict, overt elicitation by attachés, and clandestine agents. Most HUMINT by
volume is overt or semi-overt; clandestine agent running is the small, high-cost tip.
In law enforcement the same discipline is called informant or CHIS handling — see
`defence-public-safety.criminal-investigation.confidential-source-management.registering-and-handling-a-confidential-informant`.

## 2. The lifecycle (doctrinal model)
1. **Spotting** — identifying people with *access* to the information requirement.
   Access is the first test; enthusiasm without access is worthless.
2. **Assessment** — motivation, vulnerabilities, reliability, security risk, and whether
   the person may already be working for someone else.
3. **Development** — a relationship built over time; most prospects are dropped here.
4. **Recruitment** — an explicit, understood agreement. Ambiguous "recruitments" are a
   classic source of later disputes and false reporting.
5. **Handling** — tasking against requirements, debriefing, welfare, payment records,
   security review. Tasking should come from validated requirements, not handler curiosity.
6. **Termination** — planned resettlement or closure. Badly terminated sources become
   security risks or public grievances.

## 3. Why people cooperate
- **MICE** (Money, Ideology, Coercion/Compromise, Ego) is the Cold War shorthand. Coercion
  produces the least reliable sources and the worst ethical exposure.
- **RASCLS** (Reciprocation, Authority, Scarcity, Commitment, Liking, Social proof), adapted
  from Cialdini, reframes cooperation as ordinary social influence — more realistic for
  most overt and liaison relationships.
- Gotcha: motivation shifts over time. Reassess it; a source motivated by ideology may
  later be motivated by money, grievance or fear, and their reporting changes with it.

## 4. Validation: the core professional duty
Historic HUMINT failures are mostly validation failures, not collection failures:
- **Fabricators** — "Curveball" (Iraqi WMD, 2002–03): a single uncorroborated source never
  met by the consuming service, whose reporting fed a national estimate.
- **Doubled sources** — the Cuban double-agent revelations (1987) showed much of a
  service's Cuban network had been controlled by Havana for years.
- **Dangle / walk-in risk** — volunteers may be offered deliberately.
Checks taught in the open literature: corroboration from independent collection (imagery,
SIGINT, open source), testing with known-answer questions, consistency over time, plausibility
of claimed access, and whether reporting conveniently matches what the customer wants.

## 5. Debriefing and report hygiene
- Separate **what the source saw**, **what the source was told** (sub-source chains), and
  **the source's opinion**. Analysts discount each differently.
- Record source access to *this* piece of information, not just general access.
- Use reliability/credibility grading (e.g. NATO/Admiralty A–F for source reliability,
  1–6 for information credibility). Grade the source and the information separately; a
  reliable source can relay a rumour.
- Do not let the handler "improve" wording; the analyst needs the source's words.
- Protect the source in dissemination: sanitise identifying detail; many reports are
  burned by unique facts only one person could know.

## 6. Ethics, law and oversight
Domestic HUMINT is regulated (UK RIPA/CHIS Code with Investigatory Powers Commissioner
oversight; US Executive Order 12333 and Attorney General guidelines). Interrogation of
detainees is bounded by the Geneva Conventions and, in US doctrine, FM 2-22.3's approved
approaches. Duty of care to sources — including after termination — is now treated as
an obligation, not a courtesy. Uncertainty: oversight regimes differ widely by country
and change; check current law in your jurisdiction.
