# Responding to calls and making field contacts

**Goal:** handle a call for service or a self-initiated contact so that it is safe,
lawful at every step, and documented well enough to survive review by a supervisor,
a court and the public. This is academy-level procedure; your agency's policy and your
jurisdiction's law always override it.

## 1. Receiving the call
- Acknowledge on the radio, confirm the address and the nature code. Ask dispatch for
  what you need *before* arrival: weapons mentioned, history at the address (prior calls,
  warnings), number of people involved, whether the caller is still on the line.
- Decide whether you need a second unit now, not after you arrive. Domestic disputes,
  weapons calls, crimes in progress and mental-health crises are normally two-officer
  calls by policy.
- Plan the route and the approach point. For most calls you park short of the address
  and walk the last stretch so you can see the scene before the scene sees you.

## 2. Arrival and approach
- Tell dispatch you are on scene (location, not just "arrived"). Activate body-worn
  camera per policy — usually on dispatch or before contact, not after something goes wrong.
- Observe before acting: who is present, hands, exits, vehicles, dogs, anything that
  doesn't fit the call.
- Use distance and cover as default positioning; avoid standing in doorways and avoid
  silhouetting yourself against light.
- With a partner, agree roles: one officer talks (contact), the other watches (cover).

## 3. The three tiers of encounter (US model; equivalents exist elsewhere)
| Tier | Threshold | What you may do |
|---|---|---|
| Consensual encounter | none | Talk, ask questions; person is free to leave and should be able to tell |
| Investigative detention ("Terry stop") | reasonable, articulable suspicion of crime | Brief detention to confirm or dispel suspicion; frisk only with separate suspicion of armed and dangerous |
| Arrest | probable cause | Custody, search incident to arrest, rights advisement before custodial questioning |

Gotchas:
- The tier is judged objectively from what a reasonable person would feel, not from
  what you intended. Blocking someone's path, keeping their ID, or a commanding tone can
  turn a "consensual" chat into a detention without the needed suspicion.
- Articulate suspicion in specifics (time, place, behaviour, information received),
  never "furtive" or "nervous" alone.
- In the UK, stop and search powers (e.g. PACE s.1) carry their own grounds and
  recording duties (the GOWISELY explanation); in Canada, investigative detention follows
  *R v Mann*. Check your jurisdiction — the table above is the US frame only.

## 4. Running the contact
- Introduce yourself and the reason for the contact; most complaints stem from people
  never being told why they were stopped.
- Separate parties in disputes, get each account, then compare.
- Keep the scene stable before investigating: medical needs first, then safety, then evidence.
- Run names and vehicles through dispatch or the in-car terminal; treat a warrant hit
  as unconfirmed until the originating agency confirms it.

## 5. Clearing and documenting
- Clear with a disposition code; say if a report will follow.
- Write the report the same shift. Record what you saw and heard, what you did, *why*
  (the legal basis at each step) and the outcome. Use direct quotes for key statements.
- Note body-camera status and any gaps.

## Common failure modes
- Tunnel vision on the call type ("noise complaint") and missing what is actually there.
- Approaching straight to the door of a weapons call alone.
- Escalating from consensual encounter to detention without noticing, then losing the evidence.
- Reports that state conclusions ("subject was aggressive") instead of observed behaviour.

## Verify
- [ ] Before contact you can name the encounter tier and the legal basis for it.
- [ ] Backup decision made on information, before arrival.
- [ ] Report states facts, actions, and the reason for each escalation.
- [ ] Body-camera activated per policy and its status recorded.

*Uncertainty:* thresholds and terminology are summarised from US case law (Terry v.
Ohio) with brief pointers to UK/Canadian equivalents; local policy on camera activation
and two-officer calls varies widely.
