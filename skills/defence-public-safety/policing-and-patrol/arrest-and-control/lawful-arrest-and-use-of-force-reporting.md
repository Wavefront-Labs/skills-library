# Lawful arrest and use-of-force reporting

**Goal:** make an arrest that is lawful in its grounds, proportionate in the force used,
safe for the person in custody afterwards, and documented so that a reviewer can see why
every decision was made. Physical defensive-tactics skills are taught hands-on by
certified instructors and are deliberately not described here.

## 1. Grounds first
- **US:** probable cause that the person committed an offence (warrant, or warrantless
  for felonies and in-presence misdemeanours depending on state law).
- **England & Wales:** PACE s.24 — reasonable grounds to suspect an offence *and* that
  arrest is *necessary* for a listed reason (e.g. to prevent harm, to allow prompt and
  effective investigation). Necessity is a separate test and is often what fails on review.
- Tell the person they are under arrest and why, as soon as practicable. Give the rights
  advisement (Miranda in the US before custodial interrogation; the caution in the UK on arrest).

## 2. The force standard
- **US:** *Graham v. Connor* (1989) — force is judged by "objective reasonableness" from the
  perspective of a reasonable officer on scene, considering severity of the crime, whether
  the person poses an immediate threat, and whether they are actively resisting or trying to flee.
  *Tennessee v. Garner* limits deadly force against fleeing persons.
- **UK:** force must be reasonable in the circumstances (Criminal Law Act 1967 s.3, common
  law), and is judged on necessity and proportionality.
- Agency policy is usually **stricter than the law** (de-escalation requirements, bans on
  chokeholds, warning before deploying certain tools). You are disciplined on policy, not just law.
- Force is a continuum that moves both ways: when resistance stops, force must stop.

## 3. De-escalation is part of the force decision
- Time, distance and cover create options; talking is a tactic, not a delay.
- Consider whether the person *can't* comply (medical crisis, intoxication, hearing
  impairment, language, mental illness) rather than *won't*.
- One officer gives commands; conflicting commands from several officers make compliance impossible.

## 4. After control: custody care
- Move the person off their chest into a recovery or seated position as soon as they are
  handcuffed. Prolonged prone restraint, weight on the back and exhaustion are associated
  with in-custody deaths (positional/restraint asphyxia).
- Check handcuffs for fit and double-lock per your training.
- Watch for medical red flags: difficulty breathing, sudden calm after extreme agitation,
  very high temperature, chest pain. Summon EMS early; "acute behavioural disturbance"
  is treated as a medical emergency in many agencies.
- Provide or summon medical aid after any significant force; many policies require it.
- **Duty to intervene:** officers present must stop excessive force by colleagues and report it.

## 5. The report
A good use-of-force report answers, in order:
1. What you were doing and why you were there.
2. What the person did (specific behaviour, words, and threat cues — not labels like "combative").
3. What you perceived and believed at each moment.
4. What force you used, in sequence, and how the person responded to each application.
5. When and how force ended, injuries to anyone, medical aid given.
6. Witnesses, body-camera footage, supervisor notification.
Write your own account before watching footage if policy allows, and note differences
honestly — memory under stress is incomplete and reviewers know it.

## Gotchas
- "He resisted" is a conclusion. "He pulled his right arm away and braced against the car" is evidence.
- Arrest grounds and search grounds are separate; a bad arrest taints the search incident to it.
- Copy-paste boilerplate across reports destroys credibility in court.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state the legal ground for arrest and (UK) the necessity criterion used.
- [ ] Force decreased as resistance decreased, and the report shows that sequence.
- [ ] Person moved out of prone, monitored, and medical aid considered/documented.
- [ ] Supervisor notified within policy timeframe.

*Uncertainty:* summary of US federal and England & Wales frameworks only; state, provincial
and agency policy differ materially (e.g. vascular-neck-restraint bans).
