# Planning and commanding a protest or event operation

**Goal:** let a lawful protest or large event happen safely, protect the rights of
participants and the public, and keep any disorder small — through planning, command
clarity and communication rather than force. This covers the planning and command
discipline; formation and unit tactics are taught in accredited public-order training.

## 1. Command structure
- **UK:** Gold (strategic) sets the strategy and tolerances; Silver (tactical) writes the
  tactical plan; Bronzes (operational) run geographic or functional sectors.
- **US:** Incident Command System (ICS) under NIMS — Incident Commander, Operations,
  Planning, Logistics, Finance/Admin sections; unified command with fire/EMS/event organisers.
- Whatever the model: one commander per level, written decision logs, and clearly delegated
  authority for specific decisions (e.g. who can authorise dispersal orders or containment).

## 2. Strategy rests on rights
- Start from the presumption that the gathering is lawful and must be **facilitated**
  (ECHR Articles 10 and 11 in Europe; First Amendment in the US with content-neutral
  time/place/manner limits).
- Any restriction must be lawful, necessary and proportionate, and recorded with reasons.
- Write the strategy as a short set of objectives, e.g. "facilitate the march; protect
  counter-protesters and public; prevent crime and disorder; minimise disruption".

## 3. Intelligence and planning inputs
- Organiser engagement: route, timings, numbers, stewarding, speakers, end point.
  Open, early dialogue is the most effective risk reducer.
- Community impact assessment: who else is affected (businesses, places of worship, transit).
- Historic data from similar events; open-source information on expected groups.
- Venue/route survey: pinch points, dead ends, construction, egress routes, medical points.
  Crowd density above about 4–5 people/m² moving becomes dangerous; build capacity from
  the narrowest point, not the widest.

## 4. Crowd psychology — why the approach matters
The Elaborated Social Identity Model (ESIM) research shows crowds are not inherently
irrational. Indiscriminate police action against a mostly peaceful crowd can unify it
against police and make a minority's violence seem legitimate to the majority. Practical
consequences:
- Differentiate: deal with the specific people causing harm, not the whole crowd.
- Communicate: explain what police are doing and why (PA, social media, liaison officers).
- Keep visible posture proportionate to the current risk; scale up only on need.

## 5. Police liaison / dialogue teams
Plain-clothed or distinctively marked officers whose only role is dialogue with organisers
and participants. They gather mood, defuse misunderstandings and carry messages both ways.
Don't use them for intelligence-gathering on individuals — doing so destroys their trust.

## 6. The written order
Use a standard format (UK: IIMARCH — Information, Intent, Method, Administration, Risk
assessment, Communications, Humanitarian issues; military-style SMEAC elsewhere). Include
tolerances (what behaviour will and won't trigger intervention), legal powers available and
who authorises them, medical plan, radio channels, and contingency triggers.

## 7. During and after
- Keep the decision log live with times and rationale.
- Hold a hot debrief the same day and a structured debrief within days: what was planned,
  what happened, why the difference, lessons for next time. Feed back to organisers.

## Gotchas
- Plans built on the worst-case group mislabel the whole crowd.
- Unclear authority for dispersal/containment decisions causes delays and legal exposure.
- Kettling/containment is lawful only in tightly limited circumstances (e.g. *Austin v UK*);
  plan welfare and release criteria if it's ever contemplated.

## Verify
- [ ] Strategy written, signed off and rights-based.
- [ ] Organiser engagement logged.
- [ ] Order includes tolerances, powers, authorisers and medical plan.
- [ ] Debrief completed and lessons recorded.
