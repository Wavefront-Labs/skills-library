# Running a SARA problem-solving project

**Goal:** stop answering the same calls at the same place by treating them as a *problem*
with causes you can change, usually with partners outside the police. SARA (Scanning,
Analysis, Response, Assessment) is the standard model taught in community and
problem-oriented policing.

## 1. Scanning — pick a real problem
A problem is a **recurring set of similar harmful events** that the community expects police
to handle. Good candidates come from:
- Call-for-service data: top addresses, repeat callers, repeat victims.
- Beat officer knowledge and community meetings.
- Partner data (housing, schools, licensing, hospital A&E assault data).
Define it narrowly: "thefts from cars in the station park-and-ride, weekday daytime" not
"car crime". Check that it's genuinely recurring and not a one-off spike.

## 2. Analysis — the step most projects skip
Use the **problem analysis triangle** (from routine activity theory): an offender, a target
or victim, and a place come together, *without* an effective controller on any side:
- **Handler** — someone who influences the offender (parent, probation officer).
- **Guardian** — someone who protects the target (owner, staff, CCTV monitor).
- **Manager** — someone responsible for the place (landlord, shop manager, council).
Questions to answer with data, not assumptions:
- When, where exactly, and how does it happen? Map it and chart it by hour/day.
- Who are the victims; is victimisation repeated? (Repeat victims are usually the best
  predictor of the next event.)
- What do offenders gain, and what makes it easy?
- Which place manager has leverage that hasn't been used?
Read the relevant Problem-Oriented Guide from the POP Center first — there is one for most
common problems and it saves weeks.

## 3. Response — situational and shared
Search widely before choosing; enforcement alone rarely lasts. Situational crime prevention
gives five families of response:
1. Increase the effort (target hardening, access control).
2. Increase the risk (natural surveillance, lighting, staffing).
3. Reduce the rewards (remove or mark property).
4. Reduce provocations (manage queues, closing times).
5. Remove excuses (clear rules, signage).
CPTED (Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design) applies these to place layout.
Assign each action to an owner — often a partner (council, landlord, business). Write a
short plan with dates.

## 4. Assessment — prove it
- Measure the same indicator as at baseline for a comparable period (account for seasonality).
- Check for **displacement** to nearby places/times — and for **diffusion of benefits**, which
  is more common in research than full displacement.
- Assess process too: was the response actually implemented as planned?
- If it didn't work, go back to Analysis, not straight to a new response.

## Gotchas
- Projects that jump from Scanning to Response are "solution-shopping" and usually fail.
- Arrest counts are outputs, not outcomes. Measure harm or incidents.
- Partners agree in meetings and then don't act; get commitments in writing with names.
- Officer rotation kills projects; document so a successor can continue.
- Community engagement should follow procedural-justice principles: voice, neutrality,
  respect, trustworthy motives. Treat residents as co-owners, not an audience.

## Verify
- [ ] Problem statement names the place, time, behaviour and harm in one sentence.
- [ ] Triangle analysis identifies at least one controller with unused leverage.
- [ ] Each response has an owner and a date.
- [ ] Before/after comparison with displacement check written up.

*Uncertainty:* model content is standard and well documented; local partnership structures
(e.g. UK Community Safety Partnerships) vary.
