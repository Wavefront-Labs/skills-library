# K9 team deployment, records and legal standards

**Goal:** deploy a police dog (tracking, building/area search, apprehension, detection) in a
way a court and a review board will accept, and keep the dog healthy. The handler–dog
training itself is a hands-on discipline taught by certified trainers; this skill covers the
professional framework around it.

## 1. Certification and records are the case
- A detection dog's alert is evidence only as good as its documented reliability.
  *Florida v. Harris* (2013, US): satisfactory performance in a certification or training
  programme can establish probable cause, but the defence may challenge the records.
- Keep, for every dog: initial certification and recertification dates (agency or national
  body), every training session (date, scenario, hides/aids used, result, false alerts and
  misses), and every field deployment and its outcome.
- Record field "unconfirmed alerts" honestly (residual odour can explain some); hiding them
  ruins credibility when discovered.
- Maintenance training standards are commonly set at a minimum number of hours per month
  (often ~16 h in US practice) — check your accrediting body.

## 2. Deployment decisions
- **Apprehension (patrol) dogs** are a use of force. Policy usually limits them to serious
  offences or people believed armed/violent, and requires a **loud, repeated warning**
  ("Police K9, come out or I'll send the dog") with time to comply, unless that would put
  officers at risk. Log the warnings and the time allowed.
- Consider bystanders, children and the subject's ability to hear and understand.
- Call the dog off as soon as the subject is under control; seek medical aid for any bite
  and photograph injuries. Bite reports go through the use-of-force review process.
- **Tracking:** protect the start point from contamination; get the time of the last sighting
  (track age matters); brief the cover officer who moves with the team.
- **Detection:** a sniff of a car's exterior during a lawful stop is not a search in US law
  (*Illinois v. Caballes*), but extending a stop to wait for a dog needs its own reasonable
  suspicion (*Rodriguez v. US*, 2015). A sniff at a home's front door is a search
  (*Florida v. Jardines*). Other countries treat sniffs differently — check local law.

## 3. Handler cueing (the "Clever Hans" problem)
Dogs read handlers. Research (e.g. Lit et al., 2011) has shown handler beliefs about where a
target is can increase false alerts. Mitigations:
- Regular **blind and double-blind** training where the handler doesn't know hide locations.
- Don't circle back repeatedly to the same spot in the field; document search pattern.
- Report the full alert behaviour you trained ("final response"), not an interpretation of
  "change of behaviour" alone.

## 4. Welfare and safety
- Heat kills police dogs in vehicles: use heat-alarm/auto-door systems, check them each shift,
  never rely on the A/C alone.
- Hydrate, rest and cool after work; watch for heatstroke signs (heavy panting, staggering,
  bright red gums) and treat as an emergency.
- Veterinary checks per schedule; record injuries. Plan retirement and rehoming.
- Know veterinary first aid basics and carry a kit.

## Gotchas
- Training logs written up weeks later are easy to impeach.
- Odour-imprinted dogs (e.g. cannabis) may need re-certification or retirement after legal
  changes in your jurisdiction.
- A dog bite without a recorded warning is often indefensible.

- [ ] Each apprehension deployment records warning, compliance time and call-off.
- [ ] Blind training sessions occurring regularly.
