# Response driving and pursuit risk decisions

**Goal:** get to an emergency without creating a second one, and treat every pursuit as a
risk decision that is reassessed continuously and ended early. Driving skills themselves are
taught in an Emergency Vehicle Operations Course (EVOC) or equivalent on a closed track.

## 1. The legal frame
- Emergency vehicle exemptions (exceeding limits, proceeding through red signals) apply only
  while using the required warning devices and only while driving with **due regard for the
  safety of all persons**. The exemption is not a defence to reckless driving.
- In the UK, exemptions are tied to the purpose (Road Traffic Regulation Act 1984 s.87,
  Road Traffic Act 1988 amendments) and to trained drivers; in the US, state vehicle codes
  set the conditions. Civil liability often turns on policy compliance.

## 2. Is this a true emergency?
Lights-and-siren response is justified when there is a real and immediate threat to life or
serious harm, or a serious crime in progress. Many calls (alarm activations, minor collisions
without injury) don't qualify under most policies. Research on EMS shows small time savings
from lights-and-siren on typical urban runs, with markedly higher crash risk — the trade is
rarely worth it for low-acuity calls.

## 3. Response-driving habits that prevent crashes
- **Intersections are where emergency vehicles crash.** Slow approaching any red or
  uncontrolled junction, be prepared to stop, and clear each lane one at a time — make eye
  contact with drivers; never assume they have heard the siren.
- Sirens are hard to hear in modern closed cars; change tones approaching junctions.
- Avoid overtaking on the right/nearside and passing stopped traffic on the side drivers will pull into.
- Speed: closing speed shortens decision time faster than you think. A useful self-check is
  whether you could stop within the distance you can see is clear.
- Radio and terminal use while driving fast is a distraction; let the passenger or dispatch handle it.
- Fatigue at end of a night shift degrades reaction time similarly to alcohol impairment.

## 4. Pursuits: a continuous risk assessment
Before initiating and at every moment after, weigh:
- **Seriousness of the offence** and the danger of the person remaining at large. Many
  policies now restrict pursuits to violent felonies; traffic offences and property crimes
  often do not justify one.
- **Known identity:** if you know who it is, you can arrest later.
- **Environment:** pedestrians, school zones, traffic density, weather, light, road type.
- **Driving of the suspect** and your own vehicle/training level.
- **Speeds** relative to conditions.
Communicate continuously: location, direction, speed, traffic, reason for pursuit. A
supervisor must monitor and can order termination.

## 5. Ending it
- Terminate when the risk to the public outweighs the need to apprehend now. Deactivate
  lights and siren, slow to the limit, turn off the route and confirm on the radio.
- Intervention techniques (tyre deflation devices, tactical contact manoeuvres) are
  policy-restricted and require specific certification; do not improvise them.
- Post-pursuit: adrenaline is high — the use-of-force risk at the end of a pursuit is well
  documented. Supervisors should be present for the arrest phase where possible.
- Every pursuit is reported and reviewed, including terminated ones.

## Gotchas
- Pursuit "tunnel vision" makes drivers miss the environment changing around them.
- Too many units joining; policy typically limits active units (often two).
- Treating termination as failure. Reviews treat early termination as good judgement.

## Verify
- [ ] Response mode matched to call acuity and policy.
- [ ] Every red-light/junction crossing cleared lane by lane.
- [ ] Pursuit justification and conditions radioed and recorded throughout.
- [ ] Termination criteria known before initiating.

*Uncertainty:* legal references are summary only; pursuit policies vary sharply between agencies.
