# Reading fire behaviour and coordinating interior attack

**Goal:** make the first 10 minutes of a building fire predictable: read what the
fire is doing, control air before adding it, put water where it changes the outcome,
and search without becoming a casualty. This is a reference for trained crews and
students; it never replaces your service's SOPs, live-fire training or officer direction.

## 1. Fire behaviour you must recognise
Modern compartment fires are usually **ventilation-limited** by the time crews
arrive: synthetic furnishings have consumed the available oxygen, and the room is
full of hot, unburned fuel (smoke). Any new air supply — a door you open, a window
that fails, a vent you cut — can drive rapid fire growth within seconds to a couple of
minutes. The UL FSRI and NIST experiments of the 2010s are the basis for this view;
NFPA 1700 (guide for structural fire fighting, first edition 2021) codifies it.

Read the smoke for **volume, velocity, density and colour** (VVDC):
- **Turbulent, fast, dark, thick** smoke pushing from openings = high heat, close to
  flashover or a flow path already established.
- Smoke **pulsing** in and out of gaps, or being drawn back in = ventilation-starved,
  backdraft risk; do not open up without water ready.
- **Rollover** (flames in the smoke layer overhead), a rapidly dropping neutral plane,
  and heat forcing you to the floor are flashover warnings. Get out or cool the gas layer now.
Colour alone is unreliable; velocity and density matter more.

## 2. Flow paths and door control
A **flow path** is the route between an inlet (fresh air in, often low) and an exhaust
(hot gases out, high). Anyone standing in the flow path between the fire and the
exhaust is in the most dangerous place in the building. Practical habits:
- **Close the door** behind you (or keep it partly closed with a crew member on it)
  until water is flowing on the fire; a closed door is the cheapest fire control there is.
- **Ventilation is coordinated, not automatic.** Horizontal or vertical ventilation is
  done on order, after the hose team is in position or water is on the fire.
- **Vent-Enter-Isolate-Search (VEIS):** enter a room through a window, *close that
  room's door first*, then search. Isolating the room is the point.

## 3. Interior attack basics
- Size up all sides you can (a 360° walk-round is standard where practical).
- Pick a line large enough for the fire load; stretch it fully before charging.
- Where conditions allow, a short burst of water from the exterior into the fire room
  ("transitional" or offensive-exterior attack) can reset conditions before entry —
  check whether your service permits it and when.
- Inside, cool the gas layer with short pulses, then hit the seat of the fire. Watch
  for steam burns from over-application in a small compartment.
- Keep crew integrity: in together, out together, on air limits (see the breathing
  apparatus skill `defence-public-safety.firefighting.breathing-apparatus.scba-air-management-and-use-discipline`).

## 4. Primary search
Search **closest to the fire first** (most at risk) and work out, or search from the
most likely victim locations (bedrooms at night, near exits, behind doors, under
windows). Maintain wall or hose contact, sweep with a tool, and report "primary search
negative/positive" for each area to command. Thermal imaging cameras help but can
miss a person under bedding or behind furniture.

## Gotchas
- Opening the front door to "see" is ventilation. Treat it that way.
- Wind-driven fires (wind into the fire room window) can make interior hallways
  untenable; consider attack from the upwind side or wait for exterior control.
- Basement and attic fires behave differently and collapse risk is higher; follow SOP.
- Lightweight engineered floors can fail fast with little warning; uncertain
  collapse times vary by construction (confirm locally).

## Verify
- [ ] You can name the inlet, exhaust and flow path of the fire you are attacking.
- [ ] Doors were controlled and ventilation happened only on order with water ready.
- [ ] Every crew member knows the flashover signs and the way out.
- [ ] Primary search results were reported by area to command.
- [ ] Your actions matched your service's SOP; anything you did differently was briefed.
