# Fireline safety, LCES and wildland fire behaviour

**Goal:** stay alive and effective on a wildfire by reading fire behaviour from fuel,
weather and terrain, and by never working without lookouts, communications, escape
routes and safety zones. Written as a reference for trained wildland crews and
students (US NWCG terminology; Australian, Canadian and European services use
equivalent but differently named systems — check your agency's handbook).

## 1. The fire behaviour triangle
Wildland fire behaviour is driven by **fuels, weather and topography**.
- **Fuels:** fine dead fuels (grass, needles) respond to humidity within about an
  hour and carry fast fire; heavy fuels burn longer and hotter. Continuity (gaps
  vs. continuous bed) and ladder fuels (that let fire climb into crowns) matter as
  much as load.
- **Weather:** low relative humidity (RH), high temperature and wind drive
  intensity. Watch for wind shifts from cold fronts, thunderstorm outflows, and the
  afternoon RH low (typically mid-afternoon). A **Red Flag Warning** (NWS) means critical fire weather conditions are occurring now or will shortly.
- **Topography:** fire runs faster upslope (preheating fuel ahead); chimneys,
  saddles and narrow canyons funnel wind and fire; south/southwest aspects (in the
  northern hemisphere) are drier.

## 2. LCES — before any fireline work
- **Lookouts** with a view of the fire and the crew, who know the trigger points.
- **Communications** — everyone on the right channel, check-ins scheduled.
- **Escape routes** — identified, known to everyone and re-checked as conditions change.
- **Safety zones** — areas where crew can survive without fire shelters. Guidance sizes them by flame length: the separation distance is several times flame height, much larger on slopes or in wind (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
Fire shelters are a last resort, not a plan.

## 3. The Orders and Watch Outs
The **10 Standard Firefighting Orders** (grouped in the IRPG under fire behaviour, fireline safety, organisational control and overall intent) and the **18 Watch Out Situations** (e.g. crews without identified safety zones and escape routes, building line downhill with fire below, taking a nap without a lookout posted) are carried in the Incident Response Pocket Guide (IRPG). Every
watch-out you hit raises the bar for continuing; several together means stop and reassess.

## 4. Fireline construction basics
- **Anchor and flank:** start the line from a secure anchor (road, rock, burned area)
  so the fire cannot outflank you, then work along the flanks toward the head.
- **Handline:** scrape to mineral soil wide enough for the fuel type; clear aerial
  fuels above it; undercut downhill lines to catch rolling material.
- **Direct vs indirect:** direct line on the fire edge keeps the black (burned area)
  as a safety zone; indirect line ahead of the fire needs burnout, which is a
  supervised firing operation only done on order.
- **Mop-up:** cold-trail and feel for heat with the back of your hand near the edge.

## Gotchas
- The most dangerous fire is often a "routine" small fire in the afternoon in light
  fuels with a forecast wind shift.
- Downhill line construction with fire below is a recognised high-risk situation.
- Fatigue, dust and smoke compromise judgement — take the scheduled breaks.
