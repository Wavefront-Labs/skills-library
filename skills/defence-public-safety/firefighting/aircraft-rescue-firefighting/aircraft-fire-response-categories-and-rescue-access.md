# Aircraft fire response: categories, timing and rescue access

**Goal:** understand how an airport fire service is sized and timed, and how crews
approach a crashed or burning aircraft so that occupants can evacuate or be rescued.
Reference for students and new ARFF/RFFS personnel; your aerodrome's emergency plan,
type-specific aircraft familiarisation and national regulator rules take precedence.

## 1. Standards that define the service
- **ICAO Annex 14** and the **Airport Services Manual (Doc 9137, Part 1)** set the
  international framework: an **aerodrome rescue and firefighting category (1–10)**
  based on the overall length and fuselage width of the largest aircraft regularly using the aerodrome (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked). Higher category = more extinguishing agent, discharge rate and vehicles.
- **Response time:** ICAO's objective is to reach any point of each operational runway
  within **2 minutes** (not exceeding 3 minutes) in optimum conditions, and the standard is 3 minutes to the end of each runway (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- **US:** FAA 14 CFR Part 139 uses Index A–E by aircraft length (A under 90 ft up to E at 200 ft or more, §139.315) with its own vehicle/agent tables, and requires the first vehicle to reach the midpoint of the farthest air-carrier runway within 3 minutes of the alarm (§139.319). NFPA 403 is the voluntary consensus standard (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- Foam: application rates and tactics differ by foam type — follow your service's current foam guidance.

## 2. Hazard zones around aircraft
- **Jet engine intakes** (suction) and **exhaust** (blast and heat) — stay out of
  them until engines are confirmed shut down; turboprop and helicopter rotors.
- **Hot brakes and wheels:** approach tyres from the front or rear, never in line
  with the axle (wheel/tyre failure throws fragments sideways).
- **Fuel** spill and vapour; oxygen systems; batteries (including lithium); composites
  that produce hazardous fibres and dust when burned (respiratory protection during overhaul).
- **General aviation** aircraft may carry ballistic recovery parachute rockets and
  military aircraft ejection seats — identify and avoid trigger areas.

## 3. Tactics in outline
- Position vehicles upwind/uphill where possible, with the aim of **creating and
  protecting a rescue path** from occupied doors and exits — controlling fire against the
  fuselage is the priority, not extinguishing every pool fire.
- Apply agent to keep fire away from the fuselage and escape slides; turret first, then
  handlines for closer protection.
- Penetrating nozzles and high-reach extendable turrets can treat cabin fires from
  outside on equipped vehicles.
- **Access:** use normal doors and emergency exits first. Each type has marked
  cut-in areas on the fuselage, documented in the operator's crash charts — learn them
  for the types that use your airfield.

## 4. Evacuation and triage
Occupants self-evacuating via slides will move unpredictably; protect the slide foot,
direct people upwind and away, and establish a casualty collection point with EMS.
Count and reconcile against the passenger manifest.

## Gotchas
- Category is about the aircraft, not the airport size; seasonal schedule changes can
  change the required category.
- Low-visibility and night operations lengthen response — practise them.
- Fuel tanks can be under the cabin floor and in the wings; a fire beneath a wing
  root threatens the cabin quickly.

- [ ] You can point to the doors, exits and cut-in areas on those types' crash charts.
- [ ] You can describe a rescue-path-first attack plan.
