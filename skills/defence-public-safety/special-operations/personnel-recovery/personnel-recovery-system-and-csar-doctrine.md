# Personnel recovery: the system and CSAR doctrine

**Goal:** understand personnel recovery (PR) as joint doctrine describes it — a system
that prepares people before they become isolated, coordinates their recovery, and brings
them home properly afterwards. Conceptual, unclassified overview; no codes, frequencies,
authentication methods or current procedures.

## 1. Definitions
- **Isolated personnel** — military or designated civilians separated from their unit in a
  situation where they must survive, evade, resist or escape (e.g. downed aircrew, a
  compromised patrol, detained personnel).
- **Personnel recovery** — the sum of military, diplomatic and civil efforts to prepare for
  and execute the recovery and reintegration of isolated personnel (JP 3-50, paraphrased).
- **Combat search and rescue (CSAR)** — one *method* of recovery: tactics and forces
  dedicated to recovering isolated personnel in hostile environments, historically centred
  on downed aircrew. PR is the broader system; CSAR is a subset.

## 2. The PR system: four components
1. **Commanders and staffs** — own the PR plan for their operations.
2. **Forces** — dedicated recovery forces and any unit that might be tasked.
3. **Isolated personnel themselves** — prepared through training and pre-mission
   preparation.
4. **Coordination structures** — personnel recovery coordination cells (PRCC) at component
   level and a joint personnel recovery centre (JPRC) at joint level.
Doctrine organises activity under **preparation, planning, execution and adaptation**.

## 3. The five execution tasks
Every recovery, whatever the method, follows the same logic:
1. **Report** — recognise and report that someone is isolated, quickly and accurately.
2. **Locate** — establish where they are and authenticate who they are.
3. **Support** — sustain them physically and psychologically, and support their families.
4. **Recover** — the physical recovery by the chosen method.
5. **Reintegrate** — structured debriefing, medical and psychological care, and return to
   duty.
Time matters most in the first hours: survivors are usually easiest to recover before the
adversary organises a search.

## 4. Recovery options (conceptual)
- **Military options** — immediate recovery by nearby forces; deliberate recovery by
  dedicated CSAR or SOF; an option may be a **raid** where personnel are held (see
  `defence-public-safety.special-operations.direct-action.raid-doctrine-and-mission-area-overview`).
- **Diplomatic options** — negotiation, prisoner exchanges, third-party help.
- **Civil options** — partner nations, NGOs, host authorities.
- **Unassisted** — the isolated person returns by their own efforts.
- **Non-conventional assisted recovery** — recovery through networks such as resistance or
  partner forces (historically, WWII escape lines).

## 5. Preparing the individual
- **SERE training** (Survival, Evasion, Resistance, Escape), tiered by the individual's risk
  of isolation.
- **Code of conduct** (US) or national equivalents guiding behaviour if captured.
- **Pre-mission preparation** — authentication data on file and an **evasion plan of
  action** for each mission. The *existence* of these products is public doctrine; their
  content is protected and never published.

## 6. Reintegration
Doctrine treats reintegration as a phased process — immediate medical care, then
decompression and debriefing, then return to family and duty. Done badly, it can harm the
individual and lose intelligence; done well, it supports long-term recovery.

## 7. Case studies
| Case | Lesson usually drawn |
|---|---|
| Son Tay raid (1970) | PR by direct action; intelligence failure (prisoners moved) but improved treatment of POWs afterwards |
| Bat 21, Vietnam (1972) | Escalating losses in a recovery effort; when to use alternative methods |
| Gulf War CSAR (1991) | Few successful recoveries; slow reporting and location (Whitcomb) |
| Scott O'Grady, Bosnia (1995) | SERE preparation plus effective locate-and-recover |
| Ongoing reforms | Interagency PR, civilian contractors and hostage cases now within PR thinking |

## Gotchas
- Treating PR as just "the rescue helicopter" — preparation, coordination and reintegration
  are most of the system.
- Delays in **reporting** — the most common cause of failed recoveries.
- Neglecting families and reintegration.
