# Special reconnaissance: the mission area and reporting doctrine

**Goal:** understand special reconnaissance (SR) as doctrine defines and PME teaches it —
why a commander tasks people to observe when sensors exist, how SR plugs into the
intelligence cycle, and what makes it succeed or fail. Conceptual and unclassified; no
field TTPs.

## 1. Definition and distinguishing features
JP 3-05 describes SR as reconnaissance and surveillance actions conducted as a special
operation in hostile, denied or diplomatically and/or politically sensitive environments
to collect or verify information of strategic or operational significance. Compared with
conventional reconnaissance:
- **Depth and sensitivity** — beyond the reach or political tolerance of conventional
  patrols.
- **Clandestine or low-visibility** — success usually means the opponent never knew.
- **Human judgement on the ground** — SR answers questions technical sensors cannot:
  intent, pattern of life, ground truth, confirmation of ambiguous imagery.
- **Strategic or operational value** — the information should change a senior commander's
  decision.

## 2. Typical task types (doctrinal categories)
- **Environmental / area reconnaissance** — terrain, hydrography, weather, infrastructure.
- **Threat assessment** — confirming presence, strength and activity of a force.
- **Target acquisition and assessment** — locating and characterising targets for other
  forces; **post-strike reconnaissance / BDA** after an attack.
- **Confirmation of other sources** — validating SIGINT or imagery leads.
These are categories, not methods; how a team actually conducts them is restricted
material outside this skill's scope.

## 3. Fitting SR into the intelligence cycle
SR is a collection asset and should be tasked like one:
1. **Requirement first** — tie every task to a priority intelligence requirement (PIR) or
   commander's critical information requirement (CCIR). An SR team without a clear question
   is risk without return.
2. **Cross-cue** — use cheaper or lower-risk collectors (imagery, open source, SIGINT) to
   narrow the question before committing people.
3. **Reporting criteria agreed up front** — what triggers an immediate report vs routine;
   what "negative" reporting means (absence observed is information).
4. **Exploitation and dissemination** — the product is only valuable if it reaches the
   decision-maker in time; plan communications and reporting chains as carefully as the
   insertion.

## 4. Reporting discipline (the transferable part)
The habit SR instils is relevant to any observer:
- Report **what was seen**, separately from **what you think it means**.
- Use a fixed format so nothing is forgotten — the classic mnemonic is **SALUTE**: Size,
  Activity, Location, Unit/uniform, Time, Equipment.
- Grade confidence and say how you know (direct observation vs inference).
- Time-stamp everything; stale information presented as current is dangerous.

## 5. Risk and command considerations
- SR teams are small and far from support; **compromise** can turn an intelligence task
  into a recovery problem (see
  `defence-public-safety.special-operations.personnel-recovery.personnel-recovery-system-and-csar-doctrine`).
- Commanders must weigh the value of the answer against the risk to the team and the
  political cost of exposure in a sensitive area.
- **Mission creep** — pressure to turn observers into strikers — is a recurring PME warning;
  it compromises the collection and the team.

## 6. Historical case studies
| Case | Period | Lesson usually drawn |
|---|---|---|
| Long Range Desert Group | 1940–43 | Long-range mobility and road-watch reporting feeding theatre intelligence |
| Allied coastwatchers (Solomons) | 1942–43 | Persistent human observation giving strategic early warning |
| Gulf War SOF in western Iraq | 1991 | Scud-hunting; terrain and weather assumptions (several patrols compromised, e.g. "Bravo Two Zero", whose accounts conflict) |

## Gotchas
- Tasking SR for questions a satellite pass or open-source search could answer.
- Vague requirements producing voluminous, unusable reporting.
- Treating popular memoirs as authoritative; cross-check against official histories.
