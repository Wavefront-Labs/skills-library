# Infiltration and exfiltration: method-selection doctrine

**Goal:** understand how special-operations planners think about getting a force to and
from an objective — the families of methods, the factors that decide between them, and
why extraction must be planned first. Conceptual overview at the level of published
doctrine and military history; no altitudes, profiles, equipment settings or procedures.

## 1. Why it is its own specialism
In SOF case studies, failures cluster in getting there and back rather than at the
objective. Infiltration and exfiltration (infil/exfil) depend on joint partners —
aviation, naval, airlift — and on weather, distance and detection risk the ground force
cannot control. Desert One (1980) never reached its objective: the mission failed during
the infiltration phase, which is why the Holloway Report focused on joint aviation and
integration.

## 2. The four domains (doctrinal families)
| Domain | Methods at a conceptual level | Typical strengths | Typical limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Air — fixed-wing** | Airland; parachute (static line, military freefall as a discipline) | Range, speed, reach into deep areas | Weather; drop-zone and aircraft detection; injury risk; no organic extraction |
| **Air — rotary-wing** | Landing; methods for when an aircraft cannot land | Precision, flexibility, also extracts | Range, noise, vulnerability to air defence, brownout and weather |
| **Maritime** | Surface craft; subsurface (submarine, swimmers — see `defence-public-safety.special-operations.combat-diving.combat-diving-as-a-discipline-and-safety-framework`) | Low signature from the sea; coastal access | Sea state, tides, beach conditions, long timelines |
| **Land** | Vehicle; foot; stay-behind (forces already in place) | Self-sufficiency, persistence | Slow; terrain; border and population exposure |

Methods are often **combined** — e.g. air to a staging point, then land or maritime to
the objective.

## 3. Selection factors
Planners weigh these against the mission's purpose (see the six principles in
`defence-public-safety.special-operations.direct-action.raid-doctrine-and-mission-area-overview`):
- **Distance and time** — range of platforms, refuelling, time on the ground.
- **Threat and detection** — air defence, surveillance, population density; the method's
  **signature** (noise, visual, electronic).
- **Environment** — weather, illumination, terrain, sea state, altitude and temperature
  effects on aircraft and people.
- **Load and force size** — what must go in, and who and what must come out (casualties,
  recovered personnel, exploited material).
- **Political constraints** — overflight, basing and host-nation permissions.
- **Proficiency and readiness** — the force and the platform crew must be current in the
  method; complex methods carry training and injury costs.
- **Exfiltration feasibility** — an infil method with no realistic exfil is not a plan.

## 4. Planning principles
- **Plan backwards from extraction.** Exfil and recovery shape the infil choice, not the
  reverse.
- **Contingency layers.** A primary, alternate, contingency and emergency structure (the
  **PACE** idea) for routes, methods and communications; agreed abort and go/no-go criteria.
- **Joint ownership.** The aviation or maritime component plans alongside the ground force
  from the start, not as a supporting afterthought.
- **Rehearsal** of the full sequence, including the transition between methods.
- **Link to personnel recovery** — any isolated element must already have a recovery plan
  (see `defence-public-safety.special-operations.personnel-recovery.personnel-recovery-system-and-csar-doctrine`).

## 5. Historical illustrations
- **Long Range Desert Group (1940–43)** — vehicle infiltration over extreme distances as a
  strategic capability.
- **Operation Varsity / WWII airborne** — dispersion and casualties in mass parachute
  drops, informing later doctrine on small-team air infiltration.
- **Desert One (1980)** — fragmented joint aviation and command; drove the creation of
  dedicated special-operations aviation and USSOCOM.
- **Afghanistan (2001)** — long-range rotary-wing infiltration in extreme altitude and
  weather.

## Gotchas
- Choosing the most impressive method rather than the lowest-risk one that meets the
  mission.
- Under-planning exfil, especially with casualties or extra people.
- Treating weather as a planning assumption rather than a go/no-go criterion.
