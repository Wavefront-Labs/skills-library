# Direct action: raid doctrine and the mission area

**Goal:** understand direct action (DA) the way it is taught in professional military
education — as a doctrinal category with a purpose, selection criteria and a body of case
studies — so you can analyse an operation, write about one, or place DA correctly in a
campaign. This is an unclassified, conceptual overview, not a tactics manual.

## 1. What doctrine means by direct action
US joint doctrine (JP 3-05) lists DA among the SOF core activities: short-duration strikes
and other small-scale offensive actions, in hostile, denied or politically sensitive
environments, to seize, destroy, capture, exploit, recover or damage designated targets.
Key features that separate it from a conventional attack:
- **Limited scope and duration** — in, achieve a specific effect, out. Holding ground is
  not the aim.
- **Precision and discrimination** — the target is designated and the effect bounded.
- **Strategic or operational purpose** — DA is chosen when a small action has an
  outsized effect (a key node, a person, a recovery), or when political sensitivity rules
  out a larger force.
- **Planned withdrawal** — extraction is part of the plan from the start (see
  `defence-public-safety.special-operations.insertion-and-extraction.infiltration-exfiltration-method-selection-doctrine`).

UK and NATO doctrine use similar language ("offensive action", "strike"); terms differ
slightly by nation — check the specific publication you are citing.

## 2. When DA is the right tool (the selection question)
Doctrine and PME frame this as a commander's choice, not a default:
- Is the effect achievable only by a small, specially trained force? If air or
  conventional forces can do it with acceptable risk, they usually should.
- Is the target **time-sensitive or politically sensitive**?
- Does the payoff justify the **risk to the force and the risk of failure** — including
  the strategic cost of a public failure?
- Is the **intelligence** good enough? Almost every famous DA failure traces partly to
  an intelligence gap (Son Tay 1970: the prisoners had been moved).

## 3. McRaven's theory of relative superiority
The standard PME framework. A small attacking force gains a temporary advantage over a
larger, defended opponent — *relative superiority* — and the mission succeeds if it is
achieved and held until the objective is complete. Six principles support it:
1. **Simplicity** — limited objectives, good intelligence, innovation to remove obstacles.
2. **Security** — deny the opponent warning of timing and method (not of the possibility of attack).
3. **Repetition** — rehearsal until actions are routine; full-dress rehearsal matters most.
4. **Surprise** — achieved through deception, timing and exploiting vulnerabilities.
5. **Speed** — the longer the action, the more friction and enemy reaction erode the advantage.
6. **Purpose** — every member understands the primary objective and is committed to it.
McRaven's point: planning (simplicity) shapes preparation (security, repetition), which
enables execution (surprise, speed, purpose).

## 4. The case-study canon
Used in PME to test the principles; read the primary sources and histories, not summaries.
| Operation | Year | Why it is studied |
|---|---|---|
| Eben-Emael (glider assault on Belgian fort) | 1940 | Innovation and surprise against a "impregnable" target |
| St Nazaire raid | 1942 | Purpose and acceptance of cost; strategic effect on a dry dock |
| Gran Sasso (Mussolini rescue) | 1943 | Speed and audacity; contested credit in the historiography |
| Son Tay | 1970 | Flawless execution, failed intelligence |
| Entebbe (Thunderbolt) | 1976 | Long-range planning under time pressure |
| Eagle Claw / Desert One | 1980 | Joint-integration failure; led to the Holloway Report, the Nunn-Cohen amendment (1987) and USSOCOM |
| Barras (Sierra Leone) | 2000 | Hostage recovery in a non-permissive African setting |
| Neptune Spear | 2011 | Interagency decision-making and strategic risk |

## 5. Command and control themes
- DA is usually controlled at a high level because of its political sensitivity; the
  tension between tight political control and mission command recurs in every case study.
- Joint integration (aviation, ISR, fires, medical, recovery) is where failures cluster.
- Branch plans and abort criteria are agreed before launch.

## Gotchas
- Calling any small attack "direct action" — the term implies SOF, specific purpose and
  limited scope.
- Judging by outcome alone: Son Tay was well executed and still failed; Desert One's
  failure was largely structural.
- Popular accounts dramatise; contested details (e.g. Gran Sasso credit, Neptune Spear
  narratives) need multiple sources.
