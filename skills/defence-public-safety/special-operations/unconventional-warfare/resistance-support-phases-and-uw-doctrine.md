# Unconventional warfare: resistance support and the phase model

**Goal:** understand unconventional warfare (UW) as doctrine and history — what it is, how
a resistance movement is structured, the standard phase model used to think about external
support, and why UW is as much political as military. Conceptual, unclassified overview.

## 1. Definition and how UW differs from its neighbours
US doctrine defines UW as activities to enable a resistance movement or insurgency to
coerce, disrupt or overthrow a government or occupying power, by operating through or with
an underground, auxiliary and guerrilla force in a denied area. Distinguish:
- **UW** — supporting a resistance *against* a government or occupier.
- **Foreign internal defence (FID)** / **security force assistance** — supporting a
  government *against* insurgency or threat. The mirror image of UW.
- **Counterinsurgency (COIN)** — the government side of the same struggle.
- **Irregular warfare** — the umbrella term for struggles over the legitimacy of and
  influence over a population; UW, FID and COIN sit under it.
The defining feature is **"by, with and through"** — the indigenous force does the
fighting; external SOF advise, assist and enable.

## 2. Components of a resistance (doctrinal model)
- **Underground** — clandestine cells, often in urban areas: intelligence, influence,
  logistics, sabotage. The most vulnerable and often the most important element.
- **Auxiliary** — part-time supporters among the population who provide supplies,
  information, shelter and early warning without being full-time fighters.
- **Guerrilla force** — the overt armed element, operating from areas the opponent does
  not fully control.
- **Shadow government / political leadership** — the movement's claim to legitimacy.
PME stresses that the population, not the guerrilla force, is the centre of gravity;
classic theorists (Mao, Galula) say the same from opposite sides.

## 3. The seven-phase model (TC 18-01)
A planning framework, not a timeline; phases overlap and may never all occur.
1. **Preparation** — strategic assessment: is there a viable resistance, does supporting
   it serve national objectives, what are the risks?
2. **Initial contact** — establishing contact and assessing the resistance's leadership,
   aims and capacity.
3. **Infiltration** — introducing the external advisory element.
4. **Organisation** — building the resistance's structure, command and training base.
5. **Build-up** — expanding capacity, logistics and the auxiliary.
6. **Employment** — the resistance conducts operations in support of the campaign.
7. **Transition** — demobilisation, reintegration and handover to a legitimate
   government. Historically the most neglected and most consequential phase.

## 4. Political, legal and ethical constraints
- UW is an **instrument of national policy** and needs high-level authorisation; in the US
  it intersects with covert-action law (Title 50) as well as military authorities (Title 10).
- The **law of armed conflict** still applies; partnering with forces that abuse civilians
  carries legal and strategic risk.
- **Alignment of aims** — external sponsors and resistance movements rarely want exactly
  the same outcome; armed groups outlive the conflict that created them.
- **Blowback** — weapons, training and networks persist after support ends.

## 5. Case studies
| Case | Lesson usually drawn |
|---|---|
| SOE and OSS in occupied Europe; Jedburgh teams (1944) | Liaison and supply to resistance in support of a conventional campaign; security failures in networks (e.g. the Netherlands "Englandspiel") |
| Burma, Detachment 101 (1942–45) | Indigenous forces (Kachin) as a theatre-level effect |
| Afghanistan (2001) | Small SOF teams with Northern Alliance forces and air power toppling a government quickly; transition and governance problems that followed |
| Resistance Operating Concept (2020s) | UW turned to defence: small states preparing national resistance before occupation (Baltic and Nordic "total defence") |

## Gotchas
- Equating UW with "guerrilla tactics" — doctrine treats it as a political-military
  campaign.
- Ignoring the transition phase; post-conflict armed groups often become the next problem.
- Taking sponsor-written histories at face value; seek the resistance's own and
  scholarly accounts.
