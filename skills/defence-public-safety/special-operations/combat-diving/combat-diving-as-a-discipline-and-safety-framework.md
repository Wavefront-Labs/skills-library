# Combat diving: the discipline and its safety framework

**Goal:** understand combat diving as a military specialism — why forces use divers,
how employment is organised, and the physiological limits that govern everything about
it. This is a conceptual, unclassified overview drawing on public diving manuals and
history. It is **not** dive training: no one should dive, and especially not with
rebreathers, from a text. Diving physiology is covered in more depth in
`health-medicine.occupational-and-environmental-medicine.undersea-and-hyperbaric-medicine.decompression-illness-and-dive-fitness`.

## 1. What combat diving is for
Combat divers are a means of **maritime infiltration with a very low signature** (see
`defence-public-safety.special-operations.insertion-and-extraction.infiltration-exfiltration-method-selection-doctrine`).
Doctrinal roles include:
- **Clandestine approach** to a coastline, harbour or vessel.
- **Hydrographic and beach reconnaissance** ahead of amphibious operations — the original
  UDT role in WWII.
- **Maritime special reconnaissance** of ports and coastal areas.
- **Defensive roles** — hull and harbour security searches, often shared with clearance
  divers.
Diving is slow, short-ranged and physically demanding; it is chosen when signature matters
more than speed.

## 2. Open vs closed-circuit employment
| | Open-circuit (scuba) | Closed-circuit (rebreather) |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | Exhaled gas vented to the water | Exhaled gas recirculated; CO2 chemically removed, oxygen added |
| Signature | Bubbles visible and audible | Little or no bubble trail |
| Main use | Training, search, non-clandestine work | Clandestine approach |
| Key limits | Gas supply; visible | Depth and time limits, oxygen toxicity, scrubber life, more complex failure modes |

Military oxygen rebreathers are restricted to **shallow depths** because breathing pure
oxygen under pressure risks **central nervous system oxygen toxicity** (sudden convulsion,
often fatal underwater). Exposure limits are set by the national diving manual; this
skill does not reproduce them. Mixed-gas rebreathers extend depth at the cost of
complexity.

## 3. How employment is organised
- The **dive pair** (buddy system) is the basic unit: divers are physically linked or kept
  in contact, check each other and can render immediate aid.
- **Underwater navigation** — compass and timing-based navigation in low visibility —
  is a core competence and a frequent training failure point.
- A **dive supervisor** and formal **dive plan** govern every dive: objective, maximum
  depth/time, gas and scrubber limits, emergency procedures, recompression availability,
  and abort criteria.
- **Surface swimming** often makes up much of a transit; the dive phase is kept as short
  as the mission allows.
- Integration with **delivery platforms** (boats, submarines, aircraft for parachute
  insertion into water) and with recovery plans.

## 4. The hazards that shape the discipline
- **Oxygen toxicity** (CNS and, over long exposures, pulmonary).
- **Hypercapnia** — CO2 build-up from scrubber exhaustion, flooding or overexertion.
- **Hypoxia** — in mixed-gas or poorly purged rebreathers; can cause unconsciousness
  without warning.
- **Decompression illness and barotrauma** — especially with depth changes or ascent
  problems.
- **Cold, fatigue and disorientation** — degrade judgement and navigation.
- **Caustic cocktail** — water reaching the CO2 absorbent can produce a caustic solution.
These hazards explain the culture: strict supervision, conservative planning, and
medical/recompression support.

## 5. History
- **Italian Decima MAS (1940–43)** — human torpedoes and swimmers attacked ships in
  defended harbours (e.g. Alexandria, 1941); the template for modern combat swimming.
- **British and Commonwealth units** (e.g. the SBS lineage, COPP beach reconnaissance).
- **US Navy UDTs** — beach reconnaissance and obstacle clearance across the Pacific and at
  Normandy; ancestors of the SEALs.

## Gotchas
- Thinking "rebreather = deeper" — military oxygen rebreathers are the opposite.
- Treating diving as a standalone skill; it is an infiltration method tied to platforms,
  navigation and recovery.
- Underestimating cold and fatigue.
