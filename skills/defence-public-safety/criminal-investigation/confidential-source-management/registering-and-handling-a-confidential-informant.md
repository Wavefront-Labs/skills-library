# Registering and handling a confidential informant

**Goal:** use human sources so their information is reliable and usable, the source is
protected, the handler stays honest, and the agency stays within the law. Informant
mismanagement (corrupt relationships, unrecorded payments, sources committing crimes under
cover of police protection) is a recurring cause of major scandals and acquittals.

## 1. Registration before tasking
- An individual becomes a source when they are tasked, or when a relationship is maintained
  to obtain information covertly. Informal "contacts" who are repeatedly asked for information
  must be registered — unregistered use is the classic failure.
- Registration covers identity verification, motivation (money, revenge, leniency, rivalry —
  motivation affects reliability), criminal history, suitability, and a **risk assessment**
  (to the source, the public, the handler and the investigation).
- **UK:** authorisation as a CHIS under RIPA 2000 with the CHIS Code of Practice; enhanced
  rules for juveniles and vulnerable people and for long-term use. **US federal:** Attorney
  General's Guidelines on Confidential Human Sources — suitability reviews, instructions given
  and acknowledged, approval levels for "otherwise illegal activity".

## 2. Handler / controller structure
- **Handler** — day-to-day contact; two handlers per source is best practice.
- **Controller** — supervises handlers, reviews contact records, approves tasking.
- **Authorising officer** and a central **source unit** that holds true identities separately
  from operational records.
Dedicated Source Units (UK) take handling away from investigators so the investigation's
pressure doesn't distort the relationship.

## 3. Contact discipline
- Every meeting and call planned, approved and recorded promptly: who, when, where, what was
  said, tasking given, welfare noted.
- Handlers never meet alone off the record, accept gifts, socialise, or create debts.
  Integrity is protected by the paperwork.
- Give and record clear instructions: the source has no police powers; they may not commit
  crime unless specifically authorised; they must not act as an agent provocateur.

## 4. Intelligence grading and sanitisation
- Grade each report for **source reliability** and **information validity** separately
  (UK uses the 3x5x2 system; many agencies use the Admiralty/NATO A–F / 1–6 code).
- Corroborate before acting where possible; single-source information should drive further
  enquiry, not arrests on its own.
- **Sanitise** before dissemination: remove details that only the source could know.
  Investigators get the information, not the identity.

## 5. Payments and rewards
Payments approved at set levels, made with a witness, receipted, recorded centrally, and
proportionate to the value of the information. Payments and any benefits (e.g. sentencing
assistance) are potentially disclosable.

## 6. Protecting identity in court
- Informant privilege/public-interest immunity can protect identity, but yields when
  disclosure is necessary for a fair trial (*Roviaro v. US*; UK PII procedures, *R v H & C*).
  If disclosure would be ordered, the prosecution may have to choose between revealing the
  source and dropping the case — plan evidence so the case doesn't rest on the source.

## Gotchas
- Investigators running their own informal sources outside the system.
- Sources committing serious crime while the agency looks away to protect "product".
- Over-reliance on one prolific source whose reports are never tested.
- Juvenile sources tasked against family members — heavily restricted and needing enhanced authorisation.

## Verify
- [ ] Source registered, risk-assessed, authorised before any tasking.
- [ ] Two handlers and a controller assigned; identity held centrally.
- [ ] Every contact and payment recorded and reviewed.
- [ ] Intelligence graded and sanitised before dissemination.

*Uncertainty:* framework details are UK and US federal; state/provincial and civil-law
jurisdictions differ.
