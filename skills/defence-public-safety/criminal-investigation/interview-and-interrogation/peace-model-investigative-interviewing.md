# PEACE-model investigative interviewing

**Goal:** obtain accurate, reliable and complete accounts from witnesses, victims and suspects
— information, not confessions. PEACE (developed in England & Wales after false-confession
miscarriages) and the cognitive interview are the evidence-based standard; the Méndez
Principles (2021) set the same approach internationally.

## P — Planning and preparation
- Objectives: which points to prove or issues need covering? What do you already know, and
  from which source?
- Interviewee profile: age, vulnerabilities (learning disability, mental illness, intoxication,
  language), need for an interpreter, **appropriate adult** for juveniles/vulnerable adults (UK PACE Code C).
- Legal framework: caution/rights, legal advice, recording requirements.
- For suspects, plan **evidence disclosure** — what to disclose pre-interview to the lawyer and
  what to reveal in stages during the interview.

## E — Engage and explain
- Introduce everyone, explain the purpose, the routine (recording, notes, breaks), and the
  interviewee's rights. Check they understand the caution in their own words.
- Rapport is genuine respect and active listening, not a manipulation tactic. Interviewees
  treated with respect give more information.

## A — Account, clarification and challenge
**Witnesses (cognitive interview):**
1. Mental reinstatement of context — ask them to picture the environment, sounds, how they felt.
2. **Free recall**: "Tell me everything, in as much detail as you can, even things you think
   aren't important." Don't interrupt. Silence is useful.
3. Probe topics they raised, in their order, with open questions (TED: Tell, Explain, Describe)
   before closed ones. Avoid leading questions ("Was the car red?").
4. Change of order or perspective can prompt additional detail (use with care; not with child witnesses).

**Suspects:**
1. Invite their full account first, uninterrupted.
2. Probe it topic by topic.
3. Then introduce evidence that conflicts with it, **strategically and gradually**
   (Strategic Use of Evidence research shows late, staged disclosure exposes inconsistencies
   in deceptive accounts better than early confrontation). Ask them to explain each
   inconsistency.
"No comment" interviews: still put every question and piece of evidence — in the UK it may
support adverse inferences later (CJPOA 1994 s.34–37) if the special warning is given correctly.

## C — Closure
Summarise the account in their words; ask if anything is to be added or corrected; explain what
happens next. End on a professional note — they may be interviewed again.

## E — Evaluation
Compare the account with other evidence; identify new lines of enquiry; review your own
performance (recordings make this possible).

## Why not the confession-driven model
Accusatory methods built around isolation, confrontation, minimisation and maximisation
(e.g. the Reid technique as historically taught) are linked in research to **false confessions**,
especially from young, vulnerable or suggestible people. Deception about evidence is unlawful
in many countries and risky where legal (US). Confessions that the interviewer "fed" contain
details only police knew — a red flag courts now look for.

## Statement taking
Use the interviewee's words. Record first accounts verbatim where possible. Video-recorded
evidence-in-chief for vulnerable/intimidated witnesses follows Achieving Best Evidence (UK).

## Gotchas
- Interrupting free recall within seconds — the most common error in recorded interviews.
- Over-reliance on "deception cues" (gaze, fidgeting): research shows they're near chance.
- Disclosing all evidence at the start, so the suspect can fit an account around it.
