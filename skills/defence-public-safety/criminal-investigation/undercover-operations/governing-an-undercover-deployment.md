# Governing an undercover deployment

**Goal:** make sure an undercover operation is lawful, necessary, proportionate, overseen
and survivable for the officer — the part of the specialism that managers, authorising
officers, reviewers and prosecutors own. Field tradecraft is taught only on accredited,
vetted courses and is deliberately out of scope here.

## 1. Why governance dominates this specialism
Undercover work intrudes deeply into private life, carries risk of crime by the state,
and has produced serious scandals (in the UK, long-term deployments into protest groups
including deceptive intimate relationships, now the subject of the Undercover Policing
Inquiry). Most of the professional standard exists to prevent repeats.

## 2. Authorisation
- **UK:** undercover officers are authorised as Covert Human Intelligence Sources under RIPA
  2000 Part II (and the CHIS Code of Practice), with enhanced authorisation and Investigatory
  Powers Commissioner (IPCO) notification for long-term deployments. Criminal conduct by a
  CHIS needs a separate Criminal Conduct Authorisation (CHIS (Criminal Conduct) Act 2021).
- **US federal:** Attorney General's Guidelines on FBI Undercover Operations — sensitive
  circumstances (e.g. public officials, religious or political organisations, otherwise-illegal
  activity) need Undercover Review Committee approval.
- The application must show **necessity** (why less intrusive methods won't work),
  **proportionality** (the intrusion against the seriousness of the crime), and an assessment
  of **collateral intrusion** on people who aren't targets.
- Authorisations are time-limited, reviewed at set intervals, and cancelled formally.

## 3. Entrapment and legal limits
- The officer may provide an opportunity to commit an offence that the subject would have
  committed anyway; they may not instigate or pressure someone into an offence they wouldn't
  otherwise commit.
  - UK: *R v Looseley* (2001) — the test is whether police did no more than an ordinary member
    of the public might have done; abuse of process stays the case.
  - US: the entrapment defence turns on the defendant's predisposition (*Jacobson v. US*, 1992).
- Legal professional privilege, journalistic material and confidential communications need
  special handling; plan to avoid acquiring them.

## 4. Roles kept separate
- **Authorising officer** — senior, independent of the investigation's day-to-day.
- **Operational lead / cover officer** — runs the deployment, monitors the officer's safety
  and conduct, maintains contact.
- **Investigating team** — receives product through controlled channels.
- **Welfare/psychologist** — independent of operational pressure.
Separation lets someone say "stop" without a conflict of interest.

## 5. Records
Contemporaneous records of every deployment, contact, conversation summary, and any
participation in criminality. These records are disclosable in principle (sensitive parts may
be withheld via public-interest immunity procedures) — write them assuming a judge will read them.

## 6. Welfare and exit
- Psychological screening before selection and regular assessment during and after deployments.
- Limits on deployment length; planned, supported exit; post-deployment reintegration.
- Risk assessments for the officer and their family updated throughout; legend protection
  continues after the operation ends.

## Gotchas
- Mission creep: an authorisation for one purpose quietly used for intelligence on others.
- Deployments renewed by routine without fresh necessity reasoning.
- Inadequate records making prosecutions collapse at disclosure.
- Intimate relationships under a false identity — prohibited in UK guidance and indefensible anywhere.

## Verify
- [ ] Written authorisation with necessity, proportionality and collateral-intrusion reasoning.
- [ ] Review dates diarised and met; cancellation recorded.
- [ ] Separate authorising, operational and welfare roles named.
- [ ] Entrapment risk assessed for each planned interaction type.

*Uncertainty:* statutory references are UK and US federal; other jurisdictions (Canada, Australia,
EU states) have their own controlled-operations regimes.
