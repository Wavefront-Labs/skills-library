# Exhibit packaging and chain of custody

**Goal:** be able to prove in court that the item analysed is the item seized, that nobody
could have tampered with it, and that it didn't degrade or get contaminated in between.
Each handover is a link; a missing link invites exclusion or doubt.

## 1. Seizing
- Photograph in situ (with scale where useful) before moving. Note exact location, time,
  who found it and who seized it.
- Wear gloves; change them between items. One item per package.
- Seize under a legal power (warrant, consent, search incident to arrest, statutory power) and
  record which.

## 2. Packaging by evidence type
| Evidence | Packaging | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Wet/biological (bloody clothing, swabs) | Air-dry first in a controlled area, then **paper** bags/boxes | Plastic traps moisture → mould and DNA degradation |
| Liquid blood, urine for toxicology | Sealed tubes with correct preservative, refrigerated | Per lab instructions |
| Fire debris | Unused metal cans or nylon fire-debris bags | Plastics leak or contain volatiles |
| Firearms | Made safe by a trained officer, then rigid box with cable ties; record make-safe actions | Safety first; don't put anything in the barrel |
| Sharps (knives, needles) | Rigid tube/box, labelled SHARP | Protect handlers and the item |
| Fibres, hairs, glass fragments | Folded paper (druggist fold), then envelope | Tiny items lost from envelopes' corners |
| Drugs | Tamper-evident bag; weigh and record per policy | Accurate weight drives charges |
| Phones/devices | Faraday bag if network isolation is needed; record power state; don't browse | Remote wipe and data change risk |
Always follow your lab's submission manual over a general table like this one.

## 3. Sealing and labelling
- Seal with tamper-evident tape; sign or initial **across** the seal onto the package.
- Never re-open through the original seal: a lab or court examiner opens at a different
  edge and reseals, leaving all seals visible.
- Label: unique exhibit reference (often the seizer's initials and a number), description,
  location found, date/time, seizing officer, case/crime number, and a continuity section where
  every handler signs, dates and times.
- Describe the item as found, not as interpreted ("knife with red-brown staining", not "bloody knife").

## 4. Storage and the property system
- Book into the evidence/property store promptly; record on the electronic property system.
- Correct storage conditions: dry room temperature for dried biological material; refrigerated
  or frozen for some samples; secure safes for cash, drugs and firearms.
- Every movement (to lab, to court, to interview room, return) is recorded with signatures.
- Periodic audits of high-risk items (drugs, cash, firearms).

## 5. Retention and disposal
Keep exhibits per retention policy (serious cases may need decades — cold-case reviews depend on
it). Never dispose without authority; disclosure and appeal rights may still apply.

## Gotchas
- Packaging victim and suspect items together, or in the same vehicle or room — instant
  cross-contamination argument.
- Plastic bags for damp clothing. The single most common avoidable DNA loss.
- Exhibits in an officer's car boot or desk overnight.
- Labels written after packaging and stuck on afterwards — write them first.
- Gaps in the continuity record when an exhibit is "just" taken for photography.

## Verify
- [ ] Every exhibit has a unique reference and an unbroken signed continuity record.
- [ ] Packaging matches evidence type and lab requirements.
- [ ] Seals intact and signed across; no reopening through old seals.
- [ ] Property system and physical location match on audit.

*Uncertainty:* packaging choices summarised from common US/UK guidance; laboratory-specific
requirements take precedence. For digital acquisition see
