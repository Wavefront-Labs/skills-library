# Spotting and preventing pickpocket theft

**Goal:** for a loss-prevention officer, transit or venue security team, or a business
advising staff and customers — reduce theft from the person on your site by knowing where
and when it happens, which behaviours mark it out, which controls actually cut it, and what
to do for the victim afterwards. Defender's view only.

## 1. Know your hotspots before you watch people
Theft from the person clusters tightly in space and time. Map your incident log, police open
data and CCTV notes. Typical concentrations:
- **Choke points and crush:** escalator tops/bottoms, ticket barriers, train and bus doors,
  queue funnels, venue entry/exit at kick-off and final whistle.
- **Distraction-rich places:** tourist landmarks, street performers, markets, bars and
  night-time-economy streets, festivals, sales events.
- **Unattended property zones:** café chair-backs, tables with phones on them, coat racks,
  hotel lobbies, airport and station seating.
Put staff and cameras on the top few places at the top few hours.

## 2. Behavioural red flags (patterns, not profiles)
Watch **behaviour relative to setting**, never appearance or ethnicity — profiling is unlawful
in most jurisdictions, wastes attention and ruins prosecutions.
- Eyes on **people's bags, pockets and hands** rather than the train board, the show or the shop
  display.
- **Boarding and re-alighting** without travelling; riding the same escalator repeatedly; loitering
  at a barrier without passing through.
- **Creating or seeking crowding** where there is room: pressing close at a half-empty door.
- **A distraction that coincides with closeness** — a spill, a question, a dropped item, a map or
  petition held at chest height, a staged argument — while someone else is in contact range.
- **Coats, newspapers or bags carried over the arm** in a way that screens the hands.
- **Small groups who separate and regroup**, exchange glances or signals, and pass items between
  them shortly after a contact.
One indicator is noise; a cluster in a known hotspot is the signal to monitor, radio colleagues
and get cameras on the group. *(Uncertain: the mix of methods varies a lot by city and season;
calibrate against your own CCTV reviews rather than generic lists.)*

## 3. Controls that work (situational crime prevention)
- **Design:** widen and de-clutter choke points; queue barriers that keep spacing; good lighting;
  avoid seating where bags hang out of view; secure chair clips/hooks under café tables.
- **Guardianship:** visible uniformed staff at peak crush times; plain-clothes teams only under
  trained, lawful supervision (check your jurisdiction's surveillance rules).
- **CCTV:** cameras covering the hotspot at face height as well as overview; retention long enough
  for late reports (victims often notice hours later — 30 days is a common minimum).
- **Messaging:** short, specific "keep phones and wallets in front zipped pockets" signage and PA
  announcements at the hotspot. *Gotcha:* "Beware of pickpockets" signs can make people pat the
  pocket where valuables are; pair them with staff presence, not instead of it.
- **Staff briefing:** share recent incident patterns weekly; a team that knows "this week it's the
  platform 4 doors at 17:30" will notice it.

## 4. When you suspect or see a theft
- Your **safety and the public's come first**. Observe, radio, keep CCTV on the suspects, and follow
  your organisation's policy and local law on detention or citizen's arrest — many employers
  prohibit physical intervention. Never chase into traffic or tracks.
- Note times, descriptions (clothing, direction of travel), camera IDs. Preserve footage
  immediately; don't rely on overwrite schedules.
- Hand to police with a clear timeline; teams often operate across sites, so pattern intelligence
  is valuable even without an arrest.

## 5. Victim advice (have it on a card)
1. Cancel/freeze cards via the bank app or number on the bank's site — not numbers from a stranger.
2. Use **Find My / Find My Device** to lock or wipe the phone; don't go to a location to recover it
   yourself — give it to police.
3. Change passwords and enable 2FA for email, banking and messaging if the phone was unlocked or
   its PIN was observed (shoulder-surfing then snatch is a common combination).
4. Report to police and get a crime reference number (insurers need it). Report lost ID documents
   to the issuing authority.
5. Watch for follow-on fraud and phishing texts claiming the phone has been found.

## Verify
- [ ] You have a current hotspot map by location and hour from your own data.
- [ ] Staff can name 4+ behavioural indicators and know that clusters, not appearance, drive attention.
- [ ] Choke points, seating and CCTV coverage have been reviewed against the hotspot map.
- [ ] Intervention policy (observe/radio/detain) is written and staff know it.
- [ ] CCTV preservation and police handover steps are documented.
- [ ] Victim advice card covers cards, phone lock, passwords, police report and follow-on fraud.
