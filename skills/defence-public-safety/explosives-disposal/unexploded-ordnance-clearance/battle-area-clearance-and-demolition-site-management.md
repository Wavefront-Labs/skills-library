# Battle-area clearance and demolition-site management

**Goal:** find and remove unexploded ordnance (UXO) from a defined area to a stated standard,
and dispose of it without injuring anyone. Identification of specific munitions and their
disposal procedures are taught only on accredited EOD courses; this skill covers the
organisation, standards and safety management.

## 1. For everyone: the 3 Rs
Anyone who finds a suspected munition: **Recognise** it may be dangerous, **Retreat** the way
you came without touching it, **Report** to police/military with location (mark it from a
distance if safe). Old does not mean safe — century-old ordnance still kills, and some fillings
become more sensitive with age. Explosive Ordnance Risk Education (EORE) in affected
communities teaches exactly this.

## 2. Risk assessment before clearance
- **Desk study:** historical records (bombing maps, battle records, former ranges, previous
  finds), land use since, ground conditions. UK construction practice follows CIRIA C681
  (preliminary and detailed UXO risk assessments).
- Define the **threat**: likely munition types, depth of penetration (air-dropped bombs can be
  many metres deep), condition.
- Set the **clearance requirement**: area, depth, and standard — agreed with the land user and
  the authority.

## 3. Battle-area clearance (BAC)
- Used where the threat is UXO and abandoned ordnance on or near the surface, without a
  landmine threat (otherwise the work is mine clearance — see humanitarian-demining).
- Divide the area into boxes/lanes; mark boundaries.
- **Visual search** for surface items, then **detector search** (metal detectors,
  magnetometers for deeper ferrous items) to the agreed depth. Overlap sweeps; record coverage.
- Every signal is investigated or recorded per the procedure; contamination from scrap metal
  is the main productivity challenge and is not an excuse to skip signals.
- **Recording:** each find is logged with grid location, depth, type (as identified by a
  qualified technician), condition and disposition. These records support land release and
  later quality control.
- **Cluster munition** strike areas need specific survey, as submunitions scatter across a
  footprint and many fail to function.

## 4. Demolition (disposal) site management
- Site selected away from habitation, infrastructure and livestock; ground suitable; no
  overhead lines; access controllable.
- **Safety distances** derived from the net explosive quantity and the fragmentation hazard of
  the items, per IMAS 10.20 and national standards; fragments can travel far further than blast
  overpressure. Where distances can't be achieved, engineered protective works (tamping,
  sandbag/earth works) reduce them per standard.
- Sentries on every approach with communications, warning signals (agreed sequence of warnings
  and all-clear), and a person-count before and after.
- Only qualified personnel prepare demolitions; demolition stores accounted for at issue and return.
- Medical support on site with a casualty evacuation plan and a tested route to a hospital.
- After the event: wait the prescribed time, inspect, deal with any items that didn't fully
  function per the standard, and record.

## 5. Quality management
Internal QA during the work and external quality control (sampling of cleared ground by an
independent body) before land is handed back. Handover certificates state the area, depth and
standard achieved — and its limits.

## Gotchas
- Assuming surface clearance means depth clearance.
- Moving items to "collect them up" without technician assessment.
- Losing people count at demolition time.
- Promising land users "UXO-free" rather than "cleared to the stated standard".

## Verify
- [ ] Desk study and clearance requirement documented.
- [ ] Coverage recorded per box with every signal dispositioned.
- [ ] Demolition safety distance calculated per standard; sentries and comms checked.
- [ ] QA/QC completed and handover states its limits.

and civilian standards differ.
