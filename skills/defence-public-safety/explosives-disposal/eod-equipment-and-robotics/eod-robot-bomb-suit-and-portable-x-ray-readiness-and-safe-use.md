# EOD robot, bomb suit and portable X-ray: readiness and safe use

**Goal:** have the team's core equipment work first time at an incident, and use it within its
limits. Equipment readiness is a large part of an EOD team's routine and a frequent cause of
delayed or riskier incidents. Device-specific tactics and disruption techniques are taught only
in accredited bomb-technician training and are not covered.

## 1. Remotely operated vehicles (robots)
Models range from small throwable robots to large platforms (e.g. Telerob tEODor/telemax,
Harris/L3Harris T7, Remotec/Northrop ANDROS, Foster-Miller/QinetiQ TALON, FLIR/Teledyne
PackBot). Readiness discipline is common:
- **Pre-deployment check** each shift and before use: batteries charged and rotated (log cycle
  counts), arm and gripper full range of motion, all cameras and lights, drive and tracks,
  e-stop function, tool mounts.
- **Communications:** radio link range in the actual environment (buildings and vehicles block
  signals), fibre-optic tether spool length and condition, and interaction with electronic
  countermeasures (ECM) that may be in use at the scene — coordinate frequencies in advance.
- **Operator proficiency:** regular timed courses (stairs, door opening, object manipulation)
  keep skills current; record them.
- **Recovery plan:** know how to retrieve a robot that loses comms or gets stuck without
  sending a person forward — a second robot, a tow line, or acceptance of loss.
- Post-use: decontaminate, inspect, charge, log faults and repairs.

## 2. Bomb suits
- Public-safety bomb suits in the US are tested to **NIJ Standard 0117.01** (fragmentation,
  blast overpressure, heat, and ergonomic tests). They **reduce** injury from small devices at
  distance; they do not make a technician safe near a large device.
- **Heat stress** is a leading hazard: suits are heavy (often 30–40 kg) and enclosed. Use the
  integrated cooling system, pre-hydrate, limit time in suit per policy, and have a dressing
  partner monitor for heat illness signs. Medical support must be ready to remove the suit fast.
- Check before each use: visor scratches/cracks, helmet ventilation and communications,
  fastenings, cooling unit charge, fit to the wearer.
- Inspect and log after each use; retire per manufacturer life limits and after damage.

## 3. Portable X-ray systems
- Pulsed X-ray sources with digital panels or imaging plates let technicians see inside a
  suspect item from a distance.
- **Radiation safety:** follow the national ionising-radiation regulations and the manufacturer's
  controlled-area distances; everyone not involved stays out; personal dosimeters worn and read
  on schedule; the source licensed and its use logged.
- Image quality: placement of panel and source, number of pulses, and avoiding movement;
  take multiple angles. Imaging distance and setup minimise approach time.
- Panels and sources are expensive and fragile; protective cases and charged batteries are part
  of readiness.

## 4. Records and readiness reporting
- Equipment register with serials, service intervals, battery cycle logs, calibration (X-ray
  and dosimeters), suit inspection history.
- A readiness board/report each shift: what's serviceable, what's under repair, what backup exists.
- Defects reported to the manufacturer and shared through bomb-technician information channels.

## Gotchas
- Batteries "charged" but at end of life — runtime collapses mid-task.
- Radio range tested in an open car park, used in a concrete building.
- Bomb suit worn too long in summer heat.
- X-ray used without the controlled area being cleared.

- [ ] Bomb suit inspected; cooling charged; heat-stress limits briefed.
- [ ] X-ray licence, dosimeters and controlled-area procedure current.
