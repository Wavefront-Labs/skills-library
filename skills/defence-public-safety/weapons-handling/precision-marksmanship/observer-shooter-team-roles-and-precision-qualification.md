# Observer-shooter team roles and precision qualification

**Goal:** understand how precision-marksmanship roles are organised, trained and held
accountable in military and police service, and run the team disciplines — observation,
record-keeping, environmental reading and qualification — that make first-round accuracy
repeatable. This is an orientation to the specialism; technical ballistics and employment
come from the service's own restricted courses.

## 1. What the role is (and isn't)
- **Military:** a trained pair (or larger team) providing **observation, reporting and
  precision fire** at range, often tasked as much for intelligence as for shooting. Distinct
  from the **designated/squad marksman**, an infantry member with extra training and a
  more accurate rifle, operating within the section.
- **Police:** precision officers in armed or tactical units, whose day-to-day output is
  overwhelmingly **observation, containment and live intelligence** to commanders; firing is
  rare, authorised under strict law and policy, and individually investigated.
- History: the role has existed since accurate rifles appeared (rifle regiments of the
  Napoleonic era onward), was formalised in the World Wars and is now a selected, coursed
  specialism in most forces.

## 2. The team
| Role | Duties |
|---|---|
| **Observer / spotter** (usually the more experienced) | Searches and identifies, estimates range, reads wind and environment, gives corrections, watches the shot ("trace"/impact), keeps the log and radio. |
| **Shooter** | Applies the fundamentals (see `defence-public-safety.weapons-handling.marksmanship.marksmanship-fundamentals-and-coaching-a-qualification-shoot`) under stress, manages the rifle and optic. |
Roles rotate to manage fatigue; both must be able to do both jobs.

## 3. The data book
The team's memory. For every shot or confirmation: date, rifle and optic serials,
ammunition lot, distance, light, temperature, wind estimate and actual correction, point
of impact relative to aim, and whether it was a **cold-bore** shot. Over months it shows
what *this* rifle-ammunition-shooter combination really does — far more reliable than a
generic table. Treat data books as controlled documents.

## 4. Environment, at concept level
- **Wind** is the largest variable; observers estimate it from flags, vegetation and
  **mirage** (heat shimmer seen through the spotting scope), along the whole flight path,
  not just at the firing point.
- **Range estimation** errors grow with distance; modern teams use laser rangefinders but
  still train map, reticle and eye methods as backups.
- Temperature, altitude and angle affect the result; teams record conditions and verify
  data on known-distance ranges rather than trusting one calculator.

## 5. Cold-bore discipline
The first shot from a clean, cold barrel may land differently from later ones. Teams record
cold-bore impacts in the data book, confirm zero regularly, and avoid cleaning/changes just
before an operational commitment unless their procedure says otherwise.

## 6. Selection, qualification and accountability
- Selection tests marksmanship, observation and memory, patience, fieldcraft and
  temperament; a calm, honest observer is valued over a gifted shot.
- Qualification combines known- and unknown-distance shooting, observation and reporting
  tests and decision-making; **requalification** on a fixed cycle keeps the licence.
- Every shot is subject to **ROE (military) or the law of self-defence and force policy
  (police)** and a full post-incident review. Data books, logs and radio records are evidence.

## Uncertainty
Course content, qualification standards and team structures vary widely by nation and
agency and are partly restricted; this page is a public-domain synthesis.
