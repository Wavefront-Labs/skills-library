# Patrols, incident reporting and conflict management on post

**Goal:** be the reliable eyes, ears and first responder for a site: know your
instructions, patrol unpredictably, write reports that someone else can act on, and
handle conflict without making it worse. Reference for security officers and
supervisors; your licence conditions, employer policy and local law define your powers.

## 1. Know your post
- **Assignment instructions (AIs)/post orders:** the site's rules, patrol
  requirements, access procedures, emergency contacts, alarm responses, and what you
  are and aren't authorised to do. Read them on every new site and after every change.
- **Handover:** at each shift change, get open issues, keys and equipment checks,
  and any expected visitors or contractors.
- **Emergency procedures:** fire alarm (your role in evacuation and meeting the fire
  service), medical, bomb threat (follow the site's checklist and call the police),
  lockdown, lone-worker check-ins.

## 2. Patrols
- **Purpose:** detect hazards and security issues (open doors, lights, leaks, people
  where they shouldn't be), deter by being seen, check critical points.
- **Vary times and routes** — predictable patrols are easy to work around. Guard tour
  systems (checkpoints you tag) prove coverage; don't let them make patrols routine.
- Use the senses: smell (smoke, gas), sound (alarms, running machinery), touch (warm
  doors in a fire alarm — don't open).
- Record findings and actions; report hazards to the right person, not just the log.

## 3. Writing reports
- The **daily occurrence book** (DOB) or electronic log records routine events with
  times. **Incident reports** stand alone and may become evidence.
- Use the **who, what, where, when, how** (and why if known — label opinion as opinion).
- Write in the first person, chronological, plain language; record exact words
  spoken where relevant, in quotation marks.
- Complete it as soon as practical; never alter after signing — add a dated
  supplementary note instead.
- Include witnesses, CCTV camera numbers and times, police reference numbers.

## 4. Conflict management
- **Assess first:** your safety, others' safety, exits, whether to call support or
  police before engaging.
- **De-escalate:** calm voice, open posture, respectful distance (about two arm
  lengths), give options rather than ultimatums, listen and acknowledge feelings.
- Recognise the warning signs of escalation (clenched fists, target glances, pacing,
  invading space) and **disengage** when safety requires it.
- Separate the person from an audience where you safely can.

## 5. Powers and limits
In most jurisdictions security officers have no more legal power than any other
citizen (e.g. *any-person arrest* under s.24A PACE in England & Wales, strict
conditions apply; US rules vary by state). Detention, searches (usually only with
consent or as a condition of entry) and use of force carry significant legal risk —
follow policy, use only reasonable and proportionate force when lawfully necessary,
and report every use. In the UK, front-line guarding requires an **SIA licence**;
many US states license guards. (Not legal advice — confirm local law.)

## Gotchas
- Logs written at the end of a shift from memory miss times and details.
- "Just this once" exceptions to access rules are how incidents start.
- Lone working without check-ins.

- [ ] Patrols are varied and recorded with findings and actions.
- [ ] Incident reports answer who/what/where/when/how and list evidence.
- [ ] Conflicts handled with de-escalation; any force reported.
