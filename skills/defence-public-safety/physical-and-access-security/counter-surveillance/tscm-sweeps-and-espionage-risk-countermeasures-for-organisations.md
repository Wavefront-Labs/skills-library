# TSCM sweeps and espionage-risk countermeasures for organisations

**Goal:** protect an organisation's sensitive conversations, premises and people from
hostile surveillance and industrial espionage, using a proportionate programme rather
than ad-hoc "bug hunts". This is a **defensive** reference for security managers and
those commissioning specialists; it does not cover conducting or evading surveillance.

## 1. Think about the whole leak, not just bugs
Most sensitive information leaks through **people and process**: insiders, careless
conversation, unsecured documents, compromised phones and laptops, cloud accounts and
over-shared meeting invites. Technical eavesdropping devices are real but one path
among many. A programme covers:
- **Information classification** and need-to-know.
- **Personnel security:** vetting proportionate to role, leaver processes, insider-risk
  awareness (see access control and guarding skills).
- **IT security:** device management, account security, meeting platform settings.
- **Physical security** of rooms used for sensitive discussion.
- **Technical surveillance countermeasures (TSCM)** for high-value spaces and events.

## 2. When to commission a TSCM sweep
- Before high-stakes events: M&A negotiations, board strategy sessions, litigation
  strategy, sensitive R&D reviews.
- After indicators: competitors knowing things they shouldn't, signs of tampering,
  an unexplained device, a security incident involving unescorted access.
- Periodically for executive offices, boardrooms and secure rooms.
Discuss the need **away from the suspect space** (and not on devices that may be
compromised) — a common practitioner rule.

## 3. What a competent sweep covers (to judge proposals)
- **Physical search:** furniture, fittings, ceiling voids, power and data outlets,
  gifts and new items in the room.
- **RF spectrum analysis** over a wide frequency range, comparing against the local
  baseline; checks for cellular, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth devices.
- **Non-linear junction detector (NLJD)** to find electronics whether or not powered.
- Checks of wiring (telephone, network, power) and the room's AV/conferencing systems.
- **Thermal and optical** inspection.
- A written report: scope, methods, limitations, findings and recommendations.
Evaluate vendors on qualifications, equipment currency, references and insurance;
be wary of very short or very cheap sweeps. (No universal licensing exists; some
countries restrict possession of certain equipment.)

## 4. If a device is found
Don't touch, discuss or remove it in the room. Document, preserve, and involve
**legal counsel and law enforcement** — it is evidence, and removing it tips off the
adversary. Plan the response with advisers.

## 5. Hostile reconnaissance awareness
Attackers and spies often conduct reconnaissance first. Programmes such as the UK
NPSA (formerly CPNI) "deter, detect, disrupt" guidance and "See, Check and Notify" (SCaN)
train staff to notice and report behaviour like unusual interest in security
measures, photographing entrances, or persistent loitering — and to approach
in a customer-service manner, which deters most hostile reconnaissance.

## Gotchas
- Jamming devices to "secure" a room is illegal in many jurisdictions.
- A clean sweep is valid for a point in time only.
- Phones brought into the meeting may defeat the sweep — set a device policy.

- [ ] Sweep scope and vendor chosen against the criteria above; report received.
