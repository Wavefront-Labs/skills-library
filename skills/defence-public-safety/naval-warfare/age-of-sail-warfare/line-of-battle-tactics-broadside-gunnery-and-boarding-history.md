# Line-of-battle tactics, broadside gunnery and boarding (history)

**Goal:** read, analyse and teach sailing-era naval battles (roughly 1650-1830)
with the concepts contemporaries and modern historians use — for history study,
historical fiction research, museum interpretation or tabletop wargames. Purely
historical.

## 1. The ships and why they fought in line
- Sailing warships carried guns along their sides, so firepower pointed **abeam**;
  bow and stern were weak and vulnerable.
- Hence the **line ahead**: ships following one another so every broadside bears and
  no ship masks another's fire. It also kept a fleet controllable by signals.
- The **rating system** (British; others similar) graded ships by guns: first to third
  rates were **ships of the line** fit to stand in the battle line (third-rate
  74-gun ships were the workhorse); **frigates** (fifth/sixth rates) scouted, repeated
  signals and raided commerce, and by convention were normally not fired on by a battle
  line during a fleet action unless they fired first.

## 2. Doctrine: the Fighting Instructions
- English **Fighting Instructions** codified line tactics from the Anglo-Dutch Wars.
  A long-held historians' view is that rigid adherence produced indecisive battles
  (e.g. Toulon 1744 and the courts-martial that followed); more recent work
  (Rodger, Willis) argues the instructions were more flexible than once claimed.
  Treat "rigid Instructions" as a contested interpretation.
- **Weather gage** (upwind position): lets you choose when and how to close, and
  your smoke blows onto the enemy; but heeling can stop you opening lower gun ports in
  strong winds. The **lee gage** made it easier to disengage and to fire high into
  rigging — the French were often said to prefer it.
- French doctrine frequently aimed at **dismasting** (firing high) to escape and
  preserve the fleet; British practice favoured hulling at close range. Treat as a
  generalisation, not a rule.

## 3. Decisive tactics
- **Breaking the line:** passing through the enemy line to cut it and engage part of
  it with superior numbers (the Saintes 1782; Camperdown 1797; Trafalgar 1805).
- **Raking fire:** firing along the length of an enemy ship through its bow or
  stern — every shot can traverse the whole gun deck. Breaking the line exposes enemy
  ships to raking — and exposes the attacker on the approach.
- **Doubling:** putting ships on both sides of the enemy line (the Nile 1798).
- **General chase:** abandoning the line to pursue a fleeing enemy (Quiberon Bay 1759).
- Nelson's method relied on prior explanation of his intent to captains (the
  "Nelson touch" memorandum) — an early example of mission command.

## 4. Gunnery
- Muzzle-loading **long guns** on wooden carriages, crewed by teams, run out with
  tackles; **carronades** (from the 1770s) were short, light, heavy-shot weapons for
  close range.
- **Rate of fire** came from drill. British crews in the later wars were often credited
  with faster fire than opponents (a frequently cited but hard-to-quantify claim).
- Most fighting was at short range — "pistol shot" or closer — because accuracy was
  poor and the effect of heavy shot at close range was devastating.
- Ships rarely sank from gunfire; they were captured, burned or surrendered after
  casualties and damage made fighting impossible. Fire and magazine explosion were
  the great fear (*L'Orient* at the Nile).

## 5. Boarding
Boarding decided many single-ship and small actions, particularly after ships were
locked together. Boarders, marines in the tops, and sharpshooters (Nelson was
mortally wounded by a marksman at Trafalgar) mattered most at close range.

## 6. Signals and command
Flag signals were limited until numeric signal books and Popham's telegraphic code
(1800s); poor signalling caused confusion in many battles. Read signal logs critically.

## 7. Analysing a battle (method)
1. Wind direction and who held the gage.
2. Each fleet's formation and intent.
3. Whether and where the line was broken or doubled.
4. Command and signalling quality.
5. Gunnery and seamanship differences.
6. Outcome measured as ships captured/destroyed and strategic effect.
