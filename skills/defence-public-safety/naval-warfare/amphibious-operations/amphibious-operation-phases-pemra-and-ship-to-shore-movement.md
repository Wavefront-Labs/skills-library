# Amphibious operation phases (PEMRA) and ship-to-shore movement

**Goal:** understand how an amphibious operation is structured and planned at the
doctrinal level, why it is regarded as one of the most complex military operations,
and what historical landings teach. Unclassified doctrine and history only; no real
locations, force data or current procedures.

## 1. The four types
Joint doctrine (JP 3-02) recognises:
1. **Assault** — establishing a force on a hostile or potentially hostile shore.
2. **Raid** — a swift incursion with a planned withdrawal.
3. **Demonstration** — a show of force to deceive (e.g. the US Marines afloat off
   Kuwait in 1991, which fixed Iraqi divisions on the coast).
4. **Withdrawal** — extracting forces by sea (Dunkirk and Gallipoli's evacuation are
   the classic cases — the latter is often cited as the best-executed part of the
   campaign).
Doctrine also lists amphibious support to other operations (e.g. humanitarian relief,
non-combatant evacuation).

## 2. Command relationships
The **Commander, Amphibious Task Force (CATF)** (naval) and the **Commander, Landing
Force (CLF)** are normally co-equal during planning, with a **supported/supporting**
relationship that shifts by phase — the navy is supported during movement, the
landing force once ashore. Ambiguity here is a recurring historical cause of failure
(Gallipoli suffered from divided, unclear command).

## 3. The phases — PEMRA
1. **Planning** — starts from the landing force's scheme of manoeuvre ashore and works
   *backwards* to the landing plan, the loading plan and the embarkation plan. This
   "reverse planning" is the key concept.
2. **Embarkation** — loading people and equipment. **Combat loading** puts items in
   the order they will be needed ashore, at the cost of ship space; **administrative
   loading** maximises space but makes rapid unloading impossible. A notorious
   failure mode is administrative loading for a force that then has to fight on arrival.
3. **Rehearsal** — tests timings, communications and ship-to-shore movement; often
   skipped under time pressure, which history punishes.
4. **Movement** — the sea transit to the objective area, under protection of escorts
   (see surface and anti-submarine warfare skills).
5. **Action** — the landing itself, build-up ashore, and transfer of control to the
   landing force commander.
(Shaping activity before the main force arrives — reconnaissance, hydrographic survey,
mine countermeasures — is often treated as a related phase.)

## 4. Ship-to-shore movement concepts
- **Waves and serials:** forces land in a planned sequence so that the first
  echelon can secure the beach for follow-on units, vehicles and logistics.
- **Hydrography decides:** beach gradient, tides, surf, obstacles and exits inland
  determine which landing craft can be used and when. Tarawa (1943) showed the cost of
  a tide misjudgement: landing craft grounded on the reef.
- **Ship-to-objective manoeuvre (STOM):** a modern US concept of moving forces
  directly to inland objectives by air and surface connectors without a beach build-up.
- **Logistics over the shore** remains the long pole: the landing succeeds or fails
  on the build-up rate.
- Mines in the shallows can stop an amphibious force (Wonsan, 1950); see
  `defence-public-safety.naval-warfare.mine-warfare.mine-warfare-doctrine-and-mine-countermeasures-concepts`.

## 5. Historical lessons
- **Gallipoli (1915):** unclear objectives, poor command arrangements, weak
  logistics — it shaped interwar amphibious thinking.
- **Normandy (1944):** deception, massive rehearsals and specialised equipment.
- **Inchon (1950):** surprise via an "impossible" site with extreme tides.
- **Falklands (1982):** San Carlos landing under air attack; showed the vulnerability
  of the amphibious ships during the build-up.
