# Flooding, fire and stability response organisation

**Goal:** keep a damaged warship afloat, upright and fighting. This covers the
organisation, priorities and standard methods of naval damage control (DC) as
described in publicly released training manuals. It complements, but does not
replace, a ship's own DC book and hands-on qualification — DC is learned by drilling.

## 1. Prevention starts before the hit: material conditions
Ships set graded **material conditions of readiness** — US terminology: **X-RAY**
(least closed, routine in port), **YOKE** (normal at sea), **ZEBRA** (maximum closure,
at general quarters). Every door, hatch and valve is marked with its classification.
- Gotcha: a single fitting left open (or propped open "just for a minute") defeats the
  whole compartment subdivision. Fitting discipline is the cheapest survivability there is.
- Logging and reporting every opened ZEBRA fitting is what lets DC Central know the
  true state of the ship.
(Other navies use different names — e.g. NATO/RN "State 1/2/3" conditions — with the
same principle.)

## 2. The organisation
- **Damage Control Central (DCC)** — keeps the plot of all damage, fire, flooding and
  ship's stability; directs and prioritises.
- **Repair parties / repair lockers** — teams stationed around the ship with equipment,
  each owning a zone.
- **Investigators** go first to establish and report the extent of damage.
- **Everybody is a firefighter:** all hands get basic DC training because the
  specialist party may be the casualties.

## 3. Priorities
The classic order of attack is:
1. **Fire** (it spreads fastest and can reach magazines),
2. **Flooding**,
3. **Stability** restoration,
while simultaneously keeping vital systems running (firemain, power, propulsion,
weapons) — sometimes by "reconfiguring" damaged systems around the hit.
Communicate constantly: reports to DCC by sound-powered phone or messenger when
circuits fail.

## 4. Core techniques
**Boundaries:** for both fire and flooding, first set **boundaries** — cooling or
closing the decks and bulkheads surrounding the affected space — to stop spread, then
attack. Six-sided thinking: above, below, fore, aft, port, starboard.

**Fire:** investigate, set boundaries, isolate ventilation and power, attack with the
appropriate agent, overhaul, and **reflash watch**. Then **desmoke** and test the
atmosphere before re-entry. Firefighting water adds weight high in the ship — see stability.

**Flooding:** stop or slow ingress (plugs, patches, shoring the bulkheads that
hold back water), then **dewater** with eductors and pumps. **Shoring** stiffens
weakened bulkheads and hatches; it is practiced with timber and adjustable steel
shores on drill structures.

## 5. Stability — where good damage control goes wrong
- **Free-surface effect:** partially filled compartments let water slosh to the low
  side, reducing effective GM far more than the weight suggests. Many flooded ships
  are lost to free surface, not to lack of buoyancy.
- Firefighting water accumulating on upper decks raises the centre of gravity —
  remove it as you go.
- **Counterflooding** to correct a list is a DCC decision with full stability
  knowledge, because it can reduce reserve buoyancy; never do it ad hoc.
- Know the difference between a list caused by off-centre weight and one caused by
  negative GM (loll) — corrections differ. For stability background see
  `engineering-technology.marine-naval-engineering.ship-stability.intact-stability-criteria-check`.

## 6. Case lessons
- **USS *Forrestal* (1967):** a deck fire killed many of the trained DC team early,
  leading the US Navy to train every sailor in firefighting.
- **HMS *Sheffield* (1982):** loss of firemain and power; smoke spread rapidly —
  lessons in materials, smoke control and redundant systems.
- **USS *Samuel B. Roberts* (1988):** mine damage, fire and flooding; saved by
  well-drilled crew, improvised shoring and DC leadership.
- **USS *Cole* (2000):** crew saved the ship after a large hull breach in port.
