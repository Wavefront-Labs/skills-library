# ASW concepts, layered screening and convoy protection

**Goal:** understand how navies think about finding and countering submarines —
the doctrine, the physics in outline, the force roles and the historical evidence —
as taught in unclassified naval education. No current sensor performance, tactics
or detection ranges are given; those are classified and change with technology.

## 1. ASW is a search problem first
A submarine's defence is concealment, so most ASW effort is spent *looking*. The
doctrinal chain is **detect → classify → localise → track → (engage)**, and every link
can fail: a contact must be judged to be a submarine rather than a whale, wreck or
merchant ship before localisation effort is committed. False contacts are the
normal state of ASW; managing them is a core skill.

Second key idea: **ASW is a team sport across platforms** — fixed seabed arrays,
maritime patrol aircraft, helicopters, surface escorts and friendly submarines each
cover a different part of the chain. No single platform does it all well.

## 2. The ocean in outline (why it is hard)
- Sound, not light or radar, is the main sensing medium underwater.
- **Passive sonar** listens for the target's own noise; it reveals nothing about the
  listener but depends on the target being noisier than the background.
- **Active sonar** transmits and listens for an echo; it can find quiet targets but
  announces the searcher, and the submarine typically hears the ping farther away
  than the searcher hears the echo.
- Sound bends with temperature, pressure and salinity. Layers such as the
  **thermocline** can create shadow zones; in deep water, sound can refocus at long
  range (convergence zones). Shallow, noisy littoral water is notoriously difficult.
- Consequence: detection range is a property of the day, place and depth, not of the
  sensor alone. Planners treat it as uncertain and re-estimate it continually.

## 3. Force roles
1. **Barrier operations** — covering a choke point the submarine must transit
   (the Cold War GIUK gap is the classic example).
2. **Area search** — sanitising a region ahead of a force or shipping.
3. **Direct support** — ASW units working in support of a group but not tied to it.
4. **Close escort / screen** — escorts around the protected units.
Doctrine combines these in **depth**: distant barriers and maritime patrol aircraft
thin the threat; outer screens and helicopters localise; inner escorts defend the
high-value units. See
`defence-public-safety.naval-warfare.surface-warfare.surface-force-organisation-screening-and-the-tactics-of-fleet-combat`
for screen design generally.

## 4. Convoy: the most important lesson in ASW history
- In 1917 and again from 1939, **convoy** reduced losses dramatically. The counter-
  intuitive reason: the ocean is so big that a convoy is barely easier to find than a
  single ship, so grouping ships *reduces* sightings, and it forces attackers to come
  to the escorts.
- Hunting groups searching open ocean for submarines were largely unproductive
  until combined with good intelligence; escorting was more effective.
- Air cover was decisive: closing the mid-Atlantic "air gap" in 1943 forced U-boats
  down where they were slow and blind.
- **Operational research** (Blackett and others) improved results with analysis —
  e.g. larger convoys need proportionally fewer escorts per ship, and changing the
  depth settings of air-dropped weapons raised success rates.
- Victory came from the combination: convoy, air power, signals intelligence,
  sensors, training and shipbuilding. Single-cause explanations are a common error.

## 5. Gotchas when studying or wargaming ASW
- Treating detection as certain — real ASW is mostly about probabilities and time.
- Ignoring the cost of active transmission.
- Forgetting waterspace management with friendly submarines.
- Underestimating how long ASW takes and how many assets it consumes.

Uncertainty: the relative weights of the WWII contributing factors remain debated by
historians.
