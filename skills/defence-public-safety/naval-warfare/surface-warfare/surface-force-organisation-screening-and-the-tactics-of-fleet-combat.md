# Surface force organisation, screening and the tactics of fleet combat

**Goal:** understand, at the level taught in naval academies and staff colleges, how a
surface force is organised, protected and fought — enough to read doctrine, analyse a
historical action, run a classroom tactical exercise or a wargame. This is conceptual,
unclassified doctrine; it contains no current platform data or operationally specific
procedures, and modern figures are deliberately omitted.

## 1. The core idea: attack effectively first
The central tenet in Hughes' *Fleet Tactics* is that at sea, the side that delivers
effective firepower first usually wins, because warships are concentrated stores of
combat power with limited staying power. Everything else follows:
- **Scouting is as important as firepower.** You cannot fire first at what you have not
  found, identified and tracked. A force with better reconnaissance and a worse weapon
  often beats the reverse (Midway is the textbook case).
- **Anti-scouting matters too** — denying the enemy a track on you (dispersion,
  deception, emission control) buys the first shot.
- **Staying power** (the hits a ship can absorb and keep fighting) and **defensive
  firepower** reduce what the enemy's first strike achieves.
Hughes formalised this in a simple salvo model: surviving combat power after an
exchange depends on offensive salvoes delivered, defensive interceptions, and hits
needed to put a ship out of action. Use it qualitatively: it shows why a small
advantage in striking first or in staying power swings outcomes non-linearly.

## 2. Organising the force: command by negation
Allied doctrine uses a **composite warfare commander (CWC)** concept: the **officer in
tactical command (OTC)** delegates warfare areas (surface, air, undersea, information)
to subordinate commanders, who act on their own initiative unless the OTC overrides
("command by negation"). Gotchas students miss:
- Delegation only works if the commander's intent and rules of engagement are clear
  in advance — at sea, time from detection to engagement can be very short.
- Warfare areas compete for the same ships and sensors; the OTC arbitrates priorities.

## 3. Screens, sectors and the threat axis
A **screen** is a ring or arc of escorts placed between the high-value unit (a carrier,
amphibious group or convoy) and the expected threat.
1. Define the **threat axis** — the most likely direction of attack — from intelligence
   and geography.
2. Build **defence in depth**: outer layers to detect early and engage the launch
   platforms, inner layers to deal with what leaks through.
3. Assign each escort a **sector** rather than a fixed point, so it can manoeuvre.
4. Re-orient the screen as the axis or the formation's course changes.
The classic error is a uniform, symmetric screen that spreads too few escorts thinly
everywhere instead of weighting the threat axis.

## 4. Emission control (conceptual)
Active sensors reveal you. **EMCON** policy trades your own picture against being
detected. Commanders choose a posture deliberately — silent while transiting, radiating
when an attack is expected — and accept that turning sensors on is itself information
to the enemy. Detailed EMCON plans are classified; the principle is not.

## 5. Historical lessons worth teaching
- **Jutland (1916):** poor scouting reports and signalling meant neither fleet
  commander had a clear picture; British battlecruiser losses showed the cost of
  inadequate staying power and magazine safety.
- **Savo Island (1942):** an allied screen surprised at night — failures of scouting,
  divided command and readiness rather than of weapons.
- **Falklands (1982):** loss of *Sheffield* illustrated layered-defence gaps and the
  damage-control dimension of staying power.
- **Operation Praying Mantis (1988):** a short modern surface action decided by
  detection and first effective fire.
Uncertainty note: the interpretations above are standard staff-college readings; the
historiography of each action is contested in detail.

## 6. Running a classroom exercise
Give each side a mission, a force list in abstract terms (number of shooters, scouts,
interceptors, hits-to-disable), and fog of war (detections arrive late or wrong).
Debrief on: who found whom first, how intent was delegated, and whether the screen
was weighted to the real threat axis.
