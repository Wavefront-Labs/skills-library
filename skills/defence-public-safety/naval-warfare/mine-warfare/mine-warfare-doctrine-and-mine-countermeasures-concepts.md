# Mine warfare doctrine and mine countermeasures concepts

**Goal:** understand mine warfare as a strategic and doctrinal subject — why mines
have outsized effects, how their use is governed by law, and how navies approach
mine countermeasures (MCM) — at the level of unclassified naval education.
**Scope limit:** this skill deliberately contains no device construction, firing
mechanisms, laying procedures or methods for defeating countermeasures. It is a
doctrine, law, history and defensive-concepts reference.

## 1. Why mines matter
- Mines are cheap, persistent and hard to find, and a *threat* of mines is often
  enough to close a waterway: shipping and insurers will not accept the risk.
- They impose disproportionate cost: clearing an area takes far longer and far more
  resources than threatening it.
- Since 1945 mines have damaged more US Navy ships than all other weapons combined
  (a frequently cited public figure; treat as indicative).
- They are the classic **sea denial** and **anti-access** weapon (see
  `defence-public-safety.naval-warfare.submarine-warfare.submarine-roles-patrol-doctrine-and-undersea-campaign-history`
  for the sea-denial concept).

## 2. Purposes of minefields (doctrinal categories)
- **Offensive** — in waters controlled by an opponent.
- **Defensive** — in international waters to protect one's own sea lines.
- **Protective** — in one's own waters to protect harbours and coasts.
Each has different legal and notification implications.

## 3. The law
- **Hague Convention VIII (1907)** restricts unanchored mines that do not become
  harmless and requires precautions for neutral shipping.
- The **San Remo Manual (1994)** restates the customary rules: mines must be
  effectively monitored, neutral shipping must have safe routes, and minefields must
  be notified and cleared after hostilities.
- Post-conflict clearance obligations are a large part of real-world mine warfare
  (e.g. the clearance after both World Wars took years).

## 4. Mine countermeasures — the two families
**Passive MCM** (reduce risk without touching the mine):
- **Avoidance** — route shipping around suspected areas; maintain pre-surveyed
  routes (Q-routes) so changes on the seabed can be noticed.
- **Signature management** — ships reduce the physical signatures they emit;
  degaussing (reducing a ship's magnetic field) is the long-established public example.
- **Intelligence and surveillance** — detecting minelaying is far easier than finding
  mines afterwards.

**Active MCM** (deal with the mines):
- **Minesweeping** — dragging gear or emitting simulated ship signatures to cause
  mines to be cut free or to function harmlessly away from ships.
- **Mine hunting** — detect, classify, identify and neutralise individual objects,
  using sonar, remotely operated vehicles, autonomous underwater vehicles and
  clearance divers.
- Trend: moving the people out of the minefield — unmanned systems launched from
  stand-off platforms.

## 5. Thinking in residual risk
No clearance is ever 100%. MCM planners express results as an **estimated percentage
cleared** or **residual risk** to transiting ships, and commanders decide what risk is
acceptable for the operation. Gotchas:
- Seabed clutter (rocks, wrecks, debris) drives search time and false contacts.
- Time is the scarcest resource; MCM must start early in any amphibious or port
  operation plan.
- One unexpected mine resets confidence in the whole area.

## 6. Historical lessons
- **North Sea Mine Barrage (1918):** a vast defensive field; clearance afterwards
  was lengthy and dangerous.
- **Wonsan (1950):** a large amphibious force was delayed for days by mines laid with
  simple means — "We have lost control of the seas to a nation without a navy" is the
  oft-quoted reaction.
- **Operation Earnest Will (1988):** USS *Samuel B. Roberts* was badly damaged by a
  mine; the crew saved her through damage control (see
  `defence-public-safety.naval-warfare.shipboard-damage-control.flooding-fire-and-stability-response-organisation`).
- **Gulf War (1991):** two US warships mined; mine threat influenced the decision
  not to make an amphibious assault.
