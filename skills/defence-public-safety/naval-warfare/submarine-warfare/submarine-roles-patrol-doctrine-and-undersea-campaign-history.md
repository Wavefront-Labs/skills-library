# Submarine roles, patrol doctrine and undersea campaign history

**Goal:** explain what submarines are for, the principles that shape how they are
employed, and what the great undersea campaigns teach — at the level of an
unclassified staff-college lecture or a naval history seminar. No current
performance figures, patrol areas or operationally specific procedures are given.

## 1. Types and what they imply
- **SSN (nuclear attack):** effectively unlimited submerged endurance and high
  sustained speed; can transit and reposition while staying hidden.
- **SSK (diesel-electric), some with AIP:** very quiet on battery, but must snort or
  surface to recharge (less often with air-independent propulsion), which is its
  moment of greatest exposure. Endurance and transit speed are limited, so SSKs suit
  defensive, choke-point and littoral roles.
- **SSBN (ballistic-missile):** exists to stay hidden; its value is the *uncertainty*
  of its location, which underpins second-strike deterrence.

## 2. Roles
1. **Sea denial** — making waters unusable to an opponent. Corbett's distinction
   between sea *control* and sea *denial* is the frame: submarines are pre-eminently
   denial weapons.
2. **Anti-submarine warfare** — submarines hunting submarines.
3. **Anti-surface / commerce warfare** — the historical guerre de course.
4. **Intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance** — covert presence.
5. **Strategic deterrence** — the SSBN mission.
6. **Special forces insertion and land attack** in some navies.

## 3. Principles of employment
- **Stealth is armour.** A detected submarine loses most of its advantage. Doctrine
  therefore treats every emission, transmission and exposure as a cost
  (the "indiscretion" concept for SSKs).
- **Command by mission orders.** Communicating with a submerged boat is slow and
  risks detection, so commanders get intent and boundaries, not detailed direction.
  This is mission command in its purest form.
- **Waterspace management.** Because friendly submarines cannot easily see each other
  or be seen by friendly forces, operating areas are allocated and de-conflicted in
  advance to prevent mutual interference and fratricide. Students often overlook how
  much planning effort this takes.
- **Endurance and positioning dominate.** Where a boat *is* at the critical time
  matters more than what it carries; transit time drives the force numbers needed to
  keep one on station (roughly: several hulls per continuous station, once
  maintenance, training and transit are counted — exact ratios vary by navy and
  are a planning assumption, not a law).

## 4. Deterrent patrols (public-domain concepts)
Continuous at-sea deterrence means at least one SSBN on patrol at all times. Publicly
discussed features: the boat's priority is remaining undetected rather than engaging;
communications are largely one-way; and the force structure (number of hulls) is set
by the need for unbroken coverage through refit cycles.

## 5. Campaign lessons
- **WWI and WWII Atlantic:** German U-boat campaigns nearly severed Britain's supply
  lines; they were defeated by convoy, air cover, intelligence (signals decryption)
  and technology together — no single measure. See
  `defence-public-safety.naval-warfare.anti-submarine-warfare.asw-concepts-layered-screening-and-convoy-protection`.
- **US Pacific campaign (1941-45):** initially crippled by defective torpedoes and
  over-cautious doctrine; once fixed, submarines destroyed most of Japan's merchant
  fleet. Lesson: test weapons realistically and select aggressive commanders.
- **Falklands (1982):** the sinking of *General Belgrano* by an SSN kept the Argentine
  surface fleet in port thereafter — sea denial achieved largely by threat.
- **Cold War:** the "silent" contest of acoustic advantage (Cote's *Third Battle*)
  shows quieting as a decisive, invisible arms race.

Uncertainty: historical figures and causal weightings are debated; cite specific
works when making quantitative claims.
