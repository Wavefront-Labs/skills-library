# Carrier flight-deck cycles and embarked air wing practice

**Goal:** understand how a carrier and its embarked squadrons generate flights safely
and repeatedly — the planning rhythm, the deck organisation and the recovery
procedures — at the level of publicly released training manuals and
high-reliability-organisation research. This is about the *process* of operating
aircraft at sea, not mission tasking.

## 1. The air plan and the cycle
The day's flying is fixed in an **air plan** issued the evening before: launch times,
aircraft, missions in outline, fuel and recovery times. Flying is grouped into
**cycles** — historically around 1 hour 15 minutes to 1 hour 45 minutes in US
practice (an *approximate* public figure; it varies with mission and air wing).
In **cyclic operations** each cycle has a launch followed by recovery of the
previous launch, so the deck alternates between launching and landing.
**Flex deck** (more common on smaller decks or for helicopters) launches and recovers
continuously as aircraft are ready — more flexible, harder to manage.

Gotchas:
- The cycle is constrained by *deck space* as much as aircraft: a jet can't land if the
  landing area is fouled, so aircraft must be "respotted" forward between events.
- The ship must steam into wind for launch and recovery, which drives the ship's
  track — and can pull the carrier away from where the screen expects it.
- Fuel is the hard limit for airborne aircraft; tankers and divert fields are planned
  in from the start.

## 2. Who does what (public, well-documented roles)
- **Air Boss** in Primary Flight Control ("Pri-Fly") owns the flight deck and
  airspace close to the ship.
- **Handler** in Flight Deck Control plans where every aircraft sits (historically
  using a scale model on a table — the "Ouija board").
- **Air operations** coordinates the wider airborne picture; **CATCC** controls
  approaches in poor weather.
- Deck crew are identified by **jersey colour**: yellow (aircraft handling officers and
  directors), green (catapult and arresting gear, maintenance), red (ordnance,
  crash and salvage), purple (fuel — "grapes"), blue (handlers, chocks and chains),
  brown (plane captains), white (safety, LSOs, medical, inspectors).
  Colour conventions are US Navy; other navies differ in detail.

## 3. Recovery procedures
- **Case I:** good weather, day — aircraft hold overhead and break into the landing
  pattern visually.
- **Case II:** intermediate weather — start as instrument, finish visual.
- **Case III:** night or bad weather — aircraft are stacked in a **marshal** pattern
  and fly controlled, timed approaches one at a time.
The **landing signal officer (LSO)** watches every approach, talks the pilot onto the
glide path and grades every pass; grades feed pilot training and qualification.
STOVL carriers (ski-jump, vertical landing) use different launch and recovery methods
but the same deck discipline.

## 4. The deck as a high-reliability system
Research on carriers (Rochlin, La Porte, Roberts) treats the flight deck as a model
high-reliability organisation: rigid procedures for routine work, but authority for
**anyone** to halt operations when they see a hazard, constant re-briefing, and
learning from every incident. Practices to copy:
- **FOD walkdown** — the whole crew walks the deck in a line picking up debris before
  flight operations, because a small bolt can destroy a jet engine.
- Hand signals and positive control of every aircraft movement.
- Ordnance, fuel and moving aircraft are kept separated by rules, and the deck is
  "cleaned up" between cycles.

## 5. Beyond the big-deck carrier
Destroyer and frigate helicopter decks, amphibious ships and maritime patrol aircraft
squadrons share the same principles: a published plan, clear deck authority,
ship-aircraft wind limits, and standardised recovery procedures.
