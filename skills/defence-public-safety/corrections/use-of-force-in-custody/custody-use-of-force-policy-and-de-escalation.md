# Custody use-of-force policy and de-escalation

**Goal:** use force on a person in custody only when it is lawful, necessary and
proportionate, stop it as soon as it is no longer needed, keep the person medically safe, and
record every decision so it can be independently reviewed.

**Scope note.** Physical control, restraint and team techniques are taught and certified
hands-on by accredited instructors and are deliberately **not** described here. Only use
techniques and equipment you are currently trained and authorised to use, within your agency's
policy. Policy is often stricter than the law, and you are held to both.

## 1. The legal tests (headline only)
- **US, convicted prisoners (Eighth Amendment):** whether force was applied "in a good-faith
  effort to maintain or restore discipline, or maliciously and sadistically to cause harm"
  (*Whitley v. Albers*, 1986; *Hudson v. McMillian*, 1992 — serious injury is not required for
  a violation). Factors: need for force, relationship between need and amount, threat perceived,
  efforts to temper the response, extent of injury.
- **US, pre-trial detainees (Fourteenth Amendment):** *objective* unreasonableness
  (*Kingsley v. Hendrickson*, 2015) — the officer's intent is not required.
- **England & Wales:** reasonable force only where necessary; Prison Rules (rule 47 in the
  Prison Rules 1999) prohibit unnecessary force and more force than necessary. Criminal and
  civil liability and the ECHR (Art. 3) apply.
- **International minimum:** UN Mandela Rules 43, 47–49 and 82 — no force or restraint as
  punishment; restraints only when no lesser alternative works, for the minimum time.

In every system: **force must end when the need ends.** Force on a compliant or restrained
person who is no longer a threat is, at best, a policy breach and frequently a crime.

## 2. De-escalation comes first wherever feasible
- Ask: is force *necessary now*, or can time, distance and talk resolve this? In custody there
  is usually time — the person is already contained.
- Use a single communicator; others stay back and quiet.
- Find out *why*: a grievance, fear, withdrawal, mental-health crisis, cognitive impairment or
  a medical problem (hypoglycaemia, head injury) can look like defiance.
- Record the de-escalation attempts — reviewers look for them first.

## 3. Spontaneous vs planned force
- **Spontaneous:** an immediate threat (assault in progress, self-harm in progress) leaves no
  time to plan. Use the minimum effective force, call for help and a supervisor, and transition
  to medical care and recording as soon as control is achieved.
- **Planned (calculated) intervention:** where there is time (e.g. a person barricaded in a cell
  but not at immediate risk), policy normally requires: supervisor/manager authority; further
  de-escalation attempts; healthcare informed and present; a briefing; trained team; **video
  recording from the briefing through to the end**, including the medical check; and a
  documented reason why force is still needed.

## 4. Restraint and medical safety
- Get the person off the chest and into a safer position as soon as possible; avoid weight on
  the back, neck or chest. Prolonged prone restraint is linked to restraint-related deaths.
- Treat "I can't breathe", sudden quiet, loss of consciousness, very high temperature or extreme
  agitation as a **medical emergency**: stop, reposition, summon healthcare immediately.
- Mechanical restraints only as authorised, never as punishment, never in stress positions, and
  reviewed and removed as soon as the need ends; record times applied and removed.
- A healthcare check after every use of force, and treatment for anyone injured.

## 5. Duty to intervene and to report
Officers present must intervene to stop force that is excessive or no longer necessary, and
report it. Failure to intervene can carry personal liability. Use the professional-standards
route if the normal chain is compromised.

## 6. Recording and review
Each officer writes an **individual** report before conferring, covering: the circumstances;
the person's specific behaviour (actions and words, not labels); what you perceived and
believed; de-escalation tried; each application of force and its effect; when force ended;
injuries and medical care; witnesses and footage. Late, identical or vague reports are red flags on review.

## Gotchas
- "Non-compliant" is not a threat in itself; passive non-compliance usually calls for time and talk.
- Force continuing after handcuffs are on needs very specific justification.
- Do not use force to enforce a minor rule when a disciplinary report would do.

## Verify
- [ ] Necessity and proportionality can be articulated for each application of force.
- [ ] De-escalation attempts recorded (or why none were feasible).
- [ ] Planned interventions authorised, briefed and recorded on video throughout.
- [ ] Person repositioned promptly; healthcare check done and recorded.
- [ ] Individual report written before conferring; restraint times logged.

*Uncertainty:* case law is summarised at headline level for US federal and England & Wales
only; rule numbers and policy titles change (e.g. UK training programmes have been renamed) —
confirm against your current agency policy and legal advice.
