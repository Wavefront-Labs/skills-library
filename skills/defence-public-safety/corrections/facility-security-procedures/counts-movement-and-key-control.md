# Counts, movement and key control

**Goal:** always know exactly *who* is in the facility, *where* each person is, and *who holds
every key and tool* — and prove it on paper — because escapes, assaults and weapons nearly
always exploit a gap in one of these three routines.

## 1. Counts (roll checks)
- **Formal counts** happen at fixed times set by policy (typically several per 24 h, including
  overnight). Movement stops; every person is counted where they are.
- **Principle:** count *a living person*, not a body shape, door card or bed. At night you must
  see skin and signs of life (breathing, movement) — the same standard as a welfare check.
- **Two-officer counts** where policy requires: count independently, compare, then report.
- **Report up, reconcile centrally:** each area reports its number to the control room (or count
  office), which reconciles against the master roll, including people out to court, hospital,
  work parties and other units.
- **Never "make the count fit".** A discrepancy is reported immediately as it stands.
- **Discrepancy procedure (typical):** recount the area → name-check (photo ID/card against the
  person) → full facility lockdown and emergency count if still wrong → escape / missing-person
  procedure (see `defence-public-safety.corrections.custodial-emergency-response.disturbance-hostage-and-escape-response-planning`).
- **Emergency counts** may be called at any time (after an incident, fog, power loss, alarm).

## 2. Movement control
- Every movement off a unit is **authorised and recorded**: destination, time out, time in,
  escorting officer or pass/movement slip, as policy requires.
- Control the *numbers* moving at once (controlled or "mass" movement periods are supervised
  from fixed posts; individual movement under escort or by pass).
- Keep **separation and non-association** lists in front of you during movement — moving two
  people who must be kept apart through the same corridor is a classic preventable assault.
- At destination, the receiving officer confirms arrival; overdue arrivals are chased at once.

## 3. Key control
- Keys are issued against a record (tally/chit system, signature, or electronic key cabinet),
  carried on a secured chain or pouch, **never shown, put down, or handed to a person in
  custody**, and never taken home.
- Check your keys on draw and on return; count the keys on the ring, not just the ring.
- **Lost, missing or compromised key** (seen by a prisoner long enough to copy, photographed,
  dropped in a public area): report immediately; policy will usually treat it as a security
  breach requiring lock changes. Delay is the failure, not the loss.
- Restricted keys (perimeter, armoury, emergency keys) have tighter issue rules and audits.

## 4. Tool and equipment control
- Tools in workshops, kitchens and maintenance are **classed by risk** and controlled by
  shadow boards, tags or tool-check sheets, counted out and in at each session.
- Contractors' tools are inventoried at the gate on entry and exit.
- A missing tool stops the session; nobody leaves until it is found or the incident is escalated.
- Kitchen knives and similar items are usually tethered or issued individually under signature.

## 5. Perimeter and gate procedure
- Scheduled and random checks of fences, walls, detection systems, lighting and cameras; log
  defects and report them for urgent repair.
- Gate/sallyport rule: one gate open at a time; identify every person and vehicle; search
  vehicles to policy before entry and exit.

## Records
Count sheets, movement logs, key registers, tool-check sheets and perimeter-check logs are
legal documents. Fill them in **at the time**, in ink or in the system, with the true time.
Retrospective entries must be marked as late entries with the reason.

## Gotchas
- The count most often goes wrong when people are "out": hospital escorts, court, segregation.
  Reconcile those first.
- Complacency kills these routines: the 1,000th clean count feels pointless — the 1,001st isn't.
- Never leave keys in a door "for a second".
- A tool counted as "probably in the bin" is a missing tool.

## Verify
- [ ] Every count you took saw a living person and was reported without adjustment.
- [ ] You know your establishment's discrepancy and lockdown sequence.
- [ ] Keys counted on draw and return; any concern reported at once.
- [ ] Tool sheets balance at session end.
- [ ] All logs show real times; late entries marked as such.

*Uncertainty:* the general structure is common across UK, US and Australasian correctional
practice, but count times, two-officer rules, key-loss thresholds and terminology are local
policy — this is a reference outline, not a substitute for your standing orders.
