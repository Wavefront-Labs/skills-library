# Disturbance, hostage and escape response planning

**Goal:** when something goes badly wrong in a custodial setting, the first officers raise the
alarm and contain it, a clear command structure takes over, specialist teams are used only
under authority, life is protected — staff, prisoners and public — and everything is recorded.

Emergencies in custody are run from **written contingency plans** that each establishment is
required to hold and exercise. This file explains the shape of those plans and the individual
officer's part in them; it is not a substitute for your local plans. Tactical team methods are
taught to certified teams and are deliberately not covered here.

## 1. Common first-officer actions (any emergency)
1. **Raise the alarm** by the approved method (radio code, alarm point) with location and nature:
   "Code red/blue/… at [location], [what], [how many]". Use your establishment's codes exactly.
2. **Preserve life, including your own.** Don't go in alone into an unsafe area to "sort it".
3. **Contain:** secure doors and gates to stop spread; withdraw staff and uninvolved prisoners
   from the affected area if safe.
4. **Observe and report** — numbers, names, weapons, injuries, fire, barricades — to control.
5. **Start a log**: times, decisions, who said what. Hand it to the commander when relieved.
6. Await instructions; don't negotiate terms or make promises unless you are the authorised
   negotiator.

## 2. Command structure
Most systems use a tiered command model (England & Wales: Gold/Silver/Bronze — strategic,
tactical, operational; US agencies commonly use the Incident Command System). One commander,
one plan, one log. The command suite opens; specialist resources (tactical response teams,
negotiators, healthcare, fire/police/ambulance) are requested through it. Notification to
headquarters and external agencies follows the plan's timelines.

## 3. Disturbance / concerted indiscipline
- Contain and isolate; lock down the rest of the facility; run an emergency count.
- Commander considers resolution options in rising order of intrusion: dialogue and time first,
  then planned intervention by trained teams under authority (see
  `defence-public-safety.corrections.use-of-force-in-custody.custody-use-of-force-policy-and-de-escalation`).
- Protect vulnerable people within the area where possible; plan for medical needs and fire.
- Record grievances raised; addressing legitimate ones through proper routes afterwards is part
  of preventing recurrence.

## 4. Hostage and barricade incidents
- First officers: contain, withdraw others, get information to command, **do not trade or
  promise anything**, keep any communication calm and noted until trained negotiators arrive.
- Trained negotiators lead communication; command controls all decisions. Time is generally on
  the side of a safe resolution.
- Staff held hostage: established policy in many jurisdictions is that hostage staff have no
  authority while held, and orders from them are not to be followed.
- Barricades (one person in a cell) are usually handled as planned interventions after
  de-escalation fails, with healthcare involved — check for self-harm risk first.

## 5. Escape and missing prisoner
- Count discrepancy or sighting → immediate report → lockdown → emergency count and name-check
  (see `defence-public-safety.corrections.facility-security-procedures.counts-movement-and-key-control`).
- Notify police with an up-to-date photo and description from the records system, risk
  information and any intelligence; follow the plan's notification list (victims' services may
  need to be told).
- Preserve the scene of the breach for investigation; search the establishment systematically
  in case the person is still inside.

## 6. Other plans you will be expected to know
Fire and evacuation (including how a locked establishment evacuates by zone), medical
emergencies including mass casualty, loss of utilities or communications, bomb threat,
external protest, severe weather, and pandemic. Know your role in each.

## 7. After the incident
- Medical check for everyone involved; trauma-support offer to staff and prisoners.
- Hot debrief, then a formal debrief; individual statements written before conferring.
- Preserve evidence and footage; potential criminal referral.
- Lessons learned fed back into the contingency plan and the next exercise.

## Gotchas
- The biggest failures are late alarms and officers acting alone. Call early; you can stand it down.
- Radio discipline: one voice, plain language, location first.
- Unplanned "rescue" by untrained staff can turn a contained incident into a fatal one.
- Plans that are never exercised don't work on the night.

*Uncertainty:* structure reflects publicly described UK and US practice; code words, command
names, hostage-staff policy and notification duties are local and should be checked against
current establishment contingency plans.
