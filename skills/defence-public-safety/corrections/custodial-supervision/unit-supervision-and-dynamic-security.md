# Unit supervision and dynamic security

**Goal:** keep a housing unit (wing, pod, block) safe, orderly and decent by *knowing the
people in it*, delivering the regime predictably, and holding professional boundaries —
so that problems are seen early and resolved by talking, not by force.

## 1. The three layers of security
- **Physical** — walls, doors, cameras, locks. Fixed; fails silently if nobody looks.
- **Procedural** — counts, searches, key control, movement rules (see
  `defence-public-safety.corrections.facility-security-procedures.counts-movement-and-key-control`).
- **Dynamic** — staff who are present, talk to people in custody, notice change and act on it.
  Most serious incidents are preceded by signals that *someone on the unit could have seen*:
  a usually chatty person gone quiet, people buying canteen for others, a crowd at one cell,
  a request to move unit, a rise in "accidental" injuries. Dynamic security is the only layer
  that picks those up.

Direct-supervision units (officer posted inside the living area, not behind glass) depend on
this layer most; podular-remote designs depend on it less but do not remove it.

## 2. A working shift
1. **Read the handover first:** unit log, observation lists (self-harm/suicide watch levels),
   new arrivals, people on restrictions, outstanding requests, anything flagged by the last shift.
2. **Walk the unit** early: see every person, not just the count. Note mood and atmosphere.
3. **Deliver the regime as published** — meals, exercise, phone, showers, visits, medication
   times. Unexplained changes to routine are a top driver of tension; if it must change, say why.
4. **Rounds and checks** at the frequency your policy and each person's observation level
   require. A welfare check means confirming the person is alive and well (movement, breathing,
   response), not glancing at a shape under a blanket. Log the *actual* time, never pre-fill.
5. **Handle requests and complaints** through the formal route and tell the person what will
   happen and when. An answered "no" is better than an ignored request.
6. **Write it down:** significant conversations, changes in behaviour, intelligence, and any
   incident, contemporaneously in the unit log or the intelligence/security report system.
7. **Handover** face to face where possible: risks, people of concern, unfinished business.

## 3. Communication that keeps order
- Firm, fair, consistent: the same rule applied the same way by every shift. Inconsistency
  between officers is exploited and resented.
- Use names, explain decisions, keep promises small and keep them.
- De-escalate early: listen, acknowledge the grievance, offer a legitimate route, give time.
  Use-of-force policy expects you to have tried this where feasible (see
  `defence-public-safety.corrections.use-of-force-in-custody.custody-use-of-force-policy-and-de-escalation`).
- Know the vulnerable: new arrivals, first-time prisoners, people with mental illness or
  learning disability, older prisoners, people targeted for their offence or identity.

## 4. Professional boundaries and conditioning
Conditioning (also "grooming of staff") is a gradual process in which a person in custody —
sometimes with outside help — moves an officer from small favours to rule-breaking.
Red flags in *yourself or a colleague*:
- Sharing personal details (home area, family, social media, money worries).
- Small favours outside policy ("just this once": an extra phone call, passing a note).
- Feeling a special rapport; defending one prisoner against colleagues.
- Spending disproportionate time with one person; being asked to keep secrets.
Any sexual contact with a person in custody is a criminal offence in essentially every
jurisdiction regardless of claimed consent (US: PREA standards and state custodial-sexual-
misconduct laws). Report boundary concerns through the counter-corruption/professional
standards route; early reporting protects the officer as well as the institution.

## 5. Legal and policy frame
Treatment must meet domestic law and agency policy, informed by international minimum
standards (UN Standard Minimum Rules — the *Nelson Mandela Rules*; European Prison Rules).
Collective punishment, unofficial "regime cuts", and informal punishment are prohibited;
sanctions go through the formal disciplinary/adjudication process.

## Gotchas
- A quiet unit is not a safe unit. Sudden silence or everyone in cells early is a warning sign.
- Pre-filling observation logs is a common finding in deaths-in-custody inquiries and can
  lead to criminal charges.
- Shouting commands across a unit invites an audience; take conversations somewhere quieter.
- "Good relationships" without boundaries are conditioning; boundaries without relationships
  produce no intelligence. You need both.

- [ ] All checks were done at the required frequency and logged at their real times.
- [ ] Regime changes were explained to the people affected.
- [ ] Concerns and intelligence were written up, not just mentioned verbally.
- [ ] You can name one boundary red flag and the reporting route in your establishment.

*Uncertainty:* written from widely published correctional-training content (UK HMPPS,
