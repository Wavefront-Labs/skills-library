# The system of motorcycle control — plan every hazard the same way

**Goal:** ride smoothly and safely at legal pace by processing each hazard (bend,
junction, vehicle, pedestrian) through one consistent system, and using observation
and position to buy time. Based on the UK police "Roadcraft" approach used by IAM
RoadSmart and RoSPA; formal advanced training with an observer is the best way to learn it.

## 1. The system: IPSGA
Applied in order at every hazard, skipping phases not needed:
1. **Information** — take (look all around, mirrors, shoulder/"lifesaver" check), use
   (plan), give (signals, horn, lights where helpful). Runs through all phases.
2. **Position** — the safest position for the hazard, then the best view, then
   stability/progress.
3. **Speed** — adjust with throttle and brakes so you can stop in the distance you can
   see to be clear on your side of the road.
4. **Gear** — select the gear for the new speed once speed is set (not to slow down).
5. **Acceleration** — leave the hazard with smooth, appropriate acceleration.

## 2. Observation
- Look far ahead, middle distance, near — and back to far. Scan; don't stare.
- Read clues: a hedge line or telegraph poles show the bend's direction, parked-car
  gaps hide pedestrians, wheels of waiting cars show whether they're about to move,
  a bus at a stop means people crossing in front of it.
- **Limit point** (vanishing point): the furthest point to which you can see the road
  surface. If it's coming toward you, slow down; if it holds steady, keep speed; if it
  moves away, accelerate gently.
- Two-second following gap in dry; at least four in wet. Count it.

## 3. Positioning
- **Safety first:** keep away from hazards — move away from parked cars, junction
  mouths, oncoming lorries; give space for errors.
- Then **view:** e.g. on a left-hand bend (left-hand traffic) a position toward the
  centre line (if safe) opens the view earlier; on a right-hand bend move toward the
  nearside. Mirror for right-hand traffic. Abandon a view position the moment it brings
  you close to oncoming traffic or hides you.
- Stay out of blind spots; be visible to drivers who may emerge.

## 4. Cornering
- Speed set **before** the bend (IPSGA: brakes and gear done upright), stable throttle
  through it, drive out as the limit point opens.
- Avoid apexing early; an early apex sends you wide at the exit. Keep a margin to the
  centre line — oncoming vehicles cut corners.
- See transportation-logistics.motorcycling-transport.motorcycles.braking-cornering-and-machine-control
  for the physical technique (countersteering, braking in bends).

## 5. Overtaking (three-stage)
1. Following position with a view; 2. Overtaking position (closer, offset) checking for
the opportunity; 3. Overtake decisively only when you can see enough clear road to
complete it and return safely, with no junctions or bends that hide oncoming traffic.
Ask: Can I see? Is it safe? Is it legal? Is it necessary?

## 6. Commentary practice
Speak out what you see and plan ("30 limit, parked van left, child on pavement,
moving out, covering brake…") on practice rides (into a helmet recorder if you
like). It exposes gaps in observation fast.

## Gotchas
- Using gears to slow instead of brakes (no brake light, upsets the chassis).
- Holding a "view" position into oncoming traffic.
- Rushing the system — information must run continuously; don't change gear mid-corner.
- Advanced riding is about safety margins, not speed.

## Verify
- [ ] Can apply IPSGA aloud at each hazard on a test route.
- [ ] Following gap measured and kept (2 s dry / 4 s wet).
- [ ] Speed set before each bend; limit point used to judge it.
- [ ] Overtakes planned in stages; aborted when any check fails.
- [ ] Observed ride with a qualified advanced instructor completed.

*Uncertainty:* positioning examples assume left-hand traffic; legality of specific techniques varies by jurisdiction.
