# Multi-drop city despatch — fast, reliable, still upright at the end of the day

**Goal:** earn well as a point-to-point motorcycle courier (documents, small parcels,
medical samples, urgent spares) by sequencing jobs smartly, delivering with clean
proof, and staying safe and dry through long shifts in all weather.

## 1. Legal and kit basics
- **Insurance:** ordinary commuting cover is invalid for paid deliveries — you need
  **hire-and-reward / courier** cover, plus **goods-in-transit** and public liability as
  required by the courier company. Check before the first job.
- Bike: reliable and economical (125–700 cc commonly used), luggage: lockable top box
  and/or panniers, tank bag for paperwork and phone mount with charger.
- Kit for 8–10 hours outside: two-piece waterproof textile or one-piece oversuit,
  waterproof gloves + spare pair, heated grips, neck tube, pinlock visor insert.

## 2. Taking and sequencing jobs
- Read each job fully: pickup and drop addresses, contacts, deadline, size/weight,
  special instructions (signature, ID check, cold chain, upright).
- With several jobs: sequence by **deadline first, then geography**. Rough method:
  1. List hard deadlines.
  2. Order pickups/drops so you never backtrack across the city centre more than once.
  3. Pick up before you drop when it lies on the way, but don't carry a time-critical item
     past its drop to collect another.
- Tell control early if a deadline is at risk; a heads-up 30 minutes out is a service,
  a "sorry it's late" afterwards is a complaint.
- Know where the **loading bays, motorcycle bays and building goods entrances** are for
  regular clients — parking and finding the reception often take longer than riding.

## 3. Routing in the city
- Learn the city's arteries, one-way systems, bus lanes motorcycles may use (varies by
  city), and peak congestion patterns; a nav app is a guide, not a substitute.
- Filtering where legal saves the most time — but it's also where most courier crashes
  happen (U-turning cars, opening doors, pedestrians). See
  transportation-logistics.motorcycling-transport.roadcraft.system-of-motorcycle-control.
- Avoid repeated short-cuts through residential streets with speed bumps and children.

## 4. Pickup and delivery discipline
- At pickup: check item count, packaging adequate, address label clear; photograph if
  valuable; note any damage before taking it.
- Secure items in the box (not loose in a backpack for heavy items — it loads your
  spine in a crash).
- At delivery: deliver to the named person/reception per instructions; capture **POD**
  (signature, name in capitals, time, photo where required). No safe-place drops unless
  authorised.
- Failed delivery: call control and the recipient before leaving; never leave a
  signature-required item.

## 5. Shift management
- Fuel at the start of the shift; don't run on reserve between jobs.
- Eat and drink regularly; dehydration and cold slow reactions. Take short breaks every
  2–3 hours.
- End-of-day fatigue increases risk — the last jobs are when most mistakes happen.
- Track earnings vs costs (fuel, tyres, chain, insurance) per day to know your real rate.

## 6. Bike care for high mileage
Weekly: chain clean/lube/tension, tyre pressure and tread, brake pads. Monthly: lights,
cables, oil. Couriers wear out tyres and chains fast — budget for it.

## Gotchas
- Working without hire-and-reward insurance voids cover in a crash.
- Phone handling while riding is illegal in many places — set up the route before moving.
- Deadlines tempt speeding; the fine or crash costs more than the job pays.
- Wet gloves = cold hands = slow brakes: carry spares.

## Verify
- [ ] Correct hire-and-reward and goods-in-transit insurance in place.
- [ ] Multi-job runs sequenced by deadline then geography; control warned of risks early.
- [ ] Every delivery has POD meeting the job's instructions.
- [ ] Bike checked weekly; shift includes breaks, food and water.

*Uncertainty:* insurance categories and bus-lane access differ by country and city.
