# Coastal fixes, buoyage and tides — navigating with what you can see

**Goal:** fix position and stay in safe water near land using visual references, read
buoys correctly, and work out the tide height to know if there is enough water.

## 1. Visual fixes
- **Three-point fix:** take hand-bearing compass bearings of three charted, well-spaced
  objects (ideally 60–120° apart), quickly, beam objects last (their bearings change
  fastest). Convert magnetic → true, plot. A small triangle (cocked hat) is normal; plot
  the position at the corner nearest danger if in doubt. A big triangle = an error
  (misidentified object, wrong variation) — retake.
- **Transit:** two charted objects in line give an exact position line with no compass
  error. Use them to check compass deviation too.
- Range + bearing from radar or one object gives a quick fix.

## 2. Clearing lines and leading lines
- **Leading line:** a charted transit (often leading lights/marks) that leads safely
  in — keep the marks in line.
- **Clearing bearing:** draw a line from a conspicuous mark that passes clear of a
  danger; label it "NMT 045°" (not more than) or "NLT 045°". While the bearing of the
  mark stays on the safe side, you're clear. Write them on the plan before you go.

## 3. IALA buoyage
- **Region A** (Europe, Africa, most of Asia, Australia): port-hand marks **red**
  (cans), starboard-hand **green** (cones) when entering from seaward ("direction of
  buoyage").
- **Region B** (Americas, Japan, Korea, Philippines): reversed — "red right returning".
- **Cardinal marks** (both regions): black/yellow, topmarks of two cones; pass on the
  named side. North ▲▲ (points up), South ▼▼, East ◆ (cones base to base), West ⧗
  (points together). Lights: N continuous quick flashes; E 3; S 6 + long flash; W 9
  (think clock face).
- **Isolated danger:** black with red bands, two black balls — danger right under it.
- **Safe water:** red/white vertical stripes, one red ball — deep water all round.
- **Special:** yellow. **New danger/emergency wreck:** blue and yellow.

## 4. Tidal height
- Chart depths are below **chart datum** (usually close to Lowest Astronomical Tide);
  height of tide adds to them. Drying heights (underlined) are above datum.
- From tide tables: HW and LW times and heights; **range** = HW − LW.
- **Rule of twelfths** (approximate, for semi-diurnal tides): in hours 1–6 after LW the
  tide rises 1, 2, 3, 3, 2, 1 twelfths of the range. Example: range 4.8 m, 3 h after LW
  → 1+2+3 = 6/12 → +2.4 m above LW.
- Standard/secondary port curves give better accuracy; the rule fails at places with
  odd tide curves (e.g. double high waters).
- Depth available = charted depth + height of tide; required = draught + safety margin.

## 5. Tidal streams
From the tidal atlas or chart diamonds: direction and rate each hour, springs vs neaps
(interpolate by range). Plan to carry favourable streams and avoid wind-against-tide
races at headlands.

## 6. Pilotage plan for a harbour entry
Before arrival, on a notebook page: approach waypoint, transits/leading lines, each
buoy in order with its characteristics, turning points, clearing bearings, depths at
the expected tide, VHF channel, where to wait if it doesn't look right.

## Gotchas
- Misidentifying a mark: confirm by light characteristic and shape, not colour alone at dusk.
- Using magnetic bearings on a true plot without converting.
- Falling tide: plan for the water when you leave, not just when you arrive.
- Floating marks move and drag; use fixed marks for fixes when available.

## Verify
- [ ] Fix triangle small and position consistent with depth sounder.
- [ ] Clearing bearings and leading lines prepared and written down.
- [ ] Each buoy identified by shape, colour, topmark and light.
- [ ] Tidal height calculated for the critical time with margin.

*Uncertainty:* rule of twelfths is approximate; secondary-port corrections and local tidal anomalies must come from official tables.
