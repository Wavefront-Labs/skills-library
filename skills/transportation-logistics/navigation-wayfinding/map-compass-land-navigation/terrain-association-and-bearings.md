# Terrain association and bearings — navigate on foot without a screen

**Goal:** know where you are on a topographic map at all times and travel accurately
to a target, in good or poor visibility, with a baseplate compass (Silva/Suunto type).

## 1. Read the map
- **Scale:** 1:25,000 → 1 cm = 0.25 km (4 cm = 1 km). US 7.5-minute quads are 1:24,000 (1 inch = 2,000 feet).
- **Contours:** interval printed in the legend (it varies with terrain and map scale). Close together =
  steep; V's pointing uphill = valley/stream; V's pointing downhill = spur/ridge;
  closed rings = hill tops (or depressions, with tick marks).
- **Grid references:** eastings first ("along the corridor and up the stairs").
- Note **magnetic declination** in the margin (with annual change); compute current value.

## 2. Set (orient) the map
Turn the map so features on it line up with features on the ground (or align grid north
with the compass needle, corrected for declination). Keep it set as you walk — thumb on
your position, re-fold so your route is visible.

## 3. Take a bearing from the map
1. Lay the compass edge from where you are to where you're going (direction-of-travel
   arrow pointing to the target).
2. Rotate the housing until its orienting lines are parallel to the map's north-south
   grid lines, **orienting arrow pointing to map north** (top of map).
3. Read the grid bearing at the index line.
4. **Adjust for declination:** convert grid → magnetic, working out the sign from the
   declination diagram for your area and sanity-checking it. Many compasses have an
   adjustable declination setting — set once and skip the arithmetic.
5. Hold the compass flat in front of you, turn your body until the red needle sits in
   the orienting arrow ("red in the shed"), and walk where the travel arrow points.
   Pick a feature on the line ahead, walk to it, repeat.

## 4. Distance: pacing and timing
- **Pace count:** count double-steps per 100 m on known ground; adjust uphill, in heather,
  snow or at night. Use a tally or pacing beads.
- **Timing:** time each leg with a watch, allowing for fitness, load, ascent and terrain.
  Calibrate your own speeds.

## 5. Terrain association techniques
- **Handrail:** follow a linear feature (wall, stream, ridge, path) toward the target.
- **Catching feature:** a feature beyond the target that tells you you've overshot
  (a stream, a fence).
- **Attack point:** an easy-to-find feature near the target; navigate precisely only
  for the last short leg.
- **Aiming off:** to hit a point on a linear feature (bridge on a river), aim deliberately
  to one side, so when you hit the river you know which way to turn.
- **Relocation:** if lost, stop. Note the last known point and time/distance since;
  set the map, look at slope aspect (compass on the fall line), surrounding shape;
  take back-bearings to two identifiable features to resect.

## 6. Poor visibility
Short legs between definite features, careful bearings and pacing, a partner checking
the bearing; avoid steep ground and cornices. Turn back early — a planned escape route
should exist.

## Gotchas
- Compass near metal, phones or magnets → wrong bearing.
- Orienting arrow pointed to map south: 180° error. Sanity-check the bearing roughly.
- Following a path on the ground that isn't the path on the map.
- Declination sign applied backwards (east vs west regions).

## Verify
- [ ] Declination value and direction known for the date and area.
- [ ] Bearings taken with orienting arrow to map north and sanity-checked.
- [ ] Pace count calibrated on 100 m of similar terrain.
- [ ] Each leg has an attack point or catching feature.
- [ ] You can relocate using map set + slope aspect + back-bearings.

*Uncertainty:* pace counts and timings vary widely between people and terrain; calibrate your own.
