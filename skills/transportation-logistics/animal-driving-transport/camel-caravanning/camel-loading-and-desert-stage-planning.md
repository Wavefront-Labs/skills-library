# Camel loading and desert stage planning

**Goal:** a camel string that covers distance day after day across dry country, with
loads that don't shift or sore the animals and a water plan that holds when a well is dry.

> Draft: assembled from general cameleering practice (dromedaries, Australian and
> North African trekking). Load and distance figures are wide rules of thumb; camel
> handling is regional and learned with experienced handlers. Camels kick in all
> directions and bite: never work them alone as a beginner.

## 1. Couching (making the camel kneel)
- Stand at the camel's shoulder holding the lead (halter or nose-line — use a nose
  peg line only with very light tension; it tears easily).
- Pull the head **down and slightly back** with gentle repeated tugs and give the
  local command (often "hooshta" in Australian use, "khh-khh" sounds in parts of the
  Arab world).
- The camel drops to its front knees, then hind, then settles. Keep out of the way of
  the front legs as it goes down.
- For loading, **hobble or tie a front leg folded** (knee hobble) so it can't stand mid-load.

## 2. Loading
1. Pad and saddle: pack saddle sits **over or around the hump** depending on type,
   frame clear of the hump and the spine. Check the pad for burrs and grit.
2. **Weigh both sides** and match within ~1–2 kg; heavy dense items low.
3. Typical working pack loads: ~150–250 kg for a strong, conditioned dromedary on
   moderate stages, less on long, hot or rough stages; weak or young animals far less.
   Treat this as an outer range, not a target.
4. Lash both sides at once with two people where possible; the camel rocks forward
   and back hard as it stands — loads must be tight to the frame.
5. **Water containers**: split evenly; jerricans each side, never all on one camel.
6. Stand the camel up and check: load level, no rubbing on the hump or ribs.

## 3. Stringing
- Tie each camel's lead to the saddle of the one ahead, with a **breakable link**
  (light cord) so a falling or panicking camel doesn't take the string with it.
- 4–8 camels per handler on foot; quiet, experienced camels at the front and back.
- Lead on foot at a steady walk (~4–5 km/h).

## 4. Stage planning
- Plan each stage between **known water** (wells, bores, waterholes) and confirm
  status with recent local information — never trust an old map alone.
- Daily distance with loads: commonly ~20–35 km depending on terrain, heat and
  condition; less in soft sand dunes.
- **Water for people**: plan ~5–10 L per person per day in hot desert, plus a reserve
  for at least one dry well (one extra stage).
- **Camels**: a conditioned camel can go several days without water in cooler
  weather if it has green feed; in summer heat, much less. When they drink after
  a long gap, they can take a lot (on the order of 100 L or more) — allow time.
- **Grazing**: camels need several hours of browse a day; travel early and late,
  rest in the heat, let them feed at stops.

## 5. Condition monitoring
- **Hump size and firmness**: shrinking hump = burning reserves.
- Check **saddle areas** every evening for heat, swelling or rubbed hair — move the
  load or rest the animal before it becomes an open sore.
- Feet: soft pads are cut by sharp stony ground; slow down and check.
- Behaviour: a camel lagging or lying down early is a warning sign.

## 6. Gotchas
- Bulls in rut (season) are aggressive and unpredictable — avoid them or manage with
  experienced handlers.
- A load that shifts while the camel stands is a load that will slide off at a trot.
- Unhobbled camels at night can walk many kilometres; hobble and bell them.

## Verify
- [ ] Every camel was couched and hobbled before loading.
- [ ] Load sides weighed and matched, frame clear of the hump and spine.
- [ ] Each stage ends at confirmed water, with reserve for one failed source.
- [ ] Saddle areas checked every evening; sore spots acted on.
- [ ] String links are breakable.
