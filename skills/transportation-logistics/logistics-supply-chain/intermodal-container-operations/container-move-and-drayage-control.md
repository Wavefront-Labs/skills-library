# Container move and drayage control — keep boxes moving, stop the meter

**Goal:** get each container from vessel to door (or door to vessel) through terminals,
rail ramps and trucks on time, and avoid the demurrage, detention and per-diem
charges that often exceed the drayage itself.

## 1. Know the clocks
- **Demurrage:** charged by the line/terminal while the full container stays in the
  terminal or rail yard past **free time** (often 3–5 days at the port).
- **Detention / per diem:** charged while the container (and possibly the line's chassis)
  is outside the terminal past free days, until the empty is returned.
- **Last free day (LFD)** and **empty return date** — record both per container, from
  the carrier/terminal, in your tracker. Terminal storage fees can stack on top.
Free time terms are negotiable in the ocean contract — ask before you need it.

## 2. Import sequence
1. Before arrival: confirm customs status, freight paid/released (B/L surrendered or
   telex/express release), and delivery appointment at the consignee.
2. Vessel discharged → container **available** (all holds cleared: customs, line, terminal).
3. Book a **terminal appointment** for pickup (most big ports run appointment systems);
   arrange **chassis** (pool, line-provided or trucker-owned, depending on region).
4. Drayage to consignee: **live unload** (driver waits, typically 2 free hours) or
   **drop** (driver leaves the box on chassis, comes back for the empty).
5. Return the empty to the location the line designates (not always the same
   terminal) before the detention clock runs out; get the **out-gate/in-gate (EIR)**
   receipts.

## 3. Export sequence
1. Booking → release number for an empty → pick up the right size/type (check
   condition, food-grade if needed).
2. Load at shipper; seal; record seal number and **VGM**.
3. Return full to the terminal inside the **receiving window** (early return date) and
   before **port cut-off**; documentation and VGM cut-offs are separate and earlier.

## 4. Intermodal rail legs
- Inland points served by rail ramps: carrier haulage (line arranges rail + truck,
  through B/L) vs merchant haulage (you arrange). Carrier haulage often extends free time.
- Ramp has its own free time and storage rules; tracking events come from the railroad.
- Domestic intermodal (53' containers in North America) loads double-stack; respect the
  railroad's weight and loading rules (heavy at bottom, specific load-securing standards).

## 5. Reducing cost and empty miles
- **Street turn:** reuse an import empty directly for an export booking with the same
  line, size and nearby location — needs line approval; saves a terminal trip.
- Pre-pull containers to a yard before LFD when the consignee can't receive yet —
  yard storage plus a second move is often cheaper than demurrage.
- Track per container in a simple board: ETA, available, LFD, picked, delivered,
  empty returned, return deadline. Review daily; exception = within 2 days of a deadline.

## Gotchas
- "Available" in one system ≠ available at the gate — check all holds, including
  customs exams that move the box off-terminal.
- Weekend/holiday closures may still count toward free time (contract-dependent).
- Overweight containers need tri-axle chassis or permits; check before dispatch.
- Lost EIRs make detention disputes unwinnable — keep gate records and timestamps.

## Verify
- [ ] Every open container has LFD and return-by date recorded and owned.
- [ ] Pickup appointments booked before LFD with a day's margin.
- [ ] Empties returned to the designated depot with EIR saved.
- [ ] Demurrage/detention invoices checked against your own timestamps.
- [ ] Street-turn opportunities reviewed weekly with import and export teams.

*Uncertainty:* free-time lengths and chassis models differ widely by port, country and carrier; numbers are illustrative.
