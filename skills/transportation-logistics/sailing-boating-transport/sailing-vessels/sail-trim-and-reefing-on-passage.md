# Sail trim and reefing on passage

**Goal:** make efficient progress under sail — especially upwind — while keeping the
boat balanced, the crew rested and the rig unstressed.

*Draft: reefing points and sail combinations are boat-specific; the true-wind
thresholds given are typical for a ~10–12 m cruising yacht. Learn your boat's.*

## 1. Headsail trim (telltales)
- Telltales near the luff, on both sides. Steer or trim so **both stream aft**.
  - Windward telltale lifting/fluttering → you're pinching or sail too eased: bear
    away or sheet in.
  - Leeward telltale stalling → too far off or sheet too tight: head up or ease.
- **Sheet car position:** with the car correct, all windward telltales (top to bottom)
  lift at the same time as you head up. Top lifts first → move car forward (top too
  open); bottom first → car aft.
- Halyard tension: wrinkles from the luff (horizontal) → more tension; vertical crease
  → less.

## 2. Mainsail trim
- **Mainsheet** controls leech tension and twist; **traveller** controls the boom's
  angle to the centreline. Upwind: sheet for twist (top batten roughly parallel to
  boom), traveller for angle.
- **Kicker/vang** holds the boom down off the wind when sheet tension can't.
- **Outhaul and cunningham:** tighter flattens the sail (depower in more wind); looser
  for power in light air/chop.
- **Balance:** lots of weather helm = too much main power for the wind. Ease the
  traveller down, flatten, or reef — rudder angle is drag.

## 3. Reef early
- Rule: **if you're wondering whether to reef, reef.** Shaking out a reef is easy;
  putting one in when overpowered is hard and risky.
- Typical triggers for a cruising boat: first reef ~15–18 kn true, second reef
  ~20–25 kn, third/storm sails above (*boat-specific*). Or reef when heel regularly
  exceeds ~20–25°, or weather helm becomes heavy.
- Reef **before dark** if the forecast suggests you'll need it overnight.

### Slab (jiffy) reefing sequence
1. Ease the mainsheet (and vang) to depower; head slightly up.
2. Take weight on the topping lift if fitted.
3. Lower the halyard to the reef mark; hook the tack cringle (or tension tack line).
4. Re-tension the halyard.
5. Haul in the clew reef line hard (pulls the foot flat and the leech down).
6. Re-trim; tie in loose sail if required (ties around the sail, not the boom fittings).

Roller-furling headsail: reduce in matching steps to keep balance; very deep reefs
make a poor shape — consider a dedicated heavy-weather jib.

## 4. Upwind passage strategy
- Sail for **VMG** (velocity made good toward the waypoint), not pointing angle;
  footing a little faster often gains more.
- Tack on headers (wind shifts that force you away) when shifts are regular.
- With tidal streams, plan legs to have the tide under the lee bow if possible.

## 5. Heavy weather
- Storm jib and/or trysail, or deeply reefed main; secure everything below, jacklines
  and tethers on.
- **Heave-to** to rest, cook or make repairs: tack without releasing the jib, so it's
  backed; ease the main; lash the helm to windward. The boat lies quietly ~45–60° off
  the wind and drifts slowly.

## 6. Short-handed technique
Lead lines aft to the cockpit; use the autopilot/windvane while trimming; plan sail
changes at watch change so two people are awake.

## Gotchas
- Reefing with the boom sheeted in hard — the sail won't come down.
- Over-tight headsail sheet in chop: the boat stalls.
- Forgetting to ease the vang before reefing.

## Verify
- [ ] Headsail telltales streaming on both sides when on course.
- [ ] Helm balanced (small rudder angle) after each trim change.
- [ ] Reef triggers written down for your boat and followed.
- [ ] Crew has practised reefing and heaving-to in moderate conditions.
