# Guest briefing and anchorage selection

**Goal:** guests have a safe, relaxed trip; the boat comes back undamaged; and
nobody's night is spent dragging anchor.

*Draft: commercial requirements (licences, coding/flag rules, maximum guests,
mandatory briefings) vary by country. Anchoring figures are rules of thumb.*

## 1. Safety briefing (before leaving the dock)
Keep it short, practical and on deck, and repeat key points later:
- **Lifejackets:** where they are, how to put one on (actually do it), when they
  must be worn (night, dinghy rides, bad weather, non-swimmers always).
- **Moving around:** one hand for you, one for the boat; no bare feet on deck work.
- **Man overboard:** shout, point, throw — and who does what.
- **Heads:** what goes in (nothing that wasn't eaten first), how to pump, seacock
  positions. Blocked heads ruin charters.
- **Gas:** turn on at the bottle/solenoid, off after cooking; smell gas → turn off,
  no switches, ventilate.
- **Emergency equipment:** liferaft, fire extinguishers, first aid, VHF (how to make
  a Mayday call), grab bag.
- **Dinghy:** lifejackets, kill cord, no night trips without lights, return times.
- Ask about medical conditions, swimming ability, seasickness.

## 2. Itinerary
- Start with the **weather for the whole week** and plan the longest or most exposed
  passage for the best day; have a plan B for every leg.
- Match guest goals: short sails and long swims vs. covering miles. Ask on day one.
- Keep daily sailing to what's enjoyable — typically 3–5 hours for holiday guests.
- Book or know alternatives for marinas in peak season; arrive early at popular
  anchorages.

## 3. Choosing an anchorage
- **Shelter** from current and *forecast* wind, including overnight shifts (sea
  breezes die; land breezes or fronts swing the wind).
- **Holding ground:** sand and mud good; weed and rock poor. Check charts/pilot guides,
  look through clear water.
- **Depth and swinging room:** enough room for your scope and others' swing in all
  directions. Note boats on all-chain vs rope.
- Exit route at night if the wind turns onshore.

## 4. Setting the anchor
1. Approach into the wind, stop the boat, lower the anchor to the bottom (don't drop
   the pile on top of it).
2. Fall back as you pay out **scope**: commonly 4–5× depth with all-chain in good
   conditions, 7× or more with rope or in strong winds (depth includes bow height and
   tide rise).
3. **Set it:** gentle reverse, then increase to firm astern power; take transits
   (two objects lined up abeam) — they shouldn't move.
4. Snubber/bridle on for chain.
5. **Anchor alarm** on (plotter/app) with a sensible radius; note your swing circle.
6. Check again before sleeping and at any wind change.

## 5. Leading the small crew
- Brief the day's plan each morning; give guests jobs they want (lines, helming)
  with clear instructions.
- Keep watch on alcohol and sun; stop activities before people are tired.
- One skipper voice in manoeuvres — explain afterwards, not during.

## Gotchas
- Anchoring for today's wind when a shift is forecast at 0300.
- Guests on deck barefoot during anchoring with chain running.
- Dinghy left tied short behind the boat in swell.

## Verify
- [ ] Safety briefing given and lifejacket fitting done on day one.
- [ ] Each day's leg chosen against the weekly forecast with a plan B.
- [ ] Anchor set with power and transits/alarm checked.
- [ ] Overnight wind forecast checked against the anchorage's shelter.
