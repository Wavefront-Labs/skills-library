# Density altitude, canyon turns and one-way strips

**Goal:** fly into terrain where the airplane performs worse than you're used to and
there's often no second chance — while always keeping an escape.

> Draft: general mountain-flying course material (e.g. US Forest Service / Idaho
> backcountry style). Real mountain flying requires dual instruction from a
> mountain-experienced instructor; the numbers below are rules of thumb only.

## 1. Density altitude go/no-go
- DA ≈ pressure altitude + 120 ft × (OAT − ISA temp). A 5,000 ft pressure-altitude strip at 30 °C (ISA 5 °C) gives 8,000 ft by this rule; an exact ISA density calculation (dry air) gives about 7,800 ft, so the rule is a slight over-estimate here. The 120 ft/°C figure is a common approximation, not an FAA-stated value.
- Rough effect for normally aspirated singles: takeoff roll increases at least ~10% per 1,000 ft of DA by one popular rule; the FAA density-altitude publication (P-8740-02) gives no fixed percentage and points to the POH or the Koch chart, so use those. Climb rate drops dramatically.
- Use POH charts at actual weight, then apply a margin (e.g. ×1.5 for an unprepared
  strip (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)). **Fly early in the morning** — DA is lowest, air is smoothest.
- Lean the mixture for best power before takeoff at high DA (per POH procedure).

## 2. Weather and wind
- Wind over ridges: **> ~20–25 kt at ridge level** means strong turbulence and
  downdrafts on the lee side — many instructors won't fly mountain routes then (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked).
- Lenticular and rotor clouds = mountain wave.
- Plan to fly on the **upwind (lift) side** of a valley, and approach ridges where
  updrafts help.

## 3. Ridge crossing
- Arrive at least **1,000–2,000 ft above the ridge** (more in wind).
- Approach the ridge at **45°**, so a turn away to lower terrain is short if you meet
  sink or turbulence. Never approach head-on.
- Commit to crossing only when you can see over it and are clearly above it.

## 4. Canyon flying and the canyon turn
- Fly along **one side** of the canyon (usually the right side, traffic convention;
  also the lift side if wind allows) — this leaves the whole width to turn.
- Slow to maneuvering speed or below with some flap (per your type and training)
  to tighten the radius *before* you need to turn.
- **Canyon turn** (one taught version): from the side of the canyon, a positive,
  coordinated turn of ~45–60° bank toward the open side, using the whole width,
  keeping speed — do not let the nose drop or speed decay. Practise at altitude first.
- Rule: never fly up a canyon you can't turn around in or climb out of.

## 5. One-way strips
- Many backcountry strips are **one-way**: land uphill, take off downhill regardless of
  light wind — go-around after a certain point is impossible.
1. Recon pass: surface, obstructions, animals, windsock, slope, the **go-around /
   abort point** on approach.
2. Fly a stabilised approach at the right speed; if not stable or not on target by the
   abort point → go around **before** it.
3. Takeoff: compute ground run; mark an **abort point** on the strip (e.g. the 50%
   distance where you must have ~70% of rotation speed — a common rule of thumb (gated: unconfirmed, source is paywalled or blocked)). Not
   achieved → abort.

## 6. Self-sufficiency
- Survival kit for the terrain (water, shelter, fire, signaling, PLB/ELT, first aid).
- File a flight plan or leave a detailed route with someone; terrain blocks radio.
- Extra fuel — reroutes around weather are long.

## Gotchas
- An airplane that just climbed fine at sea level may barely climb at 9,000 ft DA with
  three people.
- Downdrafts can exceed the climb rate of a light airplane; that's why 45° and margin.
- Visual illusions: upsloping strips look "too high" causing low approaches; the horizon
  hidden by terrain confuses pitch attitude.
