# Rejected takeoff and SOP flows (multi-crew jets)

**Goal:** a takeoff where both pilots know in advance exactly what will make them stop,
who decides, and what each hand does — so a reject at 130 kt is a drill, not a debate.

> Draft: general Airbus/Boeing-style practice. Your operator's FCOM/FCTM and SOPs
> define the actual speeds (e.g. 80 vs 100 kt check), callouts and who may call
> "stop". This is study material, not type training.

## 1. Flows and checklists
- **Flow then checklist**: each pilot does their area-of-responsibility flow from
  memory (panels in a fixed scan order), then the checklist *confirms* critical items.
  The checklist never replaces the flow, and vice versa.
- Read-do vs challenge-response: know which your operator uses for each phase.
- Interrupted checklist: restart from the top of the section, not "where we were".

## 2. The takeoff briefing (keep it short, specific)
Cover: runway, intersection and **runway remaining**; flap and thrust setting; V1/VR/V2
and how they were calculated; wet/contaminated; the **engine-failure procedure**
(EO SID or straight-ahead, acceleration altitude); threats (terrain, wind shear,
birds); and the **reject criteria**:
- *Typical:* below a low-speed check (80 or 100 kt): stop for any significant
  failure or caution.
- Between that speed and V1: stop **only** for engine failure/fire, predictive wind
  shear or a warning that makes the aircraft unsafe or unable to fly (operator list).
- **At or above V1: continue** — handle the failure in the air.

## 3. Callouts during the roll (illustrative)
- PF: sets thrust → PM checks thrust set ("thrust set" / "N1 set").
- PM: "80 knots" (or "100 knots") → PF crosschecks airspeed ("checked").
- PM: "V1" (some aircraft call automatically) → PF removes hand from thrust levers
  (operator dependent — many keep the captain's hand on until V1).
- PM: "Rotate" at VR. PF rotates at ~3°/s (type-specific).
- PM: "Positive rate" → PF: "Gear up".

## 4. Executing a rejected takeoff
Who calls: commonly **only the captain decides "STOP"**, whoever is flying.
Simultaneously and immediately:
1. **Thrust levers to idle.**
2. **Autobrake RTO** activates (if armed) — or maximum manual braking.
3. **Reverse thrust** as required (max reverse on most types for an RTO).
4. Speedbrakes/spoilers deploy (check, deploy manually if not).
5. Maintain centreline with rudder, then nosewheel steering as speed reduces.
6. PM calls deviations ("no spoilers", "reversers unlocked", decelerating speed).
7. Stop the aircraft, set parking brake, PA to cabin ("attention crew, at stations").
8. Fire/evacuation assessment, then the relevant checklist (e.g. evacuation or
   rejected takeoff checklist).
9. Consider **brake temperatures** — a high-energy RTO can lead to fuse plug release
   or brake fire; keep fire services informed and don't set off taxiing.

## 5. Gotchas
- Most RTO overrun accidents historically involved a reject initiated **after V1**, or
  late. The go/no-go decision must be made before V1; V1 is the latest point to *act*.
- A blown tyre or a caution at high speed is usually better handled airborne.
- Never retard thrust "a bit" to see — commit to one decision.
- Assumed temperature/derated takeoffs reduce margins on a contaminated runway;
  confirm the performance basis.
- Brief the RTO even on the hundredth takeoff — complacency is the threat.

## Verify
- [ ] The briefing stated V-speeds, runway remaining and the exact reject criteria.
- [ ] Both pilots know who can call "stop".
- [ ] Callouts at 80/100 kt, V1 and VR are made and acknowledged.
- [ ] You can recite the RTO actions in order without a checklist.
- [ ] After any reject, brake temperatures and fire risk were addressed before taxiing.
