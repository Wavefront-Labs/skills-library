# Threat and error management (TEM) in the cockpit

**Goal:** anticipate what can bite this flight, catch your own and each other's errors
before they become an undesired aircraft state, and make decisions both pilots own.

> Draft: based on the widely taught ICAO/University of Texas TEM model and common
> airline CRM practice. Your operator's CRM programme, SOPs and callouts take precedence.

## 1. The model
- **Threats**: things outside the crew's control that increase risk — weather, terrain,
  ATC changes, aircraft defects (MEL items), unfamiliar airports, fatigue, time pressure.
- **Errors**: crew actions or inactions that deviate from intention or expectation —
  wrong altitude set, missed checklist item, wrong frequency, mis-handled automation.
- **Undesired aircraft state (UAS)**: the airplane where it shouldn't be (off altitude,
  unstable approach, wrong taxiway). Recover the state first, then the error.
Mismanaged threats → errors → UAS → incident. Each layer is a chance to trap.

## 2. Anticipate: brief the threats
- At each briefing (departure, top of descent), name the **top 2–3 threats** for this
  phase, and the **specific countermeasure** for each. E.g. "Threat: tailwind on a short
  wet runway. Countermeasure: autobrake 3/MED, if not stable by 1,000 ft we go around."
- Avoid reciting generic lists — a briefing that could apply to any flight protects none.
- Invite input: "Anything you'd add?" — said as an actual question.

## 3. Trap errors: monitoring and cross-checking
- **Verbalise, verify, monitor**: the PF announces mode/altitude selections, the PM
  confirms on the FMA/instrument, both monitor the result.
- **Sterile cockpit**: below 10,000 ft (or operator-defined phase), only
  operationally relevant talk.
- **Area of responsibility**: each pilot knows what they must check; the PM's primary
  job is monitoring the flight path, not paperwork.
- **Checklists** catch omissions — run them at low-workload moments, not mid-turn.

## 4. Speak up: graded assertiveness
Escalate until the issue is resolved (one widely used model is **PACE**):
1. **Probe**: "I see we're 20 knots fast — are we planning to slow down?"
2. **Alert**: "We're 20 knots fast and above the profile."
3. **Challenge**: "We need to go around — we're not stable."
4. **Emergency**: take action / "I have control" per SOP.
**Two-challenge rule** (in many operators): if the PF fails to respond to two challenges
about a flight-safety issue, the PM may take control.
The captain sets the **authority gradient** — too steep and juniors stay silent, too
flat and decisions stall. Open with "If you see anything you don't like, say so."

## 5. Decide: a structured model
**FOR-DEC** (Facts, Options, Risks & benefits, Decide, Execute, Check) or **DODAR**
(Diagnose, Options, Decide, Assign, Review):
- Fly the aircraft first; give the decision time if you have it (hold, extend).
- Both pilots state options; **captain decides**, aloud.
- Assign tasks (who flies, who runs the checklist, who talks to ATC/cabin).
- Review: is it working? Set a time or point to reassess.

## 6. Debrief
After every flight with something notable (good or bad): what were the threats, which
errors happened, how were they trapped, what would we do differently? Five minutes,
no blame.

## Gotchas
- Monitoring degrades during long, quiet automated flight; plan PM "active" checks.
- Automation errors (wrong mode) are now among the most common errors — verbalise every
  FMA change.
- Fatigue is a threat to brief, not a weakness to hide.

## Verify
- [ ] Each briefing named specific threats with specific countermeasures.
- [ ] Mode and altitude changes were verbalised and cross-checked.
- [ ] Any concern was raised with escalating clarity until resolved.
- [ ] Non-normal decisions followed a structured model and were reviewed.
- [ ] A short debrief captured threats, errors and traps.
