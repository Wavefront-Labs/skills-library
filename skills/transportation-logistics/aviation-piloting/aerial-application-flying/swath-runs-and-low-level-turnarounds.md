# Swath runs and low-level turnarounds (aerial application)

**Goal:** even coverage at the label rate, chemical on target and not drifting, and an
airplane that never turns low and slow or meets a wire it didn't know about.

> Draft: general ag-flying practice. Requirements vary (e.g. US Part 137 operator
> certificate, product labels, state licensing; equivalent rules elsewhere). Turn
> techniques and heights are type-, weight- and pilot-specific — learn them with a
> qualified ag instructor. This is orientation, not training.

## 1. Field reconnaissance (every field, every time)
- Fly the perimeter **above wire height** first (typically several hundred feet, then
  lower passes as appropriate).
- **Wires**: look for poles, insulators, transformers, guy wires and cleared lines in
  trees; assume wires connect every pole to every building. Note which run direction
  passes under/over them.
- Towers, trees, buildings, turbines, irrigation pivots; sensitive areas (houses,
  water, organic crops, beehives, schools) and buffer zones.
- Wind direction and strength; any **temperature inversion** (smoke layering, still
  air early morning) — inversions trap fine droplets and move them off target.

## 2. Swath and run planning
- **Swath width** from calibration of your system (pattern testing with cards/string),
  not from the brochure.
- Plan runs **across the wind** where possible so drift carries the spray into the
  next swath; start on the **downwind side** of the field and work upwind so you don't
  fly through your own spray.
- Patterns: **back-and-forth** (turn every run) or **racetrack** (skip runs, gentler
  turns). Racetrack reduces turn severity at the cost of distance.
- Load the GPS guidance (e.g. SatLoc, Ag-Nav) with the field boundary and swath width;
  confirm lane numbering before the first pass.

## 3. The run
- Typical application height for spraying: commonly ~8–15 ft above the crop for many
  liquids (check product label and nozzle setup); dry products differ.
- Hold airspeed and height constant through the run; boom on at field edge, off at
  the far edge (or per GPS).
- **Pull up early** for wires/trees at the end; plan the pull-up point on each run.

## 4. The turnaround (where most accidents happen)
1. Pull up to a safe height with **speed in hand** — climb first, turn second.
2. Use the procedure your instructor taught (e.g. a **procedure turn**/"teardrop":
   climb, turn away ~45° and then reverse ~225° back to the next lane). Bank in the
   turn is moderate and **coordinated**; never pull hard at low speed.
3. Watch airspeed, not the field, in the turn. Stall speed rises with bank: at 60°
   bank it is ~1.4× the level stall speed.
4. Roll out lined up, descend to height before the field edge.
**Heavy and hot**: early loads are heaviest — widen turns until weight burns off.

## 5. Load management and emergencies
- Calculate **load per field area**: rate (e.g. L/ha) × area = total; number of loads =
  total ÷ hopper capacity at the max weight for the conditions (density altitude!).
- Know the **hopper emergency dump** — practise the reach until it's reflex. An engine
  problem or wire strike at low level: dump, fly straight, land ahead.
- Fatigue: ag days are long and repetitive; take breaks; complacency in the 30th turn
  kills.

## 6. Drift control
- Stop if wind exceeds the label limit or if it's dead calm with an inversion.
- Use coarser droplets (nozzle selection, pressure) near sensitive areas; leave
  buffers.
- Record conditions (wind, temp, humidity) for every application.

## Verify
- [ ] Every wire and obstacle in and around the field was located before the first run.
- [ ] Swath width came from your own pattern test.
- [ ] Runs started downwind and progressed upwind.
- [ ] Turnarounds were climb-then-turn, coordinated, with airspeed watched.
- [ ] Load and drift conditions were recorded and within label limits.
