# Berthing with pivot point and propwalk — let the forces work for you

**Goal:** bring a ship alongside and away under control at low speed, using the
ship's own turning behaviour and the environment rather than fighting them.
Ship-specific manoeuvring data (wheelhouse poster, pilot card) overrides the
general rules below.

## 1. The forces you're managing
- **Pivot point:** the point the ship appears to turn about. Going ahead it moves to
  about 1/4–1/3 of the length from the bow; going astern it moves aft toward the
  stern quarter; stopped and pushed by a tug or thruster it's near the centre of
  lateral resistance (roughly amidships). A bow thruster is most effective when the
  pivot point is aft (moving astern or stopped); least effective going ahead.
- **Propwalk (transverse thrust):** single right-handed fixed-pitch propeller going
  **astern** swings the stern to **port** (bow to starboard). Hence the classic: berth
  **port side to**, as going astern to stop pulls the stern in. Left-handed / CPP
  (often left-hand when going astern effectively) — check your ship.
- **Rudder:** needs flow. A short burst of ahead power with hard rudder (**kick ahead**)
  turns the stern with little gain in speed.
- **Wind:** high-sided ships in ballast are dominated by wind; the bow usually falls
  off downwind when stopped; windage area × wind² — doubling wind quadruples force.
- **Current:** ship moves with the water; stem current where possible (better steerage
  at low ground speed).
- **Bank and interaction:** in a channel the stern is sucked toward the near bank and the
  bow pushed away (bank cushion); passing ships attract each other.

## 2. Plan the approach
- Final approach line and angle (often 10–20° to the berth for a port-side-to landing),
  points to reduce speed; use the **ship's stopping data** (head reach at slow astern).
- Rough speed targets (commonly used, adjust for size): ~1 kn per 1,000 ft (~300 m) of
  remaining distance in the final approach; under 0.5 kn lateral when landing — very
  large ships land at a few cm/s (berthing velocity limits on fenders).
- Lines plan and order (often head and back spring first in an offshore wind).
- Tugs: positions and lines agreed (see the tugboats and harbour-pilotage skills).

## 3. Landing (port side to, single right-handed screw, no wind)
1. Approach at a small angle, reducing speed in stages.
2. Abeam the berth position, put the engine astern: propwalk swings the stern to
   port, closing it on the berth and straightening the ship.
3. Use bow thruster to bring the bow in parallel; land flat, at minimal lateral speed.
4. Spring line out early; use it to stop and hold.

## 4. Unberthing
- **Springing off** (no thruster): stern out by going slow ahead against the forward
  spring with rudder toward the quay; the bow is held, the stern swings out; then
  let go and go astern to clear. Use fenders at the bow.
- Or bow out with thruster in an offshore wind; let the wind take the ship off.
- Check propeller clearance from the quay and lines out of the water before using engines.

## 5. Common corrections
- Too fast: you lose options — go astern early and expect propwalk; a tug made fast aft
  can brake.
- Wind pushing on: approach more upwind, keep more lateral control (tugs or thruster
  margin); delay final approach rather than force it.
- Bow not coming round: kick ahead with full rudder briefly; or thruster while making sternway.

## Gotchas
- Bow thrusters lose much effect above ~2–4 kn ahead.
- Shallow water increases turning circle and reduces response.
- Engine "astern" response delay (minutes on some slow-speed diesels) — know it.
- Fender and berth limits: lateral landing speed matters more than how it looks.

## Verify
- [ ] Manoeuvring data, propeller handedness and thruster limits checked before approach.
- [ ] Speed reduced to plan at each marker; final lateral speed within berth limits.
- [ ] Lines plan and tug plan briefed to stations and tugs.
- [ ] Post-manoeuvre debrief: what the ship did vs what you predicted.

*Uncertainty:* pivot-point locations, speed rules of thumb and propwalk behaviour are generalisations — individual hulls and propulsion types differ.
