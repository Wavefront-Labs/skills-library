# Ship assist and girting avoidance — useful to the pilot, never capsized

**Goal:** assist ships in berthing, unberthing and transits as a harbour tug — get the
line up safely, deliver the force the pilot asks for, and never get pulled over by
your own towline (girting/girding/tripping), which is the classic fatal tug accident.

## 1. Know your tug type
- **Conventional (single/twin screw, hook amidships):** very vulnerable to girting
  when the line leads abeam; manoeuvre limited.
- **ASD (azimuth stern drive):** drives at the stern, winch usually forward (and aft);
  works "bow to ship" or "stern to ship". Excellent push-pull, but escorting stern-first
  at speed is risky.
- **Tractor (Voith/azimuth forward, towing point aft):** inherently resistant to
  girting because the towing point is behind the propulsion; strong indirect towing.
Know your **bollard pull**, max safe speed through the water for each operation and
the towing point position relative to your pivot/CLR.

## 2. Pre-job
- Tow gear inspection log current: line condition (chafe, cut strands, hockling),
  pennant/stretcher, shackles, winch brake test, **emergency release** tested
  per schedule (hook quick-release / winch brake release).
- Master–pilot information: tug position (e.g. centre lead forward, port shoulder,
  aft centre lead), line type (ship's or tug's), expected speed, bollards and
  fairleads on the ship, VHF channel.

## 3. Making fast at the bow (highest-risk position)
- Approach from the shoulder, match speed, keep the **ship's speed low**
  (commonly ≤ 5–6 kn for making fast forward; follow local rules).
- Beware **interaction**: at the bow the ship's pressure field first pushes you away,
  then sucks you in under the flare; keep power ready and don't linger.
- Messenger/heaving line passed, towline up, crew clear of the bight and snap-back
  zones. Confirm "made fast" by radio before applying load.

## 4. Working the line
- Respond to pilot orders clearly: direction (e.g. "pull 90° to port") and power
  as % or tonnes. Report when you can't comply.
- **Push-pull** at berth: tug at right angles, controlled power. At speed, a tug's
  effective force changes — at higher speeds use **indirect towing** (tractor/ASD
  hull angled to the flow so hydrodynamic lift generates line force with little engine
  power) for escort steering/braking.
- Keep the towline under control: avoid slack lines fouling propellers; avoid snatch loads.

## 5. Girting — recognise and prevent
Girting = the towline pulls the tug sideways (line abeam), heeling it; if the
heeling lever exceeds stability, the tug capsizes in seconds.
**Prevention:**
- Keep the line within the tug's safe lead; never let the ship get ahead of you while
  fast on a conventional tug's hook.
- Use a **gob rope/gog rope** on conventional tugs to move the towing point aft.
- Watch ship speed — high speed + abeam line is the danger case; ask the pilot to
  slow when necessary.
- Be particularly careful when the ship's engines go ahead while a tug is fast forward.
**Response:** at the first heel beyond normal, **release the line** immediately
(emergency release from the wheelhouse), reduce power, steer to bring the line
astern. Release must work under load — that's why it's tested.

## 6. Letting go
Agree before letting go; ship slows if needed; tug moves clear of the bow and
propeller wash, line recovered without fouling.

## Gotchas
- Watertight doors and hatches left open during work turn a heel into a capsize.
- Fatigue in long shifts degrades reaction time — rotate.
- Ship's crew handling the line may not be trained; brief by radio and watch them.

## Verify
- [ ] Emergency release tested and logged; tow gear inspected.
- [ ] Master–pilot exchange covered tug positions, speeds, line handling.
- [ ] Watertight doors closed during assist.
- [ ] Crew outside snap-back zones when load applied.

*Uncertainty:* speed limits and procedures vary by port and operator; this is general practice for harbour towage.
