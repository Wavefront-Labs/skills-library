# Failed trains, assistance and evacuation

**Goal:** turn a failure into a controlled, protected situation quickly — then move
the train or the people in the safest way, not the fastest.

*Draft: emergency protection methods (detonators, track-circuit clips, red flags,
railway emergency call on GSM-R), single-line working forms and evacuation rules are
country-specific. Exact steps come from your rulebook and module.*

## 1. Protect first (seconds to minutes)
- If another line may be obstructed (derailment, fouling equipment, people on track):
  **raise the emergency** immediately — railway emergency call / emergency radio call
  to stop trains on adjacent lines — then place protection (e.g. track-circuit clip,
  detonators, red light) per your rules.
- If only your train has failed on its own line, the signalling usually protects you,
  but tell the signaller/dispatcher now: location, nature, whether adjacent lines are
  affected.
- Secure the train: brakes applied, handbrakes if required on gradients.

## 2. Diagnose — briefly
Use the fleet's fault-finding guide (cab displays, reset procedures). Set yourself a
time limit (often ~10–20 minutes, or as control instructs) before calling for
assistance; fruitless fault-finding is the main cause of long passenger strandings.

## 3. Assistance
- Agree with control: assisting train from behind or ahead, coupling arrangements,
  speed limits for the combined train, and who drives.
- The assisting train approaches under authority at caution, stops short, then
  couples per the rules; brake tests on the combined train before moving.
- Passengers stay on board unless conditions require otherwise.

## 4. Single-line working (when one line of a double track is blocked)
- Trains run both directions over the remaining line under a special authority
  (a pilotman, token or written order system — *country-specific*).
- Points along the section secured, level crossings managed (manual operation or
  attendants), speeds reduced. The person authorising each train is the single source
  of authority; nobody moves without them.

## 5. Evacuation — a decision, not a reflex
- **Stay on board is usually safer** than walking along an electrified railway in the
  dark. Evacuate only when staying is more dangerous (fire, smoke, conditions on
  board deteriorating, very long delay with no prospect of movement) or control
  directs it.
- **Controlled evacuation** requires: all lines protected and blocked, traction
  current isolated and earthed (on electrified lines), a safe exit route, staff and
  lighting, and somewhere to take people to (another train, a platform, road access).
- Prefer train-to-train transfer or drawing into a platform.
- Self-evacuation risk: passengers will open doors themselves if they are not kept
  informed. Make PA announcements every ~10 minutes, even with no news.
- Account for vulnerable passengers: wheelchair users, children, people with medical
  needs.

## Gotchas
- Assuming the conductor rail/overhead is dead because the train lost power.
- Detrained passengers wandering onto adjacent lines — they don't know which lines
  are blocked.
- Losing radio contact: have a fallback (lineside phone, mobile, cab-to-control).

## Verify
- [ ] Adjacent lines protected or confirmed unaffected within minutes.
- [ ] Signaller/control informed with precise location and nature.
- [ ] Fault-finding time-boxed; assistance requested on time.
- [ ] Evacuation only with power isolated, lines blocked and a staffed route.
- [ ] Passengers updated at regular intervals throughout.
