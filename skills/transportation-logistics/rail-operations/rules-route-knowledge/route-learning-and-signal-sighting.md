# Route learning and signal sighting

**Goal:** know a route well enough that every signal, speed change, gradient,
station and hazard is expected before it appears — and still read what each signal
actually shows, every time.

*Draft: route-learning hours, sign-off rules and document names (Sectional Appendix,
Timetable Special Instructions, Weekly Operating Notice, bulletins/general orders)
vary by railway; the method is common.*

## 1. Build the route, in layers
1. **Paper/digital first:** gradient profile, signal numbers and positions, permanent
   speed restrictions (PSRs), stations and platform lengths, junctions and which way
   they diverge, neutral sections, level crossings, tunnels, phone locations.
2. **Ride it** in the cab with a qualified driver — in daylight, darkness *and* bad
   visibility. A signal visible from a kilometre in sun may appear late at night
   behind a bridge.
3. **Make your own route card**: per section, list in order — signal, speed, landmark,
   braking point for each stop. Draw it; the act of drawing is the learning.
4. **Test yourself**: recite the route in order from memory, both directions, then
   name each signal's possible aspects and what you'd do on each.
5. Sign-off only when you can drive it under supervision without prompts.

## 2. Reading signals — deliberate, not ambient
- **Identify the signal is yours:** on multi-track lines, check the signal's position
  (left/right of your line, gantry lane) and plate number; misreading an adjacent line's
  signal is a recognised SPAD cause.
- **Read and react on first sight:** say the aspect aloud ("single yellow, brake
  for…"). Act on a caution aspect immediately — don't wait to "see the next one".
- **Expect the restrictive:** approach every signal as though it could be at danger
  until it proves otherwise.
- **Start-from-station risk:** a large share of SPADs happen starting away from a
  platform against a red. Check the starting signal separately from the dispatch
  signal.
- **Junction signals:** check the route indicator/feather matches your train's route.

## 3. Speeds
- PSRs are part of the route. TSRs are not: read the **weekly/daily notices and
  bulletins every shift** before signing on, and mark them on your route card.
- Approach a TSR expecting the warning board; if a board is missing, still observe
  the restriction you were told about and report the missing board.
- Speed is controlled at the *rear* of the train for many restrictions — know your
  train length's implication when accelerating away.

## 4. Commentary driving (risk-triggered commentary)
Speak your observations and intentions at key points: "Signal 23 single yellow,
braking now, station next, 4-car marker." It holds attention and catches assumptions.
Do it quietly in the cab even when alone.

## 5. Keeping competence
Route knowledge decays: many railways require re-learning after a set period off a
route. Refresh after diversions, resignalling or timetable changes.

## Gotchas
- "Anticipating the green" from habit — the signal that's always green until the day
  it isn't.
- Distractions just after a station stop: phone calls, passenger alarms, paperwork.
- Low sun and wet glare can make LED aspects hard to read; slow down if unsure.

## Verify
- [ ] You can recite signals, speeds and braking points for the route in order.
- [ ] Today's TSRs and notices read and marked before departure.
- [ ] Every signal identified as yours and its aspect called aloud.
- [ ] Starting signal checked independently of dispatch at every stop.
