# Lights-and-siren intersection clearing

**Goal:** arrive quickly *and* arrive — most emergency-vehicle crashes happen at
intersections, and a crash takes two crews out of service and creates new patients.

*Draft: legal exemptions (speed, red lights, direction) and "due regard" duties are
set by each jurisdiction; agency policy is often stricter. Driver training (e.g.
EVOC in the US, blue-light courses in the UK) is required — this does not replace it.*

## 1. Decide whether to run hot
- Lights and siren saves less time than most people think (often only a few minutes
  in urban areas) and multiplies crash risk. Use it when the call's clinical/
  operational priority justifies it, per dispatch codes and local protocol.
- Exemptions only apply with warning devices on (and sometimes require both lights
  and siren) — and never remove your duty of **due regard** for everyone else.

## 2. Intersection clearing — lane by lane
1. **Approach slowing**, foot covering the brake; change siren tone (wail → yelp)
   ~100–150 m out so the change catches attention.
2. At a red light or stop sign: **come to a near stop or full stop** (agency policy),
   then proceed only when you can see each lane is stopped.
3. **Clear each lane individually**: "lane 1 stopped, lane 2 stopped, lane 3…". The
   lane you can't see — behind a stopped bus or truck — is where crashes happen.
4. Make eye contact with drivers; expect someone with music loud or panicked into
   pulling across.
5. **Second emergency vehicle:** after the first one passes, other drivers start moving
   — the second unit gets hit. Coordinate on the radio and use a different tone.

## 3. On the road
- Pass on the driver's (offside) side where possible; motorists are trained to pull
  to the kerb side.
- Use opposing lanes only when clear and at reduced speed; expect oncoming drivers
  to freeze.
- Don't tailgate a vehicle that's trying to give way; give it time and space.
- Turn the siren off and lights down when it no longer helps (e.g. stuck in stationary
  traffic with nowhere for others to go) — it just panics drivers into unsafe moves.

## 4. Patient-smooth driving (ambulance)
- Crew in the back are often unbelted during care; patients may be immobilised.
- Smooth acceleration and braking, slower corners, avoid potholes and speed humps at
  speed. Warn the crew before turns/braking ("braking", "left turn").
- For many transports, **smooth and steady beats fast**.

## 5. Arriving on scene
- Kill the siren before arrival where safe (it hinders scene communication).
- **Positioning:** fire apparatus may block to protect the scene; ambulances position
  for easy loading and exit, upstream of hazards and uphill/upwind of fuel spills
  or hazmat. Follow scene command's staging instructions.
- Leave space for other units; don't block the hydrant/fire appliance.

## 6. Driver condition
Adrenaline narrows attention (tunnel vision, auditory exclusion). Commentary driving
and a partner calling hazards help. Wear seatbelts — always.

## Gotchas
- Assuming everyone heard you — modern cars are well soundproofed.
- Green light ≠ clear intersection when crossing traffic is also running hot.
- Speeding to compensate for a late turnout.

## Verify
- [ ] Use of warning devices justified by call priority and policy.
- [ ] Every controlled intersection: slow/stop, lane-by-lane clearance.
- [ ] Second units coordinated by radio at shared intersections.
- [ ] Patient transport smooth; crew warned of manoeuvres.
