# Full-body Swedish relaxation routine — a 60-minute session that flows

**Goal:** deliver a calm, even, full-body relaxation massage that the client experiences
as one continuous piece rather than a series of disconnected strokes, while staying
inside wellness scope (no diagnosis, no treatment claims).

## 1. Before touch: intake (5 min, every session)
- Ask about recent injuries, surgery, pregnancy, skin conditions, blood-clot history,
  blood-thinning medication, fever or feeling unwell, allergies (nut-based oils!), and
  areas they want avoided or focused on.
- **Do not massage** with fever/acute infection, suspected deep-vein thrombosis (a hot,
  swollen, painful calf — refer to a doctor, never "rub it out"), contagious skin
  conditions, or over fresh injuries, open wounds, varicose veins or recent surgical
  sites. When unsure, get a GP/medical clearance first. Local rules on what needs medical
  sign-off vary — check your licensing body or insurer.
- Agree on pressure using a 1–10 scale and tell them they can change it any time.

## 2. Setup
- Table at roughly **knuckle height** with arms hanging (lower for deeper work, higher for
  light work); room warm (~24–26 °C — clients cool fast once relaxed).
- Draping: only the area being worked is uncovered; tuck the sheet firmly so it does not
  slip. Turn the client by holding the sheet up as a screen.
- Oil or lotion warmed in your hands, never poured on the skin. Start with a small amount —
  too much oil kills grip for petrissage.
- Bolster under ankles (prone) and under knees (supine) to take load off the low back.

## 3. Stroke vocabulary and order
For each region work **light → deeper → light**:
1. **Effleurage** — long gliding strokes toward the heart, whole palm, to spread oil and
   warm tissue. Always open and close a region with it.
2. **Petrissage** — kneading, wringing, lifting; the "working" stroke for large muscles
   (thighs, calves, upper trapezius).
3. **Friction** — small circular or cross-fibre strokes with thumbs/fingertips in local
   tight spots. Brief, then flush with effleurage.
4. **Tapotement** — light percussion (cupping, hacking). Energising; in a relaxation
   session use sparingly or skip.
5. **Vibration/rocking** — gentle shaking to release a limb before you leave it.

## 4. A 60-minute sequence (one common layout)
| Time | Region |
|------|--------|
| 0–15 | Back, prone (longest single area) |
| 15–25 | Backs of legs, prone (one leg fully, then the other) |
| 25–35 | Fronts of legs and feet, supine |
| 35–45 | Arms and hands, supine |
| 45–55 | Neck, shoulders, upper chest above the drape line |
| 55–60 | Face/scalp, slow closing strokes |
Some therapists start supine; either is fine — consistency matters more than order.

## 5. Body mechanics (your career depends on it)
- Lunge or horse stance; move from the legs and lean body weight in, rather than pushing
  with the arms.
- Keep wrists near neutral; swap thumbs for knuckles, palms or forearm when pressing
  hard. Thumb overuse is the classic therapist injury.
- Keep one hand in contact during transitions; breaking contact repeatedly feels jarring.

## Gotchas
- **Rhythm beats pressure** for relaxation — a steady tempo matters more than depth.
- Avoid **endangerment sites**: front and sides of the throat, the back of the knee,
  armpit, inner upper arm, the area just below the ribs over the kidneys (no heavy
  pressure or tapotement). Lists differ slightly between schools.
- Running out of time on the back and then rushing the legs is the commonest flaw;
  keep a visible clock.
- Pregnancy needs side-lying positioning and specific training — do not improvise.
- Bruising, dizziness or nausea afterward means the work was too much; note it and adjust.

## Verify
- [ ] Intake done and contraindications screened before touch; anything uncertain referred.
- [ ] Only the worked area was undraped; client was warm throughout.
- [ ] Each region opened and closed with effleurage; pressure checked at least twice.
- [ ] Session ended on time with every planned region covered at an even pace.
- [ ] Your wrists and thumbs feel fine afterward — if not, change your mechanics.
