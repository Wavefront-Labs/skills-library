# Shiatsu pressure technique and session flow

**Goal:** give a clothed, floor-based shiatsu or acupressure session with steady,
perpendicular, stationary pressure — delivered from your centre (hara) rather than your
arms — following meridian-line routines for relaxation and wellbeing. Present it as a
traditional bodywork practice; do not diagnose or claim to treat disease.

## 1. Pressure fundamentals
- **Perpendicular:** press at 90° to the body surface. Angled pressure slides and feels
  sharp.
- **Stationary:** no circling or rubbing. Lean in, hold ~3–7 s (shorter for a brisk
  feel, longer to calm), release slowly, move on.
- **Body weight from the hara:** your movement starts at your lower abdomen/pelvis; arms
  stay relaxed and mostly straight, transmitting weight. If your shoulders lift, you're
  pushing.
- **Two-hand connection:** one "mother" hand rests still and supportive, the other
  "working" hand moves along the line. This is a signature of Zen (Masunaga-style) shiatsu
  and makes pressure feel safe. Namikoshi-style work emphasises thumb pressure on points
  more. Know which tradition you trained in.
- Tools: palms (broadest, start here), thumbs, fingers, knees, elbows for large muscles.

## 2. Setup
- Futon on the floor (or a low table in some styles); client fully clothed in soft,
  loose clothing; no oil.
- Quiet room; you sit in seiza or a kneeling lunge and move around the client.
- Typical session 45–60 minutes.

## 3. A basic full-body flow (one common sequence)
1. **Prone:** palm pressure down both sides of the spine (Bladder meridian line in
   traditional terms), then buttocks, backs of the legs, soles of the feet.
2. **Side-lying:** shoulder and neck, along the side of the leg (Gall Bladder line).
3. **Supine:** gentle hara contact (light, mainly diagnostic/connective in Zen shiatsu —
   avoid deep abdominal pressure), arms and hands, fronts of the legs, then neck and face.
4. Finish with stillness — both hands resting, then slow withdrawal.

## 4. Acupressure points commonly used for relaxation
Examples (names per Traditional Chinese Medicine convention):
- **LI4 (He Gu)** — web between thumb and index finger. **Avoid in pregnancy** —
  traditionally contraindicated.
- **GB21 (Jian Jing)** — top of the shoulder, midway between neck and shoulder tip.
  **Avoid in pregnancy**, use gentle pressure.
- **SP6 (San Yin Jiao)** — inner leg ~four finger-widths above the ankle bone.
  **Avoid in pregnancy.**
- **PC6 (Nei Guan)** — inner forearm, ~three finger-widths above the wrist crease;
  widely used for nausea (some trial evidence for nausea relief).
- **Yintang** — between the eyebrows, light pressure for calm.
Hold points 30 s–2 min at a pressure the client calls "strong but pleasant".

## 5. Tui na in brief
Tui na is a Chinese bodywork system with more vigorous moving techniques (rolling,
pushing, kneading, grasping), often over clothing or a thin cloth. If you offer
"tui na style" routines, recognise it is a full system taught over long programmes;
a short course doesn't make you a tui na practitioner.

## 6. Screening
Avoid deep pressure or modify for: pregnancy (avoid the points above and deep abdominal
work), recent surgery, osteoporosis, fractures, varicose veins, skin infections, fever,
and blood-thinning medication. Avoid direct pressure on the front of the neck.

## Gotchas
- Too many points, too quickly = choppy session. Lines and rhythm matter more than
  hitting every point.
- Thumbs are your weakest link — favour palms and knees, and keep thumb joints straight.
- Clients often fall asleep; keep the pressure steady rather than waking them with
  sudden changes.
- Point locations use proportional "cun" measurements based on the client's body,
  not yours.

## Verify
- [ ] Pressure perpendicular, stationary and from body weight; your shoulders stayed down.
- [ ] Mother hand stayed in contact throughout (in Zen-style work).
- [ ] Pregnancy-contraindicated points avoided where relevant.
- [ ] Client reported "strong but pleasant", never sharp.
- [ ] Session closed with stillness rather than an abrupt end.
