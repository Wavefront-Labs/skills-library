# Working deep tissue safely — slow, sinking depth without injury

**Goal:** reach deeper muscle layers with slow, sustained pressure that the client can
relax into, using forearms and elbows to protect your hands, while staying in wellness
scope — you are easing tension, not treating a diagnosed condition.

## 1. The core principle: slow is deep
Deep work is about **speed and angle**, not force. Tissue resists fast pressure and
yields to slow pressure. Sink in at roughly the speed you would press into cold butter;
pause at resistance until it softens, then continue. A stroke along the length of a
back muscle may take 20–30 seconds.

## 2. Intake additions (on top of normal screening)
- **Anticoagulants / blood thinners**, bleeding disorders, recent steroid injections,
  osteoporosis, diabetes with neuropathy (reduced sensation = can't report pain),
  recent injury or surgery, and any numbness or radiating pain — these call for lighter
  work or medical clearance. Radiating, burning or electric pain during a stroke means
  stop: you may be on a nerve.
- Ask what they mean by "deep": many clients want firm, not painful.

## 3. Session structure
1. **Warm up (first ~5 min per region):** broad effleurage and petrissage with palms.
   Never go straight in deep on cold tissue.
2. **Work layers:** superficial first, then progressively deeper over several passes.
3. **Specific work:** stripping strokes and sustained compression on tight bands.
4. **Flush and close:** broad, lighter strokes to finish each region.
Plan to fully cover **fewer areas** — a deep 60-minute session might only do back, neck
and shoulders properly. Agree this at intake.

## 4. Tools of the body
- **Flat forearm (ulnar side):** the workhorse for long stripping along the erector
  spinae, hamstrings, quads. Broad and comfortable.
- **Elbow (olecranon):** focused pressure on glutes, upper trapezius ridge, thick
  muscle. Keep the hand relaxed; a clenched fist tenses the arm and makes pressure jerky.
  Never use the elbow near bone or over the spine.
- **Supported knuckles / thumb-over-thumb:** small areas only; thumbs wear out fastest.
- **Sustained compression** (trigger-point style): hold a tender spot at a pressure the
  client rates 5–7 out of 10 for ~30–90 s until it eases. If it gets worse, back off.

## 5. Pressure communication
Use a 1–10 scale: aim for **"good hurt" around 6–7 maximum**. Watch for guarding —
clenched fists, held breath, toes curling, the muscle tightening under you. Guarding means
you are too deep or too fast; the client cannot relax and the work stops being useful.
Tell them breathing out slowly as you sink in helps.

## 6. Body mechanics
Stack joints (shoulder over elbow over contact point), lower the table more than for
Swedish, and use your legs and body weight. If you're pushing with your shoulders, the
table is too high.

## Gotchas
- **No pain, no gain is wrong here.** Over-pressure causes bruising, protective spasm and
  next-day pain that clients blame on you.
- Endangerment sites still apply: front/side of the neck, behind the knee, armpit, inner
  upper arm, and kidney area below the ribs. Avoid direct deep pressure on the spine,
  over bony landmarks, on varicose veins, and on the sciatic notch for long holds.
- Expect some soreness for 24–48 h; warn the client. Bruising or soreness lasting longer
  means you went too far.
- Clients on painkillers or alcohol may not feel overload — work lighter.
- Don't promise to "break up adhesions or knots" or to "flush toxins" — these claims are
  not well supported. Describe it as relieving tension and helping them feel looser.

## Verify
- [ ] Contraindications (especially blood thinners, neuropathy) asked about and noted.
- [ ] Each area warmed up before going deep; depth built over multiple passes.
- [ ] Client's rating never went above ~7; no guarding left unaddressed.
- [ ] Forearm/elbow carried most heavy pressure; your thumbs are not sore.
- [ ] Aftercare given: water, gentle movement, expect mild 1–2 day soreness, call if worse.
