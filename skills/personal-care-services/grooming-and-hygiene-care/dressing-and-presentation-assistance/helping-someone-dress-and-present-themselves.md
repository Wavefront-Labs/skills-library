# Dressing and presentation assistance — helping someone dress their way

**Goal:** help a person get dressed comfortably, safely and in clothes they chose, using
techniques and aids that protect weak limbs and skin and keep them as independent as possible.

Follow the care plan and any occupational therapist (OT) or nurse instructions, especially for
compression garments.

## 1. Choice and independence
- Offer a **real choice of two or three outfits** (more can overwhelm someone with dementia);
  respect their style, culture, makeup, jewellery, perfume and how they like their hair.
- Lay clothes out **in the order they go on**. Prompt one step at a time; do only what they can't.
- Consider the weather, the day's plans and comfort (seams, tightness, swelling).
- Ensure privacy: door closed, curtains drawn, keep them covered as much as possible.

## 2. Positioning and safety
- Seat them on a stable chair or bed edge with feet flat; dress the upper body sitting.
- For lower body: trousers over the feet while seated, then stand (with aid/support as the
  care plan says) to pull up, or roll side to side in bed.
- Footwear: well-fitting, closed, non-slip, with a heel counter — slippers and loose shoes cause falls.

## 3. The weak-side rule
For someone with one weak, painful or stiff side (e.g. after a stroke, injury or arthritis):
- **Dress the weak (affected) side first; undress the strong side first.**
  ("Weak in first, strong out first.")
- Sleeves: gather the sleeve, guide their weak hand and arm through, then up over the shoulder;
  then the strong arm. Reverse to undress.
- Never pull on a weak arm or shoulder — subluxation of a stroke-affected shoulder is common and painful.

## 4. Adaptive clothing and aids
- **Clothing:** elastic waists, front-opening tops, larger neck openings, magnetic or Velcro
  closures instead of buttons, wrap-style skirts, open-back garments for people dressed in bed
  or in wheelchairs, zip pulls, slip-on or Velcro shoes.
- **Aids** (often supplied after an OT assessment): button hook, dressing stick, sock/stocking aid,
  long-handled shoe horn, elastic laces.
- Bras: front-fastening, or fasten at the front and rotate.

## 5. Compression garments
Compression or anti-embolism stockings and garments are prescribed with a size and class —
apply exactly as instructed.
- Put on **in the morning**, before swelling builds, on dry skin with no creams just applied.
- Turn the stocking inside out to the heel, slip over the foot, align the heel, then ease up the
  leg a little at a time with flat hands (gloves help grip). **No wrinkles, no rolled tops** —
  a rolled band acts like a tourniquet.
- Check skin under and around them daily for redness, marks, blisters and colour or temperature
  changes of the toes; report pain, numbness, or a mark that doesn't fade.
- Don't fold the top down to "make it fit" — report fitting problems for re-measurement.

## 6. Finishing presentation
- Glasses clean, hearing aids in and working, dentures in, hair done, any jewellery, watch or makeup
  the person usually wears. A mirror check together: "how do you feel about that?"
- Clothes clean, fitted, the right way round and suitably fastened; no labels or tags rubbing.

## Gotchas
- Dementia and refusal: try later, a different carer, or simplify the steps; don't force.
- Swollen feet or legs: shoes that fit yesterday may not fit today — report new swelling.
- Hurrying causes falls and arm injuries; allow time.
- Catheter bags and tubing: route correctly through clothing so they don't pull.

## Verify
- [ ] The person chose their clothes; privacy maintained.
- [ ] Weak side dressed first and undressed last; no pulling on weak limbs.
- [ ] Safe, fitted footwear on before walking.
- [ ] Compression garments smooth, unrolled, correct size; skin checked and changes reported.
- [ ] Glasses, hearing aids, dentures in; the person is happy with how they look.
