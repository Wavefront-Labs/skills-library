# Continence and toileting care — dignified help, right products, healthy skin

**Goal:** help a person use the toilet as independently as possible, manage continence
products correctly, and keep the skin intact, while noticing changes that need a nurse or doctor.

Follow the care plan and continence assessment; product types and sizes are usually set by
a continence nurse or service. Moving and handling should follow your training and equipment.

## 1. Toileting first, products second
- **Promote the toilet:** clear path, good lighting, raised seat and grab rails, clothes that
  undo easily. Respond quickly to requests — waiting causes accidents and distress.
- **Prompted or scheduled toileting** (e.g. on waking, after meals, before bed, and every 2-3 hours
  as the care plan says) reduces accidents, especially for people with dementia, who may not
  recognise the urge or find the toilet. Watch for cues: restlessness, fidgeting with clothes.
- Commode, bedpan or urinal when walking to the toilet isn't possible; give privacy and a
  call bell, and stay within earshot.
- Don't encourage reducing drinks to avoid accidents — concentrated urine irritates the bladder
  and raises infection and constipation risk. Follow fluid guidance in the care plan.

## 2. Assisting at the toilet
- Wash hands, gloves and apron on. Explain each step and keep them covered where possible.
- Help them sit fully, feet supported; then step out or turn away if safe — privacy matters.
- Wiping: **front to back** (urethra towards anus), using a fresh wipe or section each stroke;
  this reduces urinary tract infections, especially in women.
- Help them wash hands afterwards.

## 3. Continence products
- **Use the product and size assessed for the person** — bigger or doubled-up pads don't absorb
  better; they leak, hold moisture against skin and cause sores. **Never layer two pads.**
- Shaped pads with **fixation pants** must sit snugly in the groin, the cup shape formed by
  folding lengthways before fitting, without touching the pad's inner surface.
- **Pull-ups** for people who are mobile and can use the toilet sometimes; **all-in-ones/briefs**
  usually for people in bed or with heavy incontinence.
- Change based on the wetness indicator or schedule, and **always after faeces** — promptly.

## 4. Skin care (perineal hygiene)
- Clean with **pH-balanced no-rinse cleanser or wipes** recommended by your service, or warm water;
  avoid ordinary soap (it dries and alters skin pH) and vigorous rubbing. Pat dry, especially folds.
- Apply a **thin barrier film or cream** where the care plan says — thick layers block the pad's
  absorbency. Don't mix multiple creams.
- Watch for **incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD):** red, sore, sometimes weeping skin in the
  pad area, often in a diffuse pattern; **pressure injuries** over bony points (sacrum, heels)
  look different — report both.

## 5. Observe and report
Report (per your service) any change in pattern or new:
- burning, frequency, cloudy/smelly urine, blood, new confusion (possible UTI in older people);
- constipation (no bowel movement for the expected interval — use a stool chart such as the Bristol
  scale), diarrhoea, straining, pain;
- catheter problems (no drainage, leaking, pain);
- skin redness or breaks that don't settle.

## Gotchas
- Calling continence products "nappies" or talking about accidents in front of others removes dignity.
- Over-tight fixation pants leave marks; too loose and the pad shifts and leaks.
- Wipes flushed down the toilet block drains; bin them per local disposal rules.
- Sudden new incontinence is a symptom, not a habit — report it.

## Verify
- [ ] Toileting offered regularly and promptly; aids in place.
- [ ] Wiping front to back; hands washed for both of you.
- [ ] Assessed product size used, single pad, fitted correctly, changed promptly after faeces.
- [ ] Skin cleaned with pH-balanced product, dried, thin barrier only as planned.
- [ ] Changes in urine, bowels or skin reported and recorded.
