# Professional frameworks for touch-based intimacy work

**Goal:** practise touch-based intimacy coaching inside a recognised professional
framework — clear scope, structured consent, appropriate clinical collaboration and
records — so clients are protected and the practitioner is defensible. Industry
reference only; no technique.

## 1. Know which modality you are in
| Modality | Typical structure | Key framework |
|---|---|---|
| **Surrogate partner therapy (SPT)** | **Triadic model**: client + licensed therapist + surrogate partner; therapist directs the overall plan; surrogate works experientially with the client | IPSA training and code of ethics |
| **Sexological bodywork / somatic sex education** | Practitioner works with an individual client on body awareness and education; scope and touch are defined by training body and local law | Certification programmes (e.g. ISSE); professional associations vary |
| **Intimacy / relationship coaching with touch** | Non-clinical coaching; touch usually limited (e.g. clothed, non-genital) | No single standard — adopt a recognised code and document it |
| **Sex therapy** | Talk therapy by a licensed clinician; clinicians generally do **not** touch clients | AASECT and licensing boards |
Don't drift between these: a coach who starts treating a clinical dysfunction, or a
therapist who starts touching, has left their scope.

## 2. Legal exposure (uncertain — verify locally)
Whether a touch-based modality is lawful depends on how local law defines sexual
services, whether a clinician is involved, and licensing rules for touch professions.
Some places have no specific law; others would treat it as sex work. Practitioners
commonly seek a lawyer's view before practising and carry professional liability
insurance where an insurer will cover the modality (not all will).

## 3. Intake and informed consent
- Written intake: goals, relevant medical and sexual-health history, medication, trauma
  history (at the level the client chooses to share), current therapy.
- **Screen for contraindications/referral**: active psychosis, acute crisis, current abuse,
  untreated medical causes of the presenting issue → refer to the appropriate clinician first.
- Written informed consent covering: what the work involves in neutral terms, what it
  excludes, that consent is ongoing and withdrawable, fees, confidentiality and its limits,
  records kept, and the practitioner's training and credentials.
- Re-consent at each session for that session's planned activities (see
  consent-communication).

## 4. Ethics in practice
- **No dual relationships:** no dating, friendship or business ties with clients during
  and (by most codes) after the work for a stated period.
- Practitioner well-being: clinical supervision or peer consultation, especially in SPT.
- Sexual-health safety for both parties: testing and barrier protocols agreed in writing
  where any contact carries risk.
- Termination: planned endings and referral options, not abrupt drop-off.

## 5. Records
- Session notes kept factually, stored securely, retained per the framework's rules.
- In SPT, structured communication with the supervising therapist after each session,
  with the client's written consent to share.

## Gotchas
- Marketing language that implies cure of medical conditions invites regulatory trouble.
- Clients sometimes seek this work to avoid therapy they need; intake must catch that.
- Online "certifications" vary hugely in rigour — check what the credential actually covers.

## Verify
- [ ] Modality and scope are named in writing, and work stays inside them.
- [ ] Local legal position checked; insurance status known.
- [ ] Intake screened for referral needs; written informed consent signed.
- [ ] Clinical collaboration in place where the model requires it.
- [ ] Notes secure; supervision arranged; dual-relationship policy stated.
