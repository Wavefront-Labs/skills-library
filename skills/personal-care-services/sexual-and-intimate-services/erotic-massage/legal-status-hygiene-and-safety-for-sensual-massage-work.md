# Sensual massage work: legal status, hygiene and safety

**Goal:** someone working in or running a sensual/erotic massage service understands
how the law classifies it, avoids the licensing traps that catch people out, and runs
hygienic, safe premises. This is a compliance and harm-reduction reference: it
deliberately contains **no technique**, which is outside this library's scope.

## 1. How the law usually classifies it
Uncertain and highly local — verify for your jurisdiction:
- Most legal systems treat massage that includes sexual contact as **sex work**, so the
  sex-work framework applies (decriminalised, licensed, partly or fully criminalised —
  see sex-work-practice in this field).
- Separately, many places license **massage establishments and therapists**. Those
  regimes commonly make any sexual service grounds for closure, licence revocation or
  criminal charges, regardless of how sex work is treated otherwise.
- Some municipalities have specific "adult" or "body-rub" business licences (several
  Canadian cities have used these); conditions often cover hours, location and inspections.
- Advertising rules can apply independently of the service's legality.

## 2. Keep it separate from licensed massage therapy
- A **licensed/registered massage therapist** offering sexual services typically breaches
  their professional code outright — boards treat it as sexual misconduct, and it
  ends careers. Don't blend the two under one licence, name or premises.
- Conversely, don't present a sensual service as therapeutic or clinical treatment, or
  use protected titles ("registered massage therapist", "RMT", "LMT") without the licence.
- Be honest in advertising about what the service is and isn't, within local ad law.

## 3. Hygiene
- Fresh linens and towels per client; used linen bagged immediately, washed hot (60°C+
  where fabric allows); oil-soaked linens can **self-heat in dryers** — wash thoroughly,
  don't leave warm piles (a known laundry fire risk).
- Hand hygiene before and after every client; short nails; cover cuts.
- Oils: label and date; ask about **nut, latex and fragrance allergies** before choosing
  products. Oils degrade latex barriers — relevant to sexual-health protection.
- Don't work on broken skin, rashes, open sores or someone who is unwell; reschedule.
- Clean and disinfect the table/surface, showers and shared surfaces between clients.

## 4. Premises and personal safety
- Screening and boundaries as for any intimate service (see boundaries-and-screening and
  consent-communication).
- Clear route to the exit; don't let the client sit between you and the door.
- If working from shared premises, know what local law says about two or more people
  working together (it can be the difference between lawful and an offence).
- Payment taken first, out of sight of other cash.

## 5. Exploitation signs (for workers, landlords and neighbours)
Research on illicit massage businesses (e.g. Polaris, 2018) reports common indicators of
trafficking or labour exploitation: workers living on the premises, doors locked from
outside, workers who never leave alone, someone else holding passports or earnings,
blacked-out windows with heavy client traffic at all hours. Report concerns to an
anti-trafficking hotline rather than confronting anyone.

## Verify
- [ ] You can state how your jurisdiction treats this service and which licences apply.
- [ ] No protected therapy titles or clinical claims are used.
- [ ] Linen, hand and surface hygiene routines are written and followed.
- [ ] Allergy and contraindication questions asked before every session.
- [ ] Screening, exit plan and payment-first routines are in place.
