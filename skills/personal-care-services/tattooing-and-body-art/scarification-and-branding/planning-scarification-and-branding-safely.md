# Scarification and branding — planning, consent and healing management

**Goal:** decide whether a scarification or branding piece is appropriate, design it for how
scars actually spread, carry it out within the law and hygiene rules, and steer the healing.

This is a high-risk modification with permanent, unpredictable results. This skill covers
planning, safety and healing; hands-on cutting or branding technique must be learned under an
experienced practitioner. Status is draft: the numbers are community rules of thumb.

## 1. Is it legal, and are you allowed?
- Laws differ sharply: some jurisdictions treat scarification and branding as tattooing,
  some as a medical procedure, some ban it outright or restrict it by age (minors are almost
  universally excluded). Check your local licensing and insurance **before** offering it.
- Use the same bloodborne-pathogen controls, sterile single-use instruments and records as
  tattooing (see the studio-hygiene-and-aftercare and autoclaves skills).

## 2. Client screening and consent
- Explain in writing: **results vary more than with tattooing**; scars may spread, fade,
  raise, or barely show; touch-ups or second sessions are common; removal is not realistic.
- Ask about keloid or hypertrophic scar history (in the client and their family), healing
  disorders, diabetes, blood thinners, immune suppression and skin conditions at the site.
- People with darker skin and those with a history of keloids are at higher risk of raised,
  spreading scars; keloids can grow beyond the design and recur after treatment. Many
  practitioners decline keloid-prone clients or test with a small piece first.
- Get signed informed consent and keep photographs of the area before work.

## 3. Designing for scars
- Scars **spread**. Lines heal wider than they were cut or burned, and close parallel
  lines can merge into a single blob. Leave generous spacing between elements (community
  guidance often suggests several millimetres, more for branding) and prefer bold, simple
  shapes over fine detail.
- Avoid high-movement areas and skin over joints where tension stretches scars; flat,
  stable areas (upper back, outer thigh, chest) give more predictable results.
- **Branding** generally spreads more than cutting. Strike branding (heated metal strips) is
  harder to control than electrocautery and is less used today.
- Transfer the design with a skin-safe stencil and get the client's approval standing in a mirror.

## 4. Healing management
- Healing commonly takes **months**, and final appearance can take a year or more.
- Some clients deliberately irritate the wound during healing (to keep it open and make a
  more prominent scar). This raises infection and unpredictable-scarring risk; if you advise
  it at all, be explicit about the risk and the warning signs.
- Standard wound-care basics: wash hands before touching; gentle cleaning with mild soap and
  water; non-stick dressing early on; avoid soaking (baths, pools, hot tubs) until closed;
  avoid sun on the healing scar.
- **Refer to a doctor** for spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, red streaks, fever, or a
  scar that keeps growing beyond its borders (possible keloid).

## Gotchas
- Clients often bring tattoo-style flash with fine detail: redesign it for scar spread or decline.
- Over-ambitious first pieces: suggest a smaller test design to see how this person scars.
- Photographs of fresh work look crisp; healed scars rarely do — set expectations with healed
  photos from your portfolio, not fresh ones.
- Insurance policies for tattoo studios may explicitly exclude scarification; check.

## Verify
- [ ] Local law and insurance permit the procedure; the client is an adult.
- [ ] Keloid/healing history screened; high-risk clients declined or test-pieced.
- [ ] Written consent covers unpredictability and permanence.
- [ ] Design spacing allows for spread; no fine detail.
- [ ] Client leaves with written healing instructions and clear medical red flags.
- [ ] Healed result reviewed with photos at follow-up.
