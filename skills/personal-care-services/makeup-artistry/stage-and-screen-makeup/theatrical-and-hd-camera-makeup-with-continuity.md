# Theatrical and HD camera makeup, with continuity

**Goal:** make performers look right for the medium — readable from the back of a
theatre under strong lights, or invisible-but-flattering on an HD/4K camera — and keep
the makeup identical across scenes shot out of order.

## 1. Stage vs screen: opposite problems
| | Stage | HD screen |
|---|---|---|
| Viewing distance | metres to tens of metres | centimetres (close-ups) |
| Goal | **Project** features so they read | **Hide** makeup; look like skin |
| Intensity | Stronger contour, defined eyes and brows, deeper lip | Minimal; thin, even layers |
| Products | Cream/greasepaint, cake, high-pigment | Thin long-wear foundations, often airbrush, finely milled powders |
| Lighting | Strong, often coloured, washes out features | Controlled; camera reveals texture |

## 2. Stage technique
- **Size of house:** a small studio theatre needs near-street makeup; a large house needs
  more definition. Look at the face from the back row under show lighting at the tech
  rehearsal.
- Strong lighting flattens: reshape with **contour and highlight** (cheekbones, jaw, nose,
  brow bone). Define eyes with liner and brows a little stronger than you'd wear in daylight.
- **Sweat:** primer, cream-to-powder layering, set well with powder and setting spray.
- **Coloured gels:** test the palette under the actual rig — e.g. red light can wash out
  red tones, blue light can turn lips dark.
- Teach performers to do their own makeup for long runs: a written step list + face chart.

## 3. HD/broadcast technique
- Thin layers, spot-conceal; less is more. Airbrush is common for evenness.
- **Shine control** without flat skin: blot and powder only the T-zone; carry blotting
  papers on set.
- Check the **monitor**, not the mirror — HD shows texture, powder build-up, and colour
  mismatches at the jaw and neck.
- Presenters under studio lights need regular touch-ups between segments.
- Avoid high-shimmer products on skin; they flare. Fine highlight only.

## 4. Continuity (film/TV)
- **Script breakdown:** for each character, list every scene with story day, time of day,
  and makeup notes (injury, sweat, rain, tiredness, ageing). Scenes are shot out of order —
  the breakdown tells you what each scene needs.
- **Continuity photos:** after the look is final, photograph front, both profiles and
  close-ups of any detail (scar, bruise) with a slate or note of scene number. Repeat at
  the end of a take if anything changed (sweat, smudged lipstick).
- **Continuity notes:** products, shades, placement, "lipstick partly worn off by
  scene end", etc. Many departments use continuity apps or shared folders — follow the
  production's system.
- Keep each actor's kit separate, labelled, to avoid cross-contamination and
  shade drift.

## 5. Quick changes
- Plan them in rehearsal: what changes, in what order, who does it, how long you have.
- Pre-load brushes and products in a labelled quick-change kit by the stage.
- Choose looks that layer on top rather than requiring removal; e.g. add ageing shadows
  rather than removing a full face.

## Gotchas
- Performers' skin takes a beating: proper removal every night, moisturiser, and patch
  testing new products.
- Hair, wigs and costume touch the face — coordinate with wardrobe and wig departments
  (makeup on collars is a costume complaint).
- Don't change a product mid-shoot even if you find a better one — it breaks continuity.
- Union and production rules can determine who may apply what (e.g. hair vs makeup
  departments); learn local practice.

## Verify
- [ ] Look checked from the back row under show lights (stage), or on the monitor (screen).
- [ ] Shine and sweat managed through the performance/take.
- [ ] Script breakdown done; every scene's makeup state known.
- [ ] Continuity photos and notes taken and stored per scene.
- [ ] Quick changes rehearsed and achievable in the time available.
