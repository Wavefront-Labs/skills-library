# Matching foundation to skin tone and undertone

**Goal:** choose or mix a base that disappears into the skin — matching **depth**
(light-to-dark) and **undertone** (the hue underneath) — so there's no visible line at
the jaw, no grey or orange cast, and no surprise once it oxidises or is photographed.

## 1. Two separate axes
- **Depth (value):** how light or dark. Get this right first; a wrong depth is
  more visible than a slightly wrong undertone.
- **Undertone:** roughly **warm** (golden/yellow/peach), **cool** (pink/red/blue),
  **neutral** (balance), and **olive** (a green-grey cast many systems handle poorly).
  Many brands code shades e.g. W/C/N — codes are brand-specific and inconsistent.

Undertone clues (none is definitive — combine them): skin next to a white versus
cream fabric; how the skin looks in natural light at the jaw and neck; whether the
person tans or burns. The "vein colour" test is unreliable — don't rely on it alone.

## 2. The swatch method
1. Work on clean, moisturised, primed skin (dry skin grabs and looks darker).
2. Choose 2–3 candidate shades. **Swatch stripes on the jawline**, extending slightly
   onto the neck — not on the hand or wrist (different colour) and not the cheek
   alone (often redder).
3. Blend each stripe lightly. Check in **natural daylight** or a high-CRI, ~5000–6500 K
   light — warm room lighting hides mismatches.
4. The right shade **vanishes**. Too light looks ashy/grey; too dark looks muddy;
   wrong undertone looks pink, yellow or orange against the neck.
5. Match the **neck and chest** if they'll be visible, not just the face — faces are
   often lighter or redder than the body.

## 3. Mixing custom shades
- Keep a "mixing palette": a lightest, a deepest, a yellow, a red/pink, and a white
  or adjuster drop. Mix on the back of your hand or a steel palette with a spatula,
  not straight from bottle onto face.
- Too pink → add yellow. Too orange → add a cooler or more neutral shade. Too light →
  deepen with a darker shade of the same undertone, not a bronzer.
- Mix only **like formulas** (water-based with water-based, silicone with silicone);
  incompatible bases separate or pill.
- For deeper skin tones, a single shade often won't match both the centre of the face
  and the perimeter; using two shades (lighter centre, deeper perimeter) is common.

## 4. Oxidation and camera
- Many foundations darken or warm as they dry and react with skin oils — wait
  **10–15 minutes** and recheck before committing, especially for long events.
- **Flash photography:** products with high levels of SPF ingredients (titanium dioxide,
  zinc oxide) or some setting powders (high silica content) can reflect flash and cause a
  white cast ("flashback"). Test with a phone flash photo.

## 5. Colour correcting before base
Use complementary colours sparingly under foundation:
- Redness → green-tinted primer/corrector.
- Blue/purple under-eye on lighter skin → peach; on deeper skin → orange or red.
- Sallowness → lavender (lightly).
Then conceal with a shade at or slightly lighter than the foundation; under-eye concealer
too light looks grey on deeper skin.

## Gotchas
- **Online shade finders vary widely**; always confirm by swatching.
- Same shade name differs across brands and even formulas from one brand.
- Seasonal change: many clients need a lighter winter and darker summer shade; mix
  between them.
- Mature or textured skin: less product, thinner layers, matched carefully at the jaw.
- Never test a new product on a client without asking about allergies.

## Verify
- [ ] Depth and undertone assessed separately; jaw swatch disappears in daylight.
- [ ] Face, neck (and chest if visible) look one colour; no line at the jaw.
- [ ] Rechecked after 10–15 min for oxidation.
- [ ] Phone-flash photo shows no white cast.
- [ ] Mixed shade recorded (products + ratio) in the client's notes for repeat.
