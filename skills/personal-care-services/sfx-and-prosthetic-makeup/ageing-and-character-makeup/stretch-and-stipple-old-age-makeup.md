# Stretch-and-stipple old-age makeup

**Goal:** make a performer look decades older without prosthetic appliances, using
their own facial structure plus a latex-based wrinkle texture. It is the main ageing
method for theatre and lower-budget screen work; for close-up film, appliances usually
read better.

## 1. Read the performer's own ageing map
Ageing makeup works when it **follows the face the performer will actually have**.
- Ask them to **scrunch, frown, smile wide, raise brows, squint and purse lips**. The
  creases that appear are where wrinkles will form: forehead lines, crow's feet,
  nasolabial folds, marionette lines, lip lines, neck bands.
- Look at reference photos of the performer's parents or older relatives if available,
  and real elderly reference faces of similar build and ethnicity.
- Ageing is mostly **loss of volume and skin elasticity**: hollow temples and cheeks,
  bonier brow and jaw, heavier lids, lower jowls, thinner lips.

## 2. Highlight and shadow first (the foundation)
- Base slightly **sallower / greyer / less rosy** than the performer's own tone.
- **Shadow** (a cool brown, one or two shades darker) into hollows: temples, under the
  cheekbone, eye sockets, under the lower lip, either side of the nose, jowl creases.
- **Highlight** on the edge of every shadow facing the light: cheekbone top, brow
  bone, upper side of the nasolabial fold, the bulge of the jowl. A fold = a
  highlight next to a sharp shadow line, softened on the far side.
- Draw wrinkles **only in the creases you found**, with a fine brush in shadow and a
  thin highlight just above. Generic lines in random places read as drawn-on.

## 3. Stretch and stipple (texture)
**Liquid latex (or a latex-free old-age stipple such as a Glatzan-type or silicone
product for latex-allergic performers)**. Patch test first; keep away from eyes, brows
(or protect them with barrier) and hairline.
1. Work **one small section at a time** — e.g. the crow's-feet area on one side.
2. **Stretch** the skin firmly in two directions with your fingers (or ask the
   performer to hold a stretch).
3. **Stipple** a thin layer with a torn sponge.
4. **Dry while still stretched** (cool hairdryer setting; never hot on the face).
5. Powder lightly, then **release**: the latex wrinkles as the skin relaxes.
6. Repeat for 2–4 layers in each area for deeper texture.
Typical areas: crow's feet, under-eyes, forehead, cheeks, back of hands, neck. The
eyelids and very mobile mouth corners are usually left untextured or done very lightly.

## 4. Colour over the texture
Latex goes darker and shiny, so **re-base** with a rubber-mask grease or
alcohol-activated palette (cream makeup may not stick; a castor-oil-based or
latex-compatible base is traditional — check compatibility). Then:
- Re-establish shadows in the deepest wrinkles.
- **Age spots / liver spots** in light brown, irregular, a few, mostly on temples,
  forehead and hands.
- **Broken capillaries** on cheeks and nose with a fine red/purple brush, very sparing.
- Lips: thinner, less colour, fine vertical lines.
- **Hair:** grey or white streaks at temples and hairline with hair whitener, cream or
  coloured spray; eyebrows a little grey and less groomed.

## 5. Removal
Peel latex gently from the edges with remover or oil; cleanse and moisturise. Repeated
daily latex can irritate — monitor skin on long runs.

## Gotchas
- Too much stipple on a young, tight jawline just looks like peeling skin — the face
  needs the volume changes from highlight/shadow too.
- Hands and neck betray the ageing if forgotten.
- Stage distance hides texture and exaggerates painted lines; camera is the opposite.
  Adjust intensity to medium.

## Verify
- [ ] Wrinkles placed in the performer's own crease map, not generic positions.
- [ ] Volume loss painted with highlight/shadow before texture.
- [ ] Stipple applied stretched-and-dried in sections; no latex on eyes/brows.
- [ ] Hands, neck and hair aged to match the face; checked under show light/camera.
