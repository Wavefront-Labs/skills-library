# Out-of-kit cuts, bruises, burns and blood

**Goal:** quick, convincing injuries made from kit materials directly on the skin (no
moulds), that read on camera or from the stalls, stay put for the scene, and come off
cleanly. The same techniques are used in casualty simulation (moulage) for medical and
emergency training.

## 0. Safety and reference
- Ask about **latex allergy** before using liquid latex (use silicone or gelatine
  alternatives). Patch-test new products. No effects on broken or irritated skin, or
  near the eyes with solvent-based materials.
- **Use real reference photos** of the injury (medical or forensic references) for the
  correct colour and shape — audiences know what injuries look like even if they can't
  say why.

## 1. Bruises — colour tells the age
| Age | Colours (roughly) |
|---|---|
| Fresh (minutes–hours) | Red, pinkish, slightly raised/puffy |
| 1–2 days | Deep red-purple, blue, dark centre |
| ~5–7 days | Green and yellow edges creeping in, centre fading |
| ~10+ days | Yellow-brown, faded and diffuse |
Real bruise timing varies a lot by person and injury; use this for storytelling, not
forensics.
- Use a cream or alcohol-activated palette and a **stipple sponge** (torn edges);
  build from lightest to darkest, **darkest where the force landed** (e.g. over bone),
  blurred, irregular edges. Add a hint of **swelling** with a highlight on the raised
  part. Never one flat blob.

## 2. Cuts and gashes with wax
1. Degrease skin with alcohol; apply a thin **spirit gum or adhesive** base so wax
   grips (wax slides off oily skin).
2. Warm **scar/nose/derma wax** in your fingers; press onto the area, **feather the
   edges** to nothing with a spatula and a drop of massage oil or wax-thinner.
3. **Seal** the wax with a thin layer of sealer or liquid latex so makeup sits on it.
4. Colour the wax to the skin tone with a sponge; then cut into the wax with a blunt
   spatula's edge, opening the wound slightly.
5. Paint the inside dark red/maroon (deepest at the bottom), a little bruising and
   redness around it, then blood.
Wax is fragile — not for areas that bend a lot (knuckles, mouth corners).

## 3. Burns
- **1st-degree (redness):** stipple red/pink, a little shine.
- **2nd-degree (blisters, peeling):** layer liquid latex with tissue, dry with a cool
  hairdryer, tear and peel some edges; colour raw red under the peel, blisters with
  clear gel or a drop of glycerine under thin latex.
- **3rd-degree (charring):** gelatine or latex texture, dark browns, black, waxy
  white-yellow patches; add soot (black powder) sparingly.
- **Rigid collodion** puckers skin into a shallow scar as it dries — not on sensitive
  skin, never near eyes, and **don't reapply repeatedly** on the same spot.

## 4. Blood — choose by job
| Type | Use |
|---|---|
| Mouth-safe (edible) blood | In or near the mouth; check ingredients for allergies |
| Flowing/thin blood | Runs, drips, rig work |
| Thick/gel blood | Stays in wounds, oozes slowly |
| Scab/dried blood | Older wounds, crusting |
- Blood darkens and browns as it "ages" — fresh is bright red, older darker.
- Many stage bloods **stain** costume, skin and set; test on the actual fabric, have
  washable versions for repeat takes, and warn wardrobe.
- Blood rigs (tubes and pumps hidden under appliances or costume) are a separate
  discipline — rehearse with the actor and the AD.

## Gotchas
- Too much blood looks fake and kills continuity — start with less.
- Under hot lights, wax softens and sags; keep fixer and spare wax at hand.
- Remove wax with oil, latex by peeling gently with remover, collodion with its matching
  remover; cleanse and moisturise afterwards.

## Verify
- [ ] Allergies and skin checked; products patch-tested.
- [ ] Effect matches a real reference for the age/type of injury.
- [ ] Edges of wax/latex invisible at camera distance.
- [ ] Blood type suits the shot; stain test done on costume.
- [ ] Removed cleanly; skin intact afterwards.
