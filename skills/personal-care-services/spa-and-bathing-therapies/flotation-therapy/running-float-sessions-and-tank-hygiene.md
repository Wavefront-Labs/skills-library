# Flotation therapy — preparing the tank, orienting clients, keeping the solution clean

**Goal:** run float sessions where the solution is clean, buoyant and at skin temperature,
and where the client knows exactly what to do so they relax rather than panic or sting.

Regulation of float tanks varies (some places treat them as spas/pools, some barely regulate
them); follow local rules and your equipment maker's manual. Figures below are typical values.

## 1. The solution
- Water saturated with **Epsom salt (magnesium sulphate)**, usually several hundred kg in a
  ~25-30 cm deep tank, so the body floats effortlessly.
- **Specific gravity** typically **~1.23-1.30**; check with a hydrometer at a fixed temperature
  (readings shift with temperature). Low SG = clients sink at the hips; top up salt.
- **Temperature** close to **skin surface temperature, ~34-35.5 °C**, so the boundary between body
  and water fades. Too warm feels stuffy and can cause overheating; too cool distracts.
- Air temperature in the room/pod similar to the water, with ventilation to clear humidity.

## 2. Hygiene and filtration
- Salt concentration inhibits many microbes but doesn't sterilise.
- Full **filtration turnover after every float** (often 3-4 passes through the filter, per the
  maker), with a secondary disinfection system: commonly **UV and ozone**, sometimes hydrogen
  peroxide. Chlorine is used in some systems but is debated because it builds up without
  evaporation. Use what your manufacturer and regulator approve.
- Mandatory **pre-float shower** with shampoo; no conditioner, oils or lotions (they leave
  residue and foul the filters). Post-float shower to rinse salt.
- Clean the tank surfaces and skim the water line regularly; test and log disinfectant
  levels and pH per your protocol; change filters on schedule.

## 3. Screening
Avoid or seek medical advice with: epilepsy (uncontrolled), open wounds or recent tattoos
(stinging, infection risk), skin infections, incontinence, severe low blood pressure,
contagious illness, after alcohol or drugs, recent hair dye (can stain the solution) and
during late pregnancy without clearance. Claustrophobia: offer an open room or leave the
lid/door open — it's the client's choice.

## 4. Client orientation (before every first float)
- Show them how the door/lid works from inside, the light switch, the emergency/call button.
- **Earplugs** in (water in ears is a common distraction); a towel and spray bottle of fresh
  water inside the tank to clear salt from the eyes.
- Cover small cuts with petroleum jelly — salt stings.
- "Lie back, let your head rest in the water. If your neck feels tense, use the floating neck
  pillow. Arms by your sides or above your head. Move slowly to avoid splashing into your eyes."
- Don't shave or wax the same day.
- Session length: typically 60-90 minutes; a soft sound or light signals the end.

## 5. Session flow
1. Check SG, temperature and filter cycle complete before each client.
2. Orientation, then the client showers and enters privately.
3. Stay available; don't open the door during the float unless the client calls or an alarm sounds.
4. After: client showers; you run the filtration cycle and check the room.

## Gotchas
- Salt crystallises on everything; rinse surfaces and floors to avoid corrosion and slips.
- A client who has been stung by salt in the eyes often panics; the spray bottle and towel must be in reach.
- First-time floaters often don't relax until the second or third float; tell them.
- Humidity and salt corrode electrics; schedule inspections.

## Verify
- [ ] Specific gravity and temperature within range before each float, logged.
- [ ] Filtration turnover completed between clients; disinfection system working.
- [ ] Client screened and oriented (door, light, call button, earplugs, eye spray).
- [ ] Pre- and post-float showers completed; no oils or conditioner in the tank.
- [ ] Floors and surfaces rinsed of salt; no slip hazards.
