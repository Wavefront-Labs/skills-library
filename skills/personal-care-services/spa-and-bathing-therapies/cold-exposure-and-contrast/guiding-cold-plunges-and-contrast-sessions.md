# Cold exposure and contrast therapy — guiding plunges safely

**Goal:** guide clients through cold plunges and hot-cold contrast sessions so they get the
experience without the real risks: cold shock, breath-hold blackout and afterdrop.

Benefit claims for cold exposure (immunity, recovery, metabolism) are mixed and much weaker
than marketing suggests; describe it as an experience and stimulus, not a treatment.

## 1. The physiology you're managing
- **Cold-shock response (first ~1-3 minutes):** an involuntary gasp, rapid breathing, spike in
  heart rate and blood pressure. This is where drowning and cardiac events happen — not
  hypothermia. It peaks in the first 30-60 seconds and settles with calm breathing.
- **Afterdrop:** core temperature can keep falling for a while after getting out, as cold
  blood from the limbs returns; clients may shiver and feel worst 5-15 minutes later.
- Hypothermia takes much longer in a short plunge but becomes relevant with long immersions,
  very cold water, thin clients or repeated rounds.

## 2. Screening (ask every time, not just once)
Avoid or get a doctor's clearance with: heart disease or arrhythmias, high blood pressure,
history of stroke, pregnancy, Raynaud's, cold urticaria (hives from cold), epilepsy, recent
illness, and after alcohol or drugs. People new to cold: start warm-ish and short.

## 3. Setting up
- Plunge temperatures commonly **3-15 °C**; for beginners **10-15 °C** is plenty.
- Entry with steps and a handrail; depth so a client can stand with head well clear; a
  non-slip floor; a clock or timer visible; towels, robe and warm area close by.
- **Never** let anyone plunge alone; an attendant watches the whole time.
- Water hygiene: plunges are usually chilled and filtered; follow local rules for disinfection
  and change water as required (cold doesn't stop contamination).

## 4. Coaching the plunge
1. Explain what will happen: "you'll want to gasp; that's normal, it passes in under a minute."
2. **Enter slowly**, feet first — no jumping or diving, and no putting the head under at first.
3. **Breathing:** long, slow exhales (e.g. in for 4, out for 6-8 through pursed lips). Count
   with them; focus on the out-breath.
4. **Duration:** beginners 30-90 seconds; experienced users commonly 2-5 minutes. Longer adds
   risk, not obvious benefit. Exit if shivering is intense, coordination fades, or the client
   asks.
5. **Never combine breath-holding or hyperventilation techniques with immersion** — this is a
   known cause of shallow-water blackout and drowning.

## 5. Contrast protocols
- Common pattern: **heat (sauna/hot bath) 8-15 min → cold 30 s-3 min → rest/warm 5-10 min**,
  repeated 2-4 rounds, often ending on cold (invigorating) or warm (relaxing) by preference.
- Leave time between the sauna and the plunge to cool a little for those with blood-pressure
  concerns, or use a cool shower instead of a plunge.
- Hydrate between rounds.

## 6. Rewarming
- Dry off, dress in warm layers, gentle movement, warm drink. Avoid an immediate very hot
  shower for someone shivering hard after a long immersion (rapid rewarming can make them
  light-headed); let them recover gradually.
- Watch for afterdrop signs: worsening shivering, pallor, confusion. Get them warm and seated;
  call for medical help if confusion or drowsiness appears.

## Gotchas
- Competitive clients push durations; set limits before they get in.
- Ice baths filled by tipping in ice: water can be colder than the display shows near the ice;
  stir and measure.
- Phones and watches near the plunge: distraction; the attendant watches people, not screens.

## Verify
- [ ] Client screened today; nothing on the avoid list.
- [ ] Water temperature measured; entry is slow, feet first, never alone.
- [ ] Client coached through cold shock with slow exhales; no breath-hold drills.
- [ ] Duration within agreed limit; exit criteria explained.
- [ ] Client rewarmed and observed for afterdrop before leaving.
