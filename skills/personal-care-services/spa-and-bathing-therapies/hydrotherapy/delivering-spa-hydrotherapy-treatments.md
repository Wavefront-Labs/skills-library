# Hydrotherapy — therapeutic baths, Vichy showers, affusions and whirlpools

**Goal:** deliver water-based wellbeing treatments at safe temperatures and durations, with
water that is clean, and with clients screened for the few conditions that make heat or
immersion risky.

This is wellbeing hydrotherapy, not clinical/physiotherapy hydrotherapy for named conditions.
Temperatures are typical spa ranges; check your treatment protocols and local regulations.

## 1. Temperature bands (rough guide)
- **Cold:** below ~20 °C (affusions, cold plunges — see cold-exposure-and-contrast skill).
- **Neutral:** ~33-35 °C — feels neither warm nor cool, relaxing for long soaks.
- **Warm:** ~36-38 °C — most therapeutic baths and whirlpools.
- **Hot:** above ~38 °C — keep sessions short; **40 °C** is a commonly cited upper limit for hot
  tubs (and the US CPSC limit for hot tubs is 104 °F / 40 °C).
Always check the water with a thermometer, not your hand.

## 2. Screening
Ask about: heart disease, blood pressure (high or low), pregnancy (avoid hot immersion),
diabetes with reduced sensation in feet, epilepsy, open wounds or infections, skin conditions,
recent alcohol, and medicines that cause drowsiness or affect blood pressure. When in doubt,
lower temperature, shorten time, or decline and suggest the client asks their doctor.

## 3. Treatments
**Therapeutic bath (hydrotherapy tub):** fill to protocol temperature (often 36-38 °C), add
salts, minerals or seaweed per the product label, 15-20 minutes. Underwater massage hoses or
jets: start at low pressure, work from feet towards the heart, avoid the abdomen if pregnant
(and avoid the face, spine and joints with direct high pressure).

**Vichy shower:** client lies on a wet table under a horizontal rail of nozzles. Set and test
temperature on your wrist **before** the water reaches the client; start warm, often after a
scrub or wrap to rinse; pressure and temperature can be alternated. Keep the client covered
where not being treated, and support getting off a wet table — high slip risk.

**Experience showers:** programmed sequences of light, scent and temperature (tropical rain,
cold fog). Check programmes and nozzle cleanliness daily; explain cold sections in advance.

**Affusions (Kneipp-style guss):** a gentle stream of water from an open hose poured along the
body, typically cold after warming: start at the right foot, up the outside of the leg to
above the knee, down the inside, then the left leg; arm affusions similarly from the right hand
up. The client should be warm before and warmed afterwards (dress, move, rest).

**Whirlpool/hot tub:** 10-15 minutes per session, then get out and cool; ensure hair and loose
items can't be trapped at suction outlets (entrapment risk), and that the emergency stop works.

## 4. Water safety and hygiene
- Warm, aerated water grows bacteria fast: **Legionella** (aerosols from jets and showers),
  **Pseudomonas** ("hot-tub folliculitis" rash).
- Follow local requirements for disinfectant (chlorine or bromine) and pH, tested several times
  a day in busy whirlpools; keep logs.
- Drain, clean and disinfect single-client tubs between clients; flush and clean shower heads
  and little-used outlets regularly to limit Legionella.
- Pre-shower rule for clients reduces organic load in shared pools.

## Gotchas
- Fainting on standing up after a hot bath: help clients sit, then stand slowly.
- Bath additives can make surfaces very slippery; rinse the tub and use mats.
- Elderly or diabetic clients may not feel scalding temperatures — always measure.
- Electrical equipment near water must be rated and RCD/GFCI protected.

## Verify
- [ ] Client screened; temperature and duration adjusted to them.
- [ ] Water temperature measured with a thermometer before the client enters.
- [ ] Jet pressure started low; sensitive areas avoided.
- [ ] Disinfectant and pH within local limits and logged; tubs cleaned between clients.
- [ ] Client helped off wet surfaces and rested before leaving.
