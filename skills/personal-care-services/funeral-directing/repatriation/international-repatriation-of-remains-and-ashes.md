# International repatriation of remains and ashes

**Goal:** the deceased travels between countries legally, hygienically and on schedule,
arriving with every document the receiving country needs, so the family can hold the
funeral at home. Requirements are set **by the destination country, the departure
country and the airline** — always get the destination consulate's current list in
writing; the details below are typical, not universal.

## 1. First steps
- Establish who is paying (travel insurer, employer, family) — insurers often appoint an
  international repatriation specialist; get the authorisation before incurring costs.
- Identify the **receiving funeral director** in the destination country early; they
  confirm local requirements and meet the flight.
- Find out if a coroner/medical examiner or police investigation holds the body; release
  can take time.

## 2. Typical document set (sending)
| Document | Notes |
|---|---|
| Death certificate (certified copy) | Often needs **translation** and sometimes an **apostille** or consular legalisation |
| Embalming certificate | Many destinations require embalming for repatriation |
| Certificate of non-contagion / freedom from infection | Signed by a doctor or public-health officer |
| Out-of-country removal permission | E.g. England/Wales: notice to the coroner before removal (commonly given at least 4 clear days ahead — verify) |
| Consular / mortuary certificate or laissez-passer | Strasbourg Agreement states use a single corpse laissez-passer |
| Funeral director's statement of contents | Confirms the shipping case contains only the deceased and listed effects |
| Passport of the deceased | Usually cancelled by/returned to the consulate |
| Air waybill | Booked as **HUM** (human remains) cargo |

## 3. Preparation and packaging
- Embalming (arterial and cavity) to international standard where required.
- **Hermetically sealed coffin** — commonly a zinc or metal-lined inner coffin soldered or
  sealed — inside the wooden coffin, then an outer shipping case or air tray per airline
  rules. Some faiths object to embalming or metal; discuss and check what the destination
  will accept.
- Label outer case: "Human Remains", handling orientation, consignee details. Seal after
  any official inspection (some countries require a police or consular sealing witness).

## 4. Airline booking
- Book through the airline's cargo desk, often via a specialist agent; check the
  aircraft can take the case size and that the route allows HUM (some transit hubs require
  extra documents).
- Deliver to the cargo terminal by the airline's cut-off (often several hours before departure).
- Send scanned documents ahead to the receiving FD and consignee.

## 5. Receiving remains
- Collect from the cargo terminal with ID and the waybill; customs/health clearance.
- Inspect: case intact, ID matches documents. Opening sealed coffins may be restricted.
- Local registration requirements for a death abroad vary — some countries register it,
  others accept the foreign certificate.

## 6. Cremated remains
- Much simpler: cremation certificate, death certificate copy, sealed and **X-ray
  scannable** urn (metal or stone urns can't be screened — use a temporary container).
- Many airlines permit ashes in cabin or hold luggage with documents; confirm with the
  airline. Some countries restrict import or scattering.

## Gotchas
- Weekends and public holidays freeze consulates and registries — warn families early.
- Name transliteration errors between documents cause refusals.
- Infectious-disease deaths may bar embalming or repatriation of the body altogether.

## Verify
- [ ] Destination consulate's current requirement list obtained in writing.
- [ ] Coroner/out-of-country permission granted.
- [ ] All documents translated/legalised as required and copies sent ahead.
- [ ] Packaging meets destination and airline rules; HUM booking confirmed.
- [ ] Receiving FD confirmed collection and clearance.
