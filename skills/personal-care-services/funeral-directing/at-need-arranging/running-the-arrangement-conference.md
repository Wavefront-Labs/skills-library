# Running the arrangement conference

**Goal:** a bereaved family leaves the arrangement meeting with a funeral that reflects
the person and their own wishes, a clear written cost, and confidence they weren't
pressured — and the firm has the authorisations it needs.

## 1. Before the family arrives
- Confirm the deceased is in your care (or the transfer is booked) and what paperwork
  exists: medical certificate of cause of death, coroner/medical examiner involvement,
  any pre-need plan or written wishes (check your records — see pre-need-planning).
- Prepare the price list, brochures and a quiet room with tissues, water and no rush.
- Allow 1–2 hours; families often need more than they expect.

## 2. Establish who decides
- Identify the person with legal authority over arrangements — the executor (in England
  and Wales, generally the executor, then next of kin by priority) or, in US states, the
  statutory **right-to-control-disposition** order (designated agent, spouse, adult
  children by majority, parents...). Rules vary; know yours.
- Family disagreement over burial vs cremation is common; don't take instructions from
  someone without authority, and pause rather than proceed if authority is disputed.

## 3. Lead with prices, by the rules
- **US FTC Funeral Rule:** give the **General Price List (GPL)** to keep at the start of
  any face-to-face discussion of arrangements, prices or services; show the casket and
  outer-burial-container price lists before showing those items; provide an **itemised
  statement of goods and services selected** at the end. You may not require purchase of
  a casket for direct cremation, nor claim embalming is legally required when it isn't.
- **UK CMA Funerals Order 2021:** display and give the **Standardised Price List**
  (attended and unattended funerals), show third-party costs, and don't link
  incentives to sales pressure (verify current text).
- Always separate **disbursements** (crematorium/cemetery fees, clergy or celebrant,
  doctor or medical examiner fees, newspaper notices) from your own fees.

## 4. Walk through the choices
A typical order: disposition (burial, cremation, direct/unattended) → timing and venue →
viewing/visitation and whether embalming is wanted (explain options honestly) → coffin or
casket → ceremony type and officiant (see religion-philosophy.celebrancy-and-officiating.funeral-celebrancy.leading-a-funeral-from-family-meeting-to-committal)
→ music, readings, flowers → vehicles and cortege (see cortege-and-transport-logistics)
→ notices, memorial donations → reception (see hospitality-culinary.ceremonial-food-and-drink.funeral-reception-catering.planning-and-serving-a-funeral-reception)
→ ashes or memorialisation.
- Offer, don't upsell: present a full range including the simplest option, and say it
  without apology.
- Note faith and cultural requirements early — some require burial within 24 hours,
  washing by the community, or no embalming.

## 5. Close the meeting
- Read back the plan; give the itemised estimate/statement and payment terms in writing.
- Collect signatures on authorisations (cremation forms, embalming authorisation, release
  forms) — see documentation-and-compliance.
- Agree who the family's single contact is, and when you'll next call.
- Mention bereavement support and state benefits that help with costs (e.g. UK Funeral
  Expenses Payment) where relevant.

## Gotchas
- Grief affects memory: put everything in writing and confirm by email.
- Don't promise dates before the crematorium/cemetery and officiant are confirmed.
- Coroner/medical examiner cases can delay release — warn the family before booking.
- Words matter: "the person who has died" or their name, not "the body" or "the remains".

## Verify
- [ ] Legal authority to arrange confirmed and recorded.
- [ ] Price list given at the start; itemised statement given at the end.
- [ ] Disbursements shown separately; simplest option offered.
- [ ] Faith/cultural needs captured; authorisations signed.
- [ ] Family has a written plan and a named contact.
