# Handler teamwork and route travel with a guide dog

**Goal:** a handler and a newly matched, already-trained guide or service dog who travel
safely as one unit, keep the dog's training from drifting, and handle public access
confidently.

**Scope note:** this is the *handler's* orientation after matching. Training the dog
itself is done by the school; class/residential team training and aftercare visits come
from them. Commands and harness handling differ between schools — always use your
school's wording (the ones below are common, not universal).

## 1. What the dog does and doesn't do
- The dog **avoids obstacles** (including overhead ones a cane misses), stops at kerbs
  and steps, finds doors, crossings and seats on command, and keeps a straight line.
- The dog **does not know where you are going** and cannot judge traffic lights. The
  handler navigates and decides when to cross; the dog may refuse an unsafe command
  (**intelligent disobedience**, e.g. a car turning across) — the handler must trust a
  refusal and reassess, not force it.
- Orientation skills (landmarks, parallel traffic, mental maps) are still the handler's
  job; a dog user without them is lost the day the dog is sick.

## 2. Harness and commands
- Dog on the **left** (most schools), harness handle in the left hand, lead in the left
  hand too or looped; right hand free.
- Feel the harness: a pause or slowing means something ahead; a stop at a kerb means a
  kerb. Explore with a foot, then praise and give the next command.
- Typical commands: *forward*, *left*, *right*, *halt/wait*, *find the kerb/door/
  crossing/chair*, *straight on*, *back*. Support directions with a hand signal.
- **Follow:** follow a person (e.g. a host leading you to a table).
- Praise the moment of good work; correct promptly and consistently in the school's style.

## 3. Route work
- Teach new routes in segments: handler learns the route first (sighted guide, cane or
  description), then works it with the dog, reinforcing "find" targets at each point.
- Vary routes and speeds so the dog doesn't anticipate turns and pull the team off-plan.
- Off-harness = off-duty. Harness on means work; consistent signals avoid confusion.

## 4. Relieving and welfare
- Fixed relieving ("spending") times and command; plan them before long journeys and flights.
- Daily grooming = health check: eyes, ears, paws, skin, weight. Feed the measured ration.
- Work load: watch for fatigue, heat (hot pavements burn paws), lameness, reluctance to
  work. Report changes to the school early; they would rather visit than retrain.
- Free play and off-lead run in safe spaces matter for a working dog's motivation.

## 5. Public access (legal — check locally)
- US ADA: staff may ask only (1) is the dog required because of a disability and (2)
  what task it is trained to perform; no ID or demonstration required. UK Equality Act
  2010: refusing an assistance dog is generally unlawful discrimination for service
  providers including taxis. Other countries differ; some require ID cards.
- Carry your school's ID card anyway — it ends most disputes fast.
- Handler is responsible for the dog's behaviour: under control, toileting planned.

## Gotchas
- Members of the public distracting, feeding or petting a working dog — ask firmly,
  "Please don't distract my dog, she's working."
- Training drift: the dog starts sniffing, cutting corners or stopping short. Correct
  every time, and call the school if it persists.
- Other dogs (especially off-lead) are the most common cause of guide-dog incidents;
  have a plan (stop, body-block, call for help).

## Verify
- [ ] Handler can describe each route by landmarks without the dog.
- [ ] Commands are consistent with the school's wording; praise is timely.
- [ ] Refusals are respected and reassessed.
- [ ] Relieving, feeding and grooming routines are fixed and logged.
- [ ] Handler can state their local public-access rights and carries ID.
