# Matching and teaching adaptive daily-living aids

**Goal:** the person does the daily tasks that matter to them, as independently as they
want, with aids that actually get used — not a cupboard of abandoned gadgets.

**Scope note:** bathroom equipment that bears weight (bath lifts, grab rails, toilet
frames) is normally assessed and specified by an occupational therapist and fitted by a
competent installer. A support worker teaches and reinforces use.

## 1. Start from the task and the barrier
Ask "what do you want to do that's hard?" then identify *why*:
| Barrier | Aid families |
|---|---|
| Weak or painful grip (arthritis) | Built-up/foam handles, lever taps, jar openers, kettle tipper, key turners |
| Reduced reach / can't bend (hip replacement, back) | Reacher/grabber, sock aid, long-handled shoehorn and sponge, raised toilet seat |
| One hand (stroke, amputation) | Rocker knife, plate guard, Dycem non-slip mat, spike board, one-handed chopping board, elastic laces |
| Tremor | Weighted cutlery and cups, lidded cups, stabilising spoons |
| Low vision | Liquid-level indicator, bump-on dots, high-contrast boards, talking scales |
| Balance/fatigue | Perching stool, kitchen trolley, shower chair, bath board |
Prefer **changing the method or environment** first (sitting to dress, moving the
kettle, lighter pans) — it's free and nothing to lose.

## 2. Teaching use
1. Demonstrate the whole task once, then break into steps.
2. The person tries with you hands-off; correct only safety issues the first time.
3. Practise in the **real place** (their kitchen, their bathroom) — clinic success
   rarely transfers.
4. Written or picture step cards for multi-step aids (sock aid, bath board).
5. Recheck after a week of real use; most problems show up then.

### Classic sequences
- **Sock aid:** sitting, slide the sock on the gutter with the heel at the back, drop to
  the floor by the cords, slip the foot in, pull the cords up.
- **Dressing (hemiplegia):** affected limb in first, out last.
- **Bath board:** sit on the board from the side, lift legs over one at a time (with
  help as assessed), shuffle to the middle; never stand in a bath to transfer.
- **Reacher:** grip near the tip, keep the object close, don't lift heavy items with it.

## 3. Energy conservation
Plan, pace, prioritise, position: sit for tasks, gather everything first, split heavy
jobs over the day, keep frequently used items between hip and shoulder height.

## 4. Why aids get abandoned (and how to prevent it)
- Not wanted — the person didn't see the point or feels stigmatised. Involve them in choice.
- Doesn't fit the home (bath too narrow, nowhere to store it). Check measurements.
- Not taught properly, or taught once in hospital. Teach at home.
- Condition changed. Review periodically.
Uncertain: published abandonment rates vary widely between studies; treat "many aids
are abandoned" as the reliable takeaway, not any single figure.

## Gotchas
- Raised toilet seats must be fitted and locked to that toilet pan; a loose seat is a fall.
- Dycem loses grip when dusty — wash and air-dry it.
- Weighted cutlery can tire weak arms; trial before buying.
- Don't remove an aid an OT prescribed without discussing it.

## Verify
- [ ] Each aid links to a named task and barrier.
- [ ] Taught and practised in the person's own environment.
- [ ] Safety-critical bathroom equipment was OT-assessed and properly fitted.
- [ ] Follow-up done after real use; unused aids discussed, not ignored.
