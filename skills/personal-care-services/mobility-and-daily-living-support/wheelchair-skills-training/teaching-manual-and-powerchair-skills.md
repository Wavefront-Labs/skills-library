# Teaching manual and powerchair skills

**Goal:** a wheelchair user who can get themselves anywhere their chair can physically
go — safely, efficiently and without wrecking their shoulders — in a structured,
spotted progression rather than "learn it on the street".

## 1. Before teaching anything
- **Check the chair is set up for the person.** Rear-axle position, seat height and
  backrest angle change everything: a far-back axle is stable but heavy to push and
  nearly impossible to wheelie; a forward axle is efficient but tippy. Fit issues are
  a seating/OT job — flag them, don't teach around them.
- **Tyres inflated, brakes biting, anti-tippers fitted and their position known.**
  Soft tyres make every skill harder and can stop brakes holding.
- **Baseline assessment.** Use the Wheelchair Skills Test (WST, Dalhousie Wheelchair
  Skills Program, freely available) or its questionnaire version to score each skill as
  pass / pass-with-difficulty / fail. Re-test at the end to show progress.
- **Ask about pain, sensation and pressure care.** Long sessions on a hard surface with
  poor sensation risk pressure injury; build in pressure-relief breaks.

## 2. Propulsion (manual)
- Teach **long, smooth strokes**: contact the handrim well behind top-dead-centre, push
  through a long arc, then let the hand drop below the rim on the return (the
  "semicircular" pattern). Fewer, longer strokes load the shoulder less than short,
  choppy ones — this is the core of shoulder-preservation guidance.
- Steering is braking one rim, not pushing harder on the other.
- Practise on carpet, smooth floor, gentle slopes, cross-slopes (the downhill wheel
  needs more push) and gravel/grass.

## 3. Graded skills (typical order)
1. Rolling forward/back, turning in place, through doorways, opening doors.
2. Reaching and picking objects from the floor (lean within the base of support).
3. Ramps up (lean forward) and down (lean back, controlled friction through the rims).
4. **Wheelie (castor pop / balance)** — only with a spotter and a spotting strap tied to
   the chair frame, *never* held by the user's body. Progress: pop the castors → find the
   balance point → hold → move in a wheelie → turn in a wheelie.
5. Small kerbs up (castor pop onto the kerb, then drive the rear wheels up) and down
   (forwards in a wheelie, or backwards with lean-forward descent).
6. Falls: how to tuck the head and hands when tipping backwards, and how to get back
   into the chair or summon help.

## 4. Powerchair specifics
- Start in the lowest speed/acceleration profile and a large clear space.
- Joystick: small deliberate deflections; teach **stop = let go** before anything else.
- Drive-wheel position matters: rear-wheel-drive swings the front wide on turns,
  front-wheel-drive "fishtails" at speed, mid-wheel turns on the spot but can rock on
  kerbs. Teach turning clearance to match.
- Kerb climbing limits are set by the manufacturer — never exceed them; approach
  square-on. Check battery range and charging routine as part of the skill set.
- Tilt/recline and seat elevate: when they are safe to use while driving (often
  speed-limited or locked out).

## 5. Gotchas
- **Spotter position:** behind the chair, hands near (not on) the push handles, strap
  slack but ready. Most training falls are backwards.
- Anti-tippers stop wheelies and kerb climbs; removing them is a decision for the
  user's team, not the trainer's convenience.
- Cold, fatigue and new medication degrade skills; stop sessions before failure.
- Exact skill order and kerb heights vary between programmes — follow the user's
  clinical goals (uncertain: the WSP order above is a common default, not a rule).

## Verify
- [ ] Chair checked (tyres, brakes, anti-tippers) and baseline WST recorded.
- [ ] Propulsion uses long strokes with low-cadence return.
- [ ] Every wheelie/kerb attempt was spotted with a strap on the frame.
- [ ] Powerchair user can stop reliably and knows their kerb and range limits.
- [ ] Re-test shows which skills moved from fail to pass, and a plan for the rest.
