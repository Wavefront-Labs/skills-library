# Long-cane technique and route travel

**Goal:** a blind or low-vision traveller who can detect obstacles and drop-offs one
step ahead, know where they are, and cross streets with sound judgement — on routes
they choose.

**Scope note:** formal O&M instruction is a credentialed role (e.g. COMS in the US,
habilitation/rehabilitation officers in the UK). Street-crossing teaching in particular
should be led or signed off by a qualified instructor.

## 1. Cane selection
- **Length:** a common starting point is roughly to the sternum/armpit when standing;
  the real test is that the tip lands about one stride ahead, giving one step of
  warning at the traveller's walking speed. Faster walkers need longer canes.
- **Tip:** marshmallow or **rolling ball tip** for constant contact on rough paving;
  pencil/metal tips give crisper surface feedback but snag. Rigid vs folding: rigid
  transmits better; folding is easier to stow.
- Symbol cane (short) and guide cane are *not* long canes — make sure expectations match.

## 2. Core techniques
- **Grip:** index finger extended down the flat of the grip, hand centred in front of
  the body at waist height. Centring is the fault to correct most.
- **Two-point touch:** arc slightly wider than the shoulders, tip touching at each side,
  **in step** — tip lands on the left as the right foot steps (opposite foot), so the
  cane checks where the next foot will go.
- **Constant contact:** tip stays on the ground sweeping side to side; better for
  detecting drop-offs and kerbs, slower and catches on cracks.
- **Shorelining:** follow a wall edge, grass line or kerb with the cane to keep a line.
- **Indoors:** trailing a wall with the back of the hand, diagonal cane technique in
  familiar spaces, upper/lower body protective techniques for head-height obstacles
  the cane cannot detect.
- **Human (sighted) guide:** traveller holds just above the guide's elbow, half a step
  behind; narrow spaces = guide's arm behind the back, traveller steps in line.

## 3. Orientation
- Build routes from **landmarks** (permanent, unique: a lamp post, a change from paving
  to tarmac, a recessed doorway) and **clues** (sounds, smells, sun warmth — changeable).
- Teach the route both ways; it is effectively two routes.
- Pre-plan with tactile maps, verbal descriptions or GPS apps (BlindSquare, Soundscape-
  style apps, phone maps) — apps support, they don't replace cane skills.
- Recovery when lost: stop, use the last known landmark, ask the public with a precise
  question ("Which direction is the post office from here?").

## 4. Street crossings
- Find the kerb (cane drops), square off using the kerb edge and **parallel traffic**
  sound — line your body with the flow of traffic on the street you are crossing alongside.
- At signalled crossings: use the push button's tactile cone/rotating knob or audible
  signal; at uncontrolled crossings wait for a clear gap.
- **Go on the parallel surge** at lights (cars beside you starting off), not when it
  just goes quiet — quiet can mean a turning car.
- Electric/hybrid vehicles are hard to hear at low speed: build in extra margin and
  teach this explicitly.
- Tactile paving: blister (dropped kerb/crossing point), corduroy (hazard/steps) —
  meanings differ between countries; teach the local standard.

## Gotchas
- Cane arc too narrow = shoulders hit poles. Out of step = drop-offs found by the foot.
- Head-height obstacles (wing mirrors, branches) are invisible to the cane.
- Learners often over-rely on a guide; schedule solo segments early on safe routes.
- Weather (snow, rain on roofs, wind) masks sounds — re-teach familiar routes in it.

## Verify
- [ ] Cane length gives one step of warning at their walking pace.
- [ ] Two-point touch in step with centred hand, arc covering the shoulders.
- [ ] Traveller describes their route by landmarks both ways.
- [ ] Crossings: aligned with parallel traffic, crossing on the surge, judged by instructor.
- [ ] They have a lost-recovery plan they can state.
