# Vegetative identification of grasses, sedges and ferns

**Goal:** stop skipping the "green stuff". These groups are identified by a small
set of precise characters, most visible with a 10–20× hand lens.

## 1. Which group is it?
The old rhyme — **"sedges have edges, rushes are round, grasses have nodes"** — is a
useful start, with exceptions:
- **Grasses (Poaceae):** stems usually round and hollow with **solid swollen nodes**;
  leaves in two ranks; leaf sheath usually split; a **ligule** where blade meets sheath.
- **Sedges (Cyperaceae):** stems often **triangular** and solid, no obvious nodes;
  leaves in three ranks; sheath closed (a tube). Some sedges have round stems.
- **Rushes (Juncaceae):** stems round, often filled with pith; small six-tepalled
  flowers like tiny brown lilies.
- **Ferns:** no flowers or seeds; fronds uncoil from **fiddleheads**; spores in
  **sori** under the frond.

## 2. Grasses: the vegetative toolkit
Peel a leaf away from the stem at the junction and look at:
- **Ligule:** membranous (measure length, shape — blunt, pointed, torn), a fringe
  of hairs, or absent. One of the best characters.
- **Auricles:** small claw-like projections at the base of the blade (present in
  some ryegrasses, fescues, barley relatives).
- **Sheath:** split or fused; hairy or not; colour at the base (e.g. some
  species have purple or pink basal sheaths).
- **Blade:** flat, folded or rolled in bud (cut the young shoot across), ridges on
  the upper surface, hairiness, tip shape (boat-shaped tips in meadow-grasses).
- **Shoot cross-section** in bud: folded (V-shaped) vs rolled (circular).

## 3. Grasses in flower
- **Inflorescence:** panicle (branched), spike (spikelets directly on the stem), raceme.
- **Spikelet:** count florets; look at the **glumes** (pair of bracts at the
  base) and **lemmas**, and any **awn** (bristle) — where it arises and its length.
- Look at mature, fully emerged inflorescences; young ones mislead.

## 4. Sedges (especially Carex)
- Separate male and female spikes? Number and position (terminal male, lower female).
- **Utricle** (the sac enclosing the nut): shape, beak, nerves, hairiness. Many
  Carex need **ripe utricles**; early-season material often can't be named.
- Number of **stigmas** (2 or 3) relates to whether the nut is lens-shaped or three-sided.
- Ligule and sheath features also matter (sheath fronts can be distinctive).

## 5. Ferns
- **Division:** pinnate, bipinnate, tripinnate; how pinnules attach.
- **Sori:** shape (round, linear, along margins), position on veins, and the
  **indusium** (protective flap — kidney-shaped, absent, or from the rolled-under margin).
- **Stipe (stalk) scales:** colour, dark central stripe, density — vital in
  buckler-ferns and shield-ferns.
- Look at fertile fronds; some species have separate fertile and sterile fronds.
- Hybrids are frequent in some genera (e.g. buckler-ferns, horsetails among fern allies).

## 6. Practical gotchas
- Mown, grazed or trampled grass lacks inflorescences — learn vegetative keys for
  lawns and pasture.
- Measurements in keys are for mature material; take several.
- Specialist vegetative keys for grasses, sedges and rushes exist and are worth owning.
- Pressed specimens help later checking (see
  natural-sciences.botanical-fungal-identification.specimen-pressing-recording.pressing-plants-and-submitting-biological-records).
