# Spore print and macro features

**Goal:** take a mushroom from "brown toadstool" to genus (and often species) with
a repeatable set of observations — for knowledge and recording. **This skill is not
an edibility guide.** Eating wild fungi needs the stricter protocol in
agriculture-environment.foraging-wild-food.mushroom-foraging.positive-identification-protocol
and an experienced local expert.

## 1. Collect the whole fruitbody
Dig or lever out the **entire base**, not snap it off: the **volva** (cup or sack at
the base) of Amanitas is often buried and is the single most important feature to
miss. Collect a few specimens at different ages if plentiful. Keep different
species separate (paper bags or a compartment box, not plastic). Photograph in situ
first.

## 2. Record at the scene
- **Substrate:** soil, wood (which tree? living or dead?), dung, moss, burnt ground.
- **Associated trees** within ~10–20 m: many fungi are mycorrhizal with specific hosts.
- **Growth habit:** single, scattered, clustered from one base, in rings.

## 3. Macro features checklist
- **Cap:** size, shape, surface (dry, slimy, scaly, fibrous), colour and change
  when wet (hygrophanous caps fade as they dry), margin (striate, inrolled).
- **Underside:** gills, pores (boletes, polypores), teeth/spines, ridges (chanterelles
  have blunt, forked, decurrent ridges, not true gills), or smooth.
- **Gills:** attachment (free, adnate, decurrent, notched), spacing, colour, edges.
- **Stipe:** ring (annulus) or ring zone, cortina (cobweb veil in Cortinarius),
  texture, base bulbous or with volva.
- **Flesh:** cut and note colour change (bruising blue in many boletes), latex
  ("milk") in Lactarius — colour and change.
- **Smell:** flour, radish, aniseed, almond, phenolic/ink are useful characters.
- Avoid taste-testing unless you are trained; some keys use a tiny chewed-and-spat
  taste for Russula/Lactarius, but never do this for anything possibly Amanita.

## 4. Spore print (needed for most gilled fungi)
1. Cut off the cap; place gills down on paper that is half white, half black (or
   on glass/foil so you can test both backgrounds).
2. Cover with a bowl or cup to stop drafts; add a drop of water on the cap if dry.
3. Leave 2–12 hours (overnight is usual).
4. Scrape spores into a heap and judge colour in daylight: white, cream, pink,
   ochre/rusty brown, purple-brown, black.
Spore colour divides gilled genera into major groups. Gotcha: thin prints look
paler than heaped spores; prints from immature caps may fail.

## 5. Deadly lookalike groups to know (by feature)
- **Amanita** (death cap, destroying angels): white gills, white print, ring, **volva**.
- **Galerina** and some small brown mushrooms on wood: rusty-brown print, ring;
  confused with edible clustered wood species.
- **Cortinarius** (some contain orellanine, with delayed kidney damage):
  rusty-brown print, cortina.
- **Lepiota** (small): white print, often scaly caps.
Small brown and white gilled mushrooms should stay at genus or "sp." for most hobbyists.

## 6. Going further
Spores, cystidia and chemical reactions under a microscope are needed for many
genera. Local fungus groups run forays and ID sessions — the fastest route to skill.
Records go to national fungal recording schemes (see
natural-sciences.botanical-fungal-identification.specimen-pressing-recording.pressing-plants-and-submitting-biological-records).

## Verify
- [ ] Whole base collected; volva present or absent noted.
- [ ] Substrate and nearby trees recorded.
- [ ] Spore print colour taken from a heaped deposit.
- [ ] Gill attachment, ring, cortina, bruising, latex and smell noted.
- [ ] Lookalike groups considered and excluded with features, or ID left at genus.

*Draft note:* spore-print duration and lookalike groups are general temperate
guidance; toxic genera and regional species differ worldwide.
