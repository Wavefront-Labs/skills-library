# Year-round tree identification

**Goal:** name a tree or shrub from whatever it offers in the current season, using
a fixed sequence of characters that narrows quickly.

## 1. The first split: arrangement
Look at how leaves (or buds in winter) sit on the twig:
- **Opposite** (pairs at the same node): relatively few temperate genera. A common
  North American mnemonic is **MADCap Horse** — Maple, Ash, Dogwood, Caprifoliaceae
  (honeysuckle family incl. elder, viburnum), Horse-chestnut/buckeye. Spindle,
  lilac and some others are also opposite.
- **Alternate**: most other broadleaves (oaks, birches, cherries, willows, elms…).
- **Whorled**: rare (e.g. some catalpas).
Check several twigs: one broken or dwarf shoot can mislead.

## 2. Leaves (growing season)
- **Simple vs compound:** a compound leaf has leaflets and a bud only at the base
  of the whole leaf, not at each leaflet. Pinnate (ash, rowan, walnut) vs
  palmate (horse-chestnut).
- **Margin:** entire, toothed (single or double), lobed.
- **Base:** symmetrical or lopsided (lopsided base is typical of elms and limes/lindens).
- **Venation**, hairs on the underside, **petiole** features (glands on cherries,
  flattened in aspens).
- Sample from the outer canopy and lower branches; shade leaves and vigorous
  sucker/epicormic shoots are often bigger and atypically shaped.

## 3. Winter twigs
Use a hand lens on a current-year twig:
- **Buds:** shape, colour, number of scales (one cap-like scale in willows, many
  overlapping in oaks), clustering at the tip (oaks), stalked or not (alders).
- **Leaf scars:** shape and number of bundle scars inside (horse-chestnut scars look
  like a horseshoe with "nails").
- **Pith:** cut the twig lengthwise — solid, chambered (walnut), star-shaped in cross
  section (oaks), coloured.
- **Lenticels**, thorns vs spines vs prickles, smell of a scratched twig (cherries
  smell of bitter almond; some birches of wintergreen).
- Persistent fruits on or below the tree are excellent clues.

## 4. Bark and form
Bark changes with age — young trees often have smooth bark that becomes fissured.
Describe it (smooth, peeling, plated, ridged, lenticels in bands) but use it as
supporting evidence. Overall silhouette works best in open-grown trees and once
familiar.

## 5. Conifers
- **Needles:** single, in bundles (pines: count needles per bundle), in tufts
  (larch, cedar); flat vs four-sided; how they attach (peg, sucker-like disc, woody stalk).
- **Cones:** upright (true firs, which shatter on the tree) vs pendent (spruces);
  scale shape and bracts (Douglas-fir's three-pronged bracts).
- **Scale leaves** for cypresses, junipers, thujas — crush and smell; check cones.

## 6. Planted-tree traps
Parks, streets and gardens are full of non-native species, hybrids and cultivars
(weeping, purple, variegated, fastigiate forms) that rarely appear in wild-flora
keys. Grafted trees may show rootstock suckers of a different species. Use
dendrology or arboretum references for urban trees.

## Verify
- [ ] Arrangement confirmed on several twigs.
- [ ] Simple vs compound decided by bud position, not leaflet appearance.
- [ ] Winter ID used buds, leaf scars and pith together, not one character.
- [ ] Planted/cultivated origin considered for park and street trees.
- [ ] The final name matches leaves (or twig), bark and fruit where available.

*Draft note:* the MADCap Horse mnemonic is a North American teaching aid; European
and other floras have different opposite-leaved genera. Smell clues vary individually.
