# Keying a wildflower to species

**Goal:** get from an unknown herbaceous flowering plant to a confident species
name using a regional flora's key, and know when the material is insufficient.

## 1. Gather the right material
- Look at **several plants** in the population; individuals vary.
- You usually need **flowers and fruit**, plus **basal and stem leaves** (they
  often differ). Many keys split on fruit characters.
- Note habitat, soil (wet, chalky/limestone, acid, sandy), and height.
- Don't pick protected species or uproot plants; photograph in situ, or take a
  small piece only where legal and the population is large.

## 2. Family recognition first
Learning ~15–20 big families shortcuts most keys. Practise spotting:
- **Daisy family (Asteraceae):** "flowers" are heads of many florets on a common base.
- **Carrot family (Apiaceae):** umbels (spokes from one point), often ribbed fruits.
- **Mint family (Lamiaceae):** square stems, opposite leaves, two-lipped flowers, often aromatic.
- **Cabbage family (Brassicaceae):** 4 petals in a cross, 6 stamens (4 long, 2 short), pods.
- **Pea family (Fabaceae):** standard/wings/keel flowers, pods, often compound leaves.
- **Pink family (Caryophyllaceae):** opposite leaves, swollen nodes, often notched petals.
- **Rose family (Rosaceae):** 5 petals, many stamens, often stipules.
- **Buttercup family (Ranunculaceae):** many stamens and separate carpels.
Family keys in floras exist too — use them when you can't place it.

## 3. Examine systematically (hand lens 10×–20×)
- **Habit:** annual/perennial, upright/creeping, rosette.
- **Leaves:** arrangement (alternate, opposite, whorled), simple vs compound,
  margin, hairs (type and distribution), stipules present?
- **Inflorescence:** single, raceme, spike, umbel, head, cyme.
- **Flower:** number of sepals, petals (free or fused), stamens; **ovary superior
  or inferior** (above or below where petals attach); symmetry (radial or bilateral).
- **Fruit:** capsule, pod, achene, berry; shape and hairiness.
Count parts on several flowers; numbers vary within a plant.

## 4. Using a dichotomous key
- Read **both** leads of every couplet fully before choosing.
- If unsure, **follow both paths** and see which ends in a description that fits.
- Note the couplet numbers you took, so you can backtrack.
- Look up every glossary term you aren't sure of; "glabrous", "pubescent",
  "sessile", "cordate" etc. carry precise meanings.
- Measurements in keys are usually for typical, mature parts — measure several.
- **Multi-access (polyclave) keys**, often online, let you enter any character in any
  order; useful when your specimen lacks fruit or flowers.

## 5. Check the answer
- Read the **full species description**, not just the key lead.
- Check distribution maps and habitat: a species far out of range is more likely
  a misidentification or garden escape.
- Compare with herbarium-quality images, not a single photo.
- Consider garden escapes, hybrids and cultivated varieties near habitation.

## 6. Common gotchas
- Colour forms (white-flowered variants of normally purple species) are common.
- Leaves on flowering stems differ from basal leaves.
- Very early or late, grazed or mown plants are atypical.
- Apps' suggestions are a starting point only (see
