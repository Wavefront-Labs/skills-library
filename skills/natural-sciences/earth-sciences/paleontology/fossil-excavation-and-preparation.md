# Fossil excavation and preparation

**Goal:** get a fossil out of the ground and cleaned up with its context intact and every
treatment reversible and documented — a specimen with no locality data is scientifically
almost worthless.

## 1. Before digging
- **Permits and ownership.** Collecting rules depend on land ownership and country (e.g.
  US federal land under PRPA needs a permit and specimens go to an approved repository;
  many countries treat fossils as state property). Get written landowner permission.
  *Laws vary widely; check locally.*
- Plan the repository: where will the specimen be catalogued?
- Kit: hand tools (awls, brushes, small knives), consolidant, plaster bandages or burlap +
  plaster, foil/wet paper as separator, GPS, camera with scale, notebook, bags and labels.

## 2. Excavation
1. **Record before removing**: photograph in situ with scale and north arrow; GPS the
   locality; log the stratigraphic level (see natural-sciences.earth-sciences.sedimentology-stratigraphy.logging-a-measured-section).
2. Set a **quarry grid** (1 m squares) and map each element's position and orientation —
   bone orientation and association record taphonomy (transport, scavenging, articulation).
3. Expose from the top only enough to see the outline; work **around** bones, not under
   them yet.
4. Consolidate fragile exposed bone with a dilute reversible consolidant: **Paraloid B-72**
   in acetone (~2–5% for penetration, stronger for joins) or Butvar B-76. Avoid
   cyanoacrylate ("superglue") in the field for structural work — hard to reverse, and it
   ruins material for some chemical analyses.
5. Collect screen-washed sediment from around small vertebrate sites; microfossils
   often matter more than the big bone.

## 3. The plaster jacket
1. Pedestal the block: trench around the fossil leaving a sediment column under it.
2. Cover exposed bone with a **separator** (wet paper towel or foil) so plaster won't stick.
3. Lay plaster-soaked burlap strips over the top and down the sides, overlapping; add
   wood splints for large jackets.
4. When set, undercut the pedestal, **flip**, trim excess matrix, and plaster the
   underside to close the jacket.
5. Label the jacket in marker: field number, element, orientation (up arrow, north), date.

## 4. Laboratory preparation
- Open the jacket from the side that was underneath only if the top is well protected —
  plan which surface to expose first.
- **Mechanical:** air scribes (pneumatic chisels) for hard matrix; pin vices and needles
  under a microscope for detail; air abrasion with fine powder (e.g. sodium bicarbonate,
  aluminium oxide) for delicate surfaces — test on a hidden area.
- **Chemical:** dilute acetic or formic acid (often ~5–10%) for phosphatic fossils in
  carbonate matrix, with buffering and repeated rinse/consolidate cycles. Acid destroys
  carbonate fossils — never use it on shells in limestone.
- Keep a **prep log**: every consolidant, adhesive, concentration, date and area treated.
  Future researchers (isotopes, CT, histology) need to know what's on the bone.

## 5. Cataloguing
Specimen number, locality (precise coordinates stored securely if the site is vulnerable),
stratigraphic horizon, collector, date, identification and confidence, prep log reference.
Images and 3D scans linked to the number.

## Gotchas
- Pulling a bone out "because it's loose" — you lose orientation and association.
- Plaster directly on bone with no separator.
- Over-concentrated consolidant forms a skin that peels and doesn't penetrate.
- Wet specimens drying too quickly crack; dry slowly, in shade.
- Publishing exact coordinates of a vulnerable site invites poaching.

## Verify
- [ ] Permit/landowner permission and repository arranged before collecting.
- [ ] Every element has a quarry-map position, photo and stratigraphic level.
- [ ] All consolidants and adhesives are reversible and logged.
- [ ] Jackets labelled with orientation and field number.
- [ ] Specimen catalogued with locality and prep log linked.
