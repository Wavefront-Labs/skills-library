# Emulsion stability and preservative efficacy

**Goal:** a cream or lotion that looks and feels the same at the end of its shelf
life as on day one, and that survives contamination during consumer use.

## 1. Emulsion design
- **Type:** oil-in-water (O/W; most lotions, light feel) vs water-in-oil (W/O;
  richer, water-resistant). Test type quickly: an O/W drop disperses in water.
- **HLB (hydrophilic–lipophilic balance):** compute the oil phase's required HLB
  (weighted average of each oil's required HLB) and pair emulsifiers (typically a
  low- and high-HLB pair) to match. HLB is a starting point; polymeric and
  liquid-crystal-forming emulsifiers don't follow it neatly.
- **Stabilisers:** water-phase thickeners (xanthan, carbomer, cellulose
  derivatives) slow creaming; fatty alcohols (cetyl/cetearyl) build lamellar
  networks for body. Carbomers lose viscosity with electrolytes and need neutralisation.

## 2. Process (often as important as the recipe)
- Heat phases separately (commonly ~70–75 °C) so waxes are molten; add the
  dispersed phase to the continuous phase with shear.
- **Homogenise** to a target droplet size (high shear at temperature), then cool
  with gentle stirring; add heat-sensitive actives, fragrance and some
  preservatives below ~40 °C.
- Record shear rate/time, temperatures and cooling rate: lab and production
  mixers differ, and droplet size controls stability.

## 3. Stability protocol
Make batches in the **final packaging** and in inert glass as control. Typical set:
- **Temperatures:** 4 °C, room temperature (~25 °C), 40 °C and often 45–50 °C;
  over 1–3 months as a predictor (with real-time data continuing).
- **Freeze–thaw cycles** (e.g. 3–5 cycles of −10/−20 °C to 25 °C, 24 h each).
- **Centrifuge** (e.g. 3,000 rpm for 30 min) as a fast separation screen.
- **Light exposure** for clear packs.
- Measure at each time point: appearance, colour, odour, **pH**, **viscosity**
  (same spindle, speed, temperature), microscopy for droplet size/crystals,
  and packaging interactions (paneling, weight loss, pump function).
Failure modes: creaming, coalescence, Ostwald ripening, crystallisation of waxes or
actives, pH drift, colour change, fragrance loss.

## 4. Preservative system
- Choose broad-spectrum coverage (bacteria, yeasts, moulds) compatible with pH;
  organic-acid preservatives (sorbate, benzoate) need low pH because they work in
  their undissociated form.
- Partitioning into the oil phase and binding by non-ionic surfactants can reduce
  free preservative; **packaging** (airless pumps vs jars) changes contamination risk.
- Check permitted lists and maximum concentrations in each market (e.g. EU
  Cosmetics Regulation annexes).

## 5. Challenge test (preservative efficacy test, PET)
- Inoculate product with defined organisms (commonly *P. aeruginosa*, *S. aureus*,
  *E. coli*, *C. albicans*, *A. brasiliensis*) and count survivors at set days
  (e.g. 7, 14, 28). **ISO 11930** and **USP <51>** define methods and acceptance criteria.
- Test the final formula, ideally after accelerated storage too (preservatives can be lost over time).
- Use an accredited lab; include neutraliser validation so counts are real.

## 6. Claims support and safety
Claims ("24-hour hydration", "non-comedogenic") need supporting studies with
appropriate protocols; product safety assessment (e.g. EU CPSR) requires stability
and micro data.

## Verify
- [ ] Emulsion type confirmed; HLB-based emulsifier choice adjusted empirically.
- [ ] Process parameters recorded, including droplet size target.
- [ ] Stability programme run in final pack with pH, viscosity and microscopy tracked.
- [ ] Preservative compatible with pH and permitted in target markets.
- [ ] Challenge test passed to ISO 11930/USP <51> criteria on the final formula.

practice rather than mandated values; many companies set their own protocols.
