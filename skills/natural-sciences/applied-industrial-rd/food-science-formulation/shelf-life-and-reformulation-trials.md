# Shelf-life and reformulation trials

**Goal:** change a recipe (less sugar, salt or fat; a clean-label swap; a cheaper
ingredient) or create a new one, while keeping it safe, liked, and stable for the
claimed shelf life.

## 1. Know what each ingredient is doing
Before removing or swapping anything, list its **functions**, not just its taste:
sugar gives sweetness but also bulk, browning (Maillard/caramelisation), water
binding (lowers **water activity, aw**), freezing-point depression, and texture.
Salt seasons but also controls microbes, fermentation and protein functionality.
Fat carries flavour, gives mouthfeel and structure. A one-for-one swap usually
fixes taste and breaks one of the other functions.

## 2. Design the safety hurdles
Shelf-stable and chilled foods rely on combined **hurdles**:
- **aw:** most pathogenic bacteria are inhibited below ~0.91, most yeasts/moulds
  below ~0.80–0.88, and microbial growth generally stops below ~0.60.
- **pH:** pH ≤ 4.6 is the widely used boundary for low-acid vs acid foods
  (Clostridium botulinum control); many acidified foods target ≤ 4.2.
- **Preservatives, heat treatment, packaging (MAP, vacuum), chilled storage.**
Reformulating (e.g. reducing sugar or salt) can raise aw and remove a hurdle —
re-evaluate safety, not just taste. Measure aw with a calibrated meter and pH with a
probe suited to the food (spear probes for solids; calibrate with two buffers).

## 3. Bench iterations
- Change one function at a time; use a small DoE for multi-ingredient changes
  (see natural-sciences.applied-industrial-rd.formulation-science.doe-screening-for-new-formulations).
- Measure objectively alongside tasting: texture analyser (firmness, chewiness),
  colour (L*a*b*), viscosity, moisture, aw, pH, Brix.

## 4. Sensory testing
- **Parity (no detectable difference):** triangle or duo-trio tests with enough
  assessors (often 24–36+) and the right statistics; "no significant difference" from
  a tiny panel proves nothing.
- **Preference/liking:** consumer panels (often 60–100+) with 9-point hedonic scales
  or just-about-right (JAR) scales.
- Blind, coded, randomised presentation; palate cleansers; control lighting if colour differs.

## 5. Shelf-life studies
- Identify the **failure mode** first: microbial spoilage, rancidity (peroxide value,
  hexanal), staling/moisture migration, colour fade, carbonation loss, vitamin loss.
- **Real-time storage** at the claimed conditions is the reference.
- **Accelerated shelf-life testing (ASLT):** store at elevated temperature and model
  rate using a Q10 assumption (rate multiplies ~2–3× per 10 °C). Only valid when the
  failure mechanism is the same at high temperature — often false for texture,
  fat crystallisation and microbial spoilage.
- Sample at planned time points with enough units for all tests; include a frozen
  or fresh control where useful.
- **Challenge tests** (inoculating with relevant organisms) by an accredited lab
  for ready-to-eat chilled foods where pathogen growth is plausible.
Set shelf life with a safety margin below the first failure point.

## 6. Pilot-plant trials
Lab batches don't reproduce industrial mixing, heat transfer, hold times, or
filling. Run at pilot scale, record process parameters, and re-check aw, pH,
texture and sensory on pilot product before handover. Verify the process schedule
(e.g. heat-treatment lethality) with the process authority where required.

## Gotchas
- Sugar reduction often fails on texture and shelf life, not sweetness.
- Clean-label preservative swaps (vinegar, cultured dextrose) can change flavour
  and pH — re-test both.
- Labelling: allergen changes, nutrition panel and claims must be updated.

## Verify
- [ ] Every removed/replaced ingredient's functions are covered.
- [ ] aw and pH measured on the new recipe; hurdles re-evaluated.
- [ ] Sensory test design and panel size suit the question (difference vs liking).
- [ ] Shelf life based on real-time data or justified ASLT, with margin.
- [ ] Pilot-plant product matches bench results.
