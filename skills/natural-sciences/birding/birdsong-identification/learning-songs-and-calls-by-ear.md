# Learning songs and calls by ear

**Goal:** identify most of your local birds by sound, and know when a sound is
genuinely unknown rather than forcing it into a familiar species.

## 1. Build a local study list, not a national one
Pull the 30–50 commonest species for your area and season from eBird bar charts or
a local atlas. Learn those first; rarities are pointless until the common baseline
is automatic, because most "odd" sounds are odd versions of common birds.
Add 5–10 species per week; review older ones daily for a few minutes.

## 2. Describe sound in transferable terms
Mnemonics ("teacher-teacher") help some people but vary between guides and regions.
A more robust habit is to describe each sound by:
- **Pitch** and pitch shape (rising, falling, steady, slurred).
- **Rhythm/tempo** (even, accelerating, broken into phrases; repeated or varied).
- **Quality/tone** (whistled, buzzy, trilled, harsh, liquid, metallic).
- **Structure** (single note, phrase repeated, long variable warble).
- **Song vs call:** songs are mostly breeding-season and male-dominated in temperate
  zones; calls (contact, alarm, flight) are year-round and often more useful.

## 3. Read spectrograms (sonograms)
Time runs left to right, frequency (pitch) bottom to top, darkness = loudness.
Pure whistles are thin lines; buzzes are thick bands; harsh calls are vertical
smudges across many frequencies. Free tools: Audacity (switch track view to
spectrogram), Raven Lite, and the spectrograms on xeno-canto and the Macaulay
Library. Comparing your recording's shape with reference recordings is far more
reliable than comparing by memory.

## 4. Record what you hear
- A modern phone captures most songbirds adequately; use a voice-memo or
  recording app that saves uncompressed WAV or high-bitrate audio if possible.
- Get closer before raising gain; turn off auto-gain/noise suppression if the app
  allows — noise suppression can erase high, thin calls.
- Speak a voice note at the end (place, time, what you saw), not over the bird.
- A cheap foam windshield cuts wind roar a lot.

## 5. Gotchas
- **Mimics** (starlings, mockingbirds, some warblers and thrushes) copy others; a
  single phrase is not enough — listen for a full sequence.
- **Regional dialects** and individual variation exist; reference recordings from
  far away may not match.
- **Juveniles** in late summer give incomplete, practice songs.
- **High-frequency loss:** many people lose hearing above ~6–8 kHz with age, which
  removes species such as some kinglets/goldcrests and treecreepers. A spectrogram
  app reveals what you are missing.
- **Ventriloquial calls** and echoes in woodland mislead you about direction and distance.

## 6. Nocturnal flight calls (nocmig)
Migrants call at night; a microphone pointed at the sky and recording overnight
captures them. Review by scrolling the spectrogram for short marks, not by listening
to 8 hours of audio. Many flight calls are short, high and specialist-level hard;
treat IDs as provisional and compare against dedicated flight-call references.
Check local rules before leaving recorders outdoors.

## 7. Use apps as a tutor, not an oracle
Sound-ID apps help to point at candidates (see
natural-sciences.birding.merlin.using-merlin-sound-id-critically), but learning
happens when you find the bird, confirm it visually, and attach the sound to it.

## Verify
- [ ] You can name your 30 commonest local species by song or call blind
      (test with a shuffled playlist).
- [ ] For a new sound you can describe pitch, rhythm and quality before looking it up.
- [ ] You can match a recording against a reference spectrogram.
- [ ] Unknown sounds are logged as unknown, with a recording, not guessed.
