# Documenting a rare bird record

**Goal:** turn a sighting of a rarity (national, regional, county or seasonal) into
a record that a reviewer can accept, and if others chase it, keep access and the
bird undisturbed.

## 1. First minutes: evidence before excitement
1. **Photograph or record first**, even badly — a blurry shot showing structure
   often settles a case (see natural-sciences.birding.bird-photography-for-id.taking-diagnostic-bird-photos).
   Take video if stills are hard; frame grabs work.
2. **Then watch without the camera** and note structure, behaviour and calls.
3. **Note the time, exact location** (GPS pin or grid reference), light, distance,
   optics used, and who else saw it.
4. **Get others on it** if you can do so without disturbing the bird. Multiple
   independent observers strengthen a record.

## 2. Know which list it needs reviewing for
- **National committees** (e.g. a national bird records/rarities committee) handle
  national rarities; **regional/county recorders** handle local ones; **eBird
  reviewers** flag records above filter thresholds for a county and date.
- Each publishes a **review list** and a submission form. Check it before writing,
  because the required detail differs.

## 3. Write the description
Write it from your own notes, **not** from a field guide — reviewers recognise guide
text, and it adds nothing. Include:
- Circumstances: date, time span, location, weather/light, distance, optics.
- Size and structure relative to nearby species.
- Plumage by feather tract; bare parts; calls (with a transcription or recording).
- Behaviour.
- **Elimination of confusion species**, with the specific features that rule each out.
- Your experience of the species and the confusion species (honest, briefly).
- Anything you did *not* see, and any doubts.
- Sketches made at the time are valuable; mark them as field sketches.

## 4. Submit and accept the outcome
- Attach original, uncropped photos plus crops; keep EXIF data.
- Submit even if photos are excellent — many committees still need a form.
- "Not proven" is not "you were wrong": it means the evidence wasn't sufficient.
  Don't argue the decision; you can resubmit with new evidence.
- In eBird, add details in the species comments box; a flagged record without
  comment is likely to be left unconfirmed.

## 5. Releasing news and chasing (twitching) etiquette
- **Sensitive species** (breeding rare raptors, nesting rarities, owls at day roosts)
  — do not release publicly; tell the recorder privately. Many apps and eBird
  auto-hide some sensitive species; don't defeat it.
- Give **access information** with news: where to park, private land, permitted paths.
- When chasing: follow site instructions, keep distance, never flush the bird for a
  better view, no playback for rarities, respect residents. A twitch that annoys a
  landowner can close a site for years.

## 6. Listing hygiene
Keep a list policy you could defend: which taxonomy (Clements/eBird, IOC, or a
national list), whether heard-only birds count, and whether you count birds
not yet accepted. Taxonomy splits/lumps change totals yearly; eBird updates its
taxonomy annually and your list follows automatically.

## Verify
- [ ] Location, time, optics and distance recorded at the time.
- [ ] Description written from field notes, with confusion species eliminated.
- [ ] Media attached at full resolution with metadata intact.
- [ ] Submission sent to the right body using its form.
- [ ] Sensitive-species news handled privately; access info given with any public news.

*Draft note:* committee names, review lists and forms vary by country and region;
confirm with your local recorder.
