# Taking diagnostic bird photos

**Goal:** come away with images that show the features needed to identify (or
prove) the bird — not necessarily beautiful images. A sharp, well-exposed
side-on record shot beats an artistic backlit silhouette.

## 1. Gear that works for ID
- **Bridge/superzoom cameras** (very long equivalent focal length) are the
  cheapest effective tool for record shots of distant birds.
- **Interchangeable-lens body + 400–600 mm-class lens**: better autofocus for flight.
- **Digiscoping** (phone or camera through a spotting scope, ideally with an
  adapter): excellent for distant, static birds such as waders and gulls; poor
  for flight.
- Phones alone: fine for close birds and for filming through binoculars in a pinch.

## 2. Starting settings (adjust to light)
- **Perched:** shutter ≥ 1/500 s at long focal lengths; aperture wide-open or one
  stop down; auto-ISO with a sensible ceiling.
- **Flight:** 1/2000 s or faster, continuous AF (AF-C/AI Servo), a zone or
  tracking AF area, burst mode.
- **Exposure compensation:** white birds against dark backgrounds, −0.7 to −1.3 EV
  to keep detail in whites; dark birds against bright sky, +1 to +2 EV.
  Use the histogram — blown highlights destroy wing-tip patterns that are often diagnostic.
- Shoot **RAW + JPEG** if you can; RAW recovers shadows and highlights.

## 3. Get the diagnostic angles
Before shooting, think what separates the likely candidates (see
natural-sciences.birding.field-identification.separating-confusion-species). Then aim for:
- **Full side profile** showing bill, head pattern and wing/tail length (primary projection).
- **Upperwing and underwing** for raptors, gulls, terns, waders — often only
  visible in flight or a wing-stretch; keep firing as it takes off.
- **Tail pattern** and **rump** (visible on take-off and landing).
- **Head-on or from behind** only as extras.
- Several frames at different moments — single frames can freeze odd postures or
  feathers raised by wind.

## 4. Light and colour gotchas
- Colour is unreliable in photos: auto white balance, shade, sunset and sensor
  processing all shift it. Use structure and pattern first; treat colour cautiously.
- Backlight hides pattern; move so the sun is behind you if the bird allows.
- Heat haze over long distance softens everything — shoot early or get closer.
- Over-sharpening or noise reduction invents or removes fine streaks.

## 5. Fieldcraft
- Approach indirectly and slowly, stop when the bird changes behaviour, stay low.
- Use cars, hides/blinds and natural cover; birds are often less wary of vehicles.
- Never use playback, bait, or flushing to get shots of rarities, nesting birds or
  protected species; it may also be illegal near nests in some countries.

## 6. Keep the file evidential
- Keep the **original** file with **EXIF** (time, focal length) intact; submit
  crops alongside, never instead.
- Note any edits (crop, exposure, sharpening) when submitting to a records
  committee or eBird.
- Make sure the camera clock and time zone are correct — EXIF time is used to
  match photos to checklists.

## Verify
- [ ] At least one sharp side-profile frame with no blown highlights.
- [ ] Features that separate the confusion species are visible in some frame.
- [ ] Originals with EXIF kept; edits disclosed.
- [ ] Camera clock correct; bird not disturbed for the shot.

*Draft note:* shutter-speed and EV figures are typical starting points; they
depend on focal length, stabilisation, and the camera's metering.
