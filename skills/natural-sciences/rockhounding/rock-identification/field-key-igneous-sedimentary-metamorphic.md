# Field key: igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic?

**Goal:** put a sensible name on a hand specimen or outcrop — "fine-grained basalt",
"cross-bedded sandstone", "garnet schist" — by reading texture before colour, and
confirming it fits the local geology.

## Before you look: the map
Open the **geological map** for the area (national survey websites, or apps such as
Rockd/Macrostrat show map units by GPS). Knowing you're on a "Jurassic limestone" unit
or a "granite pluton" turns a guessing game into confirmation. Loose cobbles in rivers
and beaches may come from far away; outcrops are the honest sample.

## Step 1 — Texture: which family?
Look at a fresh surface with a 10× lens:
- **Interlocking crystals, random orientation, no layering** → probably **igneous**.
- **Grains or fragments cemented together**, layering (bedding), fossils, or it fizzes
  in acid → probably **sedimentary**.
- **Aligned minerals, banding, sheen, or wavy/foliated layers**; crystals that grew in
  place (garnets) → probably **metamorphic**. Non-foliated metamorphic rocks (marble,
  quartzite, hornfels) are the hard cases — see below.

## Step 2a — Igneous: grain size × colour
| | Light (felsic: quartz, K-feldspar) | Intermediate | Dark (mafic: pyroxene, olivine) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coarse (crystals visible, intrusive) | granite | diorite | gabbro |
| Fine (crystals too small to see, volcanic) | rhyolite | andesite | basalt |
Special textures: **porphyritic** (big crystals in fine matrix — two-stage cooling),
**vesicular** (gas holes: scoria, pumice that floats), **glassy** (obsidian, conchoidal
fracture). Colour index is an estimate; weathered basalt can look brown.

## Step 2b — Sedimentary: grain size, then composition
- **Clastic** by grain size: conglomerate (rounded pebbles) / breccia (angular) >2 mm;
  sandstone (gritty, grains visible, 1/16–2 mm); siltstone (gritty on teeth — a
  genuine field test, rinse first); mudstone/shale (smooth; shale splits in sheets).
- **Chemical/biogenic**: limestone (fizzes in dilute HCl, often fossils), dolostone
  (fizzes only when powdered), chert (very hard, conchoidal, waxy), coal, evaporites.
- Note sedimentary structures: cross-bedding, ripple marks, graded beds, mud cracks —
  they tell you environment and which way was up.

## Step 2c — Metamorphic: foliation and grade
- **Foliated**, increasing grade: **slate** (dull, splits into thin flat plates) →
  **phyllite** (silky sheen) → **schist** (visible sparkly mica, often garnets) →
  **gneiss** (light/dark mineral banding, coarse).
- **Non-foliated**: **marble** (fizzes, sugary crystals, scratched by steel) vs
  **quartzite** (doesn't fizz, scratches glass, breaks *through* grains, not around them
  like sandstone). **Hornfels** — dark, hard, fine, near intrusions.

## Common traps
- Sandstone vs quartzite: break it — sandstone fractures around grains, quartzite through.
- Basalt vs dark shale: shale is soft (scratched by steel) and layered.
- Granite counter-tops are a commercial name: "granite" in the trade includes gneiss and
  gabbro. Don't train your eye on kitchen samples.
- Weathering rinds and lichen hide everything; break a corner (where permitted).

## Record it
Note locality (GPS), outcrop vs float, map unit, texture, grain size, minerals seen, and
photos with a scale (coin or card). A name without a locality is half the information.

## Verify
- [ ] Fresh surface examined with a lens; texture assigned to a family first.
- [ ] Grain size and dominant minerals estimated and written down.
- [ ] Acid test used to separate carbonate rocks.
- [ ] Name consistent with the geological map unit (or the mismatch explained, e.g. float).
