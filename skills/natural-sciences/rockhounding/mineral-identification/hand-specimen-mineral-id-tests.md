# Hand-specimen mineral identification

**Goal:** go from "shiny rock" to a confident name for common minerals using cheap tools
and a fixed sequence, and know when a hand test cannot settle it.

## Kit (under ~$30)
10× hand lens (loupe); unglazed white porcelain tile (streak plate — the back of a
bathroom tile works); steel nail or knife (~5.5); glass plate (~5.5); copper coin
(~3.5, but modern coins vary — treat as approximate); a known quartz crystal (7);
small magnet; dropper of dilute HCl (10%) or, safer, white vinegar; notebook.

## The sequence (fresh surface first)
Weathered rinds lie. Look at a freshly broken face or clean the specimen.

1. **Lustre** — metallic (looks like metal: pyrite, galena, magnetite) vs non-metallic
   (glassy/vitreous, pearly, silky, greasy, earthy, adamantine). This splits most keys
   in two.
2. **Colour** — note it, but distrust it. Quartz is clear, pink, purple, smoky or
   black; trace impurities change colour, not identity.
3. **Streak** — rub firmly on the tile and look at the powder. Far more consistent than
   colour: hematite is always red-brown, even when the specimen is silver-black; pyrite
   streaks greenish-black; magnetite black. Minerals harder than the tile (~6.5) leave
   no streak — that itself is a clue.
4. **Hardness (Mohs)** — try to scratch the specimen with fingernail (~2.5), coin, steel,
   then try the specimen on glass. Wipe the mark: a real scratch is a groove; a powder
   line that wipes off is the *softer* object leaving residue. Test an inconspicuous spot.
   Mohs: 1 talc, 2 gypsum, 3 calcite, 4 fluorite, 5 apatite, 6 orthoclase, 7 quartz,
   8 topaz, 9 corundum, 10 diamond.
5. **Cleavage vs fracture** — flat, repeatable, light-flashing planes = cleavage.
   Count directions and angles: mica 1 (sheets), feldspar 2 at ~90°, calcite 3 not at
   90° (rhombs), halite 3 at 90° (cubes), fluorite 4 (octahedra). Quartz has none:
   **conchoidal** (shell-like) fracture.
6. **Crystal habit** — cubic (pyrite, halite, galena), hexagonal prism with pointed
   termination (quartz), bladed, fibrous, botryoidal (grape-like: hematite, malachite).
7. **Heft (specific gravity)** — compare in hand with a similar-sized quartz (SG 2.65).
   Galena (7.5) and barite (4.5) feel startlingly heavy.
8. **Special tests** — magnet (magnetite strongly; pyrrhotite weakly); acid: calcite
   fizzes vigorously in cold dilute HCl, dolomite only when powdered; taste (halite) is
   a classic test but skip it with unknowns — some minerals are toxic. Smell, feel
   (talc greasy), double refraction through clear calcite.

## Narrow it down
Use a determinative key (lustre → hardness → cleavage) from a field guide, then confirm
on **mindat.org**, which lists properties and *known localities*. If your mineral has
never been reported in that geology, reconsider.

## Common confusions
| Looks like | Could be | Separate by |
|---|---|---|
| Gold | Pyrite, chalcopyrite, mica flakes | Gold is soft, malleable, golden streak; pyrite H 6–6.5, black streak |
| Quartz | Calcite | Calcite scratched by steel, fizzes, cleaves into rhombs |
| Diamond / gem | Quartz, glass | Hardness, lustre; slag glass has bubbles |
| Feldspar | Quartz | Feldspar shows two cleavages; quartz none |

## When hand tests aren't enough
Many minerals (e.g. amphibole vs pyroxene species, clay minerals, fine-grained
mixtures) need thin sections, XRD or SEM-EDS. Local clubs and university geology
departments sometimes help; say "probable" in your label until confirmed.

## Safety
Wear eye protection when breaking specimens. Wash hands after handling arsenic-,
lead- or mercury-bearing minerals (realgar, galena, cinnabar), and keep fibrous
minerals (possible asbestos) sealed and undusted. Use acid with gloves and eye cover.
