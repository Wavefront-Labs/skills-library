# Collecting trip: planning, access and safety

**Goal:** come back from a field trip with good specimens, all fingers and eyes, and a
site that will still welcome collectors next year.

## 1. Research the site (at home)
- **mindat.org locality pages:** what minerals occur, photos of typical specimens,
  notes on access. Cross-check the date — many listed localities are closed or built over.
- **Geological map** for host rocks; satellite imagery for tracks, dumps, quarry faces.
- Club field-trip reports and guidebooks; state geological survey publications.
- Decide what you're after so you take the right tools and don't waste the trip.

## 2. Access and land status
- Identify the landowner: public land agency maps, cadastral/parcel viewers, or county
  records. "Nobody seems to own it" isn't permission.
- **Public land:** rules differ by agency. US BLM generally allows casual collecting of
  reasonable amounts of rocks and minerals for personal, non-commercial use without
  motorised equipment; National Parks and many state parks prohibit it; wilderness and
  monument rules vary. Check the local office.
- **Private land, quarries, active mines:** written permission, signed waivers and often
  hard hats/hi-vis are required; quarries usually allow access only on organised club
  trips. Mining claims (even unworked) give the claimant mineral rights.
- Note gate etiquette (leave as found) and seasonal closures (fire, nesting, hunting).

## 3. Kit
- **Eye protection always** — rock and steel chips travel far. Gloves; sturdy boots;
  hard hat below faces or in quarries.
- Rock hammer (pick end for soft rock, chisel end for splitting); a 2–4 lb crack hammer
  with cold chisels for hard rock; pry bar; small shovel; screwdriver and brushes for
  pockets.
- Wrapping: newspaper, tissue, egg cartons, zip bags, small boxes; marker and labels.
- Navigation and comms: offline map, GPS, a message to someone with your plan and return
  time; satellite messenger where there's no signal. Water, first aid, sun protection.

## 4. Technique
- **Never strike a hammer on a hammer** or use a rock hammer as a chisel driver —
  hardened faces can spall. Use a soft-headed crack hammer on chisels.
- Work along natural fractures and pocket boundaries; take the specimen with matrix
  rather than prising crystals off.
- Don't undercut overhangs or dig into loose banks; keep clear of the area below another
  collector.
- Wrap each specimen at the spot with a **field label** (GPS/locality, date, notes);
  unlabeled specimens lose most of their value.

## 5. Abandoned mines and dumps
Dumps can be productive, but **do not enter adits or shafts**: bad air (low oxygen,
CO2, radon), unstable timbering, hidden vertical shafts and old explosives kill people
regularly ("Stay Out–Stay Alive"). Watch for subsidence and open stopes near surface.
Some dumps carry arsenic, lead or radioactive minerals — gloves, no eating on site,
wash hands, and keep radioactive specimens separately and ventilated.

## 6. Ethics
- Take only what you can use or trade; leave some for others.
- Fill holes, don't leave debris on roads or in streams, pack out trash.
- Don't sell material collected under personal-use rules.
- Report significant finds (rare species, important fossils) to the relevant
  museum/club.

## Gotchas
- Weather changes access fast: clay roads become impassable after rain.
- Summer heat on dark dumps and quarry floors is extreme — start early.
- A permission granted to a club doesn't extend to you alone next month.

## Verify
- [ ] Site and target minerals researched; current access status confirmed.
- [ ] Written/explicit permission or confirmed public-land rule for the activity.
- [ ] Eye protection, appropriate hammers/chisels, first aid and trip plan shared.
- [ ] Every specimen wrapped and labelled on the spot; site left tidy.
