# Reading strata and extracting fossils

**Goal:** go home with complete, identifiable, legally collected fossils, recorded well
enough to be scientifically useful — instead of a bag of fragments from the wrong layer.

## 1. Find the right rocks
- Fossils live in **sedimentary rock**: marine shales, mudstones and limestones are the
  most productive; sandstones less so; igneous and high-grade metamorphic rocks almost
  never.
- Use the geological map (or Rockd/Macrostrat, PBDB for known occurrences) to find units
  of the right age: e.g. Jurassic marine clays for ammonites, Cambrian–Ordovician shales
  for trilobites, Cretaceous/Neogene sands for shark teeth.
- **Best places:** actively eroding coasts (fresh material each storm), quarry spoil
  (with permission), road cuts (often prohibited — check), stream beds, scree slopes.
  Falling-tide foreshore after winter storms is classic.

## 2. Rules first
Legality varies hugely and changes:
- **US federal land:** under the Paleontological Resources Preservation Act, casual
  collecting of reasonable amounts of common invertebrate and plant fossils is allowed on
  most BLM and Forest Service land for personal use; **vertebrate fossils need a permit**
  and National Parks prohibit collecting entirely. Private land = landowner permission.
- **UK:** many sites are SSSIs or have codes (Jurassic Coast, Scottish Fossil Code) that
  allow collecting loose material on the beach but no hammering of cliffs, and request
  you report scientifically important finds.
- Elsewhere, assume permission is needed and fossils may be state property (several
  countries prohibit export). Verify locally — this skill can't be a legal guide.

## 3. Searching technique
- Walk slowly with the sun behind or low; scan for **symmetry and pattern** — ribs,
  coils, segment rows, pentagonal crinoid ossicles — that rock doesn't make.
- Look at the *bottoms* of fallen blocks and along bedding planes; fossils concentrate in
  particular beds. Once you find one, identify the bed and follow it laterally.
- **Nodules/concretions** in clay often enclose ammonites or fish: weathered nodules
  split along a hidden plane.

## 4. Extraction
- Kit: geological (pick or chisel-ended) hammer, cold chisels, safety glasses, gloves,
  newspaper/tissue, bags, labels, superglue/consolidant, notebook.
- **Never hammer the fossil.** Take a block well around it, then reduce at home. Strike
  away from yourself and others; don't hammer metal-on-metal without eye protection.
- **Shale:** split along bedding with a thin blade worked in gently from the edge; the
  fossil usually lies on the plane — keep **both halves** (part and counterpart).
- **Nodules:** tap along the girdle, or take them whole for preparation.
- Fragile or pyritic fossils: wrap immediately, keep dry.
- Never dig into cliff bases or under overhangs; coastal cliffs fall without warning and
  tides cut off beaches — check tide tables and stay ≥ a cliff-height away from the face.

## 5. Cleaning and stabilising
- Remove soft matrix with water, a soft brush, dental picks, then needles; air-scribes
  (pneumatic engravers) for hard matrix — a separate skill to learn on junk specimens first.
- Consolidate crumbly fossils with **Paraloid B-72** dissolved in acetone (~5% for
  penetration, stronger for glue). It is reversible, unlike superglue and PVA.
- **Pyrite decay** (white powder, sulphur smell) destroys specimens: keep them dry
  (<~50% RH, sealed with silica gel); mild cases are treated by specialists.
- Don't use acid on calcite fossils in limestone — it dissolves the fossil too.

## 6. Identify and record
Note GPS, bed/horizon, rock type, date, collector — on the label *and* in a notebook.
Identify with regional guides, PBDB, museum ID days, or club experts. Unusual
vertebrate or exceptionally preserved finds: contact a museum before further prep.

## Verify
- [ ] Site's legal status and landowner permission confirmed before collecting.
- [ ] Bed/horizon and GPS recorded for each find; part and counterpart kept.
- [ ] Fossils consolidated with a reversible product; pyritic ones stored dry.
- [ ] Tide, cliff and eye safety observed every trip.
