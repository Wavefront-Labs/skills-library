# Collimating a reflector and star-hopping

**Goal:** a telescope that shows sharp images and an observer who can find faint objects
without a GoTo — the two skills that make the biggest difference in visual observing.

## 1. Collimate the Newtonian (reflectors, incl. Dobsonians)
Fast mirrors (f/4–f/5) need it every session; f/8 is forgiving.
1. **Centre-spot the primary** if it isn't (most come marked; a small ring sticker at the
   exact centre).
2. Point the tube horizontally (so nothing falls on the mirror) and use a **collimation
   cap** or Cheshire in the focuser.
3. **Secondary first:** adjust the secondary mirror's tilt screws (small turns!) until the
   reflection of the whole primary mirror sits centred in the secondary.
4. **Primary second:** adjust the primary's collimation knobs (back of the tube; loosen
   lock screws first) until the centre spot sits in the cap/Cheshire's ring.
5. Lasers are quick but only as good as the laser's own alignment — check it by rotating
   it in the focuser; if the dot circles, the laser needs collimating.
SCTs and refractors rarely need it; SCTs are collimated on a defocused star with the
secondary screws only.

## 2. Cool-down and star test
- Put the scope outside **30–60 min** before observing (longer for big mirrors). Warm
  mirrors create tube currents — boiling, smeared images that look like bad optics.
- **Star test:** at high power on a moderately bright star, defocus slightly both ways.
  Concentric rings with a centred shadow = good collimation. Off-centre shadow = recollimate.
  Rings that look different inside vs outside focus point to optical issues or cooling.
- Turbulent, boiling images on the star itself, even in focus, = poor **seeing**
  (atmosphere), not your scope. Planets suffer most; deep-sky less.

## 3. Choosing magnification
- Magnification = telescope focal length / eyepiece focal length.
- **Exit pupil** = eyepiece focal length / focal ratio (mm).
  - ~5–7 mm: widest fields, big faint nebulae (above ~7 mm, light is wasted).
  - ~2–3 mm: galaxies and most deep-sky objects.
  - ~0.5–1 mm: planets, double stars, lunar detail — when seeing allows.
- Typical useful maximum is ~1.5–2× aperture in mm on excellent nights; on average nights
  far less. More power on a blurry night just makes a bigger blur.

## 4. Star-hopping
1. Align the **finder** (red-dot/Telrad and optical finder) in daylight on a distant object
   or at night on a bright star at the eyepiece's centre.
2. Use a chart (printed atlas like *Pocket Sky Atlas*, or an app/Stellarium; see
   natural-sciences.amateur-astronomy.stellarium.planning-an-observing-session).
3. Start at a **bright star** you can see naked-eye near the target.
4. Learn your **field sizes**: Telrad rings (0.5°, 2°, 4°), finder field (~5–7°), low-power
   eyepiece true field (apparent field / magnification). Draw these circles on your chart.
5. Hop along patterns — triangles, lines of stars — counting field-widths in a direction.
6. Confirm the field matches the chart before deciding "it's not visible"; then look for
   the target with **averted vision** (look slightly to the side) and tap the tube to
   catch the eye's motion sensitivity.

## 5. Dark adaptation
Full dark adaptation takes ~20–30 min and is ruined by white light in seconds. Use dim red
light only; set phone screens to red/night mode at minimum brightness.

## Gotchas
- Collimating with the tube pointed up — dropped tools land on the primary.
- Over-tightening the primary lock screws (pinched optics: triangular stars).
- Dew on the finder or secondary — use a dew shield/heater.
- Judging a scope on the first 20 minutes before it cools.

## Verify
- [ ] Centre spot centred in the collimation cap; star test rings concentric.
- [ ] Scope set out to cool well before observing.
- [ ] Eyepieces chosen by exit pupil for the target type.
- [ ] Finder aligned; field-of-view circles known.
- [ ] Three targets found by star-hopping without GoTo.
