# Online shopping and payment safety — don't lose money to a store that doesn't exist

**Goal:** tell a genuine store from a fake one before entering payment details, pick a
payment method that gives real recourse if something goes wrong, and know the steps to
actually get money back.

## 1. Spotting a fake store
- **Too-good-to-be-true pricing** on branded goods (a well-known jacket at 80% off, "closing
  down sale" that's been running for months) is the single biggest tell — real retailers
  don't sell at a loss indefinitely.
- Check the site's age and reputation before buying somewhere unfamiliar: search
  "[site name] reviews scam" in a new tab (lateral reading — see
  searching-and-evaluating-sources), look for a real **physical address and phone
  number** on a Contact page, not just a form.
- A checkout with **only wire transfer, gift cards, or cryptocurrency** accepted (no
  card option) is close to a guaranteed scam — legitimate stores want you to pay by card
  precisely because card networks offer buyer protection they'd rather avoid.
- Sketchy signs in the page itself: poor/inconsistent translation, stock photos instead
  of real product photos, countdown timers that reset on refresh, and a domain
  registered only weeks ago for a store claiming to be long-established.

## 2. Choosing a payment method, in order of protection
1. **Credit card** — the strongest consumer protection; in most jurisdictions you can
   formally **dispute a charge (chargeback)** through your card issuer if goods never
   arrive or are materially not as described, independent of the merchant cooperating.
2. **PayPal or similar buyer-protected processor** — adds its own dispute/resolution
   process on top of whatever the underlying card offers.
3. **Debit card** — moves money directly from your bank account; dispute rights exist
   but are typically weaker and slower than a credit card's, and there's no credit-line
   buffer if a large fraudulent charge clears before you notice.
4. **Bank transfer, cryptocurrency, gift cards** — essentially no recourse once sent;
   reserve these only for payees you already fully trust (a person you know, a
   long-established company doing an invoice), never for an unfamiliar storefront.
- **Virtual/single-use card numbers** (offered by some banks and card apps) limit
  exposure to one merchant and one amount — worth using for a one-off purchase from a
  store you're not fully certain about.

## 3. Checking the payment page itself
- The address bar should show `https://` with the padlock once you reach checkout (see
  browser-security-and-phishing-signals) — a checkout page without it is not
  transmitting your card details securely.
- Legitimate checkout flows don't usually ask for your card's **PIN** online (only the
  card number, expiry, and CVV) — a form asking for a PIN is a red flag regardless of
  how convincing the rest of the site looks.

## 4. If an order never arrives or arrives wrong
1. Check the **actual delivery estimate and tracking** first — "slow" isn't the same as
   "scammed"; genuine delays happen, especially from overseas sellers.
2. Contact the seller through the platform's own messaging (not a personal email/phone
   number they supplied separately) and keep a record of the exchange.
3. If unresolved past the seller's own stated timeframe: open a **dispute with the
   marketplace** (eBay, Amazon, Etsy all have buyer-protection claim processes) or, if
   paid by card, a **chargeback with your card issuer** — most issuers want evidence
   you tried resolving with the seller first, so keep those messages.
4. For counterfeit goods, report to the marketplace specifically as counterfeit, which
   is often fast-tracked separately from a general "not as described" claim.

## Verify
- [ ] You checked an unfamiliar store's reviews/reputation in a separate search before
      paying.
- [ ] Checkout happened over `https://`, and no site asked for a card PIN online.
- [ ] You paid by credit card or a buyer-protected processor for anything from an
      unfamiliar seller, not bank transfer/crypto/gift cards.
- [ ] You know your card issuer's or marketplace's dispute process before you need it.
