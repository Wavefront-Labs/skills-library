# Fair use and fair dealing

**Goal:** judge whether you can quote or use a piece of someone else's work, and know when not to risk it. This is general information, not legal advice; ask a qualified lawyer for commercial or disputed cases.

## United States: fair use
Section 107 lists purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship and research as examples, and courts weigh four factors together:
1. Purpose and character of the use, including whether it is commercial or nonprofit educational. Transformative uses (adding something new, with a further purpose or different character, and not substituting for the original) are more likely to be fair. No single category is automatically fair or unfair.
2. Nature of the copyrighted work (more creative works get more protection).
3. Amount and substantiality of the portion used.
4. Effect of the use on the potential market for the original.

## United Kingdom: exceptions and fair dealing
UK guidance lists specific situations, including non-commercial research and private study (limited extracts only), criticism, review or quotation (any type of work), reporting current events (any type of work other than a photograph), teaching, and parody, caricature and pastiche. A sufficient acknowledgement is required for several of them, and use must be fair dealing.

## Practical guidance
- Short quotes with a citation for review or commentary are the typical safe case; reproducing whole works usually is not.
- Use Creative Commons or public-domain material, or licensed stock, instead where you can.
- To ask permission, state what, where and for how long; keep the written reply.
- Other countries have their own rules; check the national copyright office.
