# Bank, delivery and government impersonation scams

**Goal:** never act on a contact just because it claims to be from a trusted organisation. This page follows US FTC guidance and UK NCSC reporting routes.

## Common scripts
- **Government:** the FTC notes scammers often pretend to contact you on behalf of the government. "Someone who works for the government will never demand payment", and the FTC will never tell you to transfer money to "protect it".
- **Tech company:** a pop-up, call or text says your computer has a problem and asks for remote access. Real security pop-ups never ask you to call a number, and legitimate tech companies do not contact you out of the blue about problems.
- **Fake renewal notices** use well-known brand names. If one might be real, contact the company on a number you already know, not the one in the message.
- **Delivery:** scammers claim a parcel could not be delivered and tell you to contact them, creating urgency.
- **Money at risk:** fake government agents claim your money is at risk and tell you to move it; do not.

## Red flags
Pressure to act now, impersonation of an organisation you trust, a prize or problem, and unusual payment methods (cryptocurrency, wire transfers, payment apps, gift cards).

## What to do
1. Stop. Hang up or do not reply.
2. Contact the organisation using a number, app or website you find yourself (for example the back of your card).
3. Report: forward scam texts to 7726 (US and UK); UK scam emails go to report@phishing.gov.uk; US reports go to ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
4. If you paid, tell your card issuer or bank immediately and ask for a refund. If you shared a password, change it and anywhere it was reused.

## Verify
- [ ] Household members know that no real agency demands payment by gift card, wire or crypto, and that real security pop-ups never ask you to call a number.
