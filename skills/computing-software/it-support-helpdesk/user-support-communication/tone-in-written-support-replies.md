# tone-in-written-support-replies

Goal: written replies read as competent and human; nobody forwards your
email to their director as an example of IT's attitude.

## Principles
- **Acknowledge once, then act**: "Sorry this has held up your morning —
  here's what we're doing." Not three apologies.
- **Positive phrasing**: say what you *can* do. "I can't install that" →
  "That app isn't approved yet; I can request a review, or Excel can do the
  same job — want a quick guide?"
- **No blame**: "The file was saved locally" not "You saved it in the wrong
  place". "It looks like…" softens without being vague.
- **No sarcasm, no jokes about the problem**, no ALL CAPS, minimal
  exclamation marks, no emojis unless the org's chat culture uses them.
- **Match formality** to the user and channel: chat can be brief; email to
  an executive more formal; never casual with an upset user.
- **Use their name**, sign with yours (not "IT Support Team" only).

## Replacements
| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| "As I said before…" | "To recap…" |
| "You need to…" | "Could you… / The next step is…" |
| "That's not my department" | "The network team handles that — I've passed it to them with your details" |
| "Unfortunately there's nothing we can do" | "Here's what's possible…" |
| "Calm down" | (never) — acknowledge the impact instead |
| "ASAP" | a time |

## Angry messages
1. Don't reply instantly in kind; draft, wait a minute, reread.
2. Acknowledge the impact specifically ("missing the client deadline").
3. State facts and the plan with a time.
4. Take it to a call if it escalates in writing; involve team lead for
   abuse (policy applies to written abuse too).

## Gotchas
- Templates/macros sound robotic — personalise the first line.
- Overly casual tone in tickets can surface in audits/complaints; write as
  if the user's manager will read it.

## Verify
QA scoring on written tickets (tone criterion), CSAT comments mentioning
tone, and no complaints quoting your wording.
