# cross-cultural-and-non-native-speaker-support

Goal: users whose first language or culture differs from yours get the same
quality of help and understand the outcome.

## Speaking
- **Global English**: short sentences, common words, no idioms or slang
  ("ballpark", "ping me", "it's a no-brainer"), no sarcasm or jokes that
  depend on tone.
- Slow down slightly, don't shout or over-simplify condescendingly.
- Spell out important items using a phonetic alphabet (NATO: Alpha, Bravo…)
  for ticket numbers, codes and names.
- Accents: if you didn't understand, say so politely and ask them to repeat
  or type it in chat — guessing wastes both your time.

## Confirm understanding
- Ask them to describe the next step back, rather than "Do you
  understand?" (many cultures avoid saying no).
- Send a **written summary** after calls — easier to read (and translate)
  than to follow spoken instructions.
- Use screenshots and numbered steps.

## Translation tools
- Microsoft Translator/Teams live translation can help for simple
  exchanges; check terms of use for confidential data (use enterprise
  tools).
- Keep source sentences simple for better machine translation.
- Offer native-language support if the organisation has it (language
  routing in the ITSM/telephony).

## Cultural expectations
- Directness varies: some users won't complain or disagree openly — look
  for hesitation and ask open questions.
- Formality: titles/surnames may be expected; follow the user's lead.
- Hierarchy: a junior employee may be reluctant to escalate on their
  own behalf — offer to involve their manager when needed.

## Time zones
State times with zones ("14:00 CET / 13:00 UK"); schedule callbacks in
their working hours; follow-the-sun handover notes in plain English.

## Verify
CSAT and repeat-contact rates comparable across regions/languages; QA notes
written follow-ups after complex calls.
