# saying-no-and-offering-alternatives

Goal: the user leaves with a path forward even when the answer to what they
asked is no.

## Structure
1. **Acknowledge the need** behind the request: "You need to send large
   files to the client."
2. **State the limit briefly with the reason**: "Personal Dropbox isn't
   allowed on company laptops because we can't protect client data there."
3. **Offer what you can do**: "You can share from OneDrive with an expiring
   link — I'll set up the first one with you now."
4. **Route the exception** if the alternative doesn't meet the need: "If
   the client requires Dropbox, I'll raise an exception request with your
   manager's approval; security usually answers within 3 days."
5. **Confirm and record** the outcome in the ticket.

## Phrases
| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "No, that's not allowed." | "That one isn't approved, but here's what will do the job…" |
| "That's not my job." | "That's handled by the network team; I'll pass it with your details so you don't have to explain again." |
| "I can't do anything." | "What I can do right now is…" |
| "You'll have to wait." | "The earliest is Thursday because…; in the meantime…" |

## When no is temporary
Capacity or schedule limits: give the real date and reason, not a vague
"soon". Priority conflicts: involve the lead/manager rather than deciding
between users yourself.

## Consistency
Use the policy and catalogue as the basis, not personal judgement — two
agents should give the same answer. Exceptions go through the documented
process with approval, never "just this once".

## Gotchas
- Over-explaining sounds defensive; one reason is enough.
- Hiding behind policy without offering anything makes users go around IT
  (shadow IT).

## Verify
Ticket shows need, decision, alternative offered and any exception request;
no repeat contacts for the same request.
