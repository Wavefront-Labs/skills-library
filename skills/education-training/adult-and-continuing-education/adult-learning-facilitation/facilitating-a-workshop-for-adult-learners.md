# Facilitating a workshop for adult learners

**Goal:** a session where adults leave able to use something on Monday, felt their
experience was respected, and didn't have their time wasted. Draws on Knowles's
andragogy assumptions (adults want to know why, bring experience, are problem-centred,
are internally motivated) — treat these as useful tendencies, not laws; plenty of
adults also want clear direct instruction when the content is new.

## 1. Design around their problems
- Before the session, find out who's coming and why (short pre-survey or a call with a
  sponsor). Collect 2–3 real problems they face.
- Write outcomes as things they'll **do**: "draft a feedback message using the model",
  not "understand feedback".
- Ratio rule of thumb: no more than ~10–15 minutes of input before learners do something
  with it (discuss, try, apply to their case).

## 2. Open well (first 10 minutes)
1. Start on time — latecomers learn the norm from what you do.
2. **Why this matters:** one concrete scenario from their world.
3. Outcomes and agenda with times; breaks named.
4. Quick working agreements: phones, confidentiality of shared stories, it's OK to disagree.
5. A short activity that surfaces their experience ("in pairs: one time this went well
   and one time it didn't — 3 minutes"). This tells you the room's level and signals
   their knowledge counts.

## 3. Use experience as content
- Experiential cycle (after Kolb): concrete experience → reflection → generalisation →
  application. Run activities, then **debrief**: *What happened? So what (what does it
  tell us)? Now what (what will you do)?* The debrief is where learning happens — budget
  as much time for it as for the activity.
- Ask open questions and wait — count to seven silently before rescuing a silence.
- Harvest answers visibly (flipchart, shared doc) and connect them to the model you're
  teaching: "Two of you described exactly step 3."
- When experience contradicts the content, explore it rather than defend: sometimes
  they're right for their context.

## 4. Manage the room
- **Dominant participant:** thank them, then "let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken",
  use structured turns (round-robin, pairs report back), or talk at the break.
- **Quiet participants:** think-pair-share and small groups before plenary; written
  input (sticky notes, chat).
- **Resistant/"sent here" participants:** acknowledge it plainly, ask what would make the
  time useful for them, give them real choice where you can.
- **Expert in the room:** use them ("you've done this — what would you add?").
- Watch energy: after ~60–90 minutes adults need a break or a change of mode.

## 5. Close
- Each person writes one specific action they'll take and when; share in pairs.
- Point to follow-up resources and how to get help.
- Quick evaluation: one thing useful, one thing to change. Finish **on time** — adults
  have childcare, shifts and trains.

## Online variant
Shorter blocks (~60–90 min), cameras optional, use breakout rooms of 3–4, chat for quiet
voices, a producer to handle tech if the group is large.

## Gotchas
- Treating adults like school pupils (permission to go to the toilet, patronising tone)
  loses them in minutes.
- Over-packed agendas leave no time for debrief — cut content, not processing.
- Low literacy or language barriers may be hidden; offer instructions orally and in writing.

## Verify
- [ ] Outcomes are observable and tied to learners' real problems.
- [ ] Input blocks ≤ ~15 minutes, each followed by application.
- [ ] Every activity had a what/so-what/now-what debrief.
- [ ] Most participants spoke or contributed in writing.
- [ ] Ended on time with individual action commitments.
