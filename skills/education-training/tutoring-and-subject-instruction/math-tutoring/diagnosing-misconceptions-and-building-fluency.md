# Diagnosing misconceptions and building fluency in math tutoring

**Goal:** in each session, find *why* the student gets it wrong (not just *that* they do),
repair the underlying idea, and leave them able to do it quickly and accurately a week later.

## 1. Diagnose before you teach (first 10 minutes)
1. **Ask them to solve, thinking aloud.** "Talk me through what you're doing." Don't
   interrupt or correct yet; write down exactly what they say and write.
2. **Look for a pattern, not a slip.** A misconception produces the *same* wrong answer
   reliably. Give 3–4 near-identical items; if the error repeats, it's a rule they hold.
3. **Common culprits worth probing first:**
   - "Multiplication makes bigger" → breaks with fractions/decimals below 1.
   - Adding fractions across (1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5).
   - Longer decimal = bigger (0.125 > 0.5).
   - Equals sign as "the answer comes next" rather than "same value as" (fails on `5 = □ + 2`).
   - Distributing over everything: (a + b)² = a² + b²; √(a+b) = √a + √b.
   - Negative signs dropped when subtracting a bracket.
4. **Use a diagnostic question** — one multiple-choice item where each wrong option maps
   to a specific misconception. The choice tells you the fix.
5. **Check the prerequisite one level down.** Algebra trouble is often fraction trouble;
   fraction trouble is often place-value or division-meaning trouble.

## 2. Repair the idea
- **Create conflict, then resolve it.** Have them check their rule on a case they can verify
  (e.g. 1/2 + 1/2 by their rule gives 2/4 = 1/2 — "does half a pizza plus half a pizza
  make half a pizza?"). A student who sees the contradiction themselves keeps the fix.
- **Concrete → representational → abstract (CRA).** Manipulatives or a drawing (fraction
  bars, number line, area model, algebra tiles) before the symbols, then explicitly link
  each symbol step to the picture.
- **Worked example, then faded example.** You do a full example narrating *why* each step;
  then a near-twin with the last step blank; then with the last two blank; then independent.
  Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from unguided problem solving;
  fade as competence grows (the "expertise reversal" effect means full examples start to
  hurt stronger students).
- **Name the rule in their words** and write it at the top of their notes.

## 3. Build fluency
- **Short, frequent retrieval.** Open each session with 5 mixed questions from previous
  sessions, no notes. Errors here tell you what to reteach.
- **Interleave** problem types in practice sets so the student must choose the method, not
  just execute it. Blocked practice feels better but retains worse.
- **Space it.** Revisit a fixed skill after ~1 day, ~1 week, ~1 month (homework works).
- **Speed only after accuracy.** Timed drills on a shaky skill rehearse the error.

## 4. Session shape (60 min, adjust to age)
| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Retrieval warm-up (mixed, earlier topics) |
| 5–15 | Diagnose today's topic / homework errors |
| 15–40 | Repair: conflict → CRA → worked → faded |
| 40–55 | Independent interleaved practice; student explains one answer back |
| 55–60 | Exit question + set spaced homework; note for parent/next session |

## Gotchas
- **Doing it for them.** If your pen is moving more than theirs, stop. Hand the pen back.
- **Accepting "I get it."** Ask for a fresh, slightly different problem instead.
- **Teaching the school's trick on top of a broken concept** (e.g. "keep-change-flip")
  without meaning. Tricks fail on the first variant; teach the trick only after the idea.
- **Over-diagnosing.** A one-off slip under time pressure isn't a misconception — check
  with a repeat item before reteaching.
- **Wait time.** Count ~5 seconds after a question before helping; most tutors rescue too soon.
- Exam-board/curriculum specifics (which methods the school expects) vary — ask to see the
  student's class notes so you don't teach a conflicting method. *(Draft: session timings
  are a typical pattern, not a validated standard.)*

## Verify
- [ ] You can name the specific misconception (or confirm it was a slip) before teaching.
- [ ] The student resolved a contradiction or explained why the old rule fails.
- [ ] The student solved a *new* variant independently and explained it back.
- [ ] Next session's warm-up shows the skill retained after a gap.
- [ ] Practice was interleaved and spaced, and speed work came only after accuracy.
