# Running a writing conference that improves the writer

**Goal:** the student leaves with a better *process* and a revision plan they can carry out
alone — "better writers, not better papers" (North, 1984). You are not the editor.

## 1. Open (first 5 minutes)
- Ask: **the assignment** (read the prompt/rubric yourself), **due date**, **stage** (ideas,
  draft, final), and **what they want help with**.
- Agree an agenda of 1–3 items. If they ask for "grammar check" on a draft with no thesis,
  say what you notice and negotiate: "Can we look at the argument first, then a grammar
  pattern if there's time?"
- **Have the student read the draft aloud** (or read it aloud to them). Writers hear
  their own gaps and stumbles; you hear the structure.

## 2. Triage: higher-order before lower-order concerns
Work down this list; stop at the first real problem.
1. **Task fit** — does it answer the prompt that was set?
2. **Thesis / main claim** — can the student state it in one sentence? Is it arguable?
3. **Organisation** — does each paragraph serve the thesis, in a logical order?
4. **Development / evidence** — claims supported, evidence explained (not just quoted)?
5. **Lower-order:** sentence clarity, grammar patterns, citation format, spelling.

Fixing commas in a paragraph that will be deleted wastes the session.

## 3. Techniques
- **"Say it back."** "In one sentence, what are you arguing?" Write down what they *say*
  — it's often a better thesis than the one on the page.
- **Reverse outline.** Have the student write one line per paragraph: what it *says* and
  what it *does* for the argument. Repeats, gaps and drift become obvious to them.
- **Ask, don't tell.** "What does this quote prove?" "How would a sceptic respond?"
  "Where does paragraph 3 connect to your thesis?" Move to directive advice only when
  questions stall — tutoring research favours a mix, especially for novice and
  second-language writers, who often need explicit models.
- **Find a pattern, teach it once.** For sentence-level work, pick the most frequent
  error (e.g. comma splices), explain it on one sentence, then have the student find and
  fix the next two themselves.
- **Feedback formula:** what works (specific) → the one or two biggest issues → a
  question or next step. Praise must be specific to be believed.

## 4. Close (last 5 minutes)
- Student writes the **revision plan** in their own words (3–5 bullet actions, in order).
- Confirm they can start the first action alone.
- Note for the next session (or the instructor, if your centre reports).

## Boundaries and integrity
- **Hands off the keyboard/pen.** If you write sentences for them, it's your work. You
  may model *a* sentence on a different topic to show a structure.
- Know your institution's policy on tutoring graded take-home work and on AI writing
  tools; *policies vary widely and change often — check before advising*.
- If you suspect plagiarism, don't accuse: teach source integration and citation, and
  follow your centre's procedure.

## Gotchas
- **Appropriating the paper** — steering it toward the essay *you* would write. Ask
  whose argument it is.
- **Covering everything.** Three marked issues get fixed; twenty get ignored.
- **Only reading silently** — you lose the aloud-reading diagnostic.
- **Assuming grammar errors mean weak thinking**, especially with multilingual writers.
- Session length assumptions (30–60 min) follow typical writing-centre practice; adjust
  for school-age students and attention span. *(Draft.)*

## Verify
- [ ] An agenda was agreed in the first five minutes.
- [ ] Higher-order concerns were addressed before sentence-level ones.
- [ ] The student, not you, articulated the thesis and made the changes.
- [ ] The session ended with a written plan in the student's words.
- [ ] No text in the paper was authored by you.
