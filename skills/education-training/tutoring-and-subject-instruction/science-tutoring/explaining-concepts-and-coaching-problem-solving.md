# Explaining concepts and coaching problem-solving in science tutoring

**Goal:** replace the student's faulty intuition with the scientific model (not just
memorised wording), and give them a repeatable routine for quantitative problems.

## 1. Surface the mental model first
Ask the student to explain or predict *before* you explain: "What happens to the ball's
speed at the top of the throw? Why?" Students arrive with durable everyday intuitions
that coexist with what they recite in class. Classic ones:
- **Physics:** a force is needed to keep things moving; heavier objects fall faster;
  current is "used up" in a circuit; at the top of a throw acceleration is zero.
- **Chemistry:** bonds store energy that is released when they break (breaking *absorbs*
  energy); atoms in a gas are the same size/shape as the substance; a mole is a mass.
- **Biology:** plants get their mass from the soil (mostly from CO₂); evolution acts on
  individuals' needs ("the giraffe stretched"); we inhale oxygen and exhale "used air".

## 2. Conceptual change: predict-observe-explain (POE)
1. **Predict** — student commits to a prediction and reason, in writing.
2. **Observe** — demo, simulation (e.g. PhET), video or data table.
3. **Explain** — reconcile the result. If it contradicts their prediction, ask what
   must be true instead. Then *you* give the canonical model and link it to the event.
Follow with a **transfer item** in a new context — reciting the right answer to the
same scenario is not evidence of change.

Good explanations use one **analogy with its limits stated** (e.g. water pressure for
voltage — and where it breaks), multiple representations (diagram, graph, equation,
words), and connect the equation to physical meaning ("F = ma: what happens to *a* if
you double the mass?").

## 3. A fixed problem-solving routine
Teach one routine and insist on it until it is habit:
1. **List knowns and unknown** with units and symbols.
2. **Draw** — free-body diagram, circuit, particle diagram, energy bar chart.
3. **Choose the principle** ("Which law connects these quantities?") before any equation.
4. **Solve symbolically**, then substitute numbers.
5. **Check:** units (dimensional analysis), sign, order of magnitude, limiting cases.
For chemistry calculations, have them write the full **unit-factor chain** (g → mol →
mol → g) so each conversion is visible and checkable.

Coach with prompts, not answers: "What's acting on the block?" "What's conserved here?"
Use **worked, then faded examples** (see
`education-training.tutoring-and-subject-instruction.math-tutoring.diagnosing-misconceptions-and-building-fluency`).

## 4. Lab-report support
- Structure the discussion as **claim–evidence–reasoning (CER)**: what did you find,
  which data show it, and which scientific principle explains why.
- Check: variables identified (independent, dependent, controlled), units and
  uncertainties on data, graphs with labelled axes and a best-fit line where apt,
  sources of error that are *specific* ("heat lost through the cup lid"), not "human error".
- The report is the student's; follow the same integrity rules as for writing tutoring
  (`education-training.tutoring-and-subject-instruction.writing-tutoring.running-a-writing-conference-that-improves-the-writer`).

## Gotchas
- **Explaining over the misconception** — if you don't surface it, the student files your
  explanation alongside the old intuition and reverts in the exam.
- **Equation hunting** — matching symbols to a formula sheet without a principle. Block it
  by asking for the principle first.
- **Vocabulary ≠ understanding.** "Osmosis is the movement of water across a membrane"
  can be recited with no model of why.
- **Curriculum differences** (sign conventions, notation, required practicals) vary by
  exam board/country. Ask for the syllabus and past papers. *(Draft: misconception list is
  well documented in the literature cited but not exhaustive.)*
- Never run hazardous demos at home; use simulations or videos.

## Verify
- [ ] The student stated their own model before you explained.
- [ ] After POE, they answered a transfer question in a new context correctly.
- [ ] Their written solutions show knowns, diagram, principle, symbolic work and a check.
- [ ] Units are carried through every line.
- [ ] Lab discussions use claim, evidence and reasoning with specific error sources.
