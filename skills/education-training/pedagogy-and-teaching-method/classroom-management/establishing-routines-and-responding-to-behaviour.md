# Establishing routines and responding to behaviour

**Goal:** a room where most behaviour runs on routines nobody has to think about, so your
attention goes to teaching — and where the rare incident is handled calmly, briefly and
followed up, not argued out in front of thirty students.

## 1. Decide the routines before day one
Write down, in steps, exactly what students do for each recurring moment:
- **Entry:** where to line up (if at all), how they enter, seat, and the **Do Now** on the
  board (3–5 minutes, silent, independent, answerable without you).
- **Attention signal:** one signal (raised hand + "3, 2, 1" countdown, or a fixed phrase).
  Pick one and use only that.
- **Materials and transitions:** how books go out, how groups form, how long it takes.
- **Asking for help, leaving the room, finishing early, pack-up and exit.**
Each routine should be specific enough that an observer could tell whether it happened.
"Be respectful" is a value; "pens down, eyes on me, within 3 seconds" is a routine.

## 2. Teach routines like content
1. **Explain** the routine and why it matters (one sentence).
2. **Model** it yourself, including what *not* to do.
3. **Practise** it — actually run the transition — and time it.
4. **Re-do** it straight away if it was sloppy, neutrally: "That took 40 seconds; let's do it in 15."
5. **Reinforce** for the first 2–3 weeks, then after every holiday — routines decay.
Give instructions as **What to Do** directions: concrete, observable, sequential
("Pens down. Turn to face me. Voices off.") rather than "settle down".

## 3. Respond with the least invasive move
Escalate only as far as needed, in roughly this order:
1. **Proximity / look** — move toward the student while still teaching.
2. **Non-verbal** — a gesture or tap on the desk.
3. **Positive group narration** — "Most of row two is already writing" (describe, don't over-praise).
4. **Anonymous individual correction** — "I need one more person facing me."
5. **Private quiet word** at the desk: name the behaviour and the expected action, then walk away
   so they can comply without an audience.
6. **Stated consequence** from the school's published system, delivered flatly.
The aim is the shortest possible interruption to the lesson. Long public lectures reward the
behaviour with attention and give the student a stage.

## 4. De-escalating a heated moment
- Lower your voice and slow down; stand side-on, not face-to-face; keep distance.
- Don't argue about the behaviour in the moment. Offer a limited choice ("You can sit here
  or take the seat by the window") and some take-up time.
- Ignore secondary behaviour (the sigh, the eye-roll) once the primary instruction is met —
  deal with it later if you must.
- If anyone is unsafe, follow the school's call-for-help procedure; that is not a failure.

## 5. Follow up and repair
The consequence is not the end. Within a day, have a short private **restorative
conversation**: What happened? What were you thinking? Who was affected? What needs to happen
now? Agree one concrete thing for next lesson, and greet the student normally at the door —
a clean slate is part of the method.

## Gotchas
- **Inconsistency is the killer.** A routine enforced on Monday and ignored on Friday
  teaches students it is optional.
- **Warnings without follow-through** train students to wait for warning three.
- **Seating plans** are a routine, not a punishment; set one from lesson one and own changes.
- Supply/substitute teachers: ask for the school's behaviour system and the attention
  signal students already know before improvising your own.
- *Unconfirmed generalisation:* the right balance between strict "warm/strict" systems and
  more relational approaches is contested and varies by school context; follow your school's
  policy, which overrides anything here.
- Some behaviour is communication of unmet need (SEND, trauma, hunger). Check the student's
  support plan and the SENCO/inclusion lead before applying sanctions mechanically.

## Verify
- [ ] Every recurring moment has a written routine with observable steps.
- [ ] Routines were modelled and practised, not just announced, and re-taught after breaks.
- [ ] You can name the least invasive move you used for the last three corrections.
- [ ] Consequences come from the school system and were actually followed through.
- [ ] Each incident had a private follow-up conversation within a day.
- [ ] Transitions are timed and getting faster, not slower, over the first weeks.
