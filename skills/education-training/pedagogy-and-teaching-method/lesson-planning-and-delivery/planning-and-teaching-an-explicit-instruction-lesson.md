# Planning and teaching an explicit-instruction lesson

**Goal:** one lesson in which students learn one well-defined thing, practise it with
decreasing support, and leave with evidence (for you) of whether they got it.

## 1. Start from the end
Write, before anything else:
- **Objective (learning intention):** what students will be able to *do* — "calculate the
  area of a compound shape made of rectangles", not "understand area" or "complete worksheet 4".
- **Success criteria:** 2–4 observable checks ("splits the shape", "labels missing lengths",
  "adds the parts with units").
- **The exit task:** the question(s) that will show you who met the objective. If you can't
  write it, the objective is too vague.
One objective per lesson is usually plenty. Lessons fail far more often from too much content
than too little.

## 2. Sequence (a typical 50–60 minute lesson)
| Phase | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Do Now / retrieval | 5–8 min | 3–5 questions on prior learning, especially prerequisites for today |
| Introduce | 3–5 min | State objective, link to prior knowledge, pre-teach key vocabulary |
| **I do** — model | 8–10 min | Think aloud through 1–2 worked examples; show the *decisions* |
| **We do** — guided | 10 min | Students do steps with you; mini-whiteboards; you check every answer |
| **You do** — independent | 15–20 min | Students practise alone; you circulate to the weakest first |
| Exit check | 5 min | Exit ticket on the objective; collect it |
Adjust proportions to the content; the order (model → guided → independent) matters more than
the minutes.

## 3. Modelling that actually helps
- Use **worked examples**, then **faded examples** (later steps left blank), then full problems.
- **Narrate the expert's thinking**: "I check the units first because…". Novices can't see
  decisions you make silently.
- Put a **non-example** or a common error next to the correct example and ask what is wrong.
- Keep the board clean; one example visible at a time reduces split attention.

## 4. Check for understanding before releasing
Don't ask "Any questions?" or "Does everyone get it?" — silence is not understanding.
Instead, everyone answers at once (mini-whiteboards, fingers for multiple choice, a quick
written response) and you scan the room. Rough rule of thumb used by many teachers: release
to independent practice once most of the class (often quoted as ~80%) is getting guided
questions right; re-teach or pull a small group otherwise. *The 80% figure is a heuristic,
not a research threshold.*

## 5. Write the plan so you can use it mid-lesson
A one-page plan beats a ten-page one. Include: objective, exit task, the actual worked
examples (solved in advance — you will find errors), the guided questions, the independent
set graded from easier to harder, likely misconceptions with your response, and timings in the
margin. Prepare an **extension** task and a **scaffolded** version of the independent set.

## Gotchas
- **Activity ≠ learning.** A busy, engaged lesson can teach nothing; check the plan against
  the objective, line by line.
- **Over-long "I do".** Lectures beyond ~10 minutes for younger students lose most of them;
  break explanation into chunks with a check after each.
- **Releasing too early** produces a room of students practising errors.
- **Running out of time** — the exit check is usually what gets cut. Protect it; cut
  independent practice instead.
- Explicit instruction suits new, well-defined knowledge and procedures. For open-ended
  inquiry or discussion lessons, see
  education-training.pedagogy-and-teaching-method.questioning-and-discussion.leading-structured-classroom-discussion.

## Verify
- [ ] The objective names an observable action and has an exit task that tests it.
- [ ] Worked examples were solved in advance and include the thinking, not just steps.
- [ ] Every student responded during guided practice, and you saw the responses.
- [ ] Independent practice started only after the check, with a support and an extension route.
- [ ] Exit tickets were collected and sorted (got it / nearly / not yet) to plan the next lesson.
