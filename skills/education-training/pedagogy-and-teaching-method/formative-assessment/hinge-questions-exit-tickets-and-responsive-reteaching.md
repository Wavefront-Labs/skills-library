# Hinge questions, exit tickets and responsive re-teaching

**Goal:** know — not guess — what most of the class understands at the moments that
matter, and change what you do next because of it. Formative assessment is defined by the
*adjustment*, not by the quiz.

## 1. Write a hinge question
A hinge question sits at the point where the lesson can't safely move on unless students
have the idea.
- **Multiple choice, 3–4 options**, answerable in under a minute, decidable in ~30 seconds.
- **Each wrong option maps to a specific misconception.** If a distractor is just random,
  a student choosing it tells you nothing.
- Students should be unlikely to get it right for the wrong reason.
Example (fractions): "Which is bigger: 1/3 or 1/5? A) 1/5, bigger denominator  B) 1/3
C) They're equal  D) Can't tell." — A reveals "bigger number = bigger fraction"; C/D show
uncertainty about unit fractions.
Write the question **and** what you'll do for each answer before the lesson.

## 2. Get a response from everyone at once
Hands-up gives you the confident few. Use an all-student response:
- Fingers (1–4) on chests, then "Show me" on a count — reduces copying.
- Mini-whiteboards: write, hide, reveal together. Scan for patterns, not individuals.
- ABCD cards, or a live tool (see
  education-training.pedagogy-and-teaching-method.classroom-response-tools.running-live-quizzes-and-reading-response-data).
Follow with **cold call** on a student who picked each option: "Why might someone choose A?"
lets you discuss the error without shaming.

## 3. Decide on the spot
Pre-agree your thresholds (heuristics, adjust to class and stakes):
- **Nearly all right** → move on; pick up stragglers during practice.
- **Split** → have students who disagree discuss for 60 seconds, then re-vote; or re-teach with
  a different example.
- **Mostly wrong / one distractor dominant** → stop and re-teach that misconception directly
  with a new example; don't just repeat your first explanation louder.

## 4. Exit tickets you can sort in five minutes
- 1–3 questions on **today's objective only**, on a half-sheet or slip.
- Include one question that would reveal the main misconception.
- After the lesson, **sort into three piles** — got it / nearly / not yet. Don't write comments
  on them; the sort is the point.
- Plan tomorrow's start from the piles: Do Now targeted at the error, a teacher-table group
  for "not yet", a stretch task for "got it".

## 5. Responsive re-teaching
Re-teaching works best when it is **different**: a new representation (number line instead of
pie chart), a non-example, a worked-example comparison, or student explanations of the error.
Re-check with a parallel question afterwards — re-teaching that isn't re-checked is a hope.

## Gotchas
- **"Any questions?" / thumbs up** measure confidence, not understanding.
- **Collecting data you never act on** is just extra marking. If you won't change the next
  lesson, don't run the check.
- **Grading formative checks** turns them into performance and invites copying; keep them
  low-stakes.
- **Guessing on multiple choice**: with four options a quarter can guess right; ask for a reason
  or use two linked questions when it matters.
- *Uncertainty:* the large effect sizes often quoted from Black & Wiliam's 1998 review are
  contested in later meta-analyses; the practice is well-supported in principle but impact
  depends heavily on whether teaching actually changes.

## Verify
- [ ] Each hinge question's distractors map to named misconceptions, with a planned response for each.
- [ ] Every student answered, and you saw the distribution before moving on.
- [ ] You changed your plan at least once this week because of the evidence.
- [ ] Exit tickets were sorted into three piles and tomorrow's lesson used the piles.
- [ ] Re-teaching was followed by a fresh check on a parallel question.
