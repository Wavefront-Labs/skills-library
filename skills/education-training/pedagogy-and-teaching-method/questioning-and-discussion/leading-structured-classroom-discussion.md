# Leading structured classroom discussion

**Goal:** questions and discussion where *every* student has to think, answers get
built on rather than collected, and the talk moves the class toward the lesson's idea.

## 1. Plan the question sequence, not just the first question
Write 4–6 questions that climb:
1. **Recall / check** — "What does the graph show at 10 seconds?"
2. **Explain** — "Why is the line flat there?"
3. **Compare / apply** — "How would it look if the car were braking?"
4. **Evaluate / open** — "Is 'speed' or 'velocity' the better word here, and why?"
Also prepare **follow-up probes** ("How do you know?", "What would change your mind?") —
most deep thinking happens in the follow-up, not the opening question.

## 2. Make everyone answer in their head
- **Ask, pause, then name.** Pose the question to everyone, wait, then choose who answers.
  Naming first lets the other 29 switch off.
- **Wait time:** Rowe's research found typical teacher wait time under ~1 second; raising it to
  ~3+ seconds increased answer length and participation. Count silently. Allow a second pause
  *after* the answer before you respond.
- **Cold call** warmly and predictably ("I'll ask a few people") — it's routine, not a gotcha.
- **No opt out:** if a student says "I don't know", take an answer from someone else, then
  return to the first student to repeat or extend it.
- **Think-pair-share** before hard questions: 30–60 s alone (write it), 1–2 min with a partner,
  then cold call pairs. Silent writing first prevents the fast talker deciding for the pair.

## 3. Talk moves that deepen answers
| Move | Say |
|---|---|
| Say more | "Can you say more about that?" |
| Revoice | "So you're saying… Is that right?" |
| Press for reasoning | "Why do you think that? What's your evidence?" |
| Add on | "Who can add to what Sam said?" |
| Agree/disagree with reason | "Do you agree or disagree with Priya, and why?" |
| Restate another | "Can you put Jo's idea in your own words?" |
Resist evaluating immediately ("Good!") — it ends the thinking. Collect two or three answers,
then ask the class to compare them.

## 4. Running a structured discussion (Socratic seminar / Harkness-style)
1. **Shared text or stimulus** everyone has read and annotated beforehand.
2. **One genuinely open opening question** with more than one defensible answer.
3. **Norms posted:** refer to the text; speak to each other, not the teacher; build or challenge
   before introducing a new point; invite quieter voices.
4. **Your role:** mostly silent. Track contributions on a seating map (arrows show who spoke to
   whom) — useful feedback afterwards.
5. **Fishbowl** variant for large classes: inner circle discusses, outer circle observes and
   records, then swap.
6. **Close** with individual written reflection: "What did you think before, and now?"
Provide **sentence stems** ("I'd like to build on…", "I see it differently because…") for
younger students and EAL learners.

## Gotchas
- **Guess-what's-in-my-head questions** — closed questions dressed as open — frustrate students.
  If only one answer is acceptable, ask it as a check, not a discussion.
- **Hands-up only** lets a small group dominate; mix in cold call and all-student response.
- **Bouncing answers back too fast** — the teacher repeats every answer, so students stop
  listening to each other.
- **Cold call as punishment** makes it a threat; use it for everyone, including confident students.
- Discussion without preparation (no text, no knowledge) produces opinion swapping. Knowledge
  first, then talk.
- *Unconfirmed:* the wait-time numbers come from older, mostly US science-classroom studies;
  treat ~3 s as a practical guide.

## Verify
- [ ] The question sequence climbs from check to open, with probes written in advance.
- [ ] You paused after every question before naming a student.
- [ ] More than half the class spoke or wrote a response this lesson.
- [ ] At least one answer was built on by another student, not just evaluated by you.
- [ ] In seminars, the seating map shows student-to-student talk, not only student-to-teacher.
