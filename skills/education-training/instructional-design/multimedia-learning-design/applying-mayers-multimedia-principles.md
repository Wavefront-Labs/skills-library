# Applying Mayer's multimedia principles

**Goal:** slides, screens and videos where words and pictures work together, so the
learner's limited working memory goes on the content — not on decoding the layout.

## The model in one paragraph
People process words and pictures through partly separate channels, each with small capacity,
and learn by selecting, organising and integrating information with what they know. Design
therefore aims to (1) **reduce extraneous processing** (load caused by the design), (2)
**manage essential processing** (the inherent difficulty), and (3) **foster generative
processing** (effortful sense-making).

## 1. Reduce extraneous processing
| Principle | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| **Coherence** | Remove interesting-but-irrelevant extras | Decorative stock photos, background music, fun-fact asides ("seductive details") |
| **Signalling** | Highlight structure: headings, arrows, a cue in narration ("the second step…") | Leave learners to find what matters |
| **Redundancy** | Narration + graphic | Narration + graphic + the same words as full on-screen text |
| **Spatial contiguity** | Put labels on the diagram, next to the part | A legend or key the eye must shuttle to |
| **Temporal contiguity** | Narrate while the matching animation plays | Explain first, animate later |

## 2. Manage essential processing
- **Segmenting:** break a complex explanation into learner-paced chunks with a "Continue" control
  rather than one continuous 8-minute animation.
- **Pre-training:** introduce names and characteristics of key parts *before* the process that
  uses them (label the parts of a pump, then show it working).
- **Modality:** for complex graphics with short explanations, spoken narration usually beats
  on-screen text, because eyes stay on the picture. Keep text on screen for terms, numbers,
  steps learners must refer back to, and for non-native-language audiences.

## 3. Foster generative processing
- **Personalisation:** conversational style ("you", "let's") over formal third person.
- **Voice:** a clear human voice; Mayer's earlier work favoured human over machine voices.
  *Modern neural text-to-speech has narrowed the gap and the evidence is being revised — test
  with your audience.*
- **Embodiment / visible instructor:** gestures and eye contact can help; a talking head that just
  covers the diagram doesn't.
- Add prompts that make learners **do something** with the material: predict, explain, self-test.

## 4. Audit procedure for an existing slide or screen
1. What is the one idea on this screen? If you can't say it, split the screen.
2. Strike anything not serving that idea (logos aside).
3. Are words and the relevant part of the picture adjacent? Move labels in.
4. If narrated: is on-screen text a duplicate of the script? Cut to keywords.
5. Is the sequence learner-paced? Add segment breaks for anything over ~1–2 minutes of complex
   content (*heuristic*).
6. Is there a cue to where to look at each moment?
7. Does the learner have to *do* anything? Add a question or prediction.

## Narration scripting tips
- Write for the ear: short sentences, one idea each, active voice.
- Script to the visual: every sentence should point to something visible.
- ~130–150 spoken words per minute is a common planning rate (*varies by narrator and language*).

## Gotchas
- **Expertise reversal:** supports that help novices (worked examples, integrated labels,
  narration) can *hinder* experts. Offer a skip or a lean version for experienced audiences.
- Most principle evidence comes from short, lab-style lessons with novices and immediate tests;
  effects in long, real-world courses are often smaller. Treat them as strong defaults.
- **Accessibility vs redundancy:** captions are required for deaf and hard-of-hearing learners
  regardless of the redundancy principle — make them available (toggleable), not banned.
- "Engagement" add-ons (music, animations) often violate coherence; justify each one.

## Verify
- [ ] Each screen has one stated idea and no decorative or off-topic material.
- [ ] Labels sit on or beside what they label; narration is in sync with visuals.
- [ ] Narrated screens don't repeat the full script as on-screen text (captions available as option).
- [ ] Complex sequences are segmented and learner-paced, with key terms pre-taught.
- [ ] At least one generative prompt per section; an expert skip route exists if the audience is mixed.
