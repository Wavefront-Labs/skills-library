# Mapping and evidencing standards coverage

**Goal:** show — to yourself, a curriculum reviewer, an inspector or an accreditor — that
every required standard is taught and assessed at the level of demand it calls for, with
evidence that would survive a sceptical audit.

## 1. Get the right version of the standards
Standards, exam specifications and competency frameworks get revised. Record the **exact
document, version and date** (e.g. a state's 2023 maths standards, an exam board's spec issue
number, a professional body's competency framework edition). Mapping to a superseded version is
a common audit finding.

## 2. Unpack each standard
For each standard, write down separately:
- **Knowledge** (nouns — what students must know),
- **Skills** (verbs — what they must do),
- **Context/conditions** (e.g. "with technology", "in a real-world problem"),
- **Cognitive demand** — e.g. Webb's Depth of Knowledge (DOK 1 recall, 2 skill/concept, 3 strategic
  thinking, 4 extended thinking) or a Bloom level.
Compound standards ("analyse and evaluate…") are often several targets; split them.

## 3. Build the coverage matrix
A spreadsheet, rows = unpacked standards, columns:
| Std code | Target | DOK | Taught in (unit/lesson) | Assessed by (item/task) | Assessment DOK | Evidence link | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
Status values: *Covered*, *Partial* (taught but not assessed, or assessed below the required
demand), *Gap*. Tag each mapped item to **one primary standard**; secondary links are fine but
don't let one worksheet "cover" twelve standards.

## 4. Check alignment quality, not just ticks
Webb's criteria are a useful frame:
- **Categorical concurrence** — every standard area has enough items/tasks (a common rule of thumb
  in alignment studies is ~6 items per strand for a test; *treat as indicative*).
- **Depth-of-knowledge consistency** — assessments reach the standard's demand. A DOK 3 standard
  assessed only by DOK 1 multiple-choice is *Partial*.
- **Range** — items span the different objectives within a strand, not just one.
- **Balance** — emphasis isn't wildly skewed to the easy objectives.

## 5. Close gaps
For each *Gap/Partial*: add or adapt a lesson, add an assessment item at the right DOK, or document
why it's deliberately addressed elsewhere (another course, a later year). Prefer **adapting existing
tasks** over bolting on new ones. Update the curriculum map at the same time (see
education-training.curriculum-and-assessment-design.curriculum-mapping.building-a-scope-and-sequence).

## 6. Evidence for review
Reviewers usually want to see: the matrix; sample lesson plans and assessment items linked from it;
student work samples at different levels for selected standards; and assessment results by standard.
Use stable links or a shared folder with a consistent naming scheme (`STD-code_unit_item`). Sample
a few rows yourself and follow the evidence chain as a reviewer would.

## Gotchas
- **Keyword matching** ("this lesson mentions fractions, so it covers 4.NF.1") inflates coverage.
  Map to what students actually do.
- **Teaching ≠ assessing.** Many matrices show standards taught but never assessed.
- **Over-mapping** makes the matrix unusable. Fewer, accurate links beat dense ticks.
- **Vendor alignment claims** on purchased materials are often generous; verify a sample.
- **Local standards differ** in structure (strands, domains, competencies); this method adapts,
  but terminology and required evidence depend on the reviewing body. *Check its handbook.*

## Verify
- [ ] The exact standards document and version are recorded.
- [ ] Each standard is unpacked into knowledge, skills, conditions and demand level.
- [ ] Every row has a teaching location and an assessment at matching demand, or a documented reason.
- [ ] A sample of rows was audited end-to-end through linked evidence.
- [ ] Gaps found were fixed in the curriculum map and assessments, not just in the matrix.
