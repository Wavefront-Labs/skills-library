# Building a scope and sequence

**Goal:** a map of what is taught, in what order and for how long, where every unit builds
on earlier ones and nothing important is missing, repeated by accident or squeezed out by time.

## 1. Define the end point
Write what a student should know and be able to do at the end of the course or programme —
from the official specification/standards, the next stage's entry requirements, and your
department's priorities. For programmes, write programme outcomes first, then each course's
contribution.

## 2. List the scope
Break the end point into **components**: core knowledge (facts, concepts), procedures/skills,
and the vocabulary each needs. For each component note:
- **Prerequisites** (what must come first),
- **Difficulty / threshold status** — concepts that, once grasped, change how students see the
  subject (e.g. *opportunity cost*, *energy conservation*),
- **Estimated teaching time** (lessons or hours).
Add up time against the real calendar — teaching weeks minus exams, trips, holidays. Most first
drafts overrun by 20–30% (*rough experience-based figure*). Cut now, not in April.

## 3. Sequence
1. Draw a **prerequisite graph** (sticky notes or a spreadsheet with a "needs" column). Topics with
   no prerequisites can go early; long chains reveal where delays cascade.
2. Group components into **units** of 2–6 weeks, each with a clear question or big idea.
3. Order units by prerequisite, then by other logic: chronology (history), increasing abstraction
   (maths), or anchoring concepts used repeatedly later (science).
4. **Plan revisiting deliberately:** schedule retrieval of earlier units in later ones (a spiral or
   interleaved design), rather than hoping it happens. A simple rule: each unit names 2–3 earlier
   components it will revisit.
5. Put assessment points in the map — end-of-unit checks and cumulative assessments.

## 4. Map format
A spreadsheet works best: rows = units (in order), columns = time window, key knowledge, key skills,
vocabulary, prerequisite units, revisited content, standards/spec references, assessment, resources.
For K-12/multi-year: one tab per year and a **vertical alignment** view (rows = strands such as
"fractions", columns = year groups) to see progression.

## 5. Review the map
- **Gaps:** specification items with no unit. Filter the standards column (see
  education-training.curriculum-and-assessment-design.standards-alignment.mapping-and-evidencing-standards-coverage).
- **Overlaps:** the same content taught as new in two years with no added depth.
- **Load:** units that are too dense for their time window.
- **Horizontal alignment:** parallel classes in the same year teach the same essentials (and
  cross-subject links line up, e.g. graphing taught in maths before science needs it).
- Get colleagues who teach the *next* stage to read it: they spot missing prerequisites fastest.

## Gotchas
- **A list of topics isn't a curriculum.** The map should say what students will *know and do*,
  not just "Unit 3: Ecosystems".
- **Textbook order by default** — textbooks sequence for marketing and page count, not your students.
- **Coverage pressure** pushes revisiting out; protect it in the timings.
- **Static maps**: a scope and sequence is reviewed each year with assessment data; date versions.
- *Uncertainty:* the right balance between depth (fewer topics) and breadth is contested and often
  constrained by external specifications; state the trade-off explicitly.

## Verify
- [ ] End-of-course outcomes are written and traceable to the official specification.
- [ ] Every component has prerequisites and time estimates, and total time fits the real calendar.
- [ ] The sequence respects the prerequisite graph.
- [ ] Each unit names earlier content it revisits; assessment points are on the map.
- [ ] A gap/overlap review was done, including a check by next-stage teachers.
