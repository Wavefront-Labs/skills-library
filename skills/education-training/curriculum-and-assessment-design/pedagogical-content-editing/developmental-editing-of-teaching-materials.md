# Developmental editing of teaching materials

**Goal:** edit a chapter, lesson pack or module so that a learner working alone can actually
learn from it — a different job from copyediting (correctness of language) or SME review
(correctness of content), though you'll flag both.

## 1. Read as the target learner first
Before marking anything, read the whole unit once as the stated audience (age, prior knowledge,
language). Note every point where *you, as that learner*, would get stuck, need to reread, or
couldn't do the practice. Those notes are the core of the edit. Then read again as an editor.

## 2. Structure and sequence
- **Objectives** stated at the start and actually addressed — and assessed — in the unit.
- **Prerequisites:** is every term and skill either taught earlier or explicitly reviewed? List
  terms used before they're defined; it is the most common structural fault.
- **One new idea at a time**; if two hard ideas arrive in one section, propose a split.
- **Concrete before abstract** for novices: an example or situation, then the general rule — or
  rule immediately followed by examples. Long abstract passages with examples at the end are a red flag.

## 3. Explanation quality
For each key explanation ask:
- Does it say **why**, not just what? (Causal links, not lists of facts.)
- Is there a **representation** that fits — diagram, table, analogy — and does the text refer to it
  at the right moment, with labels on the figure rather than in a separate key?
- Does it **anticipate the known misconception** for this topic and confront it (e.g. "heavier objects
  fall faster")? If not, query the author.
- Are analogies' limits stated where they could mislead?
- Is there anything *seductive but irrelevant* (asides, decorative images) to cut?

## 4. Worked examples and practice
- Each new procedure gets **at least one complete worked example** with the reasoning shown, then a
  **faded** example, then independent practice.
- Examples **vary surface features** but keep the underlying structure, so learners don't learn the
  wrong cue.
- Practice **matches the objectives**, ramps in difficulty, and includes mixed (interleaved) review
  of earlier units.
- **Solve every problem** (or have an accuracy checker do so). Answer-key errors are among the most
  damaging and most common errors in published materials.

## 5. Load and language
- Sentence length and vocabulary suited to the audience; readability formulas (Flesch–Kincaid,
  etc.) are a rough check only — they can't see conceptual difficulty.
- Distinguish **technical vocabulary** (teach and use consistently) from **unnecessary difficult
  words** (replace). Use one term per concept throughout; synonyms confuse novices.
- Page/screen layout: related text and figures together, no page turn between a problem and its diagram.

## 6. Inclusion and accessibility
Check names, contexts and images for representation and stereotypes; culturally specific
contexts that the construct doesn't need; alt text and accessible tables for digital editions;
colour-only distinctions.

## 7. Writing the edit
- An **edit letter** (1–3 pages): the 3–5 biggest issues, with examples and proposed solutions, in
  priority order.
- **Marginal queries** for local issues, phrased as questions or suggestions with reasons
  ("Learners haven't met 'gradient' yet — define here or move after 3.2?").
- Don't rewrite the author's voice wholesale; propose patterns and show one example.
- Record decisions in the series **style guide** (terminology, notation, feature boxes).

## Gotchas
- **Scope creep**: a developmental edit isn't a rewrite; agree depth and turnaround with the
  publisher first.
- **Curriculum/spec constraints**: content order may be fixed by an exam specification; flag
  pedagogical concerns but respect the brief.
- **Rights**: images and quoted passages need permissions; flag any without a source.
- *Uncertainty:* evidence on worked examples and cognitive load is strongest for novices in
  well-structured domains (maths, science); apply more flexibly in humanities.

## Verify
- [ ] A full learner-perspective read was done before editing.
- [ ] All terms are defined before first use; prerequisites are taught or reviewed.
- [ ] Key explanations give reasons, a fitting representation, and address the known misconception.
- [ ] Each procedure has worked → faded → independent practice, and every answer was checked.
- [ ] The edit letter lists prioritised issues with examples, and decisions are in the style guide.
