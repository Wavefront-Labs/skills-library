# Writing and calibrating analytic rubrics

**Goal:** a rubric that tells students what quality looks like and lets two markers give the
same piece of work the same score — or close to it.

## 1. Choose the rubric type
| Type | Best for | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Analytic** (criteria × levels grid) | Feedback on several dimensions, most coursework | Slower to score; criteria can be double-counted |
| **Holistic** (one scale, overall descriptors) | Fast, large-scale scoring of a whole performance | Little diagnostic feedback |
| **Single-point** (criteria at "proficient" + space for comments above/below) | Formative feedback, drafts | Not suited to fine-grained grading |
This skill focuses on analytic rubrics.

## 2. Select criteria (3–6)
- Derive criteria from the **learning objectives the task assesses**, not from features that are
  easy to count (word count, number of sources, neatness).
- Each criterion should be **distinct** — "argument" and "use of evidence" overlap less than
  "argument" and "reasoning".
- Ask: if a student scored top on all criteria, would the work be genuinely excellent? If not,
  something's missing.
- Weight criteria if they matter unequally (e.g. analysis ×2), and say so on the rubric.

## 3. Write level descriptors
Usually 4 levels (an even number avoids the default middle; *convention, not rule*).
- **Describe the work, not the student:** "Claim is specific and arguable", not "Student understands".
- **Observable qualities** that change across levels: what's present, how accurately, how fully, how
  independently.
- **Parallel structure:** each level addresses the same aspects so markers compare like with like.
- Avoid descriptors that differ only by vague adverbs ("good / very good / excellent analysis") — the
  most common reason rubrics score unreliably.
- Avoid pure counting ("uses 3 sources") unless quantity really is the criterion.
Example (criterion: Use of evidence):
- *4* — Evidence is relevant, accurately reported and explicitly connected to the claim; limitations noted.
- *3* — Evidence is relevant and accurate; connection to the claim is mostly explicit.
- *2* — Some evidence is relevant; connections are implied or partly inaccurate.
- *1* — Evidence is missing, irrelevant or misreported.

## 4. Test on real work before use
Score 6–10 pieces of past or sample work across the range. Note where you hesitated — those
descriptors need rewording. Check that the rubric total ranks the pieces the way your holistic
judgement does; if it doesn't, the criteria or weights are off.

## 5. Calibrate markers
1. **Select anchor papers:** one clear example at each level for each criterion (or at key grade
   boundaries), with an agreed score and a short rationale.
2. **Calibration session:** markers independently score 3–5 new scripts, then compare and discuss
   disagreements against the descriptors and anchors.
3. **Measure agreement** on a double-marked sample: **exact agreement** (same level) and **adjacent
   agreement** (within one level). Rough targets often used: ~70%+ exact and ~90%+ adjacent on a
   4-point scale (*heuristic; high-stakes contexts use stricter statistics such as weighted kappa*).
4. **Re-calibrate** during long marking periods; drift is normal.
5. Resolve large disagreements (2+ levels) with a third marker.

## 6. Use it with students
Share the rubric with the task, plus annotated exemplars. Have students self-assess against it
before submitting. A rubric students never see is only a marking tool.

## Gotchas
- **Too many criteria** (8–12) slow marking and fragment judgement.
- **Hidden criteria**: markers rewarding something not on the rubric (handwriting, length).
- **Halo effect**: a strong first criterion lifts the rest; mark one criterion across all scripts
  for large sets.
- **Points arithmetic** can produce totals that don't match a sensible grade; map totals to grades
  deliberately.
- **Equity**: check descriptors for language that penalises dialect or non-native writing when
  language isn't the construct.

## Verify
- [ ] Every criterion traces to an objective of the task; 3–6 distinct criteria.
- [ ] Levels describe observable qualities with parallel structure, no adverb-only differences.
- [ ] The rubric was tested on real samples and reworded where markers hesitated.
- [ ] Anchor papers exist for each level, with rationales.
- [ ] A double-marked sample met your agreement targets, or the rubric was revised.
