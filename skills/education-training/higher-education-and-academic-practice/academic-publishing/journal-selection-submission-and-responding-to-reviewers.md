# Journal selection, submission and responding to reviewers

**Goal:** move a finished manuscript to acceptance with the fewest wasted rounds:
right journal first time, no desk rejection for avoidable reasons, and revisions that
close reviewers' concerns.

## 1. Agree authorship before writing the final draft
- Use the field's criteria (e.g. ICMJE in biomedicine: substantial contribution +
  drafting/revising + final approval + accountability). Record contributions with the
  CRediT roles if the journal asks. Settle author order now; it is far harder after
  acceptance.
- Every author gets an ORCID and approves the submitted version.

## 2. Shortlist journals by fit
- Look at where the papers you cite most were published; read 3–4 recent articles in
  each candidate. Does your paper look like theirs (scope, method, length, audience)?
- Check: aims & scope, article types and word limits, typical decision times (often on
  the journal site), open-access options and article processing charges (APC), and
  whether your funder mandates OA or a licence (e.g. CC BY).
- Rank 3 journals: a stretch, a good-fit, a safe option. Decide the fallback order now
  so a rejection costs a day, not a month.
- **Predatory journals:** unsolicited flattering invitations, guaranteed rapid acceptance,
  vague peer review, fees revealed late, fake indexing claims. Check the journal is
  genuinely indexed (in the index's own database, not the journal's claim) and use
  the "Think. Check. Submit." checklist.
- Preprints: most journals accept prior preprints (arXiv, bioRxiv, SSRN, field servers),
  but some still don't — check the policy before posting.

## 3. Prepare to the letter
- Follow author guidelines exactly: structure, word count, reference style (switch it in
  your reference manager), figure resolution and formats, data-availability and ethics
  statements, conflict-of-interest and funding declarations.
- Title and abstract carry the paper at the editor's triage; make the finding explicit.
- **Cover letter** (one page): what you found, why it matters to *this journal's*
  readers, confirmation it is not under consideration elsewhere, any suggested or
  excluded reviewers with reasons. Don't restate the abstract.
- Desk rejection (often within 1–2 weeks) usually means scope/fit, not quality. Reformat
  and send to the next journal promptly — but do fix anything the editor named.

## 4. Responding to reviewers
- Wait a day before drafting. Then copy every reviewer comment into a response document,
  numbered.
- For each: thank briefly (once per reviewer is enough), state **what you changed and
  where** (page/line, or quote the new text), or explain politely with evidence why
  you didn't. "We have clarified this" without a location forces the reviewer to hunt.
- Disagreeing is fine if reasoned; ignoring a point is not. If two reviewers conflict,
  say which route you took and why — the editor arbitrates.
- Track changes or highlight edits as the journal asks. Recheck word limits after adding text.
- Major revision ≠ acceptance; treat it as a serious second chance. Meet the deadline or
  ask the editor for an extension in advance.

## Gotchas
- Simultaneous submission to two journals is prohibited almost everywhere.
- Salami-slicing one study into minimal papers and self-plagiarised text get flagged by
  similarity checks.
- After acceptance, check proofs carefully — it's your last chance and the window is short (often 48–72 h).
- Uncertain: decision times, APCs and preprint policies change; verify on the journal site.

## Verify
- [ ] Authorship and order agreed in writing.
- [ ] Ranked journal list with fallback order; target confirmed legitimate.
- [ ] Manuscript matches author guidelines, statements included.
- [ ] Cover letter argues fit to this journal.
- [ ] Every reviewer point answered with location of the change or a reasoned reply.
