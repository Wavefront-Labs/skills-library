# Writing a manuscript review report

**Goal:** give the editor a clear, evidence-based judgement and give the authors a
report they can act on — in about 3–6 hours of work for a typical empirical paper.

## 1. Before you accept the invitation
- **Competence:** can you judge the core methods? If you can only review part (e.g. not
  the statistics), accept only if you tell the editor which part you cover.
- **Conflicts:** recent co-authorship, same department, personal rivalry, competing
  work in progress, financial interest. Declare or decline. If you recognise the authors
  in a blinded review, tell the editor.
- **Time:** reply within a few days; accept only if you can meet the deadline (often
  2–4 weeks). A late "yes" is worse than a prompt "no, try these two people".

## 2. Read in passes
1. **Skim** abstract, figures, conclusions: what is the claim, and is it interesting
   to this journal's readers?
2. **Close read** methods and results: does the design answer the question? Sample size,
   controls, measures, analysis choices, missing data, multiple comparisons, whether
   data/code are available as the journal requires.
3. **Check the link**: do conclusions stay within what the results show? Over-claiming
   (causal language from correlational data, generalising beyond the sample) is the
   single most common substantive problem.
4. Spot-check references for missing key work — but do not demand citations of your own
   papers unless genuinely essential (and then say so plainly).

Take notes with page/line numbers as you go.

## 3. Structure the report
1. **Summary (3–5 sentences):** what the paper claims and how, in your own words. This
   shows the authors and editor you understood it — and exposes misreadings early.
2. **Overall assessment:** strengths first, briefly and specifically; then the main
   judgement on contribution and soundness.
3. **Major issues (numbered):** problems that affect whether conclusions hold. For each:
   what the problem is, why it matters, what would resolve it (analysis, data, reframing).
4. **Minor issues (numbered, with line refs):** clarity, figure labels, typos only where
   they impede meaning. Do not copy-edit.
5. **Confidential comments to the editor** (separate box): your recommendation and
   anything not for the authors (suspected duplicate publication, ethics concerns,
   your confidence level).

Tone test: would you be comfortable signing it? Critique the work, never the people.
Phrase as "the analysis does not show X because…" not "the authors clearly don't understand…".

## 4. The recommendation
- **Accept** — rare at first round. **Minor revision** — sound, needs clarification.
  **Major revision** — conclusions might hold but need substantial new analysis or
  reframing. **Reject** — fatal design flaw, or no fit/contribution. Many journals hide
  the recommendation from authors; it goes to the editor, who decides.
- Be consistent: a "minor revision" with eight major issues confuses the editor.
- Don't ask for a different study. Ask for what *this* paper needs to support *its* claims.

## 5. Ethics and confidentiality
- The manuscript is confidential: don't share, cite, use its ideas, or upload it to
  tools that retain data (many publishers now prohibit putting manuscripts into
  generative-AI tools — check the journal's policy).
- Don't contact the authors. Report suspected misconduct (image manipulation,
  plagiarism, fabricated data) to the editor only, with specifics; COPE guidance
  describes how editors handle it.

## Re-reviewing a revision
Check the response letter point by point against the changed manuscript. Judge whether
*your* concerns were addressed; don't raise brand-new issues unless the revision
introduced them or they are serious.

## Verify
- [ ] Conflicts declared; competence limits stated.
- [ ] Summary accurately restates the claim.
- [ ] Each major issue says why it matters and how to fix it.
- [ ] Recommendation matches the severity of the issues.
- [ ] Report is courteous enough to sign; confidentiality kept.
