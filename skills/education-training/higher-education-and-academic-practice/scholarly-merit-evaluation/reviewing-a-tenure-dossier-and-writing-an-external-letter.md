# Reviewing a tenure dossier and writing an external letter

**Goal:** a judgement of a scholar's contribution that a committee outside your
specialism can rely on — specific, calibrated, fair, and tied to the institution's own
criteria. The same method applies to prize and honours committees and fellowship
elections.

## 1. Before agreeing
- **Arm's length:** most institutions exclude former supervisors, recent co-authors
  (often within ~5 years), close collaborators and personal friends. Declare any link;
  let the requesting chair decide.
- Note the deadline (letters are typically requested 2–4 months ahead) and whether the
  letter may be seen by the candidate — confidentiality rules differ by country, state
  law and institution. Write as if it might be read.

## 2. Read the criteria first
- Get the institution's criteria for **this** rank (assistant→associate with tenure,
  associate→full, research vs teaching-focused track) and the expected weighting of
  research, teaching and service. Some requests explicitly ask you not to comment on
  teaching or whether you would grant tenure at your own institution — follow the brief.
- Ask what "national/international reputation" means to them if unclear.

## 3. Read the dossier for quality and trajectory
- **Read the work**, not just the CV: the 3–5 pieces the candidate nominates as their
  best. What question does each answer, how well, and what changed in the field because of it?
- **Independence:** for early-career candidates, is there a research programme distinct
  from the PhD/postdoc supervisor's? Look at authorship patterns, sole/senior-author
  pieces, own grants, own students.
- **Trajectory:** productivity and ambition since the last review, work in pipeline,
  sustainability of the agenda.
- **Venue and field norms:** a monograph is the main currency in much of the humanities;
  conference papers in parts of computer science; multi-author papers in large-team
  sciences. Explain norms the committee may not know.

## 4. Use metrics responsibly
- Citation counts, h-index and journal impact factors vary by field, career stage and
  database (Google Scholar runs higher than Scopus/Web of Science). Never compare across
  fields raw. Statements like DORA and the Leiden Manifesto argue against using journal
  impact factor as a proxy for an individual's work — many institutions have signed them.
- If you cite numbers, give the source, date and a field-appropriate comparison; let the
  qualitative reading carry the argument.

## 5. Write the letter
Structure (often 2–4 pages):
1. Who you are, your expertise relevant to the case, and your relationship to the
   candidate (or confirmation there is none).
2. The candidate's research programme in plain terms for non-specialists.
3. Assessment of the key works: contribution, originality, rigour, influence — with
   specific examples.
4. Standing: comparison with peers at a similar career stage, if requested. Use named
   comparators only if asked; otherwise describe the reference group.
5. Other requested areas (grants, mentoring, service, public engagement).
6. Summary judgement against **their** criteria.

Calibration: committees read inflated language as default. "Excellent" can read as
lukewarm; reserve superlatives and back each with evidence. Silence on a topic is
read as a negative signal. If you have reservations, state them clearly and fairly —
coded hints help no one.

## Committee side (prizes/promotions)
Agree criteria and scoring before reading; declare conflicts and leave the room;
discuss each case against criteria, not against the last case; record reasons.

## Verify
- [ ] Conflicts declared; arm's-length confirmed.
- [ ] Letter addresses the institution's stated criteria and only what was asked.
- [ ] Assessment rests on reading named works, with specific examples.
- [ ] Any metrics are sourced, field-normalised and secondary.
- [ ] Judgement is explicit, calibrated and defensible if read by the candidate.
