# Scoping, searching and synthesising a literature review

**Goal:** produce a review that a sceptical examiner could reproduce and that *argues*
something (where the field stands and what is missing), rather than an annotated list
of everything you read. This covers the general/narrative and scoping review used in
theses, grant backgrounds and paper introductions. For full systematic review and
meta-analysis method see
`social-sciences.social-research-methods.systematic-review-meta-analysis.protocol-screening-effect-sizes-and-random-effects`.

## 1. Bound the question before you search
- Write the review question in one sentence. Break it into 2–4 **concepts** (e.g.
  population, intervention/phenomenon, context, outcome — PICO/PCC frames help).
- Decide the **boundaries up front**: date range, languages, publication types (peer-
  reviewed only? grey literature? theses?), disciplines. Write the reason for each.
- Pick the review type honestly: *narrative* (expert-selected, argued), *scoping* (map
  what exists, no quality appraisal required), *systematic* (exhaustive, protocol-
  driven, appraised). Calling a narrative review "systematic" is the most common way to
  get marked down.

## 2. Build a search you can rerun
- For each concept list synonyms, spelling variants (behaviour/behavior), and the
  database's controlled vocabulary (MeSH in PubMed, ERIC thesaurus descriptors).
- Combine: synonyms with `OR` inside a concept, concepts with `AND` between them. Use
  truncation (`teach*`) and phrase quotes (`"formative assessment"`). Syntax differs per
  database — check each one's help page; Google Scholar ignores much of it.
- Search at least two subject databases plus one multidisciplinary index (Scopus or
  Web of Science where your institution has access). Google Scholar is useful for
  chaining and grey literature but is not reproducible (results shift, 1,000-result cap).
- **Log every search**: database, date, exact string, filters, hit count. Put it in a
  spreadsheet now; reconstructing it at write-up time is painful and usually wrong.
- Calibrate: do 3–5 papers you *know* are central appear? If not, the string is missing
  a synonym.

## 3. Screen against criteria, in two passes
- Export to a reference manager (see
  `education-training.higher-education-and-academic-practice.reference-managers.building-a-clean-citable-library-in-zotero-class-tools`)
  and deduplicate.
- Pass 1: title/abstract against written inclusion/exclusion criteria. Pass 2: full text.
  Record the exclusion reason at full-text stage — a PRISMA-style flow count
  (identified → screened → full text → included) is expected for scoping/systematic
  work and is good practice for any thesis chapter.
- **Citation chaining:** check reference lists of included papers (backward) and "cited
  by" (forward). This routinely finds 10–30% more relevant work than database searching
  alone (rough figure; varies hugely by field).

## 4. Synthesise, don't summarise
- Build a **synthesis matrix**: rows = sources, columns = the themes/variables you
  care about (method, sample, finding, limitation, theoretical lens). Themes emerge down
  the columns, not across the rows.
- Write paragraphs by **theme or debate**, each citing several sources and saying how
  they agree, conflict, or differ in method. "Smith (2019) found… Jones (2020) found…"
  paragraph chains are the hallmark of a weak review.
- Weigh evidence: note sample sizes, designs and contexts; a single small study is not
  "the literature shows".
- End with the **gap** that follows from the synthesis — not "no one has studied X in
  my town" but a conceptual, methodological or contextual gap that matters.

## Gotchas
- Stopping too early vs never stopping: stop when new searches and chaining return
  mostly papers you already have (saturation), and say so.
- Seminal older work falls outside narrow date limits — add it by chaining and justify.
- Over-reliance on review articles makes your review a review of reviews; go to primary studies.
- Uncertain: exact database syntax and institutional access vary; verify locally.

## Verify
- [ ] Question, concepts and boundaries written down with reasons.
- [ ] Search log lets someone else rerun every search.
- [ ] Known key papers are retrieved by the strings.
- [ ] Inclusion/exclusion criteria applied and counts recorded.
- [ ] Paragraphs are organised by theme, each synthesising multiple sources.
- [ ] The stated gap follows logically from what you reviewed.
