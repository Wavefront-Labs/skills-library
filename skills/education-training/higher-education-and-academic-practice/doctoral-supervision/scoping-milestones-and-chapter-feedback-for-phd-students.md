# Scoping, milestones and chapter feedback for PhD students

**Goal:** a student who finishes on time with a defensible thesis and becomes an
independent researcher. Most late or failed completions trace back to an
over-scoped project, unclear expectations, or feedback that arrived too late to use.

## 1. Set expectations in the first month
Agree and write down (a one-page supervision agreement works):
- Meeting frequency and format (e.g. fortnightly, 45 min, student sends agenda and
  progress note 24 h before; student writes the action log after).
- Feedback turnaround (e.g. 10 working days for a chapter) and how drafts are sent.
- Authorship norms for papers from the project.
- Working hours, leave, and how to raise problems — including with a co-supervisor
  or graduate director if the issue is you.
- Institutional milestones and their dates: confirmation/upgrade/qualifying exam,
  annual reviews, maximum registration period, funding end date. The funding end date
  is the real deadline.

## 2. Scope a finishable project
- Help the student frame 2–4 research questions, each producing one substantial chapter
  or paper. Ask: what is the minimum set of results that makes a thesis? What is
  nice-to-have?
- Identify the **critical-path risks** early: access to data/participants/equipment,
  ethics approval lead times (often months), skills to learn. Build a plan B for the
  riskiest one.
- Draft a timeline backwards from submission: writing-up typically needs 6–12 months in
  practice even when everyone plans for less. Revisit scope at every annual review —
  scope creep is the norm, cutting is the skill.

## 3. Run the meetings
- Student-led: they bring progress, problems, decisions needed. You ask questions before
  giving answers ("what do you think the options are?").
- Shift over time from directive (year 1) to consultative (final year). By the end the
  student should be correcting you on their topic.
- Watch for avoidance (repeated "nearly finished", missed meetings) — it is usually
  stuck work or wellbeing, not laziness. Ask directly and refer to student support where
  appropriate; you are not their counsellor.

## 4. Chapter feedback that builds independence
- Ask what kind of feedback they want on this draft (structure? argument? polish?).
  Early drafts get **structural** feedback; line editing an early draft wastes both your times.
- Read once without commenting, then write a **summary note**: 3–5 priority issues in
  order, what works, and the single most important next step. Then margin comments.
- Comment on patterns once ("tense shifts — see p. 4 example, please fix throughout")
  instead of correcting every instance.
- Distinguish "must change" from "suggestion". Never rewrite whole paragraphs — the thesis
  must be the student's work, and examiners can tell.
- Keep turnaround promises; if you can't, say when you will.

## 5. Preparing for the viva/defence
- Choose examiners with the student (per local rules): expert, fair, arm's-length.
- Run a **mock viva** with a colleague 2–4 weeks before: opening "summarise your
  contribution", method justifications, limitations, "what would you do differently",
  and the hostile-question rehearsal.
- Student re-reads the thesis, prepares a list of known errata, and knows the latest
  papers in the area published since submission.
- Explain outcomes (pass, minor/major corrections, resubmit) and that corrections are
  normal.

## Verify
- [ ] Written agreement covers meetings, turnaround, authorship and escalation.
- [ ] Project has a minimum viable thesis and a plan B for the top risk.
- [ ] Timeline works back from funding end, revisited annually.
- [ ] Feedback leads with prioritised summary and matches draft stage.
- [ ] Mock viva done; student can state their contribution in two minutes.
