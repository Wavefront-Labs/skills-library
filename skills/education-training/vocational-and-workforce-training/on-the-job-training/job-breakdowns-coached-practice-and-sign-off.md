# Job breakdowns, coached practice and sign-off for on-the-job training

**Goal:** every new or cross-trained worker learns the job the same correct way, quickly,
from a peer who knows how to teach — and you have a record of who is competent at what.

## 1. Pick what to train and track it (skills matrix)
- Rows = people, columns = tasks; cells = level (e.g. 0 not trained, 1 in training,
  2 can do with supervision, 3 independent to standard, 4 can train others).
- Prioritise tasks that are **safety-critical, quality-critical, frequent, or only one
  person can do** (single points of failure).

## 2. Write a job breakdown sheet (per task)
Have the best experienced worker do the job while you note, in three columns:
| Important steps | Key points | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| what is done — short, verb-first (≈3–4 words) | anything that makes or breaks the step: **safety**, **quality**, a **knack** that makes it easier | why each key point matters |
Example (tightening a flange): Step "Snug bolts" — key point "star pattern, by hand" —
reason "seats gasket evenly; prevents leaks".
Keep it to roughly 3–8 steps per breakdown; split long jobs. The sheet is the
*trainer's* notes, not a handout to replace training. Validate it by having another
expert follow it.

## 3. Teach with the four steps (TWI Job Instruction)
1. **Prepare the worker** — put them at ease, say what the job is, find out what they
   already know, get them interested, position them to see it from their own angle.
2. **Present the operation** — do it while telling each **important step**; do it again
   stating steps **and key points**; again with steps, key points **and reasons**.
   Present no more than they can master at once.
3. **Try-out performance** — the learner does it while you watch and correct errors
   immediately; then does it again explaining each step; again explaining key points;
   again with reasons. Continue until you know they know.
4. **Follow up** — put them to work, name who to ask for help, check frequently, taper
   off coaching.
Motto: **"If the worker hasn't learned, the instructor hasn't taught."**

## 4. Shadowing and coached practice plan
For a role, not just a task:
- **Week 1:** shadow — observe, ask questions, do safe sub-tasks; trainer explains reasons.
- **Weeks 2–3:** coached practice — learner does, trainer beside them, tasks in order of
  difficulty from the matrix.
- **Week 4+:** supervised independence — trainer nearby, scheduled check-ins.
Write the plan per role with dates and the trainer's name. Durations vary hugely with
job complexity — *these are illustrative, not standards*.

## 5. Sign-off
- Use a **checklist derived from the breakdown**: each key point observed, on real work,
  on more than one occasion (e.g. 3 consecutive correct performances).
- Signed by a qualified assessor (not only the buddy who trained them, for safety-critical
  tasks), dated, with the document revision used.
- Update the skills matrix; set **refresh/recheck** dates for rarely performed or
  high-risk tasks.

## Gotchas
- **"Sit with Nellie"** shadowing with no structure — the learner inherits Nellie's
  shortcuts and bad habits.
- Too many key points: if everything is key, nothing is. Keep only make-or-break items.
- Teaching too much at once; overload causes errors that look like attitude problems.
- Sign-off as a formality ("watched once, signed"). Auditors and incident
  investigators will read these records.
- Breakdowns drift from the real process after equipment or SOP changes — version them.
- Pressure from production to skip follow-up.

## Verify
- [ ] A skills matrix exists and flags single points of failure.
- [ ] Each priority task has a validated breakdown with steps, key points and reasons.
- [ ] Trainers can demonstrate the four-step method.
- [ ] Role plans have dated shadow, coached and independent phases with a named trainer.
- [ ] Sign-offs are evidence-based, dated, versioned and reflected in the matrix.
