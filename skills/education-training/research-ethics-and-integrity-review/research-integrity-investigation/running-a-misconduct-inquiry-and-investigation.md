# Running a misconduct inquiry and investigation

**Goal:** a fair, timely, well-documented process that reaches a defensible finding on
whether fabrication, falsification or plagiarism (FFP) occurred — protecting the
research record, the complainant and the respondent.

*US PHS-funded research follows 42 CFR 93; the 2024 revision took effect in 2026 with
changed procedures and timelines — **check the current text and your institution's
policy**. NSF has its own rule (45 CFR 689). Other countries use national codes (e.g. UK
Concordat, Australian Code). Not legal advice; involve counsel.*

## 1. Definitions that drive everything
- **Fabrication:** making up data or results. **Falsification:** manipulating materials,
  equipment, processes, or changing/omitting data so the record is inaccurately
  represented. **Plagiarism:** appropriating another's ideas, processes, results or words
  without credit.
- Misconduct requires a **significant departure** from accepted practices, committed
  **intentionally, knowingly or recklessly**, proven by a **preponderance of the
  evidence**. **Honest error and differences of opinion are not misconduct.**
- Authorship disputes, sloppiness and QRPs usually fall outside FFP — route them to other
  processes.

## 2. Stages
1. **Assessment** — RIO decides whether the allegation falls within the definition and
   is sufficiently credible and specific to proceed.
2. **Sequestration** — at or before the respondent is notified, secure **all** relevant
   records: lab notebooks, instrument raw files, servers, emails, images in original
   format. Make forensic copies, log chain of custody, give the respondent copies so
   research can continue.
3. **Inquiry** — preliminary fact-finding: is an investigation warranted? Not a finding of
   misconduct. Short written report; respondent may comment.
4. **Investigation** — a committee with relevant expertise and **no conflicts**: examine
   all evidence, interview complainant, respondent and witnesses (record/transcribe; let
   interviewees correct transcripts), pursue leads, including additional instances.
5. **Findings report** — allegations, evidence reviewed, analysis for each element,
   finding per allegation, respondent's response addressed, recommended actions.
6. **Institutional decision** and, where required, reporting to the funder/ORI;
   correction of the record (journals, retractions).
Timelines are regulatory; track every deadline and request extensions in writing.

## 3. Evidence triage
- **Images:** check for duplication within and across figures (rotated, flipped,
  rescaled, spliced lanes) — tools such as ORI's forensic actions, Proofig or ImageTwin
  can flag candidates; **always compare against original raw files**. A duplicated image
  that matches a different original file is far stronger evidence than a figure
  comparison alone. Image-processing note: legitimate adjustments applied to the whole
  image differ from selective alteration.
- **Data:** compare reported numbers with raw instrument output and notebooks;
  impossible statistics (e.g. GRIM-type inconsistencies, implausibly low variance, digit
  distributions) are flags, not proof. Check file metadata (creation dates vs reported
  experiment dates).
- **Text:** similarity reports (e.g. iThenticate) need human reading — methods
  boilerplate and properly quoted text are not plagiarism; look for unattributed ideas
  or substantial verbatim copying of results and discussion.
- **Intent:** look for patterns, concealment, prior warnings, and whether the respondent
  had training and knew standards.

## 4. Fairness and protections
- Confidentiality for complainant, respondent and witnesses as far as possible.
- Protect good-faith complainants and witnesses from **retaliation**.
- Respondent: notice of allegations, access to evidence, chance to respond at each stage,
  advisor allowed per policy.
- Restore reputations when allegations are not upheld.
- Bad-faith allegations are themselves a matter for action.

## Gotchas
- **Late sequestration** — data vanish once the respondent knows.
- Treating a forensic-tool flag as a finding.
- Committee members who are collaborators or competitors.
- Drifting into judging the science's merit rather than FFP.
- Respondent leaving the institution doesn't end the institution's obligations.
