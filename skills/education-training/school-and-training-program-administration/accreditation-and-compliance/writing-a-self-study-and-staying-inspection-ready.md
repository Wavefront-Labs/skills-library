# Writing a self-study and staying inspection-ready

**Goal:** go into any accreditation review or inspection with evidence already
organised, a self-assessment that matches what reviewers will find, and no compliance
gaps discovered for the first time on the day. The specific standards come from your
accreditor or inspectorate (regional/institutional and programmatic accreditors,
national inspectorates such as Ofsted or ERO, VET regulators); the method transfers.
For the auditor's side of the table see
`law-government.regulatory-inspection-and-certification.certification-and-accreditation-audit.management-system-certification-audit`.

## 1. Build the standards crosswalk (start 12–24 months out for accreditation)
A spreadsheet, one row per standard / criterion / sub-criterion:
| Standard | What it requires (in your words) | Owner | Evidence (file links) | Our rating | Gap / action | Due |
- Assign a **named owner** per row, not a department.
- Evidence = documents that show practice, not just policy: minutes showing a decision
  was made and acted on, data reports, samples of student work, completed checklists,
  training records. A policy with no evidence of use is a finding waiting to happen.
- Keep evidence in one controlled repository with a naming convention
  (`Std3.2_assessment-moderation-minutes_2026-03.pdf`). Reviewers often get read access.

## 2. Write the self-study / self-evaluation
- **Evaluative, not descriptive.** For each standard: what we do → evidence of impact →
  honest judgement → what we're improving. "We have a mentoring scheme" is description;
  "New-teacher retention rose from 70% to 85% after the scheme; mentor training remains
  inconsistent, addressed by…" is evaluation.
- Name weaknesses yourself with a credible plan. Reviewers trust institutions that
  know their weaknesses; hiding one they then find damages every other claim.
- Follow the accreditor's template, length limits and required data tables exactly.
- One editor for voice consistency; section drafts come from owners.

## 3. Keep a compliance calendar (always, not just before visits)
Recurring obligations with due dates and owners, e.g.:
- statutory returns and data submissions; annual reports to the accreditor;
- safeguarding/child-protection training renewals and background-check rechecks;
- fire drills, health & safety inspections, first-aid certificate expiries;
- policy review dates; staff qualification/registration checks; complaints log review.
Review it at a standing monthly meeting. Most findings are expired items nobody owned.

## 4. Rehearse the visit
- Mock review 3–6 months before: a colleague from another institution or an internal
  team samples rows from the crosswalk and asks to see evidence and talk to staff.
- Brief staff, students and governors on what reviewers may ask — prepare them to speak
  honestly from experience, not to recite scripts (reviewers notice).
- Logistics: base room, schedule, document access, named liaison, list of who is
  available when.

## 5. After the visit
- Log every recommendation/finding as an action with owner and date; report progress
  to the board/governors. Many accreditors require a formal follow-up report.
- Roll the crosswalk forward — it becomes next cycle's starting point.

## Gotchas
- Standards get revised between cycles; check you're using the current version.
- Safeguarding/statutory failures can override strong teaching evidence in many
  inspection frameworks — check those first.
- Uncertain: timelines and report formats vary greatly by accreditor and country.

## Verify
- [ ] Every standard has an owner, evidence links and a candid rating.
- [ ] Self-study is evaluative and names weaknesses with plans.
- [ ] Compliance calendar has no overdue items.
- [ ] Mock review held; staff briefed.
- [ ] Findings tracked to closure with board oversight.
