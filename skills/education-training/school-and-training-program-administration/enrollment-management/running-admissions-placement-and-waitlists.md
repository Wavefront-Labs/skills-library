# Running admissions, placement and waitlists

**Goal:** hit enrolment targets with the right students in the right classes, using
rules that are applied identically to everyone and that you could defend to an
auditor, a regulator or an aggrieved parent. Applies to schools, colleges and training
providers; legal rules on admission criteria vary a lot — check yours.

## 1. Treat intake as a funnel and count every stage
Enquiry → application started → complete application → offer/admitted → accepted/
deposit → registered → **attending on census/count day** → retained at term 2.
- Track weekly against the same week last year. A drop at one stage tells you where to
  act (e.g. many incomplete applications = form too long or missing documents).
- Forecast intake from historical conversion rates per stage, not from applications alone.

## 2. Write the rules before applications open
- **Eligibility:** age, residence/catchment, prerequisites, documents required (identity,
  proof of address, prior transcripts, immunisation where required). List acceptable
  documents explicitly.
- **Oversubscription criteria** in priority order (e.g. siblings, distance, lottery) —
  for public schools these are often prescribed by law or admissions codes; publish them.
- **Placement:** which assessments, cut scores or bands, who decides borderline cases,
  how a student/parent appeals. Placement tests should place, not exclude; check that
  cut scores predict success in the course and review them yearly.
- Ensure processes don't discriminate (protected characteristics, disability adjustments
  for placement tests, language access for families).

## 3. Process applications consistently
- One checklist per application; status codes in the SIS/CRM (incomplete, complete,
  offered, declined…). Never keep the real status in someone's inbox.
- Chase incompletes on a schedule (e.g. day 3, day 10) with the specific missing item.
- Second-person check on decisions near a cut-off or involving discretion.

## 4. Waitlists that stay fair
- Rank by the published criteria; record the rank and the date.
- Offers from the waitlist carry a **response deadline** (e.g. 3–5 working days) — then
  move on. Log every contact attempt.
- Tell people their position or band honestly; don't keep families hanging "just in case"
  without telling them the realistic odds.
- Freeze the waitlist at a published date or after census.

## 5. Prevent melt
"Melt" = accepted students who never show. Counter it with: a named contact, reminders
for each remaining task (forms, fees, orientation, placement), an orientation that feels
like belonging, and calling no-shows on day 1–3 rather than waiting for the count.

## 6. Track retention
- Define withdrawal reasons as a fixed list and record one for every leaver.
- Early-warning flags from attendance, assessment and engagement data (e.g. attendance
  under 90%, failing two courses) trigger a named staff follow-up within a week.
- Review retention by cohort, programme and subgroup each term; act on gaps.

## Gotchas
- Inconsistent exceptions ("we let one family in late") create precedent and complaints.
- Students counted on census day but gone a week later inflate funding claims —
  regulators audit attendance evidence.
- Data protection: application documents are personal data; restrict access and set retention periods.
- Uncertain: lawful admission criteria and census rules are jurisdiction-specific.

## Verify
- [ ] Funnel counts tracked weekly with year-on-year comparison.
- [ ] Eligibility, priority and placement rules published before opening.
- [ ] Every waitlist offer has a deadline and a contact log.
- [ ] Day-1 no-shows contacted within three days.
- [ ] Every leaver has a recorded reason; early-warning flags have owners.
