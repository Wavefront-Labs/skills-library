# Running observation and feedback cycles

**Goal:** measurable improvement in classroom practice across a school or training
programme, by observing often, focusing feedback on one change at a time, and
separating growth conversations from formal evaluation as far as local rules allow.

## 1. Set up the system
- **Frequency beats length:** 10–15 minute visits every 1–3 weeks per teacher give far
  more data and trust than one annual hour-long observation. Calendar the visits
  (block them like meetings) or they will be eaten by operations.
- **Shared language:** use one teaching framework the staff know (a published rubric such
  as Danielson's Framework for Teaching or Marzano, a state/national standards framework,
  or the school's own "non-negotiables"). Feedback references it.
- **Be explicit about purpose:** is this visit developmental or evaluative? Teachers'
  candour depends on knowing. Formal evaluation procedures (number of observations,
  notice periods, union/contract rules) are set by law, contract or policy — follow
  them exactly, they are the first thing checked in any dispute.

## 2. Observe with low-inference notes
- Record what was said and done, with times: "10:42 T asks 'Who can tell me…?' — 3 hands,
  T calls on first hand." Not "students disengaged".
- Look at **students' work and talk**, not just the teacher: what proportion are doing
  the thinking? Ask two or three students "what are you learning and how do you know
  if you've got it?"
- Note one or two strengths and the single **highest-leverage** gap — the change that
  would most improve learning soonest (often routines/behaviour first, then
  checking for understanding, then rigour).

## 3. The feedback meeting (within 48 hours, ~20 minutes)
A widely used sequence (adapted from instructional-coaching practice):
1. **Praise** one specific practice, with evidence.
2. **Probe:** ask a question that leads to the gap ("What was the goal for the last
   10 minutes? How did you know who'd met it?").
3. **Name the action step:** one bite-sized, observable change ("Use cold call with
   whiteboards for the exit check"). Not "improve engagement".
4. **Plan and practise:** script it together and rehearse it in the room for 3–5
   minutes. Practice is what turns feedback into change.
5. **Set the follow-up:** when you'll come back to see it.
Write the action step down in a shared log both can see.

## 4. Follow up and close the loop
- The next visit looks first for the action step. Mastered → new step. Not yet → why
  (skill, will, conditions) and re-practise.
- Track action steps across staff in one spreadsheet: teacher, date, step, status.
  Patterns across many teachers (e.g. half working on checking for understanding) become
  **whole-staff PD** topics — PD planned from evidence, not from the catalogue.

## 5. Formal evaluation
- Gather evidence over time; no surprises at the summative meeting.
- For a struggling teacher, move early to a written support plan with specific targets,
  support offered, and review dates — this is fair to them and required for any later
  capability/performance process.
- Calibrate with fellow evaluators by co-observing and scoring separately, then comparing.

## Gotchas
- Giving five pieces of feedback means none get done.
- Observing only "problem" classrooms signals that visits are punitive.
- Uncertain: evaluation frequency, rubric choice and what may be recorded are
  jurisdiction- and contract-specific.

## Verify
- [ ] Every teacher visited on a calendared cycle.
- [ ] Notes are low-inference and include student evidence.
- [ ] Each meeting ends with one written, practised action step and a follow-up date.
- [ ] Action-step log shows movement over a term.
- [ ] PD topics trace to observed patterns.
